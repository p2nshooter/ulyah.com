At some point in almost every family the question comes up: “Everyone in my class already has a cell phone, why don’t I?” At the latest when they move on to secondary school, their own smartphone becomes an issue for many children. Parents are then caught between two worries. On the one hand, they don't want to exclude their child and know that class chats, bus connections and arrangements with friends are often done via smartphone these days. On the other hand, they have concerns about screen time, inappropriate content, cost traps and contact with strangers. These instructions help you make the decision calmly, set up the device sensibly and find rules together that everyone can live with.

## When is a child ready?

There is no fixed age at which a smartphone is appropriate. Many children get their first device when they are around ten to eleven years old, around the time they go to school. However, experts in media education recommend focusing less on the age of the child and more on the maturity of the child. A few questions that you can honestly answer for your child are helpful.

- Can the child stick to agreements even when no one is watching?
- Can it distinguish between advertising, fake news and real information to some extent?
- Does he tell you about things that unsettle or annoy him, or does he tend to keep such experiences to himself?
- Does he handle his things carefully and does he know how much a device in this price range costs?
- Do you really need your smartphone, for example for a long walk to school or appointments, or is it mainly about peer pressure?

If several answers are still uncertain, that is not a reason for a final no. But it can be a sign to start with an intermediate step. A simple push-button cell phone allows calls and text messages without the internet. An older smartphone that is only used at home on WiFi is a good practice phase. In this way, the child learns how to use the device before they can be online without restrictions while on the move.

### A word about children's smartwatches

Some parents are considering buying a children's smartwatch with tracking function instead of a smartphone. Caution is advised here. The Federal Network Agency in Germany banned watches with a listening function that allows parents to listen to their child's surroundings unnoticed in 2017. Even models without this function often collect a lot of location data and are not always well protected in terms of security. Anyone considering such a watch should carefully check where the data is stored and also talk to the child about whether constant tracking is really necessary.

## Which device and which tariff?

The first smartphone doesn't have to be a new top model. Children drop devices, lose them or forget them on the bus. A solid mid-range device or a well-preserved used smartphone is sufficient. However, it is important that the device continues to receive security updates for several years. An old cell phone from the drawer for which the manufacturer no longer provides updates is not a good choice for a child because security gaps remain.

Pay attention to the following points when purchasing:

- **Updates:** The manufacturer should promise at least another three to four years of security updates for the model.
- **Robustness:** A sturdy case and glass screen protector protect against the most common damage.
- **Storage:** At least 128 gigabytes makes sense so that photos, school apps and updates have enough space.
- **Battery:** A long-lasting battery ensures that the device can still be reached on the way home.

When it comes to tariffs, a prepaid contract is usually the best solution to start with. The credit is limited and there are no unpleasant surprises on the bill. A small data volume of a few gigabytes is enough for messengers, timetables and occasional research if WiFi is available at home. The **third-party block** is very important. It prevents paid subscriptions or payments to third-party providers from being billed via the mobile phone bill, for example by accidentally clicking on advertising in a game. The block can be set up free of charge with the mobile phone provider, usually in the customer account or via the hotline.

## Set up parental controls

Both large systems come with free tools that parents can use to adjust their child's smartphone to suit their age. It's best to set up the device together with your child and explain what you're setting and why. Secret surveillance destroys trust, open agreements strengthen it.

### Android: Google Family Link

On Android devices, the Google Family Link app takes on this task. Parents install the app on their own smartphone and link it to the child's Google account. A supervised account is created for children under the minimum age applicable in their country. You can then use these functions, among others:

- App installations must be approved by parents.
- Daily usage times and a bedtime can be set at which the device will be locked.
- Individual apps can be limited in time or blocked completely.
- Content in the Play Store is filtered by age ratings.
- The location of the device can be displayed if necessary.

### iPhone: Screen Time and Family Sharing

For Apple devices, you set up a child account using Family Sharing. In the settings you will then find the controls for your child’s device under “Screen Time”. There you can set a time out for the night, set app limits for certain categories such as games or social networks and filter content by age under “Restrictions”. With the “Request to Purchase” feature, every download from the App Store must be confirmed by a parent, even for free apps. It is important to assign a separate code for screen time that the child does not know and that is different from the unlock PIN.

### What technical filters cannot do

As helpful as these tools are, they do not replace accompaniment. Filters do not recognize all unsuitable content, especially not in messengers, comment columns or videos. Many children also find ways to get around restrictions surprisingly quickly, such as using a friend's Wi-Fi or a second device. The child safety lock is therefore more of a railing than a wall. It provides support in the beginning and should be gradually relaxed as it gets older so that the child learns to take responsibility for itself.

## Agree on rules together

Rules work best when children help shape them. Before the smartphone is handed over, sit down together and talk about the most important points. A proven aid is a media usage agreement. Such a contract can be put together free of charge from building blocks and printed out on the website mediennutzsvertrag.de, an offer from klicksafe and the Internet ABC. The contract makes agreements visible and gives everyone a basis if there are later discussions.

Typical points that belong in such an agreement:

- **Times:** How long can the smartphone be used on school days and weekends?
- **Places:** Does the cell phone stay outside the child's room while eating, doing homework, or at night?
- **Apps:** Which apps are allowed and how are new requests decided?
- **Photos:** Photos of other people will only be taken and sent with their permission.
- **Costs:** Who pays for the credit and what happens if it is lost or damaged?
- **Problems:** If something unpleasant happens, the child is allowed to talk about it at any time without the device being taken away immediately.

The last point is particularly important. Many children keep stressful experiences quiet because they fear they will have to give up their smartphone. When it's clear that openness won't be punished, parents are much more likely to get the information they need to help.

## Messenger, class chat and social networks

For most children, Messenger is the most important app of all, because it is where arrangements with friends and class chat take place. It's worth taking a look at the terms of use: For many messengers and social networks, the minimum age is 13 years, and for some functions it's higher. There are reasons for these limits, as the services are designed for adults and young people, not for primary school children.

Class chats are practical, but can also be stressful. Messages come at any time of day, often in large numbers, and the tone is not always friendly. Talk to your child about not having to read and respond to every message immediately and about muting chats. Also explain that images containing violence or sexual content may not be forwarded or stored under any circumstances, even “just for display.” Forwarding such content may be a criminal offense. Instead, the child should immediately inform a trusted adult.

For social networks such as Instagram, TikTok or Snapchat, it is advisable to set accounts private, not share your location and limit messages from strangers. Many platforms now offer their own parenting functions, which can be used to limit usage time or see who the child is friends with. But the most important thing remains to talk about what the child is experiencing there.

## Recognize dangers and deal with them together

Parents should be aware of some risks so that they can discuss them with their child in good time.

**Cyberbullying:** Insults, embarrassing photos or exclusion in group chats can put a lot of stress on children because they reach into their own rooms. Encourage your child not to fight back, but to screenshot evidence, block the person and let you or a teacher know. In serious cases, the school can be contacted and a report can be filed.

**Cybergrooming:** This is what it is called when adults specifically seek contact with children in order to manipulate and sexually abuse them. Perpetrators often pretend to be their own age, give compliments, give gifts in games and then push for private chats, photos or meetings. Explain to your child that no one on the Internet is allowed to ask them to keep secrets from their parents and that they should get help immediately if contact feels strange.

**Cost traps:** In many free games you can buy coins, gems or equipment for real money. With purchase confirmation from parents, third-party blocking and a discussion about such mechanisms, unpleasant surprises can be avoided.

**Lack of sleep:** The smartphone in the children's room tempts them to chat or watch videos for a long time in the evening. A fixed charging location outside the bedroom is one of the most effective rules of all.

If your child or you need advice, the number can help against grief. The children's and youth hotline can be reached free of charge and anonymously on 116 111, the parents' hotline on 0800 111 0 550.

## A timetable according to age

The following overview is not a rigid requirement, but rather an orientation on how freedoms can develop with age.

| Dude | Possible use | Accompaniment by parents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| up to about 9 years | Sharing family devices, learning apps, video calls with grandparents | Parents are there and select content |
| 10 to 12 years | First own device, messenger with family and friends, timetable, school apps | Parental controls, fixed times, purchase confirmation, regular conversations |
| 13 to 15 years | first social networks with private profile, more free time | Loosen restrictions gradually, talk about experiences |
| from 16 years | largely independent use | Remain the contact person and provide support if there are any problems |

## Be a role model

Children learn how to use smartphones primarily by observing adults. Anyone who looks at the display while having dinner or constantly reads messages while talking will have difficulty credibly demanding cell phone-free times. It's worth making the rules apply to the whole family, such as having a shared charging spot in the hallway where all devices are left in the evening. Parents should also not share photos of the child on social networks or large groups without asking. Asking for permission yourself is the best way to show what respectful treatment of images looks like.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I secretly control my child's smartphone?**
Secretly reading chats is a major invasion of privacy and can permanently damage trust. Open rules, transparent parental controls and regular discussions are better. If you have a concrete, serious suspicion of danger, you should speak to your child directly and, if necessary, get support from a counseling center.

**How long can a child use the smartphone per day?**
There is no fixed number that fits everyone. It is crucial that there is enough time for sleep, exercise, friends and homework. Many families start with a manageable daily amount of time for recreational apps and adjust it as the child sticks to agreements.

**What do I do if my child circumvents restrictions?**
Stay calm and talk about why the rule was circumvented. Often there is a need behind it, for example to be able to have a say in the class chat. Maybe the rule needs to be adjusted, maybe there needs to be a clear consequence that was previously agreed upon.

**Does a child need their own email account?**
An account is required to set up the smartphone. For younger children, parents create a supervised child account with Google or Apple. The parents initially manage the access data and gradually release them as they get older.

**What to do if your smartphone is lost?**
Using Google Family Link or Apple’s Find My feature The device can be located, locked and, if necessary, deleted. In addition, you should have the SIM card blocked by the provider and re-secure access to important accounts.

## Conclusion

The first smartphone is a big step, for the child and for the parents. What matters is not the exact age, but rather whether the child is ready to take on responsibility and whether the family supports the start. A robust device with updates, a prepaid tariff with third-party blocking, open parental controls and mutually agreed rules create a good framework. But the most important protection remains conversation. If your child knows that they can come to you with any concern, they will be better equipped for the digital world than through any filter.
