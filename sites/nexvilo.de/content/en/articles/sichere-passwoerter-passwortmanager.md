E-mail, online banking, health insurance, parcel delivery, streaming, social networks, online shops: anyone who uses the Internet today quickly has fifty, a hundred or even more accounts. No one can remember so many different, strong passwords. That's why many people use the same password everywhere or small variations of it. This is exactly what criminals take advantage of. This guide explains what makes a strong password, why a password manager is the best solution for almost everyone, how to set it up and what the new passkeys are all about.

## Why the same password is so dangerous everywhere

Online services are repeatedly hacked and millions of access data fall into the hands of criminals. These lists of email addresses and passwords are traded and automatically tried on other services. Experts call this **credential stuffing**.

If you use the same password for your favorite small online shop as you do for your email inbox, all it takes is a data leak at that shop and attackers have access to your emails. They can then use their email inbox to click “forgot password” for many other services and gain access. A single reused password can open an entire chain of accounts.

**The most important rule is therefore: Each account gets its own password.**

## What makes a strong password

A strong password is, above all, **long** and **unique**. The length is more important than complicated special characters. The Federal Office for Information Security (BSI), for example, recommends:

- **At least 8 characters** if the password consists of all four types of characters: uppercase letters, lowercase letters, numbers and special characters.
- **Or significantly longer, around 20 to 25 characters** if it consists of only two types of characters, such as a string of words.

For accounts that you need to remember yourself, a **password phrase** consisting of several random words is ideal. For example: `Wolke-Tinte-Fahrrad-Kompass-73`. Something like this is long, difficult to guess and yet noticeable.

### What you should avoid

- Names of children, pets or partners, birthdays, wedding anniversaries
- Words from the dictionary alone, even with a number attached like “Summer2026!”
- Keyboard patterns like “qwerty” or “123456”
- Well-known replacements like “P@ssw0rt” that every attacker knows
- The same password with a small change for different services, such as “Hund2024Amazon” and “Hund2024Ebay”

### A myth: change passwords regularly

The rule used to be to change passwords every few months. The BSI and other specialist bodies no longer recommend this. If you have to constantly change passwords, you usually choose weaker passwords or just count up a number. Instead, change a password **immediately** if you suspect it fell into the wrong hands, such as after a data breach or a phishing email you fell for.

## The password manager: a safe for all passwords

A password manager is a program that stores all of your passwords in encrypted form. All you have to do is remember **a single, strong master password**. The Manager:

- **generates** a long, random password for each account,
- **automatically fills out registration forms on websites and apps,
- **synchronizes** your passwords between smartphone, computer and tablet,
- **warns** you if a password is weak, used multiple times, or appears in a known data breach.

Because the password manager checks the website address before filling out a password, it also protects you from phishing: It doesn't fill out anything on a fake bank site because the address is incorrect.

### Isn't it risky to have everything in one place?

Many people ask this question. The honest answer: A well-made password manager with a strong master password and two-factor protection is **significantly more secure** than reused or written down passwords. Reputable password managers encrypt the data on your device before it is synchronized. Even the provider can't read your passwords. The risk lies primarily in a weak main password, which is why this is particularly important.

## What types of password managers are there?

### Built into system and browser

- **Apple Passwords** (formerly iCloud Keychain) on iPhone, iPad and Mac, now with its own app and can also be used on Windows via iCloud.
- **Google Password Manager** in Android and Chrome browser.
- **Microsoft Edge** and **Firefox** also have built-in password storage.

They are free, already available and a good choice for many people as long as they stay in the same “ecosystem”. It becomes more difficult if, for example, you use an iPhone and a Windows PC with Chrome.

### Standalone password managers

Programs like **Bitwarden**, **1Password**, **KeePassXC**, **Proton Pass** or **Dashlane** work on all systems and in all browsers. They often offer additional features such as secure password sharing with family, emergency access for loved ones and storage of two-factor codes.

- **Bitwarden** and **Proton Pass** have good free versions and are open source.
- **1Password** is particularly convenient, but costs a subscription.
- **KeePassXC** saves the database as a file on your computer, without a cloud account. You have to organize the synchronization yourself. This is for people who want full control.

| Art | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Built into the system/browser | free, immediate, easy | tied to an ecosystem |
| Independent with cloud | on all devices, parts and emergency access | partly for a fee, another account |
| Standalone without cloud | full control, no external servers | Organize synchronization and backup yourself |

## Setting up a password manager: step by step

1. **Choose a password manager** that suits your devices.
2. **Set a strong master password.** Use a password phrase of five to six random words. Write it down on paper and keep the note in a safe place at home, such as with important documents, away from the screen.
3. **Enable two-factor login** for the password manager itself.
4. **Install the app** on the smartphone and the **browser extension** on the computer.
5. **Import existing passwords** from the browser. Most managers offer an import function. Then delete the old passwords in the browser so that they are not duplicated.
6. **Allow autofill**: on iPhone under *Settings > General > Autofill & Passwords*, on Android under *Settings > Passwords, Passkeys and Accounts* or *Autofill Service*.

## Change step by step

You don't have to change all hundred passwords in one evening. Go by importance:

1. **Email inbox** – the master key to almost everything else.
2. **Apple ID or Google Account** and **Microsoft Account**.
3. **Online banking and payment services** such as PayPal.
4. **Password Manager** itself (master password).
5. **Social networks and messengers**.
6. **Online shops** with stored payment details.
7. **Everyone else**, little by little, whenever you log in anyway.

The password manager helps with this with a **security report** that lists weak and duplicate passwords.

## Passkeys: the future without a password

More and more services are offering **passkeys**, including Google, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, PayPal and many others. A passkey replaces the password with a key pair generated on your device. To register, you only need to confirm with your fingerprint, facial recognition or your device's PIN.

The advantages:

- **There is nothing that can be stolen or guessed.** The secret part of the key does not leave your device.
- **Passkeys are phishing-proof** because they only work on the real website.
- **Login is faster** than with a password and code.

Passkeys are stored in your system's password manager or a standalone password manager and synced between your devices. If a service offers to create a passkey, in most cases you can accept without hesitation. Keep a second login option in case of emergency, such as a password in the password manager or recovery codes.

## Check if your data appears in a leak

You can check whether your email address appears in known data leaks using reputable services:

- **Have I Been Pwned** (haveibeenpwned.com), an internationally known service.
- The **HPI Identity Leak Checker** from the Hasso Plattner Institute, which sends you the result by email.
- Many password managers check this automatically.

If your address appears, change the password on the affected service and wherever you used the same password.

## Prepare for emergencies

What happens to your accounts if something happens to you? Many password managers offer **emergency access**: a trusted person can request access, and if you don't object within a set period of time, they will receive it. Alternatively, put the main password in a sealed envelope with your important documents. Find out more in our guide to digital inheritance.

## Share passwords within the family

In many households there are accounts that multiple people use: the WiFi, the streaming service, the account with the electricity provider or the access data for the children's school portal. Instead of sending such passwords via messenger or writing them on a piece of paper on the fridge, you can share them securely:

- **Apple Passwords** allows shared groups, such as Family, where selected passwords are visible to all members.
- **Google Password Manager** can share individual passwords with members of your Google Family Group.
- **Standalone password managers** like Bitwarden or 1Password offer family plans with shared vaults.

The advantage: If you change a shared password, everyone immediately has the new one. And if someone leaves the family, such as an adult child moving out, you can revoke access with one click.

## Don't forget the WiFi password

A special case is your WiFi password. It is often on the back of the router and is usually long and random when it comes out of the box. If you change it, choose a long password again, at least 20 characters. It is best for guests to have their own guest WiFi with a separate password. ## Frequently asked questions

**Can I write down passwords on paper?**
A notebook at home is more secure than the same password everywhere. A password manager is better, but keeping the master password and recovery codes on paper safe at home makes sense.

**What if I forget the master password?**
With many password managers, the master password cannot be recovered because the provider does not know it. That's why keeping a paper copy in a safe place is so important. Some managers offer recovery keys or emergency contacts, set them up right at setup.

**Is the password storage in the browser secure enough?**
For many people, yes, especially if the device is protected with a secure lock and the account is secured with two-factor. Standalone password managers offer more features and work across all systems.

## Conclusion

The most important principle for secure accounts is: a separate, long password for each account. Because no one can remember that many, a password manager is the best solution. All you have to do is remember a strong main password and the manager does the rest, including warnings about data leaks and protection against fake websites. Where passkeys are offered, accept them. And make the change step by step, starting with your email inbox.
