Friends ask why you sent them strange links. There are confirmations in your inbox for orders that you never placed. Or you suddenly can't log in because someone changed your password. A hacked account is a shock, and the first reaction is often helplessness. Every minute now counts, because whoever has taken over your account will usually quickly try to exploit it further. These instructions take you step by step through the most important measures: first the emergency brake, then the clean-up and finally the precautions so that it doesn't happen again.

## How to tell if an account has been hacked

Not every strange message means a hack. However, these signs should be taken seriously:

- You can **no longer log in** with your usual password.
- You will receive **emails about registrations** from unknown devices or from foreign countries.
- There are **settings changed** in your account: a new phone number, a different email address for recovery, forwarding of your emails.
- Contacts receive **messages that you did not write**, such as links, requests for money, or advertising.
- There are emails in the **Sent folder** that you have not sent.
- **Orders, payments or subscriptions** appear that you don't recognize.
- **Posts** appear in your name on social networks.

However, a **data leak notice** from a service or checking site does not automatically mean that your account has been taken over, but rather that your access data may have been leaked. Then you should change the password, but don't panic.

## The emergency brake: the first 15 minutes

### 1. Stay calm and use a safe device

If you suspect that your computer itself is infected, perhaps because strange programs are running or the browser is opening pages on its own, do not change passwords on this device. Use another device that you trust, for example your smartphone with the latest updates.

### 2. Change your password if you still have access

If you can still log in, change your password immediately. Take a new, long and unique password, preferably from a password manager. For most services, this will also terminate other active sessions. If not, there is often an option like “Log out of all devices.”

### 3. Get your account back if you're locked out

If the attacker changed your password, use the service's **Account Recovery** service. Always go directly via the official website or app, not via links in emails.

- **Google:** g.co/recover. Answer the questions as accurately as possible, preferably from a device and location where you normally log in.
- **Apple ID:** iforgot.apple.com. When two-factor authentication is enabled, a trusted device or recovery contact helps. Sometimes the recovery takes a few days, this is a protective measure.
- **Microsoft:** account.live.com/acsr.
- **Instagram and Facebook:** Tap “Forgot password” or “Help signing in” in the app. Meta also offers its own help pages for hacked accounts, such as facebook.com/hacked.
- **WhatsApp:** Register again with your phone number and confirm with the SMS code. This will terminate the attacker's session. If you have 2-step verification enabled, you may have to wait seven days before you can log in without a PIN.
- **Email providers** such as GMX, WEB.DE, T-Online or Outlook have their own recovery forms on their help pages.

If all fails, contact the service's **customer support**. Have proof ready, such as previous invoices, payment method, or account creation date.

### 4. Back up your email inbox first

Your email inbox is the master key. Whoever controls it can take over almost any other account via “forgotten password”. If multiple accounts are affected, secure the email account first.

### 5. If you are in danger of losing money: call the bank

If payment services, online banking or accounts with stored payment details are affected:

- Call your **bank** or the **blocking emergency number 116 116** immediately to have your cards and online banking blocked.
- For **PayPal**: Change password, report to customer service and complain about unauthorized payments.
- In online shops: remove saved payment methods and cancel unknown orders.

## Clean up: the next few hours

### Check settings for manipulation

Attackers often set up backdoors only to come back later. In each affected account, check:

- **Recovery Email and Phone Number:** Are they still yours?
- **Two-Factor Settings:** Has anyone added their own authentication app or phone number?
- **Registered devices and sessions:** Log out any devices you don't recognize.
- **Email forwarding and filters:** Attackers like to forward all emails to their own address or set up filters that immediately delete bank warnings.
- **Connected apps:** See which apps have access across Google, Microsoft, and social media accounts. Remove unknown ones.
- **App passwords** and **passkeys** that you did not create yourself.

### Turn on two-factor authentication

If you haven't already done so, turn on **two-factor authentication** now, preferably with an authentication app, security key or passkey. Write down the recovery codes on paper.

### Other accounts with the same password

Have you used the hacked password elsewhere? Then change it there too, immediately. Attackers automatically try out stolen access data on many services.

### Warn contacts

If the attacker has sent messages on your behalf, inform your contacts, for example with a short message or post: "My account has been hacked. Please do not click on any links that you have received from me in the last few days or transfer any money." This will prevent other people from falling for the scam.

### Check devices for malware

A hack can also be caused by malware on your computer or smartphone, for example by a password thief who reads saved access data.

- **Windows:** Run a full scan with Windows Security, plus Microsoft Defender offline scan.
- **Mac:** Update system and apps, remove unknown programs and browser extensions.
- **Android:** Uninstall unknown apps, especially those with accessibility or device administrator rights. Google Play Protect checks installed apps.
- **iPhone:** Update system and remove unknown profiles under *Settings > General > VPN and Device Management*.

If you have persistent problems, resetting to factory settings will help, of course only after backing up your important files.

## Report and file a complaint

### Report to duty

Report the incident to the service concerned. Many platforms have their own forms for hacked accounts. This helps get the account back faster and prevents the attacker from causing further damage.

### Report to the police

File a **report**, especially if:

- Money has been debited or ordered in your name,
- people have been deceived in your name,
- you are being blackmailed, for example with allegedly stolen photos.

Many federal states offer **online monitoring**. Preserve evidence beforehand: screenshots of suspicious messages, login notifications, order confirmations, and bank statements.

### In case of identity abuse

When someone enters into contracts, opens accounts, or orders goods on your behalf, it is called **identity theft**. Then help:

- a **report** to the police,
- a free **self-assessment** from SCHUFA and other credit agencies to see whether contracts are running in your name,
- **written objection** to companies that send you invoices for goods not ordered, with a copy of the notification,
- Advice at the **consumer advice center**.

## Blackmail with alleged videos

A common scam is emails in which someone claims to have hacked your computer and filmed you via webcam. An old password of yours that comes from a data leak is sometimes given as evidence. Money in cryptocurrency is required.

In almost all cases this is a **bluff**. There is no video. Don't pay, don't reply, change the password provided if you're still using it, and delete the email. If you are unsure or are actually being blackmailed, contact the police.

## So that it doesn't happen again

| Measure | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| A unique password for each account | A data leak no longer opens all doors |
| Password manager | Generates strong passwords and fills them only on real sites |
| Two-factor authentication or passkeys | The password alone is not enough for the attacker |
| Keep recovery options current | You'll get back to your account more quickly |
| Updates on all devices | Closes loopholes that malware uses |
| Do not pass on codes | Prevents the most common takeover tricks |
| Turn on login notifications | You'll notice immediately when someone logs in |

## An emergency sheet for emergencies

In the moment of terror it is difficult to think clearly. Therefore, prepare an emergency sheet and put it with your important documents. It says:

- the addresses of the recovery pages of your most important accounts,
- the phone number of your bank and the blocking emergency number 116 116,
- the recovery codes for email, Apple, Google or Microsoft account and password manager,
- the number of a tech-savvy person you can call in an emergency.

Once you have created this sheet, you will not lose valuable time searching in an emergency.



## Frequently asked questions

**Will I always get my hacked account back?**
Mostly yes, if you act quickly and have evidence. For social networks it may take a few days. The better your recovery options were set up beforehand, the easier it will be.

**Do I have to delete my account?**
As a rule not. Once you get it back and secure it, you can continue using it. However, you should delete accounts that you no longer need so that they are not misused again.

**Who is liable for damage?**
In the case of unauthorized payments, bank customers in the EU are entitled to extensive reimbursement, unless they acted with gross negligence, for example by passing on a TAN over the telephone. In the event of a dispute, get advice from the consumer advice center.

## Conclusion

If an account has been hacked, the order counts: change the password from a secure device or get the account back via official recovery, back up the email inbox first and if there is a risk to your money, call the bank immediately. Then check all settings for backdoors, turn on two-factor authentication, warn your contacts and file a report. With unique passwords, a password manager and a second factor, you make it very difficult for attackers to get in again.
