100 percent in the morning, the red warning in the early afternoon: Hardly anything is as annoying in everyday digital life as a smartphone that doesn't get through the day. The good news is that with a few adjustments and new habits, you can get amazing results without sacrificing the features you value. The not so good: Many tips that have been circulating for years hardly help or even do harm. In this guide we separate one from the other.

## How a smartphone battery works

Almost all current smartphones have a lithium-ion battery. When charging, lithium ions move from one side of the cell to the other and back again when discharging. This process wears down the material a little bit each time. That's why every battery loses capacity over time, no matter how carefully you treat it.

Two things particularly accelerate this aging: **heat** and **extremes in charge level**. A battery that is constantly at 100 percent or often drains to 0 percent will age faster than one that mostly stays in the middle range. A battery left on the dashboard in summer suffers more than one in your pocket.

Manufacturers usually state that a battery should still have around 80 percent of its original capacity after a few hundred complete charging cycles. A charging cycle does not mean “plugged into the cable once”, but a total of 100 percent charge. Charging from 50 to 100 percent twice results in one cycle.

## First check: Who is using the electricity?

Before you change the settings, it's worth taking a look at the battery statistics. It shows which apps and functions have used the most energy in the last few hours or days.

- **Android:** usually under *Settings > Battery* or *Settings > Battery and device maintenance > Battery*. Depending on the manufacturer, this point is called “battery usage” or “consumption since the last full charge”.
- **iPhone:** under *Settings > Battery*. There you can see the consumption of the last 24 hours and the last ten days, broken down by apps and by screen time in the foreground and background.

Pay particular attention to apps that use a lot in the **background** even though you rarely open them. Typical candidates are social networks, navigation apps that continue to query location, and games that reload content. If an app is noticeable, you can specifically remove background activity from it instead of forcing the entire phone into save mode.

## The settings with the greatest impact

### Screen: the largest consumer

For most people, the display is the largest power consumer. Three adjusting screws are particularly helpful here:

1. **Turn on automatic brightness.** It adjusts the brightness to the ambient light and saves more power in everyday life than a screen that is permanently bright.
2. **Make your screen turn off faster.** Automatically locking after 30 seconds or a minute instead of five minutes makes a noticeable difference throughout the day.
3. **Use dark mode.** On smartphones with OLED screens, black pixels do not light up at all. The dark mode actually saves energy there, especially at high brightness. It doesn't do much good with LCD screens, but it's easy on the eyes at night.

Many newer devices also have a high refresh rate of 90 or 120 Hertz. This makes scrolling smoother, but costs electricity. If your device offers an “adaptive” setting, this is the best compromise. If the battery is really low, you can switch back to 60 Hertz.

### Background activity and location

Apps are no longer allowed to work indefinitely in the background on modern smartphones, but many make full use of their freedom. Therefore check:

- **Background refresh:** Turn off background refresh for individual apps on iPhone under *Settings > General > Background refresh*. On Android, you can set usage to “Limited” or “Optimized” under “Battery” in the app settings.
- **Location access:** Under the privacy settings you can see which apps are allowed to query your location. “Always” is almost never necessary. “When using the app” is enough for weather, maps and delivery services.
- **Notifications:** Every notification wakes up the screen or at least the wireless module. What you don't need to know immediately can remain quiet.

### Radio connections with a sense of proportion

Bluetooth, Wi-Fi and location services use less power when in sleep mode than many people believe. It is therefore not necessary to constantly switch them on and off. The situation is different with **poor mobile phone reception**: If the smartphone is constantly searching for a network in the basement, on the train or in the country, the battery will quickly empty. In such situations, it helps to briefly switch to airplane mode and just turn the WiFi back on if necessary.

5G can also cost more energy if reception is weak. Many devices automatically choose whether 5G is worth it. If your smartphone has an option like “5G automatic,” leave it active.

## Energy saving mode: when it's worth it

Both large systems have a power saving mode, on the iPhone it is called “power saving mode” and on Android it is called “power saving mode” or “battery saving mode” depending on the manufacturer. It throttles background activity, reduces animations, sometimes reduces processor performance, and pauses automatic email retrieval.

It is a good tool for everyday life when it is likely that the day will be long: when traveling, on an excursion or when you are out and about in the evening. You can set it to turn on automatically at 20 percent. It's not harmful if it's permanently on, but you'll miss out on some convenience functions.

## Load correctly: what is true and what is not

There are persistent myths surrounding charging. Here is an overview of the most important ones:

| Claim | Which is true |
|---|---|
| “A new battery first has to run completely empty a few times.” | Incorrect. This applied to old nickel batteries. Lithium-ion batteries do not need a break-in period. |
| “Charging overnight ruins the battery.” | Partially. The device stops at 100 percent, but staying at full charge for a long time causes the battery to age a little faster. The optimized charging function solves the problem. |
| “Fast charging is harmful.” | Barely. Smartphones control the charging power themselves and throttle it when the battery becomes full or warm. Heat is the problem, not fast charging itself. |
| “Closing apps saves battery.” | Mostly wrong. The system pauses apps in the background anyway. If you constantly close and restart them, you tend to use more energy. |
| “You should always charge up to 100 percent.” | Not necessary. A range between around 20 and 80 percent is ideal for lifespan, but not a must in everyday life. |

### Use the loading limit

Many newer smartphones have a feature that pauses charging at 80 or 85 percent or delays charging to 100 percent until you are likely to need your phone. On the iPhone you can find this under *Settings > Battery > Charging* or *Battery Health*, depending on the model as “Optimized Charging” or as a fixed limit. On Android devices, the function is called “battery protection”, “adaptive charging” or “charging optimization”.

If you usually charge your smartphone overnight, this is one of the most effective measures for a long service life. You forego a few percent of running time per day, but you gain significantly more over the years.

### Charger and cable

Since the end of 2024, USB-C has been the standard charging port for new smartphones in the EU. So you can usually use the same power supply for your cell phone, tablet and headphones. Make sure that the power supply and cable come from a well-known manufacturer and have a CE mark. Cheap power supplies without protective circuits can damage or overheat the device.

Wireless charging is convenient, but generates more heat than wired charging. Place the phone in the middle and remove thick cases if the device gets hot while charging.

## Heat and cold: underestimated battery killers

Lithium-ion batteries like it best between around 15 and 25 degrees. In extreme heat they age quickly, and in cold they temporarily deliver less performance.

- In summer, don't leave your smartphone unprotected in a parked car, on a windowsill in the sun or on the beach.
- Playing games or navigating while charging in the car generates a lot of heat. If the device gets hot, stop charging.
- In frost, a smartphone can suddenly switch off at 30 percent. This is not a defect: as soon as it is warm again, the charge comes back. Wear the device as close to your body as possible in winter.

## How healthy is your battery?

You can often check whether your battery has run down directly in the system:

- **iPhone:** *Settings > Battery > Battery Health*. The maximum capacity is shown there in percent. If it is below around 80 percent, Apple recommends replacing it.
- **Android:** Some manufacturers display the status in the battery or maintenance settings, for example as “battery health” or in a diagnostic app. For other devices, customer service or a diagnosis in a specialist workshop will help.

Warning signs of a worn or damaged battery include sudden shutdowns at mid-charge, a device that gets hot with even light use, and most importantly, a **bloated battery**. If the display or the back bulges, you should stop charging the smartphone and take it to a specialist workshop as soon as possible. A swollen battery can catch fire.

## Replacing the battery instead of buying a new one

A weak battery is rarely a reason for a new smartphone. Depending on the model and workshop, a replacement often costs between 50 and 150 euros and gives an otherwise good device a few more years. This protects your wallet and the environment.

When choosing a workshop, pay attention to original parts or high-quality replacement batteries and ask about a guarantee on the repair. With many manufacturers you can order the exchange directly from the official service. Since 2025, an EU regulation has also required that replacement batteries for new smartphones must be available for many years, which will make repairs easier in the future.

The following applies before replacing: **Back up data.** The device is usually not deleted during a repair, but a current backup protects you if something goes wrong.

## On the go: power bank and little helpers

If you travel a lot, a power bank is the easiest solution. A model with 10,000 mAh is sufficient for one to two full charges. Make sure it supports USB-C with Power Delivery so that it charges your smartphone quickly. On an airplane, power banks belong in your hand luggage, not in your suitcase.

Also practical are:

- a short spare cable in your pocket,
- a compact power supply with two ports for travel,
- Maps stored offline so that navigation does not have to constantly load data.

## A plan for the long day

If you know your day is going to be long, a little routine helps:

1. In the morning, briefly check the battery statistics to see whether an app is out of line.
2. Activate power saving mode as soon as you leave the house.
3. Leave the screen brightness on automatic and only stream videos on WiFi.
4. If reception is poor, use airplane mode.
5. Recharge briefly on the go instead of letting the battery run completely empty.

## Conclusion

A smartphone battery lasts longer if you protect it from heat, don't leave it constantly at 100 percent and keep an eye on the biggest consumers: the screen, background apps and the search for a weak network. Charging limitation or optimized charging is the most effective individual measure for the service life. And if the battery still fails after a few years, replacing it in the workshop is almost always a better choice than a new device.
