The video runs smoothly in the living room, the video conference breaks off in the study, and the cell phone only shows a WiFi line in the bedroom. Poor WiFi is one of the most common annoyances in everyday digital life, and with every new smart device, the home network becomes more important. Often the problem is not with the Internet connection, but with how the radio signals arrive in the apartment. This guide will help you find the cause, get the most out of your router and choose the right solution for dead spots: mesh, repeater, powerline or the good old network cable.

## First clarify: Is it the WLAN or the connection?

Before you buy new technology, check whether the problem is actually with the WiFi.

1. **Measure speed directly on the router:** Stand with your laptop or smartphone directly next to the router or connect the laptop using a network cable. Measure the speed with a speed test, such as the **broadband measurement** from the Federal Network Agency (breitbandmessen.de) or the test from your provider.
2. **Repeat measurement in the problem area.**
3. **Compare** and derive the next step from this.

If the router is already slow, the problem lies with the **connection**, the tariff or the router itself. Then a call to the provider will help. If it is fast on the router and slow in the problem area, it is the **WLAN**. Then the following steps will help.

By the way, you can create a measurement report for the Federal Network Agency's broadband measurement. If the speed of your landline connection is permanently significantly below the contractually agreed speed, you have the right to a reduction or special termination under certain conditions.

## Why WiFi is weak in some rooms

WiFi works via radio waves, and these are weakened as they travel through the apartment. Typical obstacles:

- **Walls and ceilings**, especially made of reinforced concrete, bricks with metal content or with underfloor heating
- **Metal**: radiators, mirrors, refrigerators, metal shelves, elevator shafts
- **Water**: Aquariums, washing machines, bathrooms
- **Distance**: The further away, the weaker
- **Interferences** from neighboring networks, especially in apartment buildings

In addition, there are two or three **frequency bands**:

- **2.4 GHz:** long range, goes through walls well, but slower and often crowded.
- **5 GHz:** faster, less interference, but shorter range.
- **6 GHz** (for WLAN 6E and WLAN 7): very fast and free of interference, but with the shortest range.

Modern routers automatically distribute devices to the appropriate band when the “Band Steering” function is active.

## Step 1: Set up the router correctly

The simplest and cheapest step is often forgotten. The router should:

- **as centrally** as possible in the apartment, not in a corner or in the hall closet,
- stand **elevated**, for example on a shelf, not on the floor,
- **stand freely**, not behind the TV, in a cupboard or in a metal box,
- **stand up**, if it is built for it, with antennas vertical,
- **Stay away** from other radio devices such as baby monitors, microwaves and cordless phone bases.

Unfortunately, the telephone or fiber optic socket often determines where the router is located. The location can usually be improved with a longer cable from the connection to the router. With DSL, a longer telephone cable of up to a few meters is usually unproblematic; with fiber optic, a longer network cable can be laid between the fiber optic modem and router.

## Step 2: Check router settings

- **Update firmware.** Updates often also improve Wi-Fi performance.
- **Set channel selection to automatic**. Good routers choose the channel with the least interference.
- **Offer both bands under one name** so that the devices switch automatically.
- **Turn off old WiFi standards** if there are no longer any old devices in the household. A single very old device can slow down the entire network.
- Set **transmission power** to maximum if it has been reduced.

## Step 3: Choose the right extension

If the router is positioned correctly and there are still dead spots, there are four solutions.

### WiFi repeater

A repeater receives the WiFi signal and retransmits it. It is plugged into a socket between the router and the dead zone.

- **Advantages:** cheap, simple, no cable required.
- **Disadvantages:** A simple repeater often halves the speed because it has to receive and transmit at the same time. Older repeaters form their own network, between which the devices do not switch seamlessly.
- **Tip:** Place the repeater **halfway** where the signal is still good, not in the dead zone itself.

### Mesh WiFi

A mesh system consists of several devices that together form a large WLAN. They talk to each other and automatically route your devices to the best access point. You have the same network name everywhere, and smartphones change without interruption as you walk around the house.

- **Advantages:** Large area coverage, seamless switching, central management via app, good speed, especially with a dedicated radio band for the connection between the mesh devices (tri-band).
- **Disadvantages:** more expensive than a repeater.
- **Variants:** Complete mesh systems are available from many manufacturers. If you have a **FRITZ!Box**, you can use FRITZ!Repeater as a mesh extension. Telekom and other providers also have mesh solutions. When combining, pay attention to the same manufacturer or the standard **EasyMesh**.

### Powerline (dLAN)

Powerline adapters transmit data over the power line. One adapter is located near the router and is connected to it via cable, while a second adapter is located in the target room and provides WLAN or a network connection there.

- **Advantages:** overcomes thick walls and floors where Funk fails.
- **Disadvantages:** The performance depends heavily on the electrical installation. Adapters should be plugged directly into the wall outlet, not into a power strip. It often works worse across different circuits or phases.

### Network cable (LAN)

The fastest and most stable connection is and remains the **network cable**. If there is a network socket in the study or on the television or a cable can be laid, this is the best solution. A mesh access point connected to the router via cable delivers full speed everywhere. Ribbon cables can be laid under doors and behind skirting boards.

| Solution | Speed | Effort | Rough costs | Ideal for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Change router | – | very low | 0 € | every household first |
| repeaters | medium | low | 30–80 € | single dead spot |
| Mesh system | high | low | 150–500 € | large apartments, houses |
| Powerline | medium to high | low | 80–200 € | thick walls, floors |
| Network cable | very high | medium to high | 10–100 € | Workstation, television, console |

## Step 4: Measure success

After each change you should measure whether things have improved:

- **Speed test** in every room, preferably at the same time of day.
- **WiFi analysis apps** show you the signal strength and the channels in use. The FRITZ!App WLAN from AVM, for example, is free and can also be used with other routers.
- **A floor plan with notes** helps: Draw where reception is good, average or poor.

As a rough guide: For HD streaming you need around 5 to 10 Mbit/s, for 4K streaming you need 25 Mbit/s, for a video conference you need around 3 to 5 Mbit/s for upload and download. A **stable connection** with low lag is often more important than top speed.

## WiFi and Smart Home

Many smart devices use the **2.4 GHz band** because it has a longer range and requires less power. If you have a lot of WiFi devices in your smart home, the network can become overloaded. A few tips:

- **Choose a router with sufficient capacity**. Older routers reach their limits with 30 or more devices.
- **Use Zigbee or Thread devices** instead of WLAN devices for sensors and lamps. They relieve the strain on the WLAN.
- **Guest WiFi** for smart devices that only need to be connected to the Internet.
- If you have problems with the setup: some devices need a **pure 2.4 GHz network**. Many routers allow you to temporarily offer a separate 2.4 GHz network.

## Typical living situations and suitable solutions

To help you find the right solution more quickly, here are four typical cases:

- **Two-room apartment, router in the hallway:** It is often enough to set up the router higher and more freely. If the bedroom remains weak, a single repeater or a small mesh access point in the living room will help.
- **Large old building apartment with thick walls:** Radio doesn't get through well. A mesh system with two or three access points, ideally one of them connected via cable or powerline, brings WiFi into every room.
- **Single-family house over several floors:** One access point per floor makes sense here. If there are network cables between floors, connect the mesh devices above them. Otherwise powerline adapters can establish the connection.
- **Home office in the attic:** Stability is important for video conferences. A network cable to the laptop or at least a mesh access point in the study is the best solution. Also plan the garden if you want to work outside in the summer; There are weatherproof access points for this.

In all cases the following applies: first measure, then buy. Many retailers offer a return policy that allows you to try a mesh system at home.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a new router with WiFi 7 worth it?**
If your router is WiFi 5 or older and your devices are new, a router with WiFi 6, 6E or 7 will provide noticeably more performance and capacity. However, older devices hardly benefit.

**Is WiFi bad for your health?**
According to the current state of science and the assessments of the Federal Office for Radiation Protection, the fields emitted by WLAN routers in the household are well below the limit values. If you want to take precautions, don't put the router right next to your bed and can turn off the WiFi at night using a timer.

**Why is my WiFi slower in the evening?**
In the evening, many people use the Internet at the same time, which puts a strain on both your provider's network and the WiFi channels in apartment buildings. The 5 GHz and 6 GHz bands are less affected.

## Conclusion

Better WiFi starts with a simple measurement: If it's slow on the router, the problem is with the connection; if it's only weak in certain rooms, it's the WiFi. Place the router centrally, elevated and freely and keep it up to date. If that's not enough, a mesh system is the most convenient solution for larger apartments, Powerline helps with thick walls, and the network cable remains the fastest connection for the workplace and television. Measure after each change so you know what really helps.
