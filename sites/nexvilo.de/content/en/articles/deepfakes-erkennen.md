A well-known politician says things in the video that he never said. A celebrity is allegedly promoting a dubious investment. On the phone, the voice sounds exactly like that of your own daughter, who urgently needs money. All of these are **deepfakes**: images, videos and sound recordings created or modified with artificial intelligence that are intended to appear real. The technology has become so good and so easily accessible in recent years that it is often no longer possible to detect counterfeits with the naked eye. Nevertheless, you are not powerless. This guide will show you how deepfakes work, what clues you can pay attention to and, above all, what habits you can use to reliably protect yourself.

## What is a deepfake?

The term is a combination of “deep learning”, a method of artificial intelligence, and “fake”. This refers to media content in which AI was used to:

- **Swapping faces**, such as inserting someone's face into someone else's video,
- **changing facial expressions and lips** so that a person appears to be speaking different words,
- **Clone voices** that can be deceptively imitated with just a few seconds of audio material,
- **To create complete images**, such as photos of events that never happened.

Not all AI creation is malicious. Films, satire, art and accessibility use the same technology. It becomes problematic when people are deceived, cheated or exposed.

## What deepfakes are misused for

### Celebrity endorsement scam

A very common scam: Videos appear on social networks in which well-known presenters, athletes or politicians appear to recommend an investment, a crypto investment or a miracle cure. The faces and voices are fake, the platforms behind them are fraudulent. Anyone who deposits money there usually never sees it again.

### Shock calls with cloned voice

Scammers use voice recordings from social networks to imitate the voice of a family member. You then apparently hear your own daughter or grandson on the phone who urgently needs money after an accident. This is a modern form of the grandchild trick.

### Fraud in companies

Companies are also a target: employees receive calls or video conferences with supposed superiors who order an urgent transfer. Cases have become known in which millions of dollars were stolen in this way.

### Disinformation

Fake images of disasters, protests or politicians spread quickly, especially during crises and before elections. They are intended to create a mood, stir up fear or undermine trust in the media.

### Exposure and blackmail

Fake intimate images that show people without their consent are particularly bad. They are used to humiliate or blackmail people, often women and young people. This is a punishable offense in Germany.

## How you can spot fake videos

The quality of deepfakes is constantly improving, so the following advice is not a guarantee. But they help to become suspicious:

- **Unnatural edges:** The image blurs or flickers at the transition between the face and hair, neck or background.
- **Strange facial expressions:** The face appears rigid, blinking is missing or irregular, emotions do not match what is being said.
- **Lips and tone:** The mouth movements don't exactly match the words, especially with sounds like "b", "m" and "p".
- **Teeth and tongue:** They appear washed out or change from image to image.
- **Light and shadow:** The lighting on the face does not match the surroundings, reflections in glasses or eyes are missing.
- **Hands and jewelry:** Fingers merge, earrings change shape.
- **Movements:** If the person turns their head or holds something in front of their face, distortions occur.

## How you can recognize fake images

Similar observations help with AI-generated photos:

- **Text in the image** is often illegible or makes no sense, such as on signs or T-shirts.
- **Hands, ears and teeth** are often flawed, even as models improve.
- **Backgrounds** seem illogical: buildings without entrances, stairs leading to nowhere, blurry crowds.
- **Too perfect:** Skin without pores, flawless lighting, a high-gloss look straight out of an ad.
- **Symmetry error:** Lenses, earrings or collars are different on the left and right.

## How you can recognize cloned voices

Voice clones are particularly difficult to detect, especially on phones with poor connections. Pay attention to:

- **Monotonous emphasis**, missing breathing sounds or unnatural pauses,
- **Dodging questions**, especially with personal details,
- **Time pressure and strong emotions**: crying, panic, urgency,
- **Unusual Requests**: Money, gift cards, cryptocurrency, secrecy.

The most effective countermeasure is not listening, but a simple rule: **Hang up and call back yourself** using the number you know.

## The best protective habits

Because deepfakes are becoming increasingly difficult to detect with the naked eye, habits are more important than detective work:

1. **Pause when feeling strong.** Deepfakes are intended to shock, outrage or entice. If a video makes you angry or upset, it's a good time to take a breather.
2. **Check source.** Who published the video or image first? An official account, a well-known editorial team or an anonymous account?
3. **Always call back if you have money.** No matter how genuine a voice sounds: If you want money, call the person yourself using the known number.
4. **Agree on family password.** A word that only the family knows and is queried in an emergency will immediately expose voice clones.
5. **Ignore celebrity advertising for investments.** Serious celebrities do not advertise investments with guaranteed profits on social networks.
6. **Don't forward what you haven't verified.** Every redirect helps fakes spread.

## Tools for testing

### Reverse search for images

With a **image reverse search** you can find out where an image first appeared and whether it comes from a different context:

- **Google Lens** or Google image search: upload image or insert link.
- **Bing Visual Search** and **TinEye** work similarly.

It often turns out that a supposedly current photo is years old or comes from a completely different country.

### Fact checks

Editorial teams such as the Tagesschau's **Faktenfinder**, **Correctiv** or the **dpa-Faktencheck** check viral images and videos. Type a few keywords into a search engine along with “fact check.”

### Proof of origin

Some cameras, programs and platforms now provide images with **source information** according to the **C2PA** standard (“Content Credentials”). They show whether and how an image was edited or created with AI. The absence of such information does not automatically mean that an image is fake.

### Labeling by platforms

Large platforms are increasingly labeling AI-generated content, and the EU AI regulation is gradually requiring providers to identify deepfakes as such. But don't rely on that alone, because not every fake is detected.

### AI detectors

There are programs that claim to recognize AI content. Their hit rate varies greatly and is often unreliable. Use it at most as one clue among several.

## If you have fallen victim to a deepfake

- **Fraud:** Inform the bank immediately, block the cards using the blocking emergency number 116 116 and report it to the police.
- **Fake images of you:** Secure evidence (screenshots with date and link), report content to the platform and file a complaint. Advice centers like **HateAid** support those affected, including legally.
- **Children and young people:** If blackmailed with pictures, do not pay, do not respond further, secure evidence and involve parents, school or police. Help is also available at the **number against grief** (116 111).

## A test procedure in three minutes

If you come across a suspicious video or image, perhaps in a family group or in your news feed, this quick process will help:

1. **First minute: Who says this?** Look who published the content. Is there an official account for the person or organization shown? Are reputable media reporting on this? If a supposedly earth-shattering video only appears on unknown channels, that's a warning sign.
2. **Second minute: Where did it come from?** Take a screenshot of a striking moment and load it into a reverse image search. The original often appears, from another year, another country or a film.
3. **Third minute: What do fact checkers say?** Enter the person's name and a keyword with the addition of "fact check" into a search engine. Viral counterfeits are often checked after just a few hours.

If you remain unsure afterwards: Don't share. A video that turns out to be real spreads even without you forwarding it, but a fake thrives because many people share it without checking.

## Talk to older relatives and children

Older people are particularly often the target of shock calls and fake celebrity advertisements, while young people are more often the target of manipulated images in school chats. Tell the family about these scams, show examples from fact checks and agree on the family password together. A short conversation is the most effective protection there is.



## What platforms and legislators do

Platforms and politics are also reacting. The major social networks prohibit deceptive deepfakes in their rules, especially before elections, and label AI-generated content with notices. In the EU, the Digital Services Act requires major platforms to take action against reported illegal content, and the AI ​​Regulation requires deepfakes to be recognizable as artificially generated. All of this helps, but does not replace your own critical eye, because counterfeits are often spread faster than they are deleted.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I always detect deepfakes?**
No, the best fakes can hardly be distinguished with the naked eye. This is why source checking and recall are more important than looking for image errors.

**How do I protect my own voice?**
This cannot be prevented completely. However, you can share fewer voice recordings publicly, set profiles to private and agree on a password with your family.

**Is creating deepfakes a crime?**
That depends on the content and purpose. Fraud, defamation, extortion and the distribution of fake intimate images are punishable offenses. Satire and art can be allowed if they are recognizable.

## Conclusion

Deepfakes are fake images, videos and voices that are generated using AI and are becoming increasingly difficult to detect. Clues such as unnatural edges, incorrect lip movements, faulty hands or monotonous voices help, but above all you should rely on good habits: pause when you have strong feelings, check the source, look up images backwards, use fact checks and always call back yourself when demanding money. A family password also protects against cloned voices on the phone.
