A friend asks an AI assistant about the opening times of the citizens' office and finds herself in front of a locked door the next morning. A student has literature recommended for his homework and finds that two of the five books mentioned do not exist. A man has a deadline explained to him under tenancy law and only finds out from his lawyer that the information was incorrect. Such errors happen because language models do not know what is true, but rather calculate what sounds likely. This guide explains why AI assistants invent things, when this happens most often, and how you can check their answers quickly and reliably.

## What an AI hallucination is

A **hallucination** is when an AI model produces a statement that is false or made up, but presents it as if it were true. This could be an incorrect year, a made-up quote, a non-existent law, a made-up study or a link that leads nowhere.

The tricky thing about it is that the wrong answer sounds just as fluid, confident and well-formulated as the right one. There is no warning, no hesitation, no red text. A person who doesn't know something often says, "I'm not sure about that." A language model only does this if it is made to do so.

## Why language models invent things

To understand hallucinations, it helps to take a look at how they work. During training, a language model learned which words and sentences typically follow each other. When you ask a question, it produces an answer word for word that fits the patterns it has learned.

This works great for knowledge that was very common in the training data: how photosynthesis works, what a resignation letter contains, how to cook spaghetti. It becomes difficult with questions on which there were few or contradictory texts. Then the model fills the gap with what sounds **plausible**. A book recommendation with a typical title, a typical author name and a typical publisher is just as “probable” for the model as a real book.

In addition, many models have been trained to be helpful and provide an answer. “I don’t know” is less common in this training than a full answer.

## When hallucinations are particularly common

Not all questions are equally risky. Pay particular attention to these situations:

| Type of question | Why risky? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Very current information** | The model only knows data up to a certain point in time | Prices, new laws, news |
| **Local Details** | A few texts about your place | Opening times, bus timetables, contact persons |
| **Exact numbers and dates** | Numbers are easily swapped or rounded | Deadlines, limits, years |
| **Sources and Quotes** | The model invents appropriate titles and links | Literature lists, studies, court rulings |
| **Little known people** | Hardly any training data | Resumes, awards |
| **Law, Medicine, Taxes** | Small mistakes with big consequences | Paragraphs, dosages, allowances |
| **Calculate in several steps** | Errors add up | Interest calculation, quantity conversion |

However, with general explanations, reformulating your own texts or collecting ideas, the risk is low because you can judge the result yourself.

## Five check steps for every important answer

You don’t have to suspiciously dissect every answer an AI gives. But if you want to rely on information, these five steps will help:

### 1. Ask yourself: What happens if this is wrong?

A wrong recipe leads to a failed dinner. Incorrect deadline, dosage or legal advice can cost money, health or rights. The higher the risk, the more thoroughly you check.

### 2. Request and open sources

Ask the AI for sources: "Where does this information come from? Tell me official sites." Open the links and read whether they really say what is claimed. Often the link is correct, but the message on the page is different. Sometimes the link leads nowhere, then the source was invented.

### 3. Compare with an independent source

Check important information with a reliable authority that has nothing to do with AI:

- **Authorities and offices:** official federal, state and local websites, for example for deadlines, fees and opening times.
- **Laws:** the official legal texts, for example on gesetze-im-internet.de.
- **Health:** Sites like Gesund.bund.de or information from health insurance companies, and if in doubt, the doctor's office or pharmacy.
- **Consumer questions:** the consumer advice centers.
- **Books and studies:** Library catalogs or specialist databases.

### 4. Ask and cross-check

Ask the same question again in a different way, or ask specifically: "Are you sure? What parts of your answer could be wrong?" Contradictions are a clear warning sign. A second assistant can also help, but it does not replace a real source because both can make the same mistake.

### 5. Check plausibility

Use your common sense. Does a number sound too round, too high or too low? Does a year match what you otherwise know? Is a quote too perfect? If something feels strange, it's worth taking a second look.

## How to reduce hallucinations in the first place

By asking the right questions, the risk can be reduced:

- **Allow uncertainty:** “If you don’t know something for sure, say it openly.”
- **Limit the topic:** Ask for general context rather than exact details.
- **Provide the material yourself:** If you insert text and just ask the AI ​​to summarize it, it is significantly less likely to invent something because it is relying on your text.
- **Use the search function:** Internet search wizards display sources for you to check. This is no guarantee of accuracy, but it does make the test easier.
- **Ask for justification:** “Explain how you came to this conclusion.” This way you are more likely to see errors in thinking.

## Take special care when it comes to health, law and money

Mistakes in these three areas can have serious consequences. Use AI here to **understand**, not to **decide**:

- **Health:** An AI can explain what a technical term in a doctor's note means or how a medication basically works. Dosages, interactions and diagnoses belong in the hands of doctors and pharmacies. In case of emergencies: 112.
- **Law:** The AI can explain basic terms and help prepare questions for an appointment with a lawyer or consumer advice center. Do not rely on the paragraphs and deadlines that it mentions without reading them in the text of the law.
- **Money and taxes:** Have terms such as savings allowance or business expenses explained to you, but check amounts and limits with official bodies, as they change regularly.

## Mistakes that aren't hallucinations

Not every wrong answer is made up. Sometimes there are other causes:

- **Misunderstanding:** The AI understood your question differently than what you meant. Be more precise.
- **Outdated knowledge:** The information used to be correct, but is no longer correct.
- **Ambiguity:** Terms, places or people exist multiple times. “Frankfurt” can be Frankfurt am Main or Frankfurt an der Oder.
- **Calculation errors:** When it comes to numbers, it's always worth using a calculator.

## An example from everyday life

Suppose you ask, “How long do I have to appeal a tax assessment?” The AI ​​replies: “One month after announcement.” That is fundamentally correct. But what does “announcement” mean? When is a letter considered delivered? What happens on weekends and public holidays? It is worth taking a look at the official information from the tax authorities or at ELSTER, which explains exactly how the deadline is calculated. The AI ​​has shown you the right path, you check the details at the source.

## AI answers in search engines

Search engines now also often display a summary generated by AI above the actual search results, for example on Google as “Overview with AI”. This is convenient because you can see the answer immediately without opening a website. But the same applies to these summaries as to chat assistants: they can misunderstand sources, mix statements from different sites or reproduce outdated information. Cases have already become known in which such overviews have presented ironic forum posts as serious advice.

If you have important questions, get into the habit of opening at least one of the linked sources and reading there. Pay attention to who is behind the site: An authority, a trade association or a well-known editorial team is more reliable than an anonymous forum or a site that primarily wants to sell products.

## A short checklist to print out

- Is the answer intended for an important decision? Then always check.
- Does it contain numbers, deadlines, paragraphs or dosages? Then read the official source.
- Does she mention books, studies or links? Then open it and check if it exists.
- Does she contradict herself when asked? Then remain suspicious.
- Does it sound too smooth or too perfect? Then take a second look.



## Talk to children and young people about it

Children and young people in particular often use AI assistants as a matter of course for homework and presentations. Talk to them about the fact that AI also makes mistakes and practice checking together: pick out a claim from the answer, find a second source and compare. This ability to critically examine information is more important than ever in the age of AI, both at school and in later professional life.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will the models eventually become error-free?**
The error rate decreases with each generation, especially for models with Internet search. However, errors will not disappear completely because the basic principle is based on probabilities.

**How do I recognize fictional sources?**
Enter the title and author into a search engine or library catalog. You won't find any made-up books or studies there. Links that lead to an error page are also a warning sign.

**Is an AI with internet search more reliable?**
Mostly yes, because it uses current sources. However, it can summarize sources incorrectly or use dubious sites. The exam remains your task.

## Conclusion

AI assistants sometimes make up facts, figures, sources, and quotes because they produce likely answers rather than true answers, and they do so with the same confidence as they do with correct answers. Current, local and very precise information as well as questions about health, law and money are particularly risky. You can be on the safe side with five steps: assess the risk, open sources, compare independently, ask questions and use common sense. This means the AI ​​remains a useful helper and you remain responsible for important decisions.
