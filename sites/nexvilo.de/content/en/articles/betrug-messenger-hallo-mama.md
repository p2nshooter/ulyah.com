The message comes from an unknown number: "Hello mom, my phone is broken, this is my new number. Can you delete the old one?" A few messages later, the request follows: A bill needs to be paid urgently, the new cell phone cannot yet make transfers, and whether they can help out briefly. What sounds like a harmless family situation is actually one of the most common scams in Germany. The police have been warning about this for years, and yet many people lose large sums of money every year. These instructions explain how the scam works, how you can recognize it and how you can protect your family.

## How the messenger scam works

The trick is a modern version of the grandchild trick. It almost always follows the same script:

1. **The first message:** An unknown number writes via WhatsApp, Signal or SMS. The sender poses as a son, daughter, grandson or granddaughter, often without a name: “Hello Mom,” “Hello Dad,” “Hi Grandma.”
2. **The explanation:** The old cell phone was broken, lost or fell into the water. You should delete the old number and save the new one.
3. **Build trust:** This is followed by a few everyday messages, perhaps a heart emoji or a question about how you are doing.
4. **The request:** A bill, a rent, a cell phone purchase or a medical bill still needs to be paid today. Online banking does not yet work on the new cell phone. Whether you can transfer the amount, you will pay it back tomorrow.
5. **Pressure:** It's urgent, there's a threat of a reminder, you're ashamed and don't want to make a phone call.
6. **The second round:** Anyone who has transferred once often receives another request because “something is still missing”.

The amounts required are often between a few hundred and several thousand euros. The money usually goes to accounts of so-called financial agents or abroad and is quickly gone.

## The typical sentences

These formulations appear again and again. When you read it, alarm bells should ring:

- “This is my new number, can you delete the old one?”
- “My cell phone fell into the water / broke / was stolen.”
- “I can’t make a phone call right now, the microphone doesn’t work.”
- "Can you make me a quick transfer? I'll give it back to you tomorrow."
- “My online banking is not yet activated on my new cell phone.”
- “It’s urgent, the bill has to go out today.”
- “Please don’t tell dad, I’m embarrassed.”

Particularly revealing: The sender **does not give a name** and avoids making a phone call. Real children call when they need help.

## Why the scam works so well

The fraudsters do not use a technical loophole, but rather human feelings: concern for their own children, willingness to help and the need to act quickly. Because the message comes via the same messenger that you normally use to write to your family, everything seems familiar. Many victims have children of the appropriate age and therefore respond with their own name: “Is it you, Lisa?” In doing so, they provide the fraudsters with the information they are missing.

Sometimes the perpetrators even use real profile pictures from social networks, and there are now cases in which voice messages generated with AI are sent that sound like the voice of a relative.

## Here's how to expose the scam in a minute

### Call the old number

The simplest test: **Call your child on the old number you know.** If the cell phone is apparently broken, speak to voicemail or write a message. In the vast majority of cases, your real child will contact you and not know anything.

### Ask a control question

Ask a question that only the real family member can answer and that is not on social networks: “What was the name of our first dog?”, “What did we eat last Sunday?” Fraudsters avoid or break off contact.

### Insist on a call

Write: “I will only transfer money if we speak on the phone briefly.” Fraudsters then find excuses. If you have a real call, you will recognize the voice. Since the advent of AI voices, a video call or a control question has also made sense.

### Other family members ask

Call the alleged sender's partner, sibling or friend. The matter often resolves itself in just a few minutes.

## What you should never do

- **Do not transfer** until you have verified your identity via a known method.
- **Don't mention the name** if you guess who is writing. Rather ask: “Who is there?”
- **Do not send photos of bank cards or ID cards**.
- **Do not pass on codes** that you receive via SMS. This would allow the fraudsters to take over your own Messenger account.
- **Don't buy voucher cards** and send the codes. This is a variation of the same stitch.
- **Do not click on links** in such messages.

## If you have already transferred

Speed is crucial. The sooner you react, the better chance you have of getting the money back.

1. **Call your bank immediately.** Ask them to recall the transfer. With normal transfers, this is sometimes still possible if the money has not yet reached the recipient's account or has not yet been withdrawn from there. It's more difficult with real-time transfers, but it's still worth a try.
2. **Find a report with the police**, at the station or online. Take screenshots of the chat, the sender's phone number and the transfer details.
3. **Block and report the number** on Messenger. On WhatsApp: Open chat, tap on the name at the top, then *Block* and *Report*.
4. **Inform your family** so that others are not contacted.
5. **Don't delete the chat yet** until the police secure it.

Don't be ashamed. These scammers are professionals and are very clever. Many smart, careful people have fallen for it. A quick ad helps you and others.

## Protecting the family: a conversation worth having

The best protection is preparation. Talk to your parents, grandparents, and also young people about this scam before it happens.

### Agree on family rules

- **“When it comes to money, we always talk on the phone.”** No one in the family asks for money via message without calling first.
- **A family password:** Agrees on a word or question that will be requested in an emergency and is not found anywhere on the Internet.
- **Save new numbers only after a call:** If someone reports a new number, it will only be saved after you have made a short call.
- **No shame:** If you're unsure, it's better to call the children too often. Nobody gets annoyed because you ask.

### Technical settings

- **WhatsApp:** Under *Settings > Privacy* you can set that profile picture, info and “last online” are only visible to your contacts. Calls from unknown people can be muted under *Settings > Data protection > Calls*. WhatsApp now displays messages from unknown numbers with a message indicating that the sender is not in your contacts.
- Enable **2-step verification** in WhatsApp so that no one can take over your own account.
- **Online banking:** Many banks allow you to set **transfer limits**. A lower daily limit for transfers to new recipients limits the potential damage.

### A piece of paper next to the phone

For older relatives, a simple piece of paper next to the phone or in the cell phone case helps: "Children never ask for money via message. Always call the old number first!" Plus the children's phone numbers and the bank number.

## Related stitches

The scammers constantly vary their story. Also pay attention to:

- **Fake cops** on the phone claiming your money is in danger and wanting to “keep it safe”. The real police never collect money or valuables.
- **Shock calls** in which a relative allegedly caused an accident and a deposit was required.
- **False bank employees** who call to carry out an alleged security check and ask for TANs.
- **Competition and package messages** asking for a small fee.

The following applies to everyone: **Hang up, call yourself back** using a known number, and never give out money or codes.

## Young people are also affected

It's not just parents and grandparents who receive such messages. Teenagers and young adults are contacted with similar scams, for example from supposed friends who urgently need a code or from “classmates” with a new number who want to borrow money briefly. Other variants promise quick money for simple tasks, such as forwarding transfers via your own account. Anyone who accepts this will unknowingly become a financial agent and may be liable to prosecution themselves.

So talk to your children about the scam. The rule is the same: Anyone who requests money or codes via message will first be called via a known channel. And no one makes their bank account available for third-party transfers, no matter how tempting the offer sounds.

## A sample conversation that you can practice

A good reaction could look like this:

> **Unknown number:** Hello mom, my phone is broken, this is my new number.
> **You:** Hello, who is it? I'll call you right away on your old number.
> **Unknown number:** It doesn't work anymore, can't make calls right now.
> **You:** Then I'll wait until we can talk on the phone.

You don't have to write anything else. Then call your child on the known number, block the unknown number and report it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where did the scammers get my number?**
They are often randomly generated numbers or numbers from data leaks. The perpetrators write to a lot of people at the same time and hope that some of them will fit.

**Is my phone hacked if I get a message like this?**
No. The message itself is harmless as long as you don't reply, don't transfer anything and don't open any links.

**Should I reply to the scammers to annoy them?**
Better not. Any reply shows that the number is active and you may receive further messages. Blocking and reporting is enough.

## Conclusion

The “Hello Mom” scam works because it relies on trust and helpfulness. But he almost always fails because of a single call to the old, familiar number. Agree with your family on the rule “When it comes to money, we talk on the phone”, ask verification questions, never give a name yourself and don’t transfer anything until you have verified your identity. And if it does happen: call the bank immediately and report it.
