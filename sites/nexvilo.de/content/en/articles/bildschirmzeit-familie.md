“Just one more episode.” “In a moment, I just have to answer briefly.” Almost all families know such sentences. Smartphones, tablets, game consoles and televisions have become an integral part of everyday life, and they have many good sides: children learn with explanatory videos, grandparents see their grandchildren via video calls, young people keep in touch with friends, parents organize appointments and shopping. At the same time, many people feel that the devices take up too much space. Conversations become shorter, sleep suffers, and arguments regularly arise over the question of when to turn off the tablet. This guide shows how families can find a healthy balance that fits their everyday life and how rules can be created that everyone can adhere to, including adults.

## Why the mere number of minutes doesn't say everything

Often the discussion revolves around a single question: How many minutes per day are allowed? While this number is a useful guide, it only tells part of the story. An hour in which a child spends a video call with their grandfather or watches a nature documentary with their parents is completely different than an hour in which they alone watch short videos in an endless sequence. Experts therefore distinguish between several questions that are just as important as the duration.

- **What is used?** Creative and educational content, communication with familiar people or, above all, passive consumption?
- **How ​​to use?** Alone or together, consciously chosen or out of boredom?
- **When is it used?** In the afternoon after homework or late at night in bed?
- **What is being suppressed?** Is there enough time for sleep, exercise, friends, games and conversations?

The last question in particular is a good compass. If a child gets enough sleep, gets exercise, maintains friendships, attends school and is in a good mood, screen time is usually not a big problem. If, on the other hand, sleep, exercise and real meetings become less and less, it's worth taking a closer look.

## Guide values according to age

Even if the minutes aren't everything, guidelines help with orientation. Media educational offers such as klicksafe or LOOK HIN! recommend approximately these sizes for leisure use. School tasks on the computer usually don't count.

| Dude | Guideline per day | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| under 3 years | Avoid screen time if possible | Exception: Video calls with family, always accompanied |
| 3 to 6 years | about 30 minutes | selected content, if possible together |
| 7 to 10 years | about 45 to 60 minutes | fixed times, discuss content |
| from around 10 years old | more of a weekly budget, e.g. B. one hour per year of life per week | Children learn to manage their own time |

The weekly budget for older children has a big advantage. An eleven-year-old child would therefore have around eleven hours per week and can decide for themselves whether they want to play for a longer afternoon on the weekend and use less during the week. In this way, they practice taking responsibility for their own time instead of just reacting to an expired clock.

## First, measure honestly

Before new rules are drawn up, it is worth taking an honest look at the current situation for all family members. Most people significantly underestimate their own screen time. Both major smartphone systems come with built-in statistics.

- **iPhone and iPad:** In Settings under “Screen Time” you can see daily and weekly usage, broken down by apps and categories, as well as the number of unlocks and notifications.
- **Android:** In the settings under “Digital Wellbeing and Parental Controls” a diagram shows which apps were used and for how long. Depending on the manufacturer, the menu item may have a slightly different name.
- **Game consoles and smart TVs:** Many devices have their own parental controls or usage overviews, which can be found in the system settings.

Look at the numbers together after a normal week, without accusations. The result is often more surprising for parents than for children. This is exactly a good basis for a conversation at eye level: it is not the adults who decide about the children, but the whole family thinks about what they would like to change.

## Rules that really work

Good rules are clear, understandable to everyone and, if possible, apply to the entire family. Rules that relate to places and times have proven particularly useful because they are easier to adhere to than pure minute quotas.

### Screen-free places

- **Dining table:** When eating together, all devices are out of reach. This is one of the simplest and most effective rules of all.
- **Bedroom:** Smartphones and tablets are charged overnight at a shared charging point in the hallway or kitchen. If you need an alarm clock, you get a classic alarm clock.
- **Car for short trips:** Short trips are a good opportunity for conversation or music for everyone to hear.

### Screen-free times

- **Before Sleep:** Screens should be off about an hour before bed. Bright light and exciting content make it difficult to fall asleep.
- **After school:** First break, meal and homework, then free time in front of the screen.
- **Family time together:** A trip, a game night or a walk on the weekend without devices creates space for experiences.

### Clear transitions

Many conflicts arise not because of the total time, but at the moment of switching off. A game cannot be interrupted in the middle of a level, nor a sequence in the middle. It is helpful to have a warning of around five to ten minutes and to agree that the current round or episode can still be finished, but then it is over. A timer that the child sets himself feels less like a punishment than an announcement from outside.

## Use technical helpers sensibly

The systems offer tools that can be used to support agreements. On Android this is Google Family Link, on iPhone it is Screen Time in conjunction with Family Sharing. This allows you to set daily limits, a night's sleep and limits for individual app categories. These functions are also useful for adults. A self-imposed limit for social networks or a focus mode in the evening helps to change your own habits.

It is important not to see technology as a replacement for conversations. A child who understands and has helped shape the rules is more likely to follow them than one whose device is simply blocked. Technical limits are a railing that provides support in the early stages. As they grow older, they should be relaxed so that young people learn to control themselves.

In addition, a few simple settings that make the smartphone less tempting can help:

- **Reduce notifications:** Only calls and messages from important people are allowed to report, everything else remains silent.
- **Turn off autoplay:** With video apps and streaming services, you can often deactivate the automatic play of the next episode.
- **Clean up the home screen:** Apps that take up a lot of time go into a folder on the second page.
- **Grayscale:** A screen in black and white is less attractive. The function can be found in the operating aids.

## Parents as role models

Children pay less attention to what adults say than to what they do. Anyone who asks children to put their cell phones away while eating but reads the news loses credibility. Many children find it unfair that strict rules apply to them while their parents seem to be constantly on their smartphones.

This does not mean that adults are no longer allowed to use their devices. It often helps to say out loud what you are doing, such as “I'm just checking to see when the bus is leaving” or “I'll quickly answer Grandma's message”. This helps children understand that the smartphone is a tool and not just a pastime. Anyone who consciously includes cell phone-free moments, for example when reading aloud, picking them up from school or while playing, shows that undivided attention is something valuable.

## When things get difficult

Sometimes things get out of balance. Warning signs can be that a child hardly wants to do anything else, reacts very irritably when the device is taken away, neglects friends and hobbies, sleeps significantly less or is slacking off in school. Secretiveness, such as playing under the covers at night, is also a signal.

In such cases, it helps to remain calm and ask why first. Often there is something else behind excessive use: boredom, loneliness, stress at school or the desire to belong to a group. If only the device is banned, the actual problem remains. If the situation does not improve despite discussions, parenting advice centers, the pediatrician's office or specialist centers for media addiction are good places to start. The parents' hotline against grief can be reached free of charge on 0800 111 0 550.

## A five-step family media plan

If you want to make a change, you can start with a simple plan.

1. **Measure:** Honestly observe the screen time of each family member for a week.
2. **Speaking:** Discuss together what is going well and what is bothering everyone, without assigning blame.
3. **Set:** Select two or three rules that apply to everyone, such as cell phone-free dining table and hallway charging space.
4. **Write it down:** Hang the rules in a visible location, such as on the refrigerator, or fill out a media usage agreement.
5. **Review:** After a month, see together what works and adjust the rules.

Small steps are better than big bans. Anyone who tries to change everything overnight often fails after just a few days. On the other hand, if you introduce one rule after another, you create lasting habits.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does homework on the tablet count as screen time?**
Mostly not. The guideline values ​​refer to recreational use. Nevertheless, it makes sense to pay attention to breaks, because studying on a screen also tires your eyes and head.

**Is TV better than your smartphone?**
Not necessarily. The content and type of use are crucial. Watching TV together and talking about what you've seen is more valuable than time spent alone on your smartphone, but the TV can also become a constant source of entertainment.

**Should I use screen time as a reward or punishment?**
This is usually not a good idea. Using screen time as a reward makes it even more desirable, and deprivation as a punishment often leads to arguments without changing actual behavior. Fixed, previously agreed rules are better.

**What if all friends are allowed more?**
Every family has its own values. It helps to explain your reasons and listen to the child. Sometimes it's also worth talking to other parents in the class, because other people's rules are often not as relaxed as the children say.

**Does this also apply to adults?**
Yes. Adults also benefit from screen-free time, especially before bed. Those who set their own limits often sleep better, are more concentrated and have more time for their family.

## Conclusion

Healthy use of screens does not come from strictly counting minutes alone, but rather from good habits. Pay attention to what is used, when and what is left out. Fixed screen-free places such as the dining table and bedroom, clear transitions when switching off and rules that apply to the whole family defuse daily arguments. Technical helpers support, but do not replace, the conversation. And if you set a good example as an adult, you give children the most important tool: the ability to consciously put the device aside.
