Video calling your grandchildren, seeing photos of the family, looking up the bus timetable, calling the pharmacy or getting help quickly in an emergency: a smartphone can make life easier and richer in old age. At the same time, it seems complicated to many older people. The font is small, the symbols are puzzling, and messages that you don't understand constantly appear. The good news: Modern smartphones can be set up to be simple, clear and secure. These instructions help with the purchase, with the most important settings and show relatives how they can provide good support.

## Which device fits?

### Senior cell phone or normal smartphone?

There are two ways:

- **Special senior cell phones and smartphones** have large buttons, a loud speaker function, an emergency call button on the back and a very simplified interface. They are suitable for people who primarily want to make phone calls and perhaps see a few photos. However, the apps and updates are often limited, and some models only receive security updates for a short time.
- **Normal smartphones** from well-known manufacturers offer more options, longer updates and can also be simplified, for example with “Simple Mode” on Samsung or “Assisted Operation” on the iPhone.

For most older people who want to use photos, WhatsApp and video calling, a regular smartphone with a simplified interface is a better choice. For someone who really just wants to make calls and can't handle touchscreens, a push-button cell phone with an emergency call button may make more sense.

### What to pay attention to when buying?

- **Large, bright display:** From around 6.5 inches, text is easy to read without the device becoming unwieldy.
- **Loud, clear speaker** and good compatibility with hearing aids. iPhones and many Android devices support connecting directly to hearing aids via Bluetooth.
- **Long battery life** so the device doesn't run out of battery every evening.
- **Long update promise** to keep the device secure for a long time.
- **Same system as the family:** If children and grandchildren use an iPhone, it is often easier if grandma also has an iPhone, and vice versa. Then it will be easier to help.
- **Robustness:** A sturdy case with an edge and a protective film prevent damage in the event of a fall.

A **charging cradle** or wireless charging makes charging easier when plugging in the cable is a hassle.

## The most important settings for better readability

### Larger font and symbols

- **iPhone:** *Settings > Display & Brightness > Text Size* and *Bold Text*. There are even larger levels under *Accessibility > Display & Text Size > Larger Text*. All elements can be enlarged under *Display & Brightness > Display Zoom*.
- **Android:** *Settings > Display > Font size and style* and *Display size* or *Screen zoom*. Many devices have additional options under *Accessibility*.

### Simplified interface

- **Samsung:** *Settings > Display > Easy Mode* shows larger icons and a clearer home page.
- **Other Android devices:** Many manufacturers have a similar “Easy Mode” or “Senior Mode”. Alternatively, there are starter apps with large tiles.
- **iPhone:** The **Assisted Access** under *Settings > Accessibility* only shows selected apps with large tiles and simplified functions, such as calls, messages, camera and photos. It is particularly suitable for people with memory or concentration difficulties.

### Less distraction

- **Clean up the start screen:** Only the apps that are really needed on the first screen. Everything else in a folder or on a second page.
- **Reduce notifications:** Games, messaging apps and shopping apps can be silent.
- **Uninstall unnecessary apps** that only confuse you.

### Listening and speaking

- Set **ringtone loud and clear**, possibly additional **vibration** and **flash on calls** (iPhone: *Accessibility > Audio & Visual*; Android: *Accessibility > Flash notification*).
- **Live Captions** display spoken content as text, even during video calls.
- **Voice input** instead of typing: The microphone symbol on the keyboard allows messages to be dictated.
- **Voice assistant:** With “Hey Siri, call Peter” or “Hey Google, call Maria” you can make calls without searching in your contacts.

## Security and emergency

### Emergency contacts and emergency passport

Set up an **emergency pass** together that rescue workers can see without unlocking: name, date of birth, previous illnesses, medications, allergies, blood type and emergency contacts.

- **iPhone:** In the *Health* app under *Emergency Pass*. Enable Show When Locked.
- **Android:** *Settings > Security and Emergency* or in the *Personal Security* app.

### Emergency call

The **112** always works, even without unlocking and without SIM credit. Many smartphones have an **emergency call SOS**: If you press the side button several times quickly, the emergency call is automatically dialed and emergency contacts receive the location. Try out together how this works, of course without actually triggering the emergency call. You can find the setting on the iPhone under *Emergency SOS*, on Android under *Security and Emergency*.

**Fall Detection:** Apple Watch and some other smartwatches detect serious falls and automatically call for help if the person is unresponsive. This can be a valuable addition for people who live alone. There are also home emergency call systems offered by welfare associations.

### Share location

With the consent of the older person, the location can be shared with relatives, for example via “Where is?” (iPhone) or Google Maps (location sharing). This provides security during walks or when dementia begins. It is important that the person knows and agrees that their location is visible.

## Protection against fraud

Unfortunately, older people are often the target of fraudsters. A few rules help:

- **Do not answer unknown calls** or hang up immediately if someone wants money. Many smartphones can silence calls from unknown numbers.
- **Never pass on codes** that come via SMS and never give bank details on the phone.
- **If you ask for money, always call back** using the known number. The scams “Hello mom, this is my new number” and shock calls with alleged accidents are widespread.
- **Do not click links in SMS** from parcel services or banks.
- Agree on a **family password** that can be requested over the phone.

Write the most important rules on a piece of paper and put it next to the phone or in the phone case.

## For relatives: support patiently

The best technology is of little use without good accompaniment. A few tips for children, grandchildren and friends:

1. **Don't take over, show.** Instead of typing quickly, let the person do the steps themselves. It's slower, but it's the only way to learn.
2. **One thing per meeting.** Video call today, send photos next time. Too much to overcome at once.
3. **Written instructions** with large letters and screenshots help with repetition. You can also print out a small quick guide and put it next to the phone.
4. **Repetition is normal.** If it doesn't fit the third time, it will fit the fifth time.
5. **No technical terms.** Instead of “open the app”, it is better to “tap on the green image with the telephone receiver”.
6. **Set up remote help:** With apps for remote maintenance, you can look at your smartphone and help if you are not there. Only use reputable programs from well-known manufacturers and explain clearly that no one else is allowed to have such access, especially no stranger on the phone.
7. **Maintain dignity.** Older people have lives full of experiences. Technology is just a tool, and there is no shame in being insecure with it.

## Courses and help on site

Many cities and municipalities offer smartphone courses for older people, for example at adult education centers, in senior offices, libraries, welfare associations or in multi-generational homes. Digital angels and other initiatives also sometimes come directly to the home or offer consultation hours. Learning in a group with like-minded people is often more fun than learning alone with your grandchild.

## The first few weeks: a gentle learning plan

Anyone who gets a new smartphone often wants to understand everything at once and then quickly becomes overwhelmed. A learning plan in small steps, in which only one or two new skills are added each week, works much better. Once something is secure, it stays, and the good feeling of having accomplished something makes you want to take the next step.

The first week is just about making calls: turning on the device, unlocking it, calling a contact from the favorites list and answering a call. This includes putting the device on the charging cable in the evening, ideally always in the same place. In the second week, messages are added, for example writing a short message to the daughter or opening a photo of the grandchildren. In the third week the camera follows, so take a photo and find it in the gallery. Only then is it worth trying out video telephony, the timetable or the weather app.

A small paper cheat sheet next to the device is helpful. The most important processes are written in large font in your own words, such as “Press the green receiver, then tap the name”. It also includes the unlocking PIN in a form that only the person understands and the telephone number of a trusted person if they have any questions. It is important that the piece of paper stays at home and is not in the cell phone case so that anyone who finds the device does not immediately get the PIN.

## Typical stumbling blocks and how to avoid them

Some situations repeatedly unsettle older users. Anyone who knows them can defuse them in advance.

- **Accidental touches:** A light swipe across the screen suddenly opens a menu. A slightly longer touch delay can be set in the accessibility features, and a sturdy case with an edge prevents the palm from touching the screen.
- **Disappeared Icons:** An app icon was accidentally moved or removed. On Android, a simplified home screen with a locked layout helps. On the iPhone, you can use “Supported Access” to set up an interface with a few fixed apps.
- **Constant Warnings:** Confuse pop-ups with updates, advertisements or queries. Unnecessary apps should be uninstalled and notifications should be limited to the most important things, i.e. calls, family messages and appointments.
- **Too quiet or too loud:** Ringtone and handset volume are separate settings. It's best to set both together and try it out with a test call.
- **Battery suddenly empty:** A charging cradle or wireless charging pad makes daily charging easier because there is no need to plug in a small plug.

If something goes wrong, no one should be ashamed. Mistakes are part of learning, and almost everything can be undone with just a few simple steps. A calm sentence like “It happens to everyone, we’ll look at it together” often has more effect than any explanation.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can you use a smartphone without internet?**
Calling and texting work without internet. However, for WhatsApp, video calls, emails and apps you need an internet connection, either WiFi at home or mobile data in the tariff.

**Which tariff is enough?**
A few gigabytes a month are often enough for telephony, WhatsApp and occasional video calls, especially if there is WiFi at home. Look for an all-network flat rate for calls and cost control, for example a prepaid tariff.

**What if the smartphone keeps doing something unwanted?**
Often it is due to touch that is too sensitive. You can set the accessibility features to require touches to last longer before they count, or to ignore repeated touches.

## Conclusion

A smartphone can give older people a lot of quality of life if it is selected and set up appropriately: large display, loud speakers, long updates and the same system as the family. Larger font, a simplified mode, a tidy home screen, emergency pass and SOS function make it easy and safe to use. Clear rules against fraud and patient support from relatives or on-site courses ensure that uncertainty gradually turns into self-confidence.
