Quickly check your emails in the café, upload a presentation on the ICE train, stream a film in the hotel in the evening: free Wi-Fi hotspots are practical and save mobile data volume. At the same time, we keep hearing warnings that public networks are dangerous. How big is the risk really today? And what can you do to protect yourself? This guide explains soberly what can happen on public Wi-Fi, what is no longer as easy to do as it used to be, and what settings you can use to stay safe while on the go.

## What can happen on a public WiFi

On public Wi-Fi, you share the network with strangers. An attacker on the same network or an operator with malicious intent could try:

- **Read unencrypted traffic.** If data is transmitted without encryption, someone on the same network can in principle intercept it.
- **Setting up a fake WLAN.** An attacker calls his network “Hotel_Guest” or “Telekom”, and devices connect to it because they know the name. Experts call this the “Evil Twin”.
- **Direct you to fake login pages**, such as a site that claims to require your email address and password to access Wi-Fi.
- **Attack your device directly** if it offers online file or printer sharing or has outdated software.

## Which is better today than it used to be

The good news: The vast majority of websites and apps today encrypt their connections using **HTTPS** or **TLS**. You can recognize this by `https://` at the beginning of the address. The content of the connection, i.e. passwords, messages and bank details, cannot be read by others on the WLAN. Messengers like WhatsApp and Signal also encrypt end-to-end, banking apps use their own security mechanisms.

This means that the risk of someone in the café simply reading your password is much lower today than it was ten years ago. But it's not completely gone, especially with fake WLANs, fake login pages and devices without updates.

## The most important rules for on the go

### 1. Choose the right network

- Ask the staff for the **exact name** of the WiFi, in the hotel at the reception, in the café at the counter.
- Be wary of several similar-sounding networks, such as “Flughafen_Free” and “Flughafen Free WiFi”.
- Use the official network on the train, such as “WIFIonICE” on Deutsche Bahn trains, and log in via the homepage, which opens automatically.

### 2. Be careful on the login page

When you connect, many hotspots display a login page where you confirm terms of use or enter a room number. That's normal. You should be suspicious if the site:

- requires your **email password** or social network password,
- **credit card details** are requested even though access is supposed to be free,
- asks you to install an **app or certificate**.

If in doubt: disconnect and use mobile data.

### 3. Pay attention to HTTPS

Only enter passwords and personal information on sites with `https://`. Modern browsers warn if a site is not encrypted. Take these warnings seriously and do not click Continue Anyway. You can also activate a **“HTTPS Only” mode** in the browser:

- **Chrome:** *Settings > Privacy and security > Security > Always use secure connections.*
- **Firefox:** *Settings > Privacy & Security > HTTPS Only Mode.*
- **Safari:** automatically promotes connections to HTTPS when possible.

### 4. Turn off releases

Your laptop should be invisible to others on public networks:

- **Windows:** When connecting to a new network, select the **“Public”** network profile. You can change it later in *Settings > Network and Internet > Wi-Fi > [Network] > Network Profile Type*. This means that file and printer sharing are disabled and your PC is not visible to others.
- **Mac:** Under *System Preferences > General > Sharing*, turn off all sharing that you don't need on the go and turn on the firewall under *Network > Firewall*.
- **AirDrop and Nearby Share/Quick Share:** Set to Contacts Only or Off to prevent strangers from sending you files.

### 5. Turn off automatic connection

Smartphones and laptops automatically connect to networks they have previously used. An attacker can exploit this by setting up a network with a known name.

- Delete **saved public networks** that you no longer need. On iPhone under *Settings > Wi-Fi > Edit*, on Android under *Settings > Network and Internet > Wi-Fi > Saved Networks*.
- For public networks, turn off **“Connect automatically”**.
- Turn off Wi-Fi when you're not using it when you're on the go. This also saves battery.

### 6. Install updates

Many attacks on foreign networks exploit known vulnerabilities in outdated software. A device with current updates is much more difficult to attack. Install updates before traveling.

## Do you need a VPN while traveling?

A **VPN** (Virtual Private Network) creates an encrypted tunnel between your device and a VPN provider's server. Everything you do on the internet goes through this tunnel. Others on public Wi-Fi can no longer see anything, not even which websites you visit.

### When a VPN makes sense

- If you **frequently work on public networks**, such as on business trips.
- If you need to connect to the **company network**. Your employer usually provides its own VPN.
- If you want to access your **home network**. Many current routers, such as the FRITZ!Box, offer their own VPN via WireGuard. This allows you to surf on the go using your own connection at home.
- If you are traveling in a country where certain services are blocked or monitored.

### What you should pay attention to

- **Free VPN apps** are often problematic. Some are financed through advertising or the sale of user data, and some even contained malware. If a VPN doesn't cost anything, your surfing behavior is often the product.
- A VPN only shifts trust: instead of trusting the WLAN operator, you trust the VPN provider. Therefore, choose a well-known provider with a clear data protection declaration that has been independently verified.
- A VPN **does not** protect against phishing, malware or insecure passwords.

**The following applies to most people:** With HTTPS, up-to-date devices and a little attention, you are well protected even without a VPN. A reputable VPN is a useful addition for frequent travelers.

## The alternative: your own hotspot

It is often easiest and safest not to use any third-party Wi-Fi at all. With your smartphone's **personal hotspot** you can turn your cell phone into a router for your laptop. You protect the connection between your laptop and your cell phone with your own password.

- **iPhone:** *Settings > Personal Hotspot.*
- **Android:** *Settings > Network and Internet > Hotspot and Tethering.*

Many tariffs now include generous data volumes, and your domestic tariff applies in other EU countries thanks to “Roam like at home”. However, outside the EU, for example in Switzerland, Great Britain or Turkey, high costs can arise. Check the conditions of your plan before traveling or purchase a **Travel eSIM**.

## Be particularly careful with sensitive items

Even though connections are now mostly encrypted, your own mobile network is the better choice for particularly sensitive processes. These include:

- **Online banking** and transfers,
- **Changes to passwords** and security settings,
- **Purchases** with entering card details,
- **Access to health data** or government portals.

If there is no other option, use the **official app** instead of the browser and make sure that no one is looking at your screen.

## A look over your shoulder

Not all risks are technical. In cafes, trains, and waiting halls, someone can easily look at your screen while you type a password or read a confidential email.

- Sit so that no one is behind you.
- Use a **privacy film** for laptops if you often work on the go.
- **Lock the screen** (Windows + L or on Mac Ctrl + Command + Q) when you stand up briefly, even for a moment.
- **Never leave your laptop and smartphone unattended**.

## Checklist for on the go

1. Ask the staff for the exact name of the WLAN.
2. Do not enter passwords or card details on the login page.
3. Select “Public” network profile, releases.
4. Only use sites with HTTPS, take warnings seriously.
5. It is better to use mobile data or your own hotspot for banking.
6. Delete saved public networks after the trip.
7. Lock screen when you get up.

## Hotel WiFi and holiday apartment

In hotels and holiday apartments, many people use the WiFi for several days, often for streaming and work. A few additional notes:

- **Smart TVs** in hotel rooms and holiday apartments: If you log in to Netflix, YouTube or another service there, log out again before you leave. Many services also offer a “Log out of all devices” feature in their account settings.
- **Streaming from smartphone** to TV via Chromecast or AirPlay: Check that you are actually connected to the device in your room and not to the one in the next room.
- **Holiday apartments with a private router:** Here the landlord or the previous guest may have access to the router. Treat the network like public Wi-Fi.
- **Work devices:** If you're traveling with a work laptop, follow your employer's guidelines, which often require a company VPN.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a WiFi with a password more secure than an open one?**
A password that all guests know only provides limited protection. Newer hotspots with the “Enhanced Open” (OWE) process also encrypt the connection of each device individually, even without a password, and with WPA3 a guest can no longer simply read the data traffic of other guests. Nevertheless, the rules above apply.

**Can someone access my photos over WiFi?**
Only if you have sharing turned on or your device has an unpatched vulnerability. With the current system and releases switched off, this is very unlikely.

**Are airport charging stations dangerous?**
Theoretically, data could be transferred via prepared USB ports. Modern smartphones ask questions before trusting a connected device. Don't confirm, or use your own power adapter on the wall outlet.

## Conclusion

Public Wi-Fi is now less dangerous than its reputation because almost all connections are encrypted. The main risks are fake networks, fake login pages and outdated devices. Choose the right network, don't enter passwords on login pages, turn off sharing and keep your devices up to date. For banking and other sensitive things, mobile data or your own hotspot are a safer choice, and for frequent travelers, a reputable VPN is a good addition.
