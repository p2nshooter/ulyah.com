Many people think about their own will, funeral or living will at some point. But very few people have regulated what happens to the emails, the photos in the cloud, the account on the social network, the current streaming subscriptions or the credit on the payment service. Today, almost all of us leave behind an extensive digital legacy. It can be very stressful for relatives to find out which accounts exist, how to get important documents and how to terminate contracts during an already difficult time. With a little precaution this can be made much easier. These instructions explain what belongs to the digital estate, what legally applies in Germany and how you can take precautions step by step.

## What belongs to the digital legacy?

The digital legacy includes everything that a person leaves behind on the internet and on their devices. This is usually a lot more than you think at first glance.

- **Email accounts:** Invoices, contracts, account statements and access to other services often come together here. The email inbox is therefore often the key to everything else.
- **Social networks and messengers:** Profiles, photos, messages and contacts on Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp and similar services.
- **Cloud storage:** Photos, videos and documents on iCloud, Google Drive, OneDrive or Dropbox.
- **Contracts and Subscriptions:** Streaming services, mobile communications, newspaper subscriptions, software licenses and memberships that continue on a monthly basis.
- **Money and values:** Online banking, payment services, credit in online shops, bonus points and, if applicable, crypto assets.
- **Devices:** Smartphones, computers and tablets with locally stored data, often protected by a PIN or password.
- **Own content:** Websites, blogs, purchased digital books or music, domains and online shops.

## The legal situation in Germany

For a long time it was unclear whether heirs could access online accounts of deceased people. The Federal Court of Justice fundamentally clarified this in July 2018. The case involved the parents of a deceased girl who demanded access to her Facebook account. The court ruled that the user agreement with a social network passes to the heirs like any other contract. This means that you assume the rights and obligations of the deceased person and are generally also allowed to access the contents of the account.

In practice, this means: Digital accounts are, in principle, inherited in the same way as letters, diaries or a bank account. However, providers usually require proof before granting access or deleting an account. This usually includes a death certificate and a certificate of inheritance or comparable proof of heir status. Without precautions, such procedures can take weeks or months. Some providers abroad are also difficult to reach or have their own rules. This makes it all the more important to decide during your lifetime who should take care of it and what should happen to the data.

Note: This guide does not replace legal advice. In the case of complicated inheritance cases, companies or larger assets online, it makes sense to seek advice from a notary or a lawyer specializing in inheritance law.

## Prevention in five steps

### 1. Create an overview

The most important step is a list of all important accounts and contracts. For each service, note the name, email address or username used, and what should happen in the event of death: delete, memorialize, pass on data to specific people, or cancel. The passwords themselves don't necessarily belong on this list, more on that in a moment. It is also important to list the devices, such as smartphones and laptops, and where the unlock PINs can be found.

Also think about accounts that you rarely use. An old email inbox, an account at an online shop with a saved payment method or a photo service that you tried years ago can later become a mystery to relatives. If, when creating the list, you realize that you no longer need services, this is a good opportunity to delete them straight away.

### 2. Store passwords securely

The most convenient and secure solution is a password manager. All access data is stored in encrypted form and it is sufficient to store the main password securely. You can, for example, keep this one password in a sealed envelope in a safe place, such as a bank safe deposit box, with a trusted person or with a notary. Some password managers also offer emergency access, where a named person is granted access after a waiting period.

It is not recommended to write down passwords directly in the will. A will is opened by the probate court after death and everyone involved learns its contents. You would also have to change the will every time a password changes. It is better to specify in the will or in a power of attorney who should take care of the digital estate and to keep the access data separately.

### 3. Grant power of attorney beyond death

With a power of attorney that expressly applies beyond death, a trusted person can act immediately without having to wait for the inheritance certificate. The power of attorney should state that it also applies to digital accounts, data and devices and that the authorized person can decide whether accounts are deleted, terminated or continued. The power of attorney should be signed by hand and dated. Many consumer advice centers offer templates and information about this.

### 4. Use the settings of the major services

Several major providers have introduced their own death features. It's worth setting it up today.

- **Apple:** You can use the “Estate Contact” in your Apple account settings to designate who will have access to your data stored in iCloud, such as photos, messages, and notes, after your death. You will receive an access key and must provide Apple with a death certificate. Keychain passwords and purchased media are not included.
- **Google:** The “Account Inactivity Manager” lets you control what happens when your account has not been used for a certain period of time. You can designate up to ten people to be notified and allowed to download selected data, and choose whether the account should be deleted afterwards.
- **Facebook:** Here you can designate a legacy contact who can manage a memorial profile or choose to have your account permanently deleted upon death. Relatives can also have an account memorialized or deleted on Instagram.

### 5. Capture desires

In addition to technology, it is also about personal wishes. Do you want your photos to go to specific family members? Should your profile remain as a reminder or should it disappear? Are there messages or files that you don't want anyone to read? Anyone who answers such questions in writing relieves relatives of difficult decisions. It's best to talk to the people who will be taking care of it later so that they know where to find the documents.

## For relatives: what to do after a death

When a person dies without any precautions, relatives are often faced with a confusing mountain. This order helps to create order.

1. **Keep calm:** A lot of things don't have to be done right away. Give yourself time for the first few days.
2. **Secure devices:** Do not reset or dispose of smartphones, computers and tablets. They often contain important data and references to accounts.
3. **Get an overview:** Account statements show which subscriptions and contracts are regularly debited. Post and emails also provide information about services used.
4. **Cancel contracts:** Cancel current subscriptions, mobile communications and memberships with a death certificate. Many providers have their own forms for this.
5. **Clarify accounts:** Request access to important services or have your account deleted. This usually requires a death certificate and proof of inheritance.
6. **Back up data:** Before deleting accounts, download photos and important documents if desired and permitted.

It is helpful to keep your own list of which provider has already completed which steps. Consumer advice centers offer information sheets and sample letters on digital discounts that make contact with providers easier.

## Money on the internet and crypto assets

Precaution is particularly important when there is real money online. Online bank accounts are inherited like regular bank accounts. The bank is informed after the death and releases the credit to the heirs if the status of heir is proven. Credit from payment services should also be on the overview.

With crypto assets it is more complicated. If they are on a trading platform, similar rules apply as with a bank. However, if they are in their own wallet, access is not possible without the recovery phrase, often a sequence of twelve or 24 words, not even for heirs with an inheritance certificate. This phrase must therefore be stored particularly carefully, separated from the overview and only accessible to the intended person. Otherwise the assets are irretrievably lost.

## Commercial Probate Services

There are companies that offer to track down and manage all online accounts after a death. Some also require that you deposit your passwords with them during your lifetime. Caution is advised here. Consumer advocates point out that it is not clear what will happen to the data if such a company goes out of business, and that giving all passwords to strangers poses a security risk. Anyone considering such a service should carefully examine the contract terms, costs and data protection. For most people, a good overview, a password manager and a trusted person are completely sufficient.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are heirs allowed to read the private messages of the deceased?**
According to the Federal Court of Justice ruling in 2018, heirs enter into the user agreement and generally have access to the contents of the account, including messages. If you don't want this, you should specify in writing during your lifetime what should happen to certain content.

**What happens to purchased e-books, music and films?**
Digital purchases are often just usage licenses tied to the account. Whether they can be transferred depends on the provider's conditions. Transmission is often impossible.

**How often should I update my overview?**
About once a year and whenever you use an important new service or delete an account. A fixed date, for example at the beginning of the year, helps.

**Is it enough to tell my partner the master password?**
This can be a solution if you trust each other completely. However, it is safer to leave it in writing in a protected place so that a replacement person also has access if something happens to both of them.

**What happens if no one does anything?**
Contracts initially continue and are debited as long as the bank account exists. Accounts often remain open for years. Some providers automatically delete inactive accounts after a long period of time, which can result in valuable photos being lost.

## Conclusion

Digital inheritance is a topic that people tend to put off, but it can save relatives a lot of grief. With an overview of all accounts, a password manager with a secure main password, a power of attorney after death and the estate functions of the major services, the most important things are taken care of. Additionally, note what should happen to photos, profiles and messages and talk to the person who will take care of it. An afternoon of work today can save the people you care about many weeks of uncertainty later.
