“Your account has been temporarily blocked”, “Your package could not be delivered”, “Your payment has failed”: messages like this end up in mailboxes and smartphones millions of times every day. Many of them are fake. Behind them are criminals who want to get passwords, bank details or money. This scam is called **Phishing**, a portmanteau of “password” and “fishing”, i.e. fishing for access data. The good news: With a little practice, you can spot most counterfeits in just a few seconds. This guide will show you what you need to pay attention to.

## How phishing works

Phishing almost always follows the same pattern:

1. You receive a message that looks like it comes from a well-known company: your bank, a parcel delivery service, an online shop, a streaming service, the health insurance company or even a government agency.
2. The message creates **pressure**: an account is being blocked, a payment is pending, a deadline is expiring.
3. A **link** or an **attachment** should solve the problem.
4. The link leads to a **fake website** that looks exactly like the original. There you should log in, enter card details or install an app.
5. The criminals immediately use the data to withdraw money, take over accounts or place orders in your name.

Phishing occurs via **email**, **SMS** (smishing), via **messenger**, as a **call** (vishing) and even via **QR codes** (quishing), for example on fake letters or stickers on parking meters.

## The ten most important warning signs

### 1. Pressure and threats

“Act within 24 hours”, “Last reminder”, “Your account will be deleted”. Reputable companies rarely put you under pressure with short deadlines, and especially not via email with a link. Pressure is the strongest warning signal because it is designed to stop you from thinking.

### 2. An impersonal salutation

“Dear customer” or “Hello user” instead of your name. This isn't a sure sign because some scammers know your name from data leaks, but it's a common one.

### 3. A suspicious sender

The name displayed may be “Sparkasse” or “DHL”, but the actual address may be something completely different, such as `service@kundenservice-info24.xyz`. Tap the sender name on your phone or hover over it on your computer to see the real address. Pay attention to small differences like `paypa1.com` instead of `paypal.com` or `amazon-de.support.com`.

Attention: Senders can also be forged. A real-looking address is therefore not proof.

### 4. Links that lead somewhere else

Before you click on a link, check where it actually leads:

- **On the computer:** Hover over the link without clicking. The destination address appears at the bottom of the window.
- **On smartphone:** Hold your finger on the link until a preview with the address appears. Then don’t tap “Open.”

What matters is the part of the address **immediately before the first single slash**. For `https://www.sparkasse.de/konto` that is `sparkasse.de`. For `https://sparkasse.de.konto-sicherheit.com/login` it is `konto-sicherheit.com`, and that has nothing to do with the savings bank.

### 5. Request to enter data

Your bank will never ask you via email or SMS to enter or confirm your PIN, TAN, access data or card number. This also applies to PayPal, Amazon and other large companies.

### 6. Unexpected attachments

Invoices, reminders or “delivery notes” as ZIP files, Word documents with macros or files with strange endings such as `.exe`, `.js` or `.iso` are dangerous. Don't open attachments you don't expect, even if they appear to be from people you know.

### 7. Spelling and design

In the past, phishing was often recognized by clumsy German. With translation programs and AI, texts today are often error-free. However, missing umlauts, strange syntax and mixed languages ​​are still warning signs.

### 8. Too good to be true

“You won an iPhone”, “You are entitled to a tax refund”, “Exclusive offer only today”: winnings for which you did nothing are almost always a scam.

### 9. Unusual ways

A package notification via SMS even though you haven't ordered anything, a message from the health insurance company via WhatsApp or a "Microsoft employee" on the phone: If the channel doesn't match the sender, be suspicious.

### 10. Fake websites

There are also clues on the website itself: a strange address in the address bar, missing functions other than the registration form, or a password manager that **doesn't** automatically fill in your login details. By the way, the lock symbol in the address bar only proves that the connection is encrypted, not that the site is genuine. Scammers also have encrypted sites.

## Typical phishing scams in Germany

| stitch | What is claimed | What you should do |
|---|---|---|
| Bank and savings bank | Account blocked, pushTAN procedure must be renewed | Don't click, call the bank using the known number |
| Parcel service | Parcel cannot be delivered, customs duty pending | Check the tracking number only in the official app |
| Streaming service | Payment failed, account will be terminated | Open the app and check the payment status there |
| Online shop | Order canceled, verify account | Check directly in the customer account |
| Authorities | Tax refund, fine, broadcasting fee | Authorities usually write by post; Visit the website yourself |
| Microsoft Support | Computer infected, remote maintenance required | Hang up, don't install anything |
| Mobile operator | Bill too high, credit waiting | Check in customer account |

## The golden rule: check for yourself

Instead of clicking a link in a message, go to the service **yourself**:

- Open the **official app** of your bank, parcel service or shop.
- Or type the known address **yourself** into the browser, or use a saved bookmark.
- If something is really wrong, you'll see it there.

This one habit will protect you from almost all phishing attempts, no matter how well done.

## If you are unsure

- **Call the company**, not the number from the suspicious message, but the number from the website, bank card, or your records.
- **Ask someone** you trust.
- The **Consumer Center** publishes current scam emails in the **Phishing Radar**. You can also report suspicious emails there.
- Search for a striking sentence from the message on the Internet. Often others have already reported the same scam.

## Help! I already clicked

Don't panic, but acting quickly is important.

### You just clicked on a link but didn't enter anything

Close the page. In most cases nothing happened. Keep your browser and system up to date, and run a virus scan on a computer if something is downloaded.

### You have entered login details

1. **Immediately change the password** of the affected account via the real website or app.
2. Also change it wherever you use the same password.
3. **Enable two-factor authentication** if not already done.
4. Check the account settings to see whether other devices are logged in or whether settings have been changed, such as forwarding your emails.

### You have entered bank details, TAN or card details

1. **Call your bank immediately** or the **blocking emergency number 116 116** and have your card and online banking blocked.
2. Check your bank statements and report suspicious withdrawals. You can usually return direct debits within eight weeks.
3. **Find a report** with the police, many federal states offer online surveillance.

### You have installed an app or file

If you have been instructed to install an app, such as a remote maintenance app or a supposed security app, disconnect the device from the internet, uninstall the app and inform your bank. For remote maintenance programs, a specialist should check the device. Change your most important passwords from another secure device.

## This is how you protect yourself permanently

- **Use password manager:** It only fills out access data on the real website and thus indirectly warns against fakes.
- **Two-factor authentication** for all major accounts.
- **Passkeys** where offered as they do not work on fake sites.
- Leave the **spam filter** active in the email program and mark suspicious emails as spam.
- **Install updates** for browser and system.
- Always ignore **SMS from unknown people** with links.
- **Inform family:** Older people and young people are particularly common targets. Tell us about new stitches when you hear about them.

## A short self-test

Imagine receiving this SMS: "DHL: Your package is waiting for delivery. Please confirm your address and pay €1.99 delivery fee: dhl-zustellung-de.info/xyz". What do you notice?

- You may not be expecting a package at all.
- Real parcel services do not charge a fee via SMS link.
- The address is `dhl-zustellung-de.info`, not `dhl.de`.
- The small amount is intended to get you to enter your card details, which will then be used for much larger debits.

The correct thing to do is to delete the message or report it as spam and, if you really expect a package, enter the tracking number from the order confirmation in the official app or on the real website of the parcel service.

A second example: An email from “your savings bank” asks you to “update” the pushTAN procedure by Friday, otherwise access will be blocked. The same applies here: The Sparkasse does not send you such requests with a link. Open the Sparkasse app or call your branch.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where did the scammers get my address?**
Mostly from data leaks, purchased address lists or simply by trying things out. The fact that you receive a phishing email does not mean that your computer is infected.

**Should I respond to phishing emails or click unsubscribe?**
No. Every response confirms that your address is active. Delete the message or mark it as spam.

**How do I recognize a real message from my bank?**
Many banks put important messages in the **mailbox in online banking**. If you're unsure, check there instead of clicking in the email.

## Conclusion

Phishing thrives on time pressure and trust. If you stop, check the sender and the real link address and, if in doubt, open the company's app or website yourself, you will uncover the vast majority of fakes. Never enter passwords, PINs, TANs or codes on pages that you reached via a link from a message. And if it does happen: change your password, inform the bank and file a report, the quicker the better.
