Your smartphone knows more about you than most people in your life: where you live and work, who you write to, what photos you take, when you sleep and what you are interested in. Many apps want to get this information, often for good reasons, but sometimes just to sell advertising. The good news: Android and iOS now give you very precise tools to control who sees what. This guide will show you which settings make the biggest difference and how to do them in half an hour.

## Why data protection on cell phones is important

Privacy doesn't mean you have something to hide. It's about deciding for yourself what information is collected about you. Location data shows, for example, where you live, which doctor you visit or which church, mosque or synagogue you go to. Contact lists reveal your social circle. Photos often contain location information. Such data can be shared with advertising networks and data brokers without you knowing.

A few conscious decisions are enough to stop most of this data flow without making your smartphone less useful.

## A quick overview: Data Protection Center

Both systems have a central location where you can find everything you need:

- **iPhone:** *Settings > Privacy & Security.* There you'll see all permissions by category: Location Services, Contacts, Photos, Camera, Microphone, and so on. At the bottom you will find the “App Privacy Report”, which shows which apps have accessed which data in the last seven days.
- **Android:** *Settings > Security and privacy > Privacy* or *Settings > Privacy*, depending on the manufacturer. The “Privacy Dashboard” shows which apps have used the location, camera and microphone in the last 24 hours or seven days. Under “Permission Manager” you can see all rights by category.

Open this overview at your leisure and see if anything surprises you. A flashlight app with access to your contacts or a game that constantly asks for your location are clear warning signs.

## Assign app permissions correctly

### Location

Location is the most sensitive permission. For most apps, one of these levels is sufficient:

- **Never:** for any apps that have no reason to know your location, such as games, calculators, or messaging apps.
- **When using the app:** for maps, weather, delivery services and ticketing apps.
- **Always:** only for a few exceptions, such as a family tracking app or a smart home app that is supposed to control the heating when you get home.

In addition, you can switch off the **exact position** in many apps. Then the app only sees which area you are in, but not the house number. This is completely sufficient for weather apps.

### Photos

Instead of giving one app access to all photos, you can share **only selected photos** on both systems. This is particularly useful on messengers and social networks if you only share images occasionally. Some apps don't need access at all because they use the system photo selector.

### Camera and microphone

The camera and microphone should only be used for apps where the purpose is clear: video calls, messengers, camera apps, voice recording. Both systems show a colored dot or symbol at the top of the screen when the camera or microphone is currently active. If you see this point unexpectedly, immediately check which app is behind it.

### Contacts, Calendars and Nearby Devices

When you give an app your contacts, you're also giving it other people's phone numbers and email addresses that weren't asked. Therefore, think carefully about which apps you allow this access to. On the iPhone you can now only share selected contacts.

The “Nearby Devices” or “Local Network” permission allows apps to find other devices on your Wi-Fi or via Bluetooth. Smart home and streaming apps need this, but not shopping or news apps.

### Unused apps

Android automatically revokes the permissions of apps that you haven't used for months. Make sure the “Remove permissions when app is not in use” feature is turned on. There is no direct equivalent on the iPhone; Here it is worth regularly cleaning up unused apps.

## Tracking and Advertising IDs

Many apps share data with advertising networks to recognize you across apps and websites. To do this, they use an advertising ID, a kind of identification number for your device.

- **iPhone:** *Settings > Privacy & Security > Tracking.* Turn off “Allow apps to request tracking”. Apps will then automatically be rejected and the advertising ID will not be passed on.
- **Android:** *Settings > Google > All Services > Advertising* or *Settings > Privacy > Advertising*. Select “Delete Advertising ID.” Apps will then only receive zeros instead of your ID.

In addition, you can turn off personalized advertising and pause or automatically delete web and app activity history in your Google account under “Data and data protection”. At Apple, you can control whether Apple shows you personalized advertising in the App Store under *Privacy & Security > Apple Advertising*.

## System location history and location services

In addition to the apps, the system also collects location data to improve services:

- **iPhone:** Go to *Privacy & Security > Location Services > System Services* for features such as Important Places, location-based suggestions, and product enhancement. You can switch off a lot of things without missing anything. You should leave “Find my iPhone” and “Emergency Call” active.
- **Android:** The location history in Google Maps called “Timeline” is now stored on the device. You can turn it off or have old entries automatically deleted in the Google Maps settings.

## Lock screen: what strangers see

Even without unlocking, a smartphone often shows a lot: message previews, calendar appointments, control center and voice assistants. Anyone who gets their hands on your device can read private messages or even change settings.

- **Notification Preview:** Set to only show message content after unlocking. On iPhone under *Notifications > Show previews > When unlocked*, on Android under *Notifications > Lock screen notifications > Hide sensitive content*.
- **Control Center and Quick Settings:** On the iPhone you can set what can be accessed when locked under *Face ID & Code*. For example, deactivate access to the control center so that no one can switch on flight mode and thus prevent tracking.
- **Theft protection:** Both systems now have protection functions against thieves who have spied on your PIN. On iPhone it is called “Stolen Device Protection” and on Android “Theft Protection”. Turn it on.

## Messenger, browser and cloud

### Messenger

Not every messenger is equally data-efficient. Signal and Threema collect very little metadata, WhatsApp encrypts messages end-to-end, but shares metadata with the parent company. Regardless of which one you use, you can use the privacy settings to control who sees your profile picture, your status, your last online time, and whether strangers can add you to groups.

### Browser

In the browser it helps to block third-party cookies and activate tracking protection. Safari does this by default. In Chrome, you can find the options under *Settings > Privacy and Security*. Browsers like Firefox Focus or DuckDuckGo delete everything after each session.

### Cloud backups

Backups in the cloud are convenient, but contain a lot of data. On the iPhone you can activate “Enhanced data protection for iCloud”, then almost all iCloud data is end-to-end encrypted and Apple cannot read it either. Important: You must then keep a recovery key or recovery contact safe, otherwise you will no longer be able to access your data in an emergency. Android encrypts backups with your screen lock.

## Clean photos before sharing

Many photos contain location information in the background, the so-called EXIF data. Whoever gets the picture may be able to see where it was taken, such as your home.

- **iPhone:** When sharing, tap Options at the top and turn off Location.
- **Android:** In Google Photos you can remove the location when sharing or specify in the settings that the location is generally not sent when sharing.

Messengers such as WhatsApp and Signal usually automatically remove location data from images sent, but they are often retained when sent via email or as a file.

## Your 30-minute data protection check

1. Open the data protection center and view the report from the last few days.
2. Check location access of all apps: “Never” or “When using”, exact location only where necessary.
3. Switch photo access to Featured Photos where possible.
4. Only allow microphone and camera for apps with a clear purpose.
5. Switch off tracking requests or delete advertising ID.
6. Turn off personalized advertising in your Google account or Apple.
7. Hide message previews on the lock screen.
8. Turn on anti-theft protection.
9. Delete apps that you no longer use.

Repeat this check about every six months and whenever you have a lot of new apps installed.

## Data protection for children and elders

If you're setting up a child or older family member's smartphone, it's worth taking extra care. Children like to install games that require a lot of permissions, and older people often confirm queries without reading them because they don't want to disturb them. Therefore, go through the permissions together and explain why an app does not need access to the microphone. With Family Sharing from Apple or Google Family Link, you can also specify for children that new apps are only installed after your consent.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is my phone constantly listening?**
Voice assistants wait for their activation word but, according to the manufacturers, do not process anything before it is spoken. Permanent secret eavesdropping by normal apps would reveal the systems based on the microphone display. If the idea bothers you, you can turn off the voice assistant or its wake word.

**Is it enough to turn off the location completely?**
This is possible, but impractical because navigation, weather and device search no longer work. It is better to leave the location switched on and control access per app.

**Do VPN apps provide more privacy?**
A VPN encrypts the connection to the VPN provider, but only shifts trust from your ISP to them. It doesn't help against tracking by apps. Free VPN apps often finance themselves through data.

## Conclusion

Data protection on smartphones is not rocket science. The biggest levers are consciously assigned app permissions, especially for location, photos and microphone, turning off tracking and advertising ID and a lock screen that doesn't reveal anything private. With half an hour of effort, you can close most data leaks and keep your phone just as useful as before.
