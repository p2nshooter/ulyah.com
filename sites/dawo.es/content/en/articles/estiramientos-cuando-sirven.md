For generations, stretching was a compulsory ritual before any physical activity. In PE lessons, at football training and at fun runs, the scene was always the same: legs propped on a fence, bodies leaning over and seconds counted out loud. Stretching was supposed to prevent injuries, ward off muscle soreness and improve performance.

Research over recent decades has greatly qualified these beliefs. Stretching is not useless, but it does not do everything it was credited with either. Knowing when it helps and when it does not lets you make better use of your training time. This article sums up what we know.

## Types of stretching

### Static stretching

This involves taking a muscle to a lengthened position and holding it, usually for 15 to 60 seconds. It is "classic" stretching.

### Dynamic stretching

This involves moving the joints in a controlled way through their range, repeatedly: leg swings, arm circles, lunges with a twist. The end position is not held.

### Ballistic stretching

This involves bouncing at the end of the range. It can be useful in a few very specific sports, but for most people it is not recommended because of the risk of straining the tissues.

### Proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation (PNF)

This combines contracting the muscle with stretching it. For example, the muscle is taken into a stretched position, contracted for a few seconds against resistance and then relaxed to stretch a little further. It is effective for gaining range of movement, although it requires good knowledge of the technique or help from another person.

## Stretching before exercise?

This is where our understanding has changed most. Several studies have found that **prolonged static stretching**, especially when held for more than a minute per muscle, just before an explosive effort can slightly reduce strength, power and speed for a while. The effect is small and seems to fade with short stretches and if an active warm-up follows, but it brings no clear benefit at that moment.

That is why a **dynamic warm-up** is recommended before exercise: joint mobility, activation exercises and progressive movements similar to those of the sport. It prepares the body without reducing power.

The exception is sports that demand extreme ranges of movement, such as gymnastics, dance or some martial arts, in which stretching is part of the specific preparation.

## Does it prevent injuries?

The evidence for static stretching as a way of preventing injuries in general is limited. The programmes that have been shown to reduce injuries in team sports combine a dynamic warm-up, strength, balance and landing and change-of-direction technique. Static stretching on its own does not seem to be the decisive ingredient.

That does not mean it has no role. In people with a clear mobility limitation that affects their technique, improving that mobility can be useful. But you should not expect a few minutes of stretching to make up for training mistakes such as increasing the load too quickly.

## Does it prevent muscle soreness?

No. Studies that have looked at the effect of stretching before or after exercise on muscle soreness — the pain that appears a day or two after a new or intense effort — have found very small effects or none. Soreness is reduced mainly through gradual adaptation to exercise.

## So what is it good for?

Stretching is useful for:

- **Improving flexibility and range of movement** when practised regularly over weeks.
- **Feeling looser and more relaxed** after training, which has real value for many people.
- **Complementing mobility work** in sports or activities that need wide ranges.
- **Helping to reduce the feeling of stiffness** in people who spend many hours sitting.

Interestingly, part of the improvement in flexibility gained by stretching comes from the nervous system tolerating the stretch sensation better, not just from changes in the muscle itself.

## How to stretch to gain flexibility

If your goal is to improve flexibility, these general guidelines work for most people:

- **Frequency:** several days a week, ideally every day or nearly every day.
- **Duration:** 30 to 60 seconds per stretch, which can be split into several shorter repetitions.
- **Intensity:** until you feel clear but tolerable tension, never sharp pain.
- **Timing:** after training, in separate sessions or before bed.
- **Breathing:** slow and deep, which helps you relax into the position.

## A ten-minute routine

After training or at the end of the day:

1. **Calves:** against a wall, with one leg back and the heel down. 30 seconds per side, first with the knee straight and then bent.
2. **Hamstrings:** lying on your back, with a towel around your foot, raise your straight leg. 30 to 45 seconds per side.
3. **Quadriceps:** standing, hold your ankle and bring your heel towards your bottom, keeping your knees together. 30 seconds per side.
4. **Hip flexors:** in a lunge position with your back knee on the floor, squeeze your glute and push your hips forward. 30 to 45 seconds per side.
5. **Glutes:** lying on your back, cross one ankle over the opposite knee and pull your legs towards your chest. 30 to 45 seconds per side.
6. **Chest:** with your forearm against a door frame, gently turn your body to the opposite side. 30 seconds per side.
7. **Back:** child's pose, sitting back on your heels with your arms stretched out in front. 45 seconds.

## Stretches by sport

Each activity has areas that tend to tighten or feel stiffer:

- **Runners:** calves, soleus, hip flexors and hamstrings. Stretching the hip flexors is especially useful for people who, as well as running, spend many hours sitting.
- **Cyclists:** hip flexors, quadriceps and lower back, because of the bent-over position on the bike, as well as the chest and shoulders.
- **Padel and tennis players:** the shoulder, the forearm and the muscles that rotate the trunk, especially on the dominant side.
- **Footballers:** adductors, hamstrings and hip flexors.
- **Swimmers:** chest, lats and shoulders, always gently so as not to strain the joint.

Spending a few minutes on these areas after training, or in a calm session in the evening, is a simple way of balancing out the positions that each sport repeats thousands of times.

## Stretching and mobility: not the same thing

As explained in the article on [hip and ankle mobility](/articulos/movilidad-de-cadera-y-tobillo/), mobility includes strength and control through the whole range of a joint. For sport, combining stretching with strength exercises through wide ranges usually gets better results than stretching alone. A well-controlled deep squat, a long lunge or a Romanian deadlift through its full range improve flexibility and strength at the same time.

## A five-minute dynamic warm-up

Before running, playing a match or doing strength training, this sequence prepares your joints and muscles without reducing power:

1. **Forward and backward leg swings**, holding on to a wall: 10 per leg, gradually increasing the range.
2. **Side-to-side leg swings**, crossing the leg in front of your body: 10 per leg.
3. **Walking lunges with a trunk rotation** towards the side of the front leg: 6 per side.
4. **Squats at a comfortable pace**, going a little lower each time: 10.
5. **Arm circles** forwards and backwards: 10 in each direction.
6. **Gentle skipping** or knee lifts on the spot: 20 seconds.

Then start your activity at an easy pace for a few minutes before reaching the planned intensity.

## Stretching gently at any age

Older people or those with little experience can stretch quite safely by adapting the positions: doing them sitting on a firm chair or next to a wall or worktop to hold on to, holding each position for about 30 seconds without bouncing and breathing calmly. The aim is not to reach further than anyone else, but to move more comfortably day to day. If you have a hip or knee replacement, ask your physiotherapist which positions you should avoid.

## When not to stretch

- If you have a recent muscle injury, such as a muscle tear, do not stretch the area without a professional's advice.
- If the stretch causes sharp pain, tingling or a cramping sensation, stop.
- People with joint hypermobility, who already have very flexible joints, usually benefit more from strength and control work than from more stretching.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it bad to stretch before running?

A few short stretches do not usually have much effect, but they do not add much either. A dynamic warm-up is more useful before running.

### How long does it take to improve flexibility?

With regular stretching, many people notice improvements within a few weeks. The gains are lost if you stop practising.

### Does yoga count as stretching?

Yoga combines stretching, strength, balance and breathing. It is an excellent way to improve flexibility and mobility, and it also helps you relax.

### Should I stretch if I do not do sport?

If you spend many hours sitting and feel stiff, a few minutes of stretching and mobility a day can help you feel better. But the most important thing is to move more throughout the day.

## In short

Static stretching is not the best way to prepare your body just before an explosive effort, it does not prevent muscle soreness and it does not on its own prevent injuries. But it does help to improve flexibility when practised regularly, and to feel looser. Before training, a dynamic warm-up; afterwards or at another time, calm stretching if it suits you.
