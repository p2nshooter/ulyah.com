If there were a supplement that improved muscle recovery, concentration, mood, appetite control and sporting performance, with no side effects and free of charge, everyone would take it. That "supplement" exists and it is called sleep. Yet it is probably the most neglected part of amateur athletes' training.

In a society with long working hours, screens on until midnight and late dinners, as is often the case in Spain, getting enough sleep has become a challenge. This article explains why sleep matters so much for people who do sport, how much to sleep and which habits help you rest better.

## What happens while we sleep

Sleep is not a passive state. During the night, the body and brain carry out essential tasks:

- **Tissue repair:** during deep sleep, growth hormone is released, which plays a part in muscle repair and recovery.
- **Energy restoration:** the body's energy stores are replenished.
- **Consolidating learning:** the brain processes and consolidates what was learnt during the day, including motor skills. A technical movement practised in training sticks better after a good night.
- **Regulating hormones and appetite:** sleep influences the hormones that regulate hunger and fullness.
- **Immune function:** chronic short sleep is linked to greater susceptibility to infections.

## How much sleep you need

The recommendations of the main sleep medicine societies for adults call for **seven or more hours a night** on a regular basis. Many athletes, especially those who train hard, may need a little more. Teenagers need more hours than adults.

Beyond the number of hours, **quality** and **regularity** matter: sleeping without interruption, without frequent waking, and keeping similar hours every day.

A simple sign that you are sleeping enough is waking without an alarm or easily at your usual time, and not feeling sleepy during the day.

## Sleep and sporting performance

Lack of sleep affects performance in several ways:

- **Higher perceived effort:** the same workout feels harder after a bad night.
- **Poorer endurance** in prolonged efforts.
- **Slower reaction time and less precision**, which matters a lot in sports such as padel, tennis or football.
- **Poorer decision-making** and concentration.
- **Worse mood and motivation.**

Some studies of athletes who increased their hours of sleep have found improvements in accuracy and speed, which suggests that many athletes sleep less than their bodies need.

## Sleep and injuries

Several observational studies have found that young athletes who sleep fewer hours have a higher risk of injury. Although these studies do not prove cause and effect on their own, they fit with what we know: a tired, poorly recovered body coordinates less well and tolerates load less well.

## Sleep and body composition

Short sleep is linked to increased appetite, especially for very energy-dense foods, and to poorer blood sugar control. For anyone trying to improve their body composition, sleep is as important an ally as diet and exercise.

## Habits for sleeping better

### Regular hours

Going to bed and getting up at similar times every day, weekends included, helps your body clock run regularly. Big differences between weekdays and the weekend create a kind of "social jet lag".

### Light

- **Natural light in the morning:** going outside in the first hours of the day helps to synchronise your internal clock.
- **Dim light in the evening:** reduce bright lighting in the last hour before bed.
- **Screens:** phones, tablets and computers keep the brain active, through both their light and their content. Putting them down a while before bed helps.

### The bedroom

- **Dark**, with blackout blinds or curtains.
- **Cool:** a slightly low temperature helps sleep. In summer, in much of Spain, this is a challenge; a fan and light bedding help.
- **Quiet**, or with earplugs if needed.
- **Kept for sleeping**, as far as possible, not for working.

### Caffeine and alcohol

- **Caffeine:** its effects last several hours. Avoiding coffee, tea or energy drinks in the afternoon helps many people sleep better.
- **Alcohol:** although it can help you fall asleep, it worsens the quality of your sleep in the second half of the night.

### Dinner and training times

- **Eating dinner too late or too heavily** can make it harder to sleep. A light dinner, about two or three hours before bed, is ideal.
- **Training very hard just before bed** can leave some people too wired. If you train in the evening, leave some time and finish with a calm cool-down. Other people are not affected; watch how you respond.

### A wind-down routine

Spending the last thirty minutes of the day on calm activities, such as reading a paper book, a warm shower, gentle stretching or slow breathing, helps to prepare your body for sleep.

## Naps

The siesta, so Spanish, can be a useful tool for athletes. A short nap of about twenty or thirty minutes in the early afternoon improves alertness without interfering with night-time sleep. Very long or late naps can make it harder to sleep at night. If you sleep little at night because of work or family commitments, a short nap can partly make up for it.

## Sleep before a competition

It is very common to sleep badly the night before a race or an important match because of nerves. There is no need to worry too much: what influences performance most is how you have slept on the previous nights. Try to sleep well throughout the week before and do not get obsessed if the last night is not perfect.

## A one-week plan for better sleep

Changing every habit at once is hard. This plan suggests one new adjustment each day, so that by the end of the week you have a complete routine:

1. **Monday:** choose a fixed time to get up, at weekends too, and set it on your alarm.
2. **Tuesday:** go outside for at least ten minutes during the first hour of the morning.
3. **Wednesday:** set a cut-off time for your last coffee of the day, for example early in the afternoon.
4. **Thursday:** leave your phone outside the bedroom or put it in flight mode thirty minutes before bed.
5. **Friday:** get the bedroom ready: blinds down, a window or fan to cool it, bedding suited to the season.
6. **Saturday:** have dinner half an hour earlier and make it a little lighter.
7. **Sunday:** create your thirty-minute wind-down routine and note how you slept each night of the week.

After keeping these habits up for two or three weeks, many people notice that they fall asleep sooner and wake up with more energy.

## Shifts, travel and early starts

Not everyone can choose their hours. People who work shifts, travel often or have young children have less control over their sleep. In those cases, some strategies help to minimise the impact:

- **Protect your main block of sleep** with total darkness, earplugs and your phone on silent, even if it is daytime.
- **Use short, strategic naps** before a long shift or after a short night.
- **Adjust your training** on days with less sleep: gentler, shorter sessions instead of the most intense ones.
- **Get light** at the time of day when you need to be awake, and avoid it when you need to sleep.

If your sleep is chronically affected, talking to your doctor may open up other options.

## When to seek advice

If, despite looking after your habits, you struggle to fall asleep, wake up many times, snore heavily, stop breathing for moments while asleep or feel very sleepy during the day, see your doctor. Disorders such as chronic insomnia or sleep apnoea are common and can be treated.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I catch up on lost sleep at the weekend?

Sleeping more at the weekend makes up for some of it, but not all, and it disrupts your body clock. It is better to try to get enough sleep every day.

### Are the apps and watches that measure sleep reliable?

They give a rough estimate, useful for spotting trends, but they are not as accurate as a sleep study in a laboratory. Do not get obsessed with the data: how you feel is just as important.

### Is it bad to train early in the morning if I sleep little?

If training early means regularly sleeping less than you need, it may be worth rethinking your schedule. Sleep is part of training.

## In short

Sleeping seven hours or more regularly, with steady hours, a dark and cool bedroom and a wind-down routine, is one of the most effective and simplest ways to perform better, recover sooner and reduce the risk of injury. Before looking for the latest supplement, check how you sleep.
