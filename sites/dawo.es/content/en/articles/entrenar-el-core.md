Few words have become as popular in the fitness world as "core". It turns up in gym classes, in online videos and in adverts for gadgets that promise a toned stomach in a few weeks. But behind the fashion lies an important and widely misunderstood idea: the trunk is at the centre of almost every movement we make, and training it properly goes far beyond doing hundreds of sit-ups.

This article explains what the core really is, what it is for, why classic crunches fall short and which exercises to include in your routine, whether you play a sport or simply want to feel better day to day.

## What the core is

The term "core" refers to the group of muscles that surround the spine and pelvis and work together to stabilise them. It includes:

- **The rectus abdominis**, the "six-pack" muscle.
- **The obliques**, at the sides of the abdomen.
- **The transversus abdominis**, the deepest, which wraps around the trunk like a corset.
- **The back muscles**, such as the erector spinae and the multifidus.
- **The diaphragm** above and **the pelvic floor** below.
- **The glutes and hip muscles**, which connect the trunk with the legs.

Together they form a kind of cylinder that gives the spine stability while the arms and legs move.

## What it is for

The core's main job is not so much to bend the trunk, as in a sit-up, but to **control movement**: keeping the spine stable when a force tries to move it and transferring force between the legs and arms.

Some examples:

- When you run, the trunk stops your pelvis dropping to one side with each stride.
- When you throw a ball or hit with a racket, the force starts in your legs and reaches your arm through your trunk.
- When you lift a box from the floor, the core protects your back by keeping the spine in a safe position.
- When you carry a heavy bag in one hand, the muscles on the opposite side stop you leaning over.

## Why classic sit-ups are not enough

Traditional crunches mainly work the rectus abdominis in a bending movement. They are not bad in themselves, but they have limitations:

- They train a single function, flexion, when what the trunk mostly needs is to resist movement.
- Done in large numbers, they involve a lot of repeated spinal flexion, which not every back tolerates well.
- They do not work the deep obliques, the transversus or the back and hip muscles well.

That is why a complete core programme includes **stability** exercises, in which the aim is to hold a position against a force.

## The four functions to train

### 1. Anti-extension

Stopping the lower back from arching too much.

- **Front plank:** resting on your forearms and toes, body straight, glutes squeezed. 20 to 45 seconds.
- **Dead bug:** lying on your back, arms pointing at the ceiling and knees at 90 degrees. Extend one arm and the opposite leg without your lower back lifting off the floor. 8 to 10 per side.
- **Ab wheel or towel slides**, for more advanced levels.

### 2. Anti-lateral flexion

Stopping the trunk from leaning to one side.

- **Side plank:** resting on one forearm and the side of your foot, hips raised. 15 to 40 seconds per side. With your knees on the floor to start with.
- **Suitcase carry:** walk holding a dumbbell or a heavy bag in one hand only, with your body upright. 20 to 30 metres per side.

### 3. Anti-rotation

Stopping the trunk from twisting when a force pulls on it.

- **Pallof press:** with a resistance band fixed to a point beside you at chest height, hold it with both hands and push your arms straight out in front without letting your trunk turn. Hold for a few seconds and return. 8 to 12 per side.
- **Plank with shoulder taps:** in a high plank, touch the opposite shoulder with one hand without your hips swaying. 8 to 10 per side.

### 4. Hip and back extension

- **Glute bridge:** lying on your back, lift your hips by squeezing your glutes. 12 to 15 repetitions.
- **Bird dog:** on all fours, extend one arm and the opposite leg while keeping your back stable. 8 to 10 per side.

## A fifteen-minute routine

Two or three times a week, as a complement to your training:

| Exercise | Sets | Duration or repetitions |
|---|---|---|
| Dead bug | 3 | 8 per side |
| Side plank | 2 | 20–30 s per side |
| Pallof press or plank with taps | 3 | 8 per side |
| Bird dog | 2 | 8 per side |
| Glute bridge | 3 | 12 |
| Suitcase carry | 2 | 20 m per side |

## The core in each sport

Although the principles are the same, each sport emphasises different functions of the trunk:

- **Running:** lateral stability takes centre stage. With each stride your body rests on one leg, and the gluteus medius and obliques stop your pelvis dropping to the opposite side. Side planks, single-leg bridges and suitcase carries are especially useful for runners.
- **Padel and tennis:** shots come from the rotation of the trunk, and the core must both generate that rotation and brake it. As well as the Pallof press, it is worth including controlled rotation exercises, such as throwing a medicine ball against a wall or band woodchops, from high to low and low to high.
- **Football:** it combines sprints, changes of direction, kicks and collisions. A trunk that is strong in every direction helps you keep your balance in challenges and transfer force when striking the ball. The adductors, part of the link between trunk and legs, deserve specific work: the side plank with the top leg resting on a bench, known as the Copenhagen plank, is widely used in football.
- **Cycling:** holding the same position for hours demands endurance from the back and abdominal muscles. Anti-extension exercises and the bird dog help you hold your posture without lower-back pain.

## How to progress

When an exercise becomes easy, there is no need to add minute after minute of plank. It is more useful to make it more demanding:

1. **Reduce the points of contact:** from a plank on four points to three, lifting one foot or one hand.
2. **Lengthen the lever:** in the plank, place your hands a little further forward than your shoulders; in the dead bug, extend your arms and legs further.
3. **Add load:** a light plate on your back in the plank, a heavier dumbbell in the suitcase carry.
4. **Add instability or movement:** go from holding a position to moving in it, as in the plank with shoulder taps.

As a general rule, if you can hold a front plank for more than a minute with perfect technique, it is time to move to a harder variation rather than holding it for longer.

## Breathing and abdominal pressure

An effective core is not about "sucking your stomach in" all the time. For intense efforts, such as lifting something heavy, the body creates pressure inside the abdomen by taking a breath and tensing the muscles around it, as if bracing for a small punch to the stomach. That pressure stabilises the spine. For light, everyday efforts, it is enough to keep moderate tension and breathe normally.

People with pelvic floor problems, who have recently given birth or have abdominal hernias should see a specialist physiotherapist before doing intense core exercises, because some of them greatly increase abdominal pressure.

## The core and back pain

Exercise is one of the most recommended tools for non-specific low back pain, the most common kind. Strengthening the trunk and hips, staying active and avoiding prolonged bed rest usually help. However, back pain has many causes. If it is severe, persistent, spreads down your leg or comes with tingling or loss of strength, see a doctor.

## Common myths

- **"Sit-ups burn belly fat."** It is not possible to lose fat from one specific area. Abdominal fat is reduced through the right energy balance and general physical activity.
- **"You need to do hundreds of repetitions."** Quality matters more than quantity. A few well-controlled repetitions are more useful.
- **"A strong core prevents all injuries."** It helps, but injuries depend on many factors.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many days a week?

Two or three specific sessions are enough. In addition, many strength exercises such as the squat, the deadlift or the farmer's walk already work the core.

### Can I do core work every day?

Gentle stability exercises can be done often, but the more intense ones need rest, like any other training.

### What about electrical muscle stimulation devices?

They do not replace exercise. The core is best strengthened with movements that demand coordination and control.

## In short

The core is the centre of movement, not just a group of muscles to show off. Training it with anti-extension, anti-lateral flexion, anti-rotation and hip extension exercises improves sporting performance, protects your back and makes everyday tasks easier. Fewer endless sit-ups and more control: that is the key.
