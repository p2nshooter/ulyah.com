Past a certain age, many people think that strength exercise is no longer for them. That it is for young people, noisy gyms or athletes. Nothing could be further from the truth. People over fifty are precisely the ones who benefit most from strength training, and the science clearly backs this up.

With age, the body gradually loses muscle mass and strength, a process known as **sarcopenia**. That loss is not just about appearance: it affects your ability to climb stairs, get up from a chair, carry the shopping or keep your balance. The good news is that strength training can slow it down and even partly reverse it, at any age. This article explains why and how to start.

## What happens to muscles and bones with age

- **Muscle:** from middle age, muscle mass tends to fall slowly each year, and the loss speeds up with inactivity. Power — the ability to produce force quickly — declines even faster.
- **Bone:** bone density also falls, especially in women after the menopause, which increases the risk of osteoporosis and fractures.
- **Balance:** the combination of less strength and changes in the nervous system makes balance worse, with a higher risk of falls.

Falls are one of the main causes of loss of independence in older people. Strong legs and good balance make the difference between recovering from a stumble and ending up on the floor.

## The benefits of strength training

Many studies in middle-aged and older people have shown that strength training:

- **Increases strength and muscle mass**, even in very old people.
- **Helps to maintain bone density**, especially weight-bearing exercises.
- **Improves balance and reduces the risk of falls**, above all when combined with specific balance exercises.
- **Improves blood sugar control** and metabolic health.
- **Makes everyday tasks easier** and maintains independence.
- **Improves mood** and sleep quality.

The World Health Organization's recommendations for older adults include muscle-strengthening activities on at least two days a week and varied activities that combine balance and strength, especially to prevent falls.

## Before you start

If you have a chronic condition, such as heart problems, poorly controlled high blood pressure, diabetes, diagnosed osteoporosis or significant joint problems, consult your doctor before you start. Not so that they tell you no, but to adapt the exercise to your situation. In most cases, the advice will be to keep moving, and well-dosed strength training is safe and beneficial.

If possible, a few initial sessions with an exercise professional, such as a qualified trainer or a physiotherapist, help you learn the technique and choose the right level.

## The key principles

### 1. Start gently and progress little by little

The first month is for learning the movements and getting your body used to them. Use light loads and increase them gradually.

### 2. Prioritise functional movements

The most useful exercises are those that resemble what we do in daily life: getting up from a chair, stepping up, pushing, pulling, carrying weight.

### 3. Work on power too

Once you have mastered the technique, it is worth doing some exercises with the intention of moving quickly in the lifting phase, for example standing up from the chair briskly. Power is important for reacting to a stumble.

### 4. Include balance

Balance is trained like any other ability: by practising it.

## A routine to get started

Two or three days a week, on non-consecutive days. Do 2 sets of each exercise at first and 3 when it feels comfortable.

| Exercise | Repetitions | How to progress |
|---|---|---|
| Standing up from and sitting down on a chair | 8–12 | Without using your hands; then holding a dumbbell at your chest |
| Step-ups | 8–10 per leg | A higher step or weights in your hands |
| Press-ups against a wall or worktop | 8–12 | A lower support |
| Resistance band row | 10–12 | A stronger band |
| Calf raises | 10–15 | On one leg, steadying yourself against a wall |
| Glute bridge | 10–12 | With a pause at the top or on one leg |
| Walking with bags or dumbbells | 20–30 metres | More weight |

### Balance exercises

- **Single-leg balance** next to a worktop so you can hold on if you need to: 20 to 30 seconds per side. When it gets easy, do it without support or with your eyes closed for a moment.
- **Heel-to-toe walking**, placing the heel of one foot just in front of the toes of the other: 10 to 15 steps.
- **Tai chi or balance classes**, which have been shown to reduce falls in older people.

## Checking your progress with simple tests

Beyond the weight you lift, there are simple tests that show how your strength for daily life is improving. One of the most widely used by professionals is the **30-second chair stand**:

1. Use a firm chair without arms, placed against a wall.
2. Sit in the middle, with your feet flat on the floor and your arms crossed over your chest.
3. Count how many times you stand up fully and sit down again in 30 seconds.

Another useful test is **balancing on one leg** next to a worktop, counting the seconds until you need to hold on. Repeat both tests every four to six weeks and compare yourself with yourself: it is normal to see clear improvements in the first few months of training.

## A typical week

| Day | Activity |
|---|---|
| Monday | strength, 30 to 40 minutes |
| Tuesday | brisk walking, 30 to 45 minutes |
| Wednesday | balance and mobility, 20 minutes, and a walk |
| Thursday | strength, 30 to 40 minutes |
| Friday | walking or swimming |
| Saturday | a social activity: dancing, gentle hiking, pétanque or cycling |
| Sunday | active rest |

It is not a rigid recipe: what matters is spreading strength work over two or three non-consecutive days and staying active the rest of the week. If you want to get more out of your walks, our guide to [walking 10,000 steps](/articulos/caminar-10000-pasos/) explains how.

## How much weight to use

The right weight is one that lets you complete the repetitions with good technique, finishing with the feeling that you could have done two or three more. When you can easily do the maximum number of repetitions in every set, increase the load slightly.

It is common for older people to start with loads that are too light out of fear. With correct technique and a gradual progression, most can reach loads that mean real effort for them, and it is that effort that produces the benefits.

## Food: protein matters

With age, muscle responds somewhat less well to the stimulus of training and to dietary protein. That is why many recommendations for older people suggest making sure of an adequate protein intake, spread throughout the day, from foods such as pulses, fish, eggs, dairy, meat and nuts. People with kidney disease or other conditions should ask their doctor or a dietitian about the right amount.

Vitamin D and calcium are also important for bone health. Your doctor can assess whether you need a supplement.

## Pain and joints

It is normal to feel some muscle tiredness or soreness at first. What is not normal is sharp joint pain during or after exercise. If you notice it, reduce the load or the range of movement and seek advice if it persists. People with osteoarthritis usually benefit from adapted strength exercise, which can reduce pain and improve function.

## It is never too late

One of the most hopeful messages from research is that older people, even those in their nineties, improve their strength with training. There is no age beyond which it is no longer worth it. The sooner you start, the more reserve of strength you build up for the years ahead, but starting at sixty, seventy or eighty still has clear benefits.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it dangerous to lift weights with osteoporosis?

Adapted strength exercise is usually recommended for people with osteoporosis, but some movements, such as sudden loaded bending of the spine, should be avoided. Ask your doctor and, if possible, a physiotherapist.

### Can I train on my own at home?

Yes. With a chair, a resistance band and some light dumbbells you can do a complete programme. A gym or a seniors' centre with an instructor also offers supervision and company.

### Isn't walking enough?

Walking is excellent for cardiovascular health, but it is not enough to maintain strength and muscle mass. Ideally, combine the two.

## In short

Strength training is one of the best investments in your health from the age of fifty: it slows the loss of muscle and bone, improves balance, reduces the risk of falls and maintains independence. Two or three sessions a week, starting gently, with functional exercises and some balance work, are enough to make a difference. It is never too late to get stronger.
