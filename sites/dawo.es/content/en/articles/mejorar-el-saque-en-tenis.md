The serve is the only shot in tennis that depends entirely on you. No ball arrives fast or with spin, there is no running and no guessing what your opponent intends. And yet, for many amateur players it is the most fragile shot: double faults at the important moments, first serves that never go in and second serves so soft that the opponent attacks them mercilessly.

The good news is that the serve is also the shot you can improve most by practising alone. With correct basic technique and a regular practice plan, it is possible to notice a real difference in four weeks. This article explains the essential elements of the serve and suggests a simple plan for working on them.

## The continental grip

The first change many amateurs need is the grip. It is common to serve with a forehand grip, holding the racket as if it were a frying pan. That lets you get the ball in at first, but it severely limits power and makes spin almost impossible.

The recommended grip for the serve is the **continental**, also called the "hammer" grip: you hold the racket as if you were going to drive in a nail with the edge of the frame. With this grip, the forearm can rotate naturally during the stroke, which generates speed and lets you put spin on the ball.

At first, the continental grip sends the ball off to one side and makes your serve seem worse. That is normal. After a few weeks of practice, your body learns to coordinate the forearm rotation and the ball starts going in with more power.

## The starting position

For a right-handed player:

- Stand behind the baseline, side-on, with your left foot forward pointing roughly towards the right-hand net post.
- Your right foot behind, more or less parallel to the baseline.
- Body weight evenly spread, knees relaxed.
- Racket and ball together in front of your body.

Left-handers do the same, mirrored.

## The ball toss

An inconsistent toss ruins any serve. If the ball goes up to a different place each time, your body has to correct at the last moment and the stroke loses accuracy.

Keys to a good toss:

1. **Hold the ball with your fingers**, not squeezed in your palm.
2. **Lift your arm straight**, without bending the elbow or flicking the wrist.
3. **Release the ball at roughly head height**, when your arm is extended upwards.
4. **The ball should go up a little above the point where you will hit it** and end up slightly in front of your body and towards your racket side.
5. **If the toss goes wrong, do not hit it.** Catch the ball and start again. It is allowed, and far better than forcing a bad serve.

A useful exercise is to toss the ball without hitting it and let it drop: it should land on a racket placed on the ground, a little in front of your front foot.

## The trophy position

As you toss the ball, your racket arm rises behind your body. At the moment the ball reaches its highest point, your body forms the so-called **trophy position**, because it resembles the figures on tennis trophies:

- The tossing arm, still stretched upwards.
- The racket-arm elbow, at shoulder height or slightly above.
- The knees bent, loading your weight to drive upwards.
- The shoulders turned, side-on to the net.

The power of the serve comes from that position: the legs push upwards, the trunk rotates, the shoulder turns and the arm swings up to the ball.

## Contact

At impact:

- The ball is struck **at the highest point your extended arm can reach**, slightly in front of your body.
- The forearm turns outwards at the moment of contact. This movement, called pronation, happens naturally with the continental grip.
- Your eyes stay fixed on the point of contact until after the shot.

After contact, the racket finishes its swing across the front of your body to the opposite side. Your back foot usually steps into the court to recover your balance.

## Rhythm: the lesser-known secret

Many amateurs try to serve hard with their arm, through sheer strength. The result is a stiff, slow movement. Good servers above all have **rhythm**: a fluid movement, without stops, in which each part of the body adds to the one before.

One way to find it is to count in your head: "one" as the ball and racket drop together, "two" as you toss and reach the trophy position, "three" as you strike. The movement should feel loose, not forced.

## First and second serve

In amateur tennis, a sensible strategy is:

- **First serve:** a bit more power, but aiming for a reasonable percentage. A first serve that goes in sixty per cent of the time is far more useful than a very fast one that goes in thirty per cent of the time.
- **Second serve:** with more margin over the net and with spin, so that it goes in safely. Topspin or slice makes the ball drop sooner and land in the box.

Avoid the "lollipop" second serve, pushed over with no swing. It is easy to attack and stops you progressing. Better a second serve with the same motion as the first, but with more spin and less speed.

## A four-week practice plan

Two sessions a week of twenty or thirty minutes, with a basket of balls and a free court, are enough.

### Week 1: grip and toss

- 5 minutes of tosses without hitting, checking that the ball always lands in the same place.
- 15 minutes of gentle serves with the continental grip from the service line, closer to the net, to get used to the feeling.

### Week 2: trophy position and rhythm

- Serves from the baseline, pausing for a moment in the trophy position to check your posture, and then hitting.
- Full serves aiming for a fluid movement, without worrying about power.

### Week 3: direction

- Place objects in different areas of the service box: down the T, into the body and out wide.
- Serve ten balls to each area, first from the deuce side and then from the advantage side.

### Week 4: first and second serve

- Sets of two serves: a first serve with a bit more speed and a second with spin and margin.
- Keep count of how many first and second serves go in. That percentage is your benchmark for further improvement.

## Looking after your shoulder

The serve is the most demanding shot for the shoulder. Before practising, warm up with joint mobility and resistance band exercises. If you hit a lot of serves, build up gradually over the weeks. Persistent pain in your shoulder or elbow is a signal to stop and see a professional.

## Common mistakes

1. **Tossing the ball too far back**, which forces you to arch your back.
2. **Hitting with a bent arm**, losing height and power.
3. **Looking at the service box before you hit.**
4. **Rushing between points.** Take a few seconds to breathe and prepare your serve.

## The serving rules worth knowing

- **You serve diagonally** and switch sides on every point: the first point of the game from the right.
- **You have two chances.** If both are faults, it is a double fault and a point to your opponent.
- **Foot fault:** before you strike the ball you may not touch the baseline or the court, or change position by walking or running. Jumping into the court during the motion is allowed, as long as you make contact with the ball before you touch the ground.
- **A ball that clips the net and lands in the box** is a "let": the serve is replayed without penalty, except in competitions played without that rule.
- **In doubles**, each player serves a whole game and the pair keep the same order throughout the set.

## Frequently asked questions

### Flat, slice or topspin?

The flat serve is the fastest, but it has little margin over the net. The slice curves to one side and opens up the court. The topspin (kick) serve bounces high and is the safest for the second serve, although it demands more technique. First learn a reliable serve with a little spin and add variety later.

### How many serves should I practise each session?

Between 40 and 60 serves, divided into short sets with breaks, are enough at first. It is better to serve fewer with attention than to empty several baskets with a tired arm.

### Can you serve underarm?

Yes. The rules allow an underarm serve, as long as the other rules are respected. It can be a useful surprise against an opponent who returns from far back, but it is no substitute for learning a full serve.

## In short

A good serve is built on a continental grip, a consistent toss, a balanced trophy position and a fluid movement. You do not need to be tall or very strong to serve well: you need attentive repetition. Four weeks of directed practice can turn your serve into a weapon instead of a source of nerves.
