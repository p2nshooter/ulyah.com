Running is a natural gesture. No one taught us how to do it as children, and yet, when an adult starts running regularly, questions arise about technique: whether to heel or toe first, how to move the arms, how many steps to take per minute, or how to breathe. The Internet is full of contradictory answers, and it's easy to become obsessed with details that barely matter.

The good news is that an amateur's running technique improves on its own over time, as the body adapts. Even so, there are some elements that are worth taking care of because they reduce the impact, save energy and reduce the risk of injury. In this article we review them one by one, separating what is founded from what is fashion.

## Cadence: how many steps you take per minute

Cadence is the number of steps you take in one minute. It is one of the most studied parameters and one of the most useful for the amateur runner.

When a person starts running, they tend to take long, slow strides. The problem with a stride that is too long is that the foot lands too far in front of the center of gravity. Each support then acts as a small brake, and the leg receives the impact with the knee almost extended. This pattern is associated with greater loading on the knee and hip.

Increasing your cadence slightly shortens your stride naturally and causes your foot to land closer to your body. Several studies have found that moderate increases in cadence, on the order of five to ten percent, reduce the forces supported by the knee and hip joints.

For years it was repeated that the ideal cadence was 180 steps per minute. It is a figure that comes from observations of elite runners in competition, at very high speeds. It is not a magic number for everyone. Cadence depends on height, leg length and, above all, speed: the faster you run, the higher it is.

### How to work cadence

1. **Measure your current cadence.** Count the support of one foot for thirty seconds in a comfortable roll and multiply by four. Many sports watches also display it.
2. **Raise it five percent**, no more. If you take 160 steps per minute, aim for about 168.
3. **Use a metronome or music with that rhythm** during some parts of the workout, for example five minutes out of ten.
4. **Maintain the same effort.** The goal is to take shorter steps at the same speed, not to run faster.

With a few weeks of practice, the new cadence becomes natural.

## The step: heel, midfoot or forefoot

The debate over which part of the foot should touch the ground first has generated more discussion than any other technique topic. Runners can land on the heel, midfoot, or forefoot.

The reality is that most recreational runners land on their heel, and there is no solid evidence that switching to another pattern reduces injuries overall. What does seem important is **where** the foot lands in relation to the body, rather than **which part** it touches first. A heel that hits the ground far in front of the hip, with the leg straight, is problematic. A soft heel strike, close to the vertical of the body and with the knee slightly bent, does not have to be.

Abruptly changing to a forefoot strike transfers load to the Achilles tendon and calves. Many runners who have done it cold turkey have ended up with tendinopathies. If you want to modify your stride, do it very gradually and preferably with the advice of a professional.

In practice, if you work on your cadence, your stride tends to correct itself: with shorter steps, the foot lands closer to the body and the impact is softened.

## Trunk and head posture

Good posture allows breathing to be comfortable and the body to move forward without wasting energy. Some keys:

- **Head aligned with the trunk.** Look ahead, a few meters ahead, not at your feet.
- **Upright trunk with a slight forward inclination** that originates from the ankles, not the waist. Leaning forward by bending your hips strains your lower back.
- **Shoulders relaxed and low.** When fatigue appears, the shoulders tend to rise towards the ears. Consciously lower them from time to time.
- **Loose jaw and hands.** The tension in the face and hands is transmitted to the rest of the body.

## The movement of the arms

The arms help balance the rotation of the trunk and set the rhythm of the legs. No need for exaggerated movement:

- Elbows bent at an angle close to ninety degrees.
- The movement, from front to back, without crossing the arms in front of the chest.
- Your hands are relaxed, as if you were holding something fragile without squeezing it.

On uphill slopes, a slightly more vigorous stroke helps maintain the cadence. On descents, opening your arms slightly improves balance.

## breathing

There is no universal breathing pattern. Some runners coordinate inhalations and exhalations with their steps, for example breathing in for three steps and exhaling for two, and find this useful for staying calm. Others are overwhelmed by counting. The important thing is:

- **Breathe through your nose and mouth at the same time** when the effort increases. Breathing only through your nose limits the amount of air you get in.
- **Use the diaphragm.** If you notice that you breathe only with the upper part of the chest, practice breathing at rest in which the abdomen expands when inhaling.
- **Slow down your pace if you choke.** Shortness of breath is a sign that you are going faster than your current form allows.

Flatus, that throbbing pain under the ribs, usually appears when you run shortly after eating or breathe shallowly. Slowing down, breathing deeply and gently pressing the area usually relieves it.

## Technique exercises that do help

Running technique exercises, which athletes call "drills," improve coordination and specific strength. You can do two or three of them after warming up, once or twice a week, in sections of 20 to 30 meters:

- **Low skipping:** very short and fast steps, raising the knees slightly and supporting with the front of the foot.
- **High skipping:** raising the knees to hip height, with the trunk upright.
- **Heels to gluteus:** taking the heel back with short steps.
- **Progressive strides:** stretches of 60 to 80 meters in which you gradually accelerate to a fast but controlled pace, and then brake.

Do these exercises with quality, not in a hurry. It is better to do a few good than many bad.

## The strength, the great forgotten

Many of the technical problems that appear at the end of a race do not have to do with the technique itself, but with fatigue. When the glutes and trunk get tired, the hip falls to one side with each support, the knee moves inward and the posture collapses.

Two strength sessions of twenty or thirty minutes a week do a lot for your technique: squats, lunges, glute bridges, deadlifts with light dumbbells, calf raises and planks. A strong body maintains the technique for longer.

## The technique on slopes and descents

Slopes change the way you run, and adapting to them saves energy and reduces the load on your legs:

- **Going up:** Shorten your stride and slightly increase your cadence, lean forward a little from your ankles, not your waist, and let your arms work a little more. Look a few meters ahead, not at your feet, and agree to go slower: what matters is maintaining the effort, not the pace.
- **Going down:** take short, quick steps, with the foot landing close to the body, and avoid braking with the heel too far in front, which punishes the knees and quads. Keep your shoulders relaxed and your arms slightly more open for balance.

Practicing on gentle slopes before tackling steep routes allows your legs and technique to adapt little by little.

## Myths that should be forgotten

- **"You have to run on tiptoe."** No. Running all the time on the tip of your foot overloads the calves and the Achilles tendon.
- **"180 steps per minute are mandatory."** No. It is a reference for very fast runners.
- **"Perfect technique prevents all injuries."** No. Most amateur runner injuries have more to do with increasing volume too quickly than with technique.
- **"You have to copy the technique of the professionals."** Elite athletes run at speeds that completely change the mechanics. What works for 3 minutes per kilometer is not always applicable at 6.

## How to know if you improve

An easy way to check your technique is to video yourself running sideways and forwards at your usual pace. Note where your foot lands in relation to your hip, whether your hip falls to one side, and the position of your shoulders. Repeat the recording after two or three months.

Remember that technique is a means, not an end. The goal is to run comfortably, without pain and for many years. If discomfort appears when applying any changes, go back to what you did before and consult a professional. Small adjustments, maintained over time, are worth much more than a one-week revolution.
