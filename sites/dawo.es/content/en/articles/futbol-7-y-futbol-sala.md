For most adult amateurs, playing a full eleven-a-side match is complicated: you need to gather twenty-two people, find a big pitch and have ninety minutes free. That is why, in Spain, the football that is really played among friends, workmates and amateur leagues is **seven-a-side football** (*fútbol 7*) and **futsal** (*fútbol sala*). Both are played at thousands of municipal and private facilities, and both have organised leagues for every level.

Although they share the spirit of football, they are sports with different rules, rhythms and demands. Anyone who switches from one to the other notices it straight away: the ball does not behave in the same way, the spaces are different and the way you get tired changes too. This article sums up their main differences and gives tips for adapting to each one.

## The pitch

### Seven-a-side

It is played on pitches smaller than eleven-a-side ones, often created by dividing a large pitch in two. The measurements vary according to the facility and the rules of each federation or league, but they are usually around 50 to 65 metres long by 30 to 45 metres wide. The most common surface is artificial grass.

### Futsal

It is played on an indoor court, usually wooden or synthetic, with dimensions similar to a handball court: about 40 metres long by 20 wide. The goals are smaller, three metres wide by two high, and the goal area is semicircular or D-shaped around the goal.

## The ball

This is one of the most noticeable differences:

- **Seven-a-side:** a conventional football is used, usually a size 5 for adults.
- **Futsal:** the ball is smaller and heavier, a size 4, and is designed to bounce very little. This encourages playing along the ground and controlling the ball with the sole of the foot.

A player used to futsal will notice that the seven-a-side ball "gets away" more, bounces more and calls for more striking with the instep. The other way round, someone coming from seven-a-side will discover that in futsal the ball stays glued to the floor and the sole of the foot becomes their best friend.

## Number of players and substitutions

- **Seven-a-side:** seven players per team, including the goalkeeper. In many amateur leagues substitutions are unlimited and "rolling", meaning a player can come back on after being substituted.
- **Futsal:** five players per team, including the goalkeeper. Substitutions are also unlimited and are made without stopping play, from a substitution zone in front of the bench.

Being able to rotate a lot makes both sports very intense: players can give everything for a few minutes knowing that they will be able to rest on the bench.

## Rules worth knowing

### In futsal

- **Effective playing time:** in official competition, two halves of twenty minutes of effective playing time are played, with the clock stopped whenever the ball goes out of play. In many amateur leagues a running clock is used to fit the court booking.
- **Kick-ins:** the ball is placed on the touchline and put back into play with the foot, not the hands.
- **Four seconds:** at kick-ins, corners, goal clearances and free kicks, the player has four seconds to put the ball into play. The goalkeeper also has a four-second limit for controlling the ball in their own half.
- **Accumulated fouls:** each team's fouls are added up in each half. From the sixth accumulated foul, every further foul is punished with a direct kick from further out than the penalty spot, with no wall, known as the "double penalty" (second penalty mark).
- **The goalkeeper plays with their feet**: they often take part in building play from the back, and at times in the match an outfield player acts as a "fly goalkeeper" to attack with a numerical advantage.

### In seven-a-side

The rules are closer to eleven-a-side football, although each federation or league introduces adaptations. It is common for:

- There to be no offside, or for it to apply only in an area near the goal.
- Throw-ins to be taken with the hands, as in eleven-a-side football.
- The goalkeeper to have restrictions on throwing the ball beyond the halfway line.

Before playing in a new league, it is worth reading its rules: variations are common.

## The physical demands

### Seven-a-side

It combines medium and long runs with sprints. Because the pitch is bigger than a futsal court, aerobic endurance carries a lot of weight. Players cover considerable distances, and the ability to repeat efforts with few breaks makes the difference in the second half.

### Futsal

It is a sport of very short, intense efforts: accelerations, sharp stops, changes of direction and constant jumps in a small space. Agility, reaction speed and the ability to recover between explosive efforts are key. Because of the hard surface and constant turning, the ankles, knees and adductors take a lot of load.

## Technique in each format

### In futsal

- **Control with the sole.** Stepping on the ball to stop it, turn it and shield it is the most characteristic move.
- **Short, quick passes**, with the inside of the foot.
- **Constant one-twos**: giving and receiving.
- **Toe-poke shots.** In futsal it is a legitimate and effective resource, because it lets you shoot without any back-lift and surprise the goalkeeper.
- **Shielding the ball with your body**, with your back to goal.

### In seven-a-side

- **Long passes and switches of play**, making use of a wider pitch.
- **Directional first touch** with the inside and outside of the foot.
- **Play down the wings** and crosses into the box.
- **Aerial play**, something practically absent in futsal.

## Basic tactics

In futsal, the most common systems are the **1-2-2** (a defender, two wingers, a pivot and the goalkeeper, with different variations), the **1-3-1** and the **4-0** or rotation game, in which the four outfield players constantly swap positions. Pressing, defensive cover and speed in transitions are fundamental.

In seven-a-side, systems such as the **2-3-1** or **3-2-1** are common, with a defence of two or three players, a midfield that covers a lot of ground and a target striker.

## How to adapt if you switch format

### From seven-a-side to futsal

- Get used to playing with the sole of your foot. Spend time in training controlling, turning and dribbling by stepping on the ball.
- Take some pace off your passes: the spaces are small.
- Work on agility and changes of direction.
- Wear indoor shoes with a flat rubber sole. Studded boots are no use on a court.

### From futsal to seven-a-side

- Work on striking with the instep for long passes and shots.
- Improve your aerobic endurance: the runs are longer.
- Get used to the bounce of the ball and to aerial play.
- Wear boots suited to artificial grass.

## Common injuries and prevention

In both formats, the most common injuries are ankle sprains, hamstring and calf tears, adductor strains and, less often but more seriously, knee injuries. A full warm-up before the match, strength work during the week and footwear suited to the surface reduce the risk. If you only play once a week, take special care: weekly matches with no training are one of the riskiest situations for an amateur footballer.

## Organising a match or joining a league

For a match among friends, the most practical thing is to book the court or pitch in advance at the municipal or private facility, confirm who is coming a couple of days ahead and bring two sets of bibs in different colours and a ball suited to the format. Splitting the hire cost between all the players is the norm. If you want to compete, amateur leagues organise the fixtures, referees and venues in exchange for a team entry fee. Always ask about insurance: in federated competitions, the licence includes compulsory sports insurance; in private leagues, it is worth checking what cover they offer in case of injury.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which of the two is better for getting back into football as an adult?

It depends on what you are looking for. Futsal demands less continuous running endurance, but more agility and changes of pace; seven-a-side is closer to eleven-a-side football and needs more stamina. Either way, it is best to start with friendly matches and do some strength work and running during the week.

### Can I play futsal in running shoes?

It is not a good idea. Running shoes have a high, soft sole designed for moving in a straight line, and on the sharp turns of the court they make ankle sprains more likely. Wear indoor court shoes with a flat rubber sole that grips and does not mark the floor.

### How many matches a week is it reasonable to play?

For most amateurs, one or two, with at least one rest day between them. If you want to play more, build up gradually and add strength work so that your body can tolerate the load.

## In short

Seven-a-side football and futsal are two excellent ways to keep enjoying football as an adult. One is closer to eleven-a-side and demands endurance; the other is more explosive and technical in tight spaces. Knowing their differences helps you choose, adapt and, above all, enjoy every match.
