Starting to run looks simple: a pair of trainers, your front door and the will to do it. Yet most people who try give up in the first three weeks. It is not usually a lack of willpower, but a mistake in the approach: they go out running too fast, for too long and on too many days in a row. The body protests, aches appear in the knees or shins and the experience becomes something remembered with more pain than pleasure.

This article suggests a different path. The idea is to reach thirty minutes of continuous running in eight weeks by alternating easy running and walking, with three sessions a week and rest days in between. It is a rate of progress that respects how muscles, tendons and the heart adapt, and it leaves room so that daily life does not become an obstacle.

## Before your first run

If you have not exercised for a long time, are over forty, have a cardiovascular condition, are significantly overweight or take medication regularly, it is a good idea to talk to your doctor before you start. It is not a formality: a basic check-up rules out problems you should know about and gives you peace of mind.

As for equipment, you need very little:

- **Comfortable running shoes** that do not pinch at the toes and are not worn out. You do not need the most expensive model; you need them to feel comfortable from the very first minute.
- **Breathable clothing** suited to the temperature. Sweat-soaked cotton rubs and weighs you down.
- **A watch or your phone** to time the running and walking intervals.

What you do not need at first is a heart-rate monitor, a special nutrition plan or energy gels. All of that may make sense later, but it is not what makes the difference in the first few weeks.

## The key principle: run slowly

The rule that helps beginners most is this: **run at a pace at which you can talk**. If you can say whole sentences without gasping, you are doing well. If you can only manage two or three words in a row, you are going too fast.

Running slowly may seem unambitious, but there is clear logic to it. At first, what takes longest to adapt is not your lungs but your musculoskeletal system: tendons, ligaments, bones and joints. Your cardiovascular system improves within a few weeks, and that is deceptive: you feel able to go faster before your legs are ready for the impact. Keeping to a comfortable pace reduces the mechanical stress and lets you build up minutes without ending up injured.

It is normal for that easy pace to be only slightly faster than brisk walking at first. That is fine. Speed will come by itself once you have built a base.

## What a session looks like

Every session in the plan has the same structure:

1. **Five minutes of brisk walking** to warm up. Increase the pace little by little.
2. **The main part**, with the running and walking intervals set for that week.
3. **Five minutes of easy walking** to cool down.

Afterwards you can do a few minutes of gentle mobility for your hips, ankles and calves, without forcing anything. There is no need for intense stretching straight after running; what matters is that you finish the session feeling that you could have done a little more.

## The plan, week by week

Do three sessions a week, leaving at least one rest day between them. A comfortable arrangement is Monday, Wednesday and Saturday, or Tuesday, Thursday and Sunday.

| Week | Main part of each session | Time running |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8 repetitions of 1 min running + 2 min walking | 8 min |
| 2 | 6 repetitions of 2 min running + 2 min walking | 12 min |
| 3 | 5 repetitions of 3 min running + 2 min walking | 15 min |
| 4 | 4 repetitions of 5 min running + 2 min walking | 20 min |
| 5 | 3 repetitions of 8 min running + 2 min walking | 24 min |
| 6 | 2 repetitions of 12 min running + 2 min walking | 24 min |
| 7 | 20 min running + 3 min walking + 5 min running | 25 min |
| 8 | 30 min of continuous running | 30 min |

Notice that the total running time rises moderately and that week 6 does not increase the volume compared with week 5: what changes is the length of each block. Those pauses in the progression are deliberate. They give your body time to consolidate what it has gained.

### What if a week is too hard?

Repeat it. There is no problem at all in taking ten or twelve weeks instead of eight. The goal is not to stick to the calendar, but to reach thirty minutes of running feeling good. If you cannot complete the intervals in a session, finish by walking and repeat the same session next time.

In the same way, if you miss a week because of a trip or a cold, go back one or two steps rather than picking up where you left off.

## Where to run

For the first few weeks, look for even surfaces without big slopes: a park with compacted dirt paths, a seafront promenade or an athletics track. Dirt and grass cushion a little more than tarmac, but what really matters is that the ground is predictable so that you do not twist an ankle.

Hills demand much more effort and increase the load on your calves and Achilles tendon. If there is no flat ground near you, walk up the slopes for the first few weeks. Running downhill, although it seems easier, multiplies the impact on your knees; do it slowly and with short steps.

## Technique, without obsessing

You do not need to learn perfect technique to start, but three simple ideas help a lot:

- **Short, quick steps.** It is better to take lots of small steps than a few long strides. When you lengthen your stride, your foot lands well in front of your body and brakes every step.
- **An upright, relaxed body.** Look ahead, not at the ground. Loose shoulders, arms at an angle of roughly ninety degrees and hands unclenched.
- **Breathe naturally.** There is no need to follow a rigid breathing pattern. If you are short of breath, slow down.

In a separate article we explain cadence, footstrike and posture in more detail, but at first it is enough to run relaxed and unhurried.

## Normal aches and the ones that are not

It is normal to feel muscle soreness in your calves or thighs during the first few days, especially after the first sessions. It usually appears 24 to 48 hours after exercise and disappears within a few days. A gentle walk and a good night's sleep help more than any remedy.

Other signs should be taken seriously:

- **Pain that gets worse as you run** instead of easing after the warm-up.
- **Pain in one specific spot** on the shin, foot or knee, especially if it hurts when pressed.
- **Pain that makes you limp** or that persists the next day at rest.

In any of those cases, stop for a few days, replace running with walking or cycling and, if it does not improve, see a health professional. Carrying on running with pain is the quickest way to turn a one-week niggle into a two-month injury.

## Rest, sleep and food

Days without running are not wasted days: that is when your body adapts to the effort. That is why the plan leaves a free day between sessions. If you feel like moving on those days, walk, go for a gentle bike ride or do simple strength exercises for your legs and trunk, which reduce the risk of injury.

As for food, you do not need to change your diet to run for thirty minutes. Avoid going out on a very full stomach: leave one or two hours after a big meal. Drink water throughout the day and, if it is hot, carry a small bottle or plan a route past a fountain.

Sleeping well is probably the best complement for any runner. Lack of sleep increases the feeling of tiredness, slows recovery and makes every session harder than it should be.

## Staying motivated

Some strategies that work:

- **Put the session in your diary** like any other appointment, with a day and time.
- **Get your kit ready the night before.** It seems trivial, but it removes an excuse in the morning.
- **Find company.** Running with someone at your level makes it harder to skip a session and easier to keep to an easy pace, because you can chat as you go.
- **Write down what you do.** Seeing completed sessions in a notebook or an app is a real boost.
- **Notice how you feel**, not just the minutes: sleeping better, having more energy in the afternoon or climbing stairs without getting tired are also progress.

## After the eight weeks

When you complete thirty minutes of continuous running, keep that volume for two or three weeks before setting new goals. From there you can choose between several paths: running five kilometres without stopping, adding a fourth session a week or starting to play with gentle changes of pace. What matters is that each step is small and that you keep enjoying it.

Running is an activity for life. The World Health Organization's recommendations call for 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week for adults, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity. Three thirty-minute sessions of easy running, together with walking every day and a couple of strength sessions, fit perfectly within those guidelines. You need nothing more to notice the benefits.
