The day after a long run, an intense soccer game or a hard gym session, the body usually asks for a couch. The legs are heavy, the pain appears and the idea of ​​moving seems absurd. However, many people find that after a gentle walk or a leisurely bike ride, they feel better than if they had spent the day sitting still.

That practice has a name: **active recovery**. It consists of performing low-intensity physical activity after a demanding effort, with the aim of facilitating recovery. This article explains what it is, what is known about its effects and how to apply it.

## What is active recovery

It is any **low intensity** activity, which does not generate additional fatigue, performed in the recovery period after intense effort. It can be done:

- **Right after exercise**, as a way to cool down: a few minutes of gentle jogging or pedaling after an intense session.
- **In the following days**, as a light session instead of complete rest.

The key is intensity: it must be really gentle. If "recovery" becomes just another workout, it stops serving its purpose.

## What the evidence says

Research on active recovery shows mixed results, but some conclusions are reasonably consistent:

- **The return to active calm** after very intense efforts helps reduce blood lactate faster than rest. This can be useful when efforts have to be repeated in a short time, for example in tournaments with several matches in one day.
- **Feeling of recovery** and well-being generally improve with light activity, although the effect on next-day performance is less clear and varies between studies.
- **On soreness**, the effect of light activity is modest, but many people notice temporary relief while moving.

In short, active recovery does not work miracles, but it usually makes the day after more bearable and maintains the habit of movement without adding fatigue.

## Why it helps to move softly

Some mechanisms that can explain its benefits:

- **Increased blood flow** in the muscles without generating more damage.
- **Joint mobility**, which reduces the feeling of stiffness.
- **Effect on mood**: moving outdoors and without demands improves the feeling of well-being.
- **Maintenance of the routine**, which helps consistency in training.

## What activities to choose

The ideal are low-impact activities that are different from the one that caused the fatigue:

- **Walk**, at a comfortable pace, between 20 and 45 minutes.
- **Gentle bike**, on the flat or on a stationary bike, between 20 and 40 minutes.
- **Quiet swimming** or walking in the water. Water also has a gentle compression and weight-relieving effect.
- **Mobility and gentle stretching**, low-intensity yoga or Pilates.
- **Elliptical** at a comfortable pace.

If the previous effort was a run, cycling or swimming allows you to move without the impact of running. If it was a soccer game, a walk and mobility session works great.

## The right intensity

A simple reference: you should be able to **maintain a fluid conversation** throughout the activity. On an effort scale of 1 to 10, you should not go above 3 or 4. If you use a heart rate monitor, your heart rate should be kept clearly below those of your usual aerobic workouts.

## An example after a game or a race

### the same day

- Cool down for 5 to 10 minutes: jog or easy walk.
- Rehydration and a complete meal with carbohydrates and proteins.
- A shower and, if you feel like it, a few minutes of gentle mobility.

### The next day

- 30 minutes of walking or gentle cycling.
- 10 minutes of mobility: hips, ankles, spine.
- Balanced meals, good hydration and a sufficient night's sleep.

### two days later

- If the sensations are good, return to normal training, starting with a moderate session.

## Other recovery tools

### The dream

It is the most powerful recovery tool. No technique compensates for a short night. You can read more in our article on [sleep and performance](/articulos/el-sueno-y-el-rendimiento/).

### Food and hydration

Replenishing energy, protein and fluids after exertion is essential for muscles to repair.

### The massage

Massage can reduce the feeling of muscle pain and improve well-being. Its effect on performance is less clear, but if it suits you and you have access, it's a nice option.

### The foam roller

The "foam roller" or self-massage roller can temporarily relieve the feeling of stiffness and improve range of motion in the short term. Use it with moderate pressure, without looking for pain.

### The cold water

Immersions in cold water after exercise can reduce the sensation of muscle soreness. However, some studies suggest that, used regularly just after strength training, they could attenuate part of the muscular adaptations. For the amateur athlete, it can be a useful tool at specific times, such as tournaments with back-to-back matches, rather than a daily routine.

### Compression

Compression garments may improve the feeling of recovery for some people. Its effect is modest.

## Plan recovery during the week

Recovery is not something that is improvised the day after an effort: it can be planned. A balanced week for an amateur athlete alternates demanding days with easy days:

| Day | Session type |
|---|---|
| Monday | Active recovery after Sunday's game or shoot |
| Tuesday | Quality training (intervals, intense technique) |
| Wednesday | Force or light session |
| Thursday | Moderate training |
| Friday | Rest or mobility |
| Saturday | Soft session or short activation |
| Sunday | Match, race or long run |

The general rule is simple: after a hard day, an easy one. Chaining several demanding days without recovery is the shortest path to accumulated fatigue and injuries.

## Tournaments and days with several matches

In paddle tennis, 7-a-side soccer or tennis tournaments with several matches on the same day or weekend, recovery between matches is decisive:

1. **Short cool down** at the end of each game: a few minutes of walking or light jogging.
2. **Immediate hydration** with water and, if it is hot or the game has been long, a drink with salts.
3. **A carbohydrate snack** that is easy to digest: fruit, a small sandwich, a bar.
4. **Stay in the shade** and dress warmly if it's cold, so as not to get completely cold.
5. **Reactivate before the next game** with a few minutes of mobility, movements and progressive blows.

At the end of the day, a full dinner, hydration and a good night's sleep. The next day, gentle walking and mobility.

## How to know if you are recovering

There is no need for expensive devices to assess recovery. It is enough to pay attention to a few signs every morning and mentally rate them from 1 to 5:

- **Sleep quality:** if you slept straight and wake up rested.
- **Legs:** if you notice heaviness or soreness that doesn't budge when you move a little.
- **Cheer up and desire to train.**
- **Rest pulse:** If you wear a watch or take it when you wake up, a pulse several beats higher than usual for several days in a row suggests accumulated fatigue.

An isolated bad day means nothing. But if several signs worsen at the same time and for more than a week, it is advisable to lighten the load. We explain this in more detail in the article on [signs of overtraining](/articulos/sobreentrenamiento-senales/).

## If you are over forty years old or just starting out

With age, and also when the body is not yet accustomed to exercise, recovery takes longer. It is common to need two easy days after an effort that was previously assimilated in one. It is not a sign of weakness, but common sense: respecting these days allows you to continue training constantly, which is what really produces improvements, instead of chaining good weeks with breaks due to discomfort.

## Frequent errors

1. **Turning recovery into another workout**: Going too fast or too long.
2. **Thinking that absolute rest is always best**: except for injury or illness, moving gently usually feels better.
3. **Get obsessed with techniques** and neglect the basics: sleep, eat and hydrate.
4. **Not listening to the body**: If after active recovery you feel more tired, you have gone too hard.

## When not to do active recovery

- If you have an injury, follow the instructions of your physical therapist or doctor.
- If you are sick, with fever or general discomfort, rest is indicated.
- If you notice extreme fatigue that does not improve, it may be a sign of overtraining or another cause that should be evaluated.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should it last?

Between 20 and 45 minutes is usually enough. More time does not provide additional benefits and may add fatigue.

### Can I do active recovery every day?

Rest days can include light activity without a problem. The important thing is that there are really light days in the week.

### Is it better to walk or ride a bike?

Both are good options. Choose the one that is most pleasant for you and the one that least burdens the most fatigued area.

## In summary

Active recovery is simple: move gently, with little impact, the same day and the days following intense effort. Combined with sleep, good nutrition and hydration, it helps you feel better, maintains the habit and prepares the body to train with enthusiasm again.
