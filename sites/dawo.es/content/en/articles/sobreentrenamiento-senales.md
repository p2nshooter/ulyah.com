When someone becomes interested in sports, it is easy to fall into a tempting logic: if training feels good to me, training more will feel better. Thus, the three weekly sessions become five, then six, a game, a group class and a long run on Sunday are added. For a few weeks everything goes well. Until it stops going well: the fatigue doesn't go away, the workouts that were easy before become hard, the sleep gets worse and the desire to train disappears.

Training works because the body adapts to the load during recovery. If the load continually exceeds the recovery capacity, instead of improving, performance worsens. This article explains how to recognize that point and what to do.

## Normal fatigue, overload and overtraining

It is important to distinguish several situations:

### normal fatigue

It is the fatigue that appears after a demanding workout and disappears with one or two days of rest or light activity. It's part of the training process.

### functional overload

A period of intense training, lasting one or two weeks, that produces a temporary drop in performance. After a few days of rest, the body recovers and performance improves above the initial level. Some athletes look for it in a planned way before a competition.

### Non-functional overload

When intense loading is maintained for too long, performance declines and takes weeks to recover. Symptoms such as persistent fatigue or sleep problems appear.

### Overtraining syndrome

It is the most serious and least common form: a prolonged decline in performance, which can last for months, accompanied by significant physical and psychological symptoms. In amateur athletes it is rare, but the previous phases are relatively frequent.

## The warning signs

No single symptom is definitive, but the combination of several over days or weeks deserves attention:

### Performance

- Regular workouts feel much harder.
- You can't maintain the rhythms or weights you managed recently.
- Performance does not improve or worsens despite training more.

### Physics

- Persistent fatigue that does not improve with a day of rest.
- Heart rate at rest, in the morning, higher than usual for several days.
- Muscle pain that does not go away.
- More injuries or discomfort than normal.
- Frequent colds or infections.
- Loss of appetite or weight without looking for it.

### Sleep and mood

- Difficulty sleeping despite fatigue.
- Irritability, anxiety or sadness.
- Lack of motivation to train, something you previously enjoyed.
- Difficulty concentrating.

## The usual causes in the amateur athlete

In amateur sport, the problem is rarely just training. It is usually the sum of charges:

- **Sudden increases** in volume or intensity.
- **Lack of easy days**: all sessions are demanding.
- **Little sleep**, due to work, family or screens.
- **Insufficient nutrition**, especially in people who are trying to lose weight while training a lot.
- **Work or personal stress**, which the body adds to the training load.
- **Competitions too close together.**
- **Recent illnesses** without complete recovery.

The body does not distinguish between the stress of a difficult week at work and that of an interval session: both consume recovery capacity.

## The relative energy deficiency

A situation that deserves special mention is the relative energy deficiency in sport, known by its acronym in English, REDs. It occurs when the energy ingested with food is not enough to cover the needs of exercise and the basic functions of the body. It can affect bone, hormonal, immune health and performance. In women, one of its signs is the alteration or disappearance of menstruation. It affects men and women. If you suspect that this may be your case, consult with a doctor and a dietitian-nutritionist.

## A common example

Imagine a person who started running in the spring and in September signs up for their first half marathon, scheduled for November. He maintains his four weekly outings, adds a series session, starts playing paddle tennis two afternoons a week with his co-workers and, as he wants to arrive at the race lighter, he reduces dinner. At the same time, you start a demanding project at work and sleep for six hours.

For two weeks everything seems to be going well. For the third time, the series are very difficult for him, the pace of soft filming slows down, he wakes up several times a night and notices that he gets irritated easily. He has pain in one calf that won't go away and a cold that lasts longer than normal.

None of the changes alone were excessive. The sum of it all, more training, less food, less sleep and more stress, has exceeded your ability to recover. The solution is to reduce the total load: put the padel on hold for a few weeks or turn it into gentle training, eat according to what you train, recover hours of sleep and adjust the goal if necessary.

## What to do if you notice the signs

### 1. Reduce the load

Reduce the volume and, above all, the intensity for one or two weeks. Replace hard sessions with light activity.

### 2. Prioritize sleep

Try to sleep more hours and with better quality. It is the most powerful recovery tool.

### 3. Check the diet

Make sure you eat enough, with carbohydrates and proteins appropriate to your activity.

### 4. Manage stress

If you are going through a time of great stress, adjust your training to that reality. Training less for a few weeks does not mean losing shape.

### 5. Consult with a professional

If symptoms do not improve after two to three weeks of reducing the load, or if they are severe, consult a doctor. Some diseases, such as anemia, thyroid problems or infections, can cause similar symptoms and should be ruled out.

## How to prevent it

1. **Plan the week** alternating demanding and easy days.
2. **Includes download weeks** every three to five weeks.
3. **Increase the load gradually.**
4. **Get enough sleep** on a regular basis.
5. **Eat according to your activity.**
6. **Listen to early signs** instead of ignoring them.
7. **Consider your life outside of sport**: in the most stressful weeks, train less.

## Simple tools to monitor fatigue

You don't need advanced technology. Some practical tools:

- **A training diary** in which you write down what you do, how you feel and how you slept.
- **The Perceived Exertion Scale**: Rate each session from 1 to 10. If sessions that should be a 4 feel like a 7, something is up.
- **The heart rate at rest**, measured when you wake up, before getting up. A sustained increase of several heartbeats compared to your usual value may indicate fatigue or the beginning of an illness.
- **Simple questions every morning**: How did I sleep? Do I feel like training? Does something hurt?

If you use a sports watch that measures heart rate variability, it can give you useful information about trends, although it should be interpreted with caution and always in conjunction with your feelings.

## What is a week of unloading like?

A deload week is not a week without doing anything, but rather a week with significantly less work so that the body can assimilate what has been trained. As guidance:

| If you do... | During discharge week |
|---|---|
| Career | reduce mileage by half or two-thirds, keep some changes of pace short and eliminate long sets |
| Gym | do half the series, with slightly lighter weights and far from failure |
| Team or racket sports | train with less intensity or only play friendly matches |

The usual thing is to end the week with more desire to train and better feelings, which is exactly what you are looking for.

## Streaks, Apps, and Group

Apps that reward consecutive days of activity, monthly challenges, and training groups are a great source of motivation, but they can also push you to train when your body asks for rest. If you find it difficult to skip a day to avoid breaking your streak or falling behind the group, remember that the goal is to improve over the years, not to complete a number on the screen. A day of rest on time is worth more than a whole week out due to an injury.

## Return to training

After a period of accumulated fatigue, it returns progressively:

- Start with short, gentle sessions.
- Increase volume before intensity.
- Reintroduce challenging sessions one at a time, checking how you respond.
- Maintain the sleeping and eating habits that helped you recover.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much rest do I need if I am overloaded?

It depends on the severity. In mild cases, one or two weeks of reduced loading is usually sufficient. In more serious cases, more time and professional supervision may be needed.

### Can I overtrain by doing sports only three days a week?

It is unlikely if the rest of your life is in balance, but it can happen if those three days are very intense and combined with little sleep, a lot of stress or insufficient nutrition.

### Is lack of motivation always overtraining?

No. It can be due to many causes, such as the monotony of training or personal problems. But if it is accompanied by fatigue and worse performance, it should be taken into account.

## In summary

Progress in sport is born from the balance between load and recovery. When fatigue does not disappear, performance drops, sleep worsens and desire fades, the body is asking for a break. Listening to it in time, reducing the load and taking care of sleep and nutrition prevents a bad streak from turning into lost months.
