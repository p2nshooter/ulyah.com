Choosing your first padel racket, or replacing the one you already have, can be more complicated than it seems. Catalogues are full of technical terms such as balance, EVA core, carbon fibre or roughness, and prices vary enormously. Many players end up buying the racket their favourite professional uses, which is usually precisely the least suitable one for someone starting out.

The ideal racket is the one that suits your level, your playing style and your build. This article explains in practical terms what each feature means and how to combine them to get it right.

## The regulation measurements

Padel rackets have measurements limited by the rules: a maximum length of 45.5 centimetres, a maximum width of 26 centimetres and a maximum thickness of 38 millimetres. They must also have holes in the hitting area and a safety cord that the player must wear around their wrist.

Within those limits, manufacturers play with the shape, weight, materials and distribution of mass.

## Shape: round, teardrop or diamond

### Round racket

The head of the racket is circular and the sweet spot — the area where shots come off best — is in the centre and large.

- **Advantages:** lots of control, forgiving of off-centre hits, easier to handle.
- **Drawbacks:** less power on smashes.
- **For whom:** beginners and players who put control and defence first.

### Teardrop racket

It has a shape halfway between round and diamond, and the sweet spot is a little higher.

- **Advantages:** a balance between control and power.
- **Drawbacks:** somewhat less forgiving than a round racket.
- **For whom:** intermediate players who want a versatile racket.

### Diamond racket

The head is wider at the top and the sweet spot is high.

- **Advantages:** maximum power on smashes and attacking shots.
- **Drawbacks:** less control, less manoeuvrable and more demanding on technique. Its vibrations can overload the elbow if the stroke is not correct.
- **For whom:** advanced players with good technique and an attacking game.

## Balance

Balance tells you where the racket's weight is concentrated:

- **Low balance**, with the weight near the handle: the racket feels light and is quick to handle. It favours control and defence.
- **Medium balance:** a balance between manoeuvrability and power.
- **High balance**, with the weight in the head: more momentum in the shot and more power, but more effort for the arm and less speed at the net.

Round rackets usually have a low balance; diamond ones, a high balance. But that is not always the case, so it is worth checking this figure separately.

## Weight

Most rackets weigh between 340 and 385 grams. A lighter racket is easier to handle and tires the arm less; a heavier one gives stability and power.

- **Beginners, players with little arm strength or a history of aches:** light rackets, roughly between 345 and 365 grams.
- **Players with more technique and strength:** can use somewhat heavier rackets.

A common mistake is choosing a heavy racket in the belief that it will give more power, when in practice it makes the stroke late and increases the risk of overload.

## The core: soft or hard

The inside of the racket is usually made of EVA rubber, foam or combinations of the two.

- **EVA**, denser and harder: returns a lot of power when the shot is hard, but on slow shots the ball comes off with less speed. It demands more strength and technique.
- **Foam**, softer: the ball comes off easily even on gentle shots, it absorbs vibration better and it is more comfortable for the arm, although it loses some control on very powerful shots.

To start with, a soft or medium-hardness core is best. It makes the game easier and protects the elbow.

## The faces: glass fibre or carbon fibre

The hitting surface is usually made of glass fibre, carbon fibre or combinations of the two.

- **Glass fibre:** more flexible, helps the ball come off the racket and is more comfortable. It is usually cheaper.
- **Carbon fibre:** stiffer, adds precision and power, but transmits more vibration. It is usually more expensive and more durable.

For a beginner, glass fibre is an excellent option. Carbon makes sense once you have mastered the technique.

## Roughness

Many rackets have a rough or textured surface, which helps to put spin on the ball in shots such as the bandeja and the víbora. It is a useful detail from intermediate level upwards, but it should not be the main criterion when choosing your first racket.

## A table to guide you

| Level and style | Shape | Balance | Core | Approximate weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Beginner | Round | Low | Soft | 345–365 g |
| Intermediate, control | Round or teardrop | Low or medium | Medium | 355–370 g |
| Intermediate, versatile | Teardrop | Medium | Medium | 355–375 g |
| Advanced, attacking | Diamond | High | Hard | 360–380 g |

This is general guidance. The best thing is to try several rackets before deciding.

## Try before you buy

Many clubs and specialist shops have demo rackets. It is worth taking advantage of them:

- **Play for at least a while with each racket**; do not just hold it in the shop.
- **Try groundstrokes, volleys and overhead shots.**
- **Notice how your arm feels** at the end. A racket that leaves your elbow aching is not the right one.

## Looking after your elbow

Epicondylitis, or "tennis elbow", is a common injury in padel, and your choice of racket plays a part. Very hard, heavy or head-heavy rackets transmit more vibration and demand more from the forearm. If you have or have had elbow pain, prioritise light rackets with a soft core and low balance, and also review your technique with a coach.

## The grip and maintenance

The racket handle usually has a covering called a *grip*, which can be replaced when it wears out. A grip in good condition stops the racket slipping and saves you having to squeeze too hard, which also tires the forearm. Some players add an overgrip to adjust the thickness of the handle to their hand.

To make your racket last longer:

- Do not leave it in the car in the sun: heat affects the materials.
- Use a protective cover.
- Avoid hitting it against the ground or the walls.

## How much to spend

You do not need to buy the most expensive racket to enjoy padel. Entry-level and mid-range rackets, with glass fibre and a soft core, are perfectly suitable for most amateur players. High-end rackets use materials and technologies that make sense when your level of play lets you take advantage of them.

## In short

If you are starting out, look for a round, light racket with a low balance, a soft core and glass-fibre faces. As you improve, you can move on to more versatile or attacking models. And remember that no racket replaces technique: good positioning and a correct stroke do more for your game than any technology.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should you change your racket?

There is no fixed rule. It depends on how many hours you play, the quality of the racket and your style. With two or three matches a week, many rackets start to lose their response after a year or eighteen months: the core softens, the ball comes off less consistently and small cracks appear in the frame or the faces. If you notice that the racket has lost its "life" or has visible damage to the frame, it is time to change it. A damaged racket not only plays worse, it also transmits more vibration to your arm.

### Is it worth buying a second-hand racket?

It can be a good option for starting out, as long as you check it carefully. Make sure the frame has no cracks, especially around the throat and the bridge that joins the head to the handle, and that the faces have no dents. Ask how long it has been used. A mid-range racket in good condition can be a better buy than a new, very basic one.

### Are there rackets specifically for women or young players?

Some brands sell lighter models aimed at women or young players. In reality, what matters is not sex or age but each person's weight, balance and strength. A woman with a lot of technique and strength may prefer a 370-gram racket, and a man who is starting out may be much more comfortable with a 350-gram one. Choose by features, not by labels.

### What if I am over fifty or coming back from an injury?

In that case it is best to prioritise comfort: a light racket with a low balance and a soft core that absorbs vibration. Combine your choice with a careful warm-up and, if you have had shoulder or elbow trouble, with strengthening exercises recommended by a physiotherapist.

### Is the racket everything?

No. A good racket helps, but it does not correct poor technique or replace positioning on the court. Many players improve more with a few lessons than with a new racket. Think of your racket as a tool that should go with your game, not as a short cut to improving it.
