For many people, badminton is that beach or garden game in which you hit a shuttlecock with no net and no rules. But competitive badminton is something else: it is one of the fastest racket sports in the world, demanding reflexes, quick feet, stamina and great precision. In professional players' smashes, the shuttle can leave the racket at speeds far beyond those of any tennis ball.

Spain also has a special relationship with the sport thanks to players who have reached the very top internationally, and in many cities there are clubs and sports centres where you can play at any age. It is a cheap sport to start, it is played indoors all year round and it is fun from the first day. This article explains the basics for taking your first steps.

## The court and the equipment

Badminton is played on a rectangular court divided by a net. For doubles, the court is 13.40 metres long by 6.10 wide; for singles, a slightly narrower width is used, marked by the inner sidelines. The net is 1.55 metres high at the posts and slightly lower in the middle.

The equipment is simple:

- **Racket:** light, usually between 80 and 100 grams. To start, a beginner's racket is enough.
- **Shuttlecock:** it can be feathered, more accurate and the norm in competition, or plastic, more durable and cheaper for training and for beginners.
- **Indoor court shoes:** with rubber soles that grip on wooden or synthetic floors. Running shoes are no good: their high, soft soles increase the risk of twisting an ankle in sideways movements.

## The basic rules

### Scoring

- Matches are the best of three games.
- Each game is won by reaching 21 points.
- A point is scored on every rally, whoever serves.
- At 20 all, you must win by two clear points, up to a limit of 30 points.

### The serve

- You serve diagonally into the opposite service court.
- The shuttle must be struck below the waist; the current rules set a maximum contact height from the floor.
- Both of the server's feet must stay in contact with the floor until the shuttle is struck.
- When the server's score is even, they serve from the right; when it is odd, from the left.

### During play

- The shuttle must not touch the floor: the rally ends as soon as it touches the court.
- Each player or pair may hit the shuttle only once before sending it to the other side.
- You may not touch the net with your racket or your body.

## The fundamental shots

### The clear

A high, deep shot that sends the shuttle to the back of your opponent's court. It buys time, lets you recover your position and forces your opponent back. It is played overhead, with the arm extended.

### The drop

A soft shot that lets the shuttle fall just over the net into your opponent's court. Combined with the clear, it forces your opponent to move forwards and back.

### The smash

The attacking shot par excellence: powerful and downwards, taking advantage of a high shuttle. It is the fastest shot in badminton.

### The drive

A fast, flat shot at waist or chest height that crosses the net on a tight line. It is very common in doubles.

### Net play

Soft, precise shots close to the net that leave the shuttle dropping right against it on the other side.

## The grip and the stance

The most common grip for beginners is the "handshake" grip: you hold the racket as if shaking someone's hand, without squeezing too hard. A relaxed grip lets you move your wrist and fingers quickly, which is essential in such a fast sport.

The ready position has the knees slightly bent, the weight on the balls of the feet and the racket in front of the body. From the centre of the court, a player should be able to reach any corner in a few steps and return to the centre after each shot.

## Footwork

In badminton, footwork is as important as arm technique. You move with side steps, cross steps and long lunges forwards to reach shuttles near the net. After each shot, you go back to the central position.

Learning to move well reduces effort and the risk of injury. Many clubs spend a large part of training on footwork without a shuttle.

## A one-hour beginners' session

| Block | Time | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Warm-up | 10 min | Ankle, hip and shoulder mobility, side steps and gentle lunges |
| Footwork without a shuttle | 10 min | From the centre, move to the four corners and back, at a controlled pace |
| Clear rally | 10 min | Clear against clear from back to back, aiming for height and depth |
| Drop and net | 10 min | One player drops the shuttle near the net, the other returns it with a net shot |
| Match | 15 min | Doubles to 21 points, applying what you have practised |
| Cool-down | 5 min | Walking, breathing and gentle stretching |

With two sessions like this a week, within a few weeks you will notice progress in your control of the shuttle and the ease of your movement.

## Doubles: where to stand

In doubles, the pair's positions change depending on who has the initiative. When you are attacking, the usual formation is **front and back:** one player stays close to the net to cut off short returns while the other smashes from the back. When you are defending, the formation switches to **side by side,** each covering half the court from front to back to deal with smashes. At first a simple rule is enough: talk to each other, call "mine" or "yours" on doubtful shuttles and always go back to your zone after hitting.

## Looking after your equipment

Keep feathered shuttles in their tube, base down, and away from heat, because dry feathers break sooner. Keep the racket in its cover, and do not leave it in the car in the sun. For beginners, a moderate string tension is more comfortable and more forgiving of off-centre hits; high tensions are for players with a lot of technique. If the strings break or lose tension, any specialist shop can restring the racket.

## The benefits of badminton

- **Aerobic, intermittent exercise:** rallies are short and intense, with brief breaks, which improves stamina and the ability to recover.
- **Agility and coordination:** the changes of direction and quick shots improve reflexes and hand-eye coordination.
- **Accessible:** the shuttle is slow at the start of its flight, so beginners can keep rallies going from the first day.
- **Social:** doubles matches, including mixed doubles, are very popular in clubs.

## Preventing injuries

Changes of direction, deep lunges and overhead shots make certain injuries more common in badminton: ankle sprains, calf strains, Achilles tendon problems, and knee and shoulder pain.

A few simple measures reduce the risk:

- **Always warm up** for ten minutes: mobility, gentle movement, lunges and progressive shots.
- **Wear suitable indoor shoes.**
- **Strengthen your legs, ankles and shoulders** with strength exercises twice a week.
- **Increase your playing time gradually,** especially if you are coming back after a period of inactivity.
- **Drink enough:** sports halls can be hot and matches intense.

If you feel a sudden pain at the back of the ankle, as if someone had kicked you, stop playing and see a doctor: it may be an Achilles tendon injury.

## How to start

1. **Find a club or sports centre** with badminton sessions. Many offer beginners' classes for adults.
2. **Start with plastic shuttles,** which last longer.
3. **Play doubles at first:** there is less court to cover and it is easier to keep the rally going.
4. **Learn the clear and the serve first;** with those you can already play matches.
5. **Be patient with overhead shots:** they take coordination and time.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to be fit to start?

No. Badminton can be adapted to any fitness level, especially in doubles and friendly games. As you play, your fitness will improve.

### Is it a sport for all ages?

Yes. There are players from childhood to old age. Older people or those with joint problems can play at a calmer pace, always after a good warm-up.

### Feathered or plastic shuttles?

For beginners and training, plastic ones are more practical and cheaper. Feathered shuttles fly more accurately and are used in competition, but they wear out quickly.

## In short

Badminton combines speed, technique and strategy in a sport that is accessible, cheap and great fun. With suitable shoes, a simple racket and a few lessons, anyone can discover why this garden game becomes, on court, one of the most intense sports in the world.
