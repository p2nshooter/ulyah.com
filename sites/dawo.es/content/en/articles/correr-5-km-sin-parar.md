Five kilometres is the most popular distance among amateur runners, and not by chance. It is a goal almost any healthy person can reach in a few months, it can be completed in a reasonable time and it opens the door to fun runs, which in Spain take place practically every weekend. For many people, crossing the finish line of their first 5K is the moment they stop "going for a run" and start thinking of themselves as runners.

This article is for anyone who can already run for about twenty or thirty minutes without stopping at an easy pace, for example after completing a plan to [start running from scratch](/articulos/empezar-a-correr-desde-cero/). If you are not at that point yet, it is best to start there: trying to run five kilometres without that base is the usual recipe for ending up with sore knees or Achilles tendon trouble.

## How long it takes to run five kilometres

The time depends on your pace. At a very easy pace of about 8 minutes per kilometre, five kilometres take about 40 minutes. At 7 minutes per kilometre it is 35 minutes, and at 6 minutes, 30 minutes. Many beginners find that, once they can run for thirty minutes without stopping, they are closer to five kilometres than they thought.

That is why your first goal should not be a particular time, but to complete the distance without stopping and feeling good. The personal best will come later.

## Knowing your pace

To plan, it helps to know your approximate pace. The simplest way is to use the GPS on your phone or a watch during an easy thirty-minute run. If you do not want to depend on technology, you can use an athletics track: the inside lane measures 400 metres, so twelve and a half laps make five kilometres.

Look at the average pace of a comfortable run. That will be your **easy pace**, the one you will use in most sessions. During this plan there will also be stretches at a **slightly brisker pace**: about 20 or 30 seconds per kilometre faster than easy. It should be an effort you can keep up for several minutes, but one at which you can no longer hold a comfortable conversation.

## The logic of intervals

An interval is a stretch of running at a set pace followed by a recovery. In this phase, intervals do two things:

- **They get your body used to slightly faster paces** without building up as much fatigue as if you kept them up the whole time.
- **They break the distance into manageable chunks**, which helps a lot mentally.

This is not about doing exhausting repetitions. For a runner preparing for their first five kilometres, one session of moderate intervals a week is enough. The rest of the training should be easy running.

## The six-week plan

The plan has three sessions a week. One is the interval session, another is an easy run of medium length and the third is the weekend long run, which gets gradually longer. Each session starts with a ten-minute warm-up (five minutes of brisk walking and five of very easy jogging) and ends with a five-minute cool-down.

| Week | Session 1: intervals | Session 2: easy | Session 3: long run |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 × 2 min at a brisk pace, 2 min jog | 25 min | 30 min |
| 2 | 5 × 3 min at a brisk pace, 2 min jog | 25 min | 33 min |
| 3 | 4 × 4 min at a brisk pace, 2 min jog | 30 min | 36 min |
| 4 | 6 × 2 min at a brisk pace, 90 s jog | 25 min | 30 min (recovery week) |
| 5 | 3 × 6 min at a brisk pace, 2 min jog | 30 min | 40 min |
| 6 | 4 × 3 min at a brisk pace, 2 min jog | 20 min easy | 5 km (time trial or race) |

Week 4 lowers the load a little. That lighter week lets your body absorb the work of the previous weeks and arrive fresh for the final part of the plan. Week 6 also reduces the volume so that you arrive rested for your goal.

If you run for forty minutes in the long run of week 5, you are very probably already covering five kilometres or getting very close. Many runners discover this then and realise that they have reached their goal even before the final test.

## How to do the interval session properly

A few guidelines so that this session helps you rather than punishes you:

1. **Always warm up.** Starting straight off with a fast stretch is an invitation to muscle injuries.
2. **The first interval should feel easy.** If the first one is already hard, the last one will be torture. Adjust your pace so that you finish every stretch at the same speed.
3. **Recover by jogging, not standing still.** Keeping on the move helps to get rid of that heavy-legged feeling and prepares you for the next stretch.
4. **Find flat ground.** A park with a marked circuit or a track is ideal.
5. **Finish feeling that you could have done one more.** That is the right intensity at this stage.

## The long run, the backbone of the plan

The weekend long run is the session most like your goal. It should be done at an easy pace, a conversational one. It is tempting to run it fast to "check" how you are doing, but that adds fatigue with no extra benefit. What builds endurance is time on your feet, not speed.

Choose pleasant routes for these runs: a large park, a riverside path or a seafront promenade. Running somewhere beautiful makes the minutes go by faster.

## What to do on non-running days

The days in between are for rest or gentle activity. Walking, an easy bike ride or swimming are good options. It is also a good time to spend twenty minutes on strength exercises: squats, lunges, glute bridges, calf raises and planks. Stronger legs and a stronger trunk absorb impact better and reduce the risk of typical running injuries.

Get enough sleep. Most of your adaptation to training happens while you rest, and a week of poor sleep shows in your legs far more than any mistake in the plan.

## If a week does not go to plan

A cold, a hectic week at work or a few days away can disrupt the plan, and that is fine. If you miss a single session, carry on with the next one without trying to make it up: squeezing two sessions into two consecutive days adds fatigue with no benefit. If you miss a whole week, repeat the last week you completed before moving on. And if you have been ill for several days, especially with a fever, go back one or two weeks in the plan and return with easy sessions. Six weeks is a guide, not an obligation: reaching five kilometres in eight weeks without any aches is much better than reaching it in six with pain.

## Can you do the plan on a treadmill?

Yes. The treadmill is a good option on rainy or very hot days, and it makes it easier to control the pace of the intervals. Set an incline of 1 % to make up for the lack of wind resistance and land softly, without holding on to the rails. Do try, though, to do at least some sessions outdoors before the race: running in the street means setting your pace yourself.

## Race day

If you decide to complete your five kilometres in a fun run, these tips will help you enjoy it:

- **Do not wear anything new.** Not shoes, not a T-shirt, not socks. Use what you have already tried in training.
- **Have your usual breakfast**, two or three hours before, something light and familiar: bread with olive oil, toast with jam, a banana or a yoghurt.
- **Arrive in good time.** Collecting your race number, going to the toilet and warming up calmly reduce your nerves.
- **Start near the back.** At the start everyone sets off too fast. If you place yourself towards the back, you will avoid getting caught up in that pace.
- **Divide the race into three parts.** The first two kilometres, very controlled; the third and fourth, holding steady; the last, pushing only if you feel good.

When you cross the finish line, walk for a few minutes before you stop, drink some water and take a moment to enjoy it. Five kilometres is a real achievement.

## Common mistakes

Some mistakes are repeated by almost every runner preparing for their first 5K:

- **Running fast every day.** If every session is demanding, none of them goes well and fatigue builds up.
- **Increasing the volume suddenly.** Going from three to five runs a week from one week to the next multiplies the risk of injury.
- **Ignoring small aches.** An ache that does not go away in two or three days deserves a break and, if it persists, a visit to a professional.
- **Comparing yourself with others.** Everyone starts from a different point. Your only useful reference is yourself a month ago.

## And then what?

Completing five kilometres opens up several paths. You can try to improve your time over the same distance, which means gradually adding a bit more pace work. You can also aim for the next big goal: [moving up from 5K to 10K](/articulos/del-5k-al-10k/). Either way, keep up the base of three sessions a week for a few weeks before changing your goal. Consolidating what you have gained is as important as moving forward.

Running five kilometres without stopping is above all a matter of patience. With a sensible plan, a comfortable pace and enough rest, the distance stops being a wall and becomes your usual Sunday run.
