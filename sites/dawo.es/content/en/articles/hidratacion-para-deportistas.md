Drinking water seems the simplest thing in the world. Yet hydration in sport has generated as many contradictory messages as food: that you must drink before you are thirsty, that you must drink two litres a day, that isotonic drinks are essential, that water alone does not hydrate you properly. The reality, for the amateur athlete, is simpler and more flexible.

This article explains why hydration matters, how much to drink depending on the type of activity, when you need mineral salts and which signs show that something is not right.

## Why hydration matters

During exercise, the body produces heat and gets rid of it mainly through sweat. Sweat carries away water and mineral salts, above all sodium. If those losses are not replaced and they build up, blood volume falls, the heart has to work harder and the ability to regulate temperature gets worse.

It has been found that dehydration of around two per cent of body weight can start to affect performance in endurance exercise, especially in the heat, as well as concentration. Greater losses increase the risk of heat-related problems.

## How much you sweat

The amount of sweat varies enormously between people and situations. It depends on:

- **Temperature and humidity.**
- **The intensity and duration of the exercise.**
- **Clothing.**
- **Body size and fitness.**
- **Acclimatisation to heat.**
- **Individual factors:** some people sweat far more than others.

That is why there is no universal amount of water. What works for one person may be too little or too much for another.

### How to estimate your sweat rate

A simple way of finding out your losses is to weigh yourself before and after an hour's training, without wet clothes and after going to the toilet:

1. Weigh yourself before exercise.
2. Train for an hour, noting down what you drink.
3. Weigh yourself afterwards.
4. The difference in weight, plus what you drank, is roughly how much you sweated.

For example, if you lose half a kilo and drank half a litre, you sweated about a litre in that hour. Repeating the test in different conditions, hot or cold, gives you a very useful reference.

## Before exercise

The most important thing is to start well hydrated. Some guidelines:

- Drink normally throughout the day before and the day of training.
- About two hours before a long or intense session, drink one or two glasses of water.
- Check the colour of your urine: pale yellow usually means you are well hydrated; dark yellow, that you should drink more. Some vitamin supplements colour urine, so it is not always an exact guide.

## During exercise

### Sessions of under an hour

For most people, in normal conditions, drinking according to thirst is enough. On hot days, it is a good idea to keep water to hand.

### Sessions of more than an hour

In prolonged efforts, especially in the heat:

- Drink small amounts regularly, guided by thirst and by what you know of your sweat rate.
- The aim is not to replace everything you lose, but to avoid significant dehydration.
- If you sweat a lot or the effort lasts more than one or two hours, a drink with sodium helps.

## Mineral salts

Sweat mainly contains sodium, and smaller amounts of potassium, magnesium and other minerals. The concentration of sodium in sweat also varies between people: some finish with white salt marks on their clothes.

Replacing sodium is important in:

- Efforts lasting more than one or two hours.
- Very hot days with heavy sweating.
- People who sweat a lot or have very salty sweat.

In those cases, a sports drink, salt tablets or simply salty foods, such as a sandwich with some cured meat, olives or salted nuts, help to replace sodium.

## Sports drinks

Sports drinks, also called isotonic drinks, contain water, carbohydrates and sodium. They are designed to:

- Replace fluids.
- Provide energy during long efforts.
- Replace sodium.

They are useful in prolonged or very intense exercise, especially in the heat. For a session of under an hour at moderate intensity, water is usually enough. Away from exercise, these drinks provide unnecessary sugar and are not a good choice for everyday use.

Energy drinks, with caffeine and other stimulants, are not sports drinks and are not designed for hydration.

## After exercise

After training, rehydrate steadily over the following hours. If you have lost a lot of weight through sweat, a common guideline is to drink a little more than you have lost, gradually, because part of the fluid is lost in urine. Meals with a little salt help you retain fluid.

Some options for rehydrating:

- Water with a meal.
- Milk, which provides water, protein, carbohydrates and electrolytes.
- Broths or soups, especially in winter.
- Fruit with a lot of water, such as watermelon or melon.

## Too much water: hyponatraemia

Although it is rare in amateur sport, drinking far more water than you lose during very long efforts, such as a slow marathon, can dilute the sodium in your blood and cause hyponatraemia. Symptoms include nausea, headache, swelling, confusion and, in severe cases, seizures. It is a medical emergency.

That is why the modern advice is not "drink as much as you can", but to drink according to thirst and to your known needs.

## Signs of dehydration

- Intense thirst.
- Dry mouth.
- Dark, scanty urine.
- Tiredness, dizziness or headache.
- Muscle cramps.

If there are symptoms of heatstroke, such as confusion, very hot skin or loss of consciousness, stop exercising, cool the body down and call 112.

## Special situations

- **Older people:** the sense of thirst declines with age, so it is a good idea to drink in a more planned way.
- **Children:** they become dehydrated easily and do not always ask for water; offer it to them regularly during exercise.
- **People with heart or kidney conditions:** they should follow their doctor's advice on the amount of fluid.

## Hydration in different sports

- **Padel and tennis in summer:** outdoor matches in the heat of the day are very demanding. Take a large bottle, use the changes of ends to drink and, if the match goes on, alternate water with a drink containing salts.
- **Football:** half-time is the key moment to drink. On hot days, some competitions introduce drinks breaks. In amateur football, always keep water on the bench.
- **Cycling:** you sweat a lot on the bike, but the breeze makes you notice it less. Take at least one bottle per hour of riding in the heat and plan fountains on long routes.
- **Hiking and mountains:** long routes, often without reliable water sources, mean working out carefully how much water to carry. At altitude, dry air increases the losses through breathing.
- **Swimming:** even though you are in the water, you sweat too. Keep a bottle at the side of the pool in long sessions.

## A home-made drink with salts

If you want a simple alternative to commercial sports drinks for long efforts, you can make one at home. A rough recipe for one litre:

- One litre of water.
- Three or four tablespoons of sugar, or a glass of fruit juice in place of some of the water.
- A quarter of a teaspoon of salt.
- The juice of half a lemon for flavour.

These proportions come close to the composition of many sports drinks: they provide energy and sodium in moderate amounts. Try it in training before using it in a competition, and remember that for short efforts water is enough.

## A practical example: two hours on the bike in summer

To put all these guidelines into practice, this is how someone going for a two-hour ride on a hot morning might organise their hydration:

1. **Before:** drink normally from the evening before, and one or two glasses of water a couple of hours before setting off.
2. **During:** take two bottles, one of water and one with a drink containing salts, and take sips every fifteen or twenty minutes. Many guides suggest between 400 and 800 millilitres per hour, adjusted to your sweat rate and thirst.
3. **If the route gets longer:** plan a fountain or a stop to refill, and add something salty to your snacks.
4. **After:** weigh yourself if you want to know your losses, and rehydrate over the following hours, drinking water with a meal.

## How to carry water

For short outings, a bottle is enough. On long runs, belts with small bottles or hydration vests let you drink without carrying anything in your hands, and in the mountains or on the bike, backpacks with a reservoir are very convenient. Wash bottles and reservoirs well after each use, especially if they have held sugary drinks, and leave them open to dry to avoid bad smells and mould.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do you need to drink two litres of water a day?

There is no universal amount. Needs depend on size, activity, climate and diet, since many foods contain water. Drinking according to thirst and checking the colour of your urine is a reasonable guide.

### Does coffee dehydrate you?

Coffee drunk in moderation contributes to hydration. Its diuretic effect is slight in people who are used to it.

### Should you drink before you are thirsty?

In short efforts, thirst is a good guide. In long efforts in the heat, it is a good idea to drink regularly without waiting until you are very thirsty, but without forcing it.

## In short

Start well hydrated, drink according to thirst during exercise, add salts and energy in long efforts or great heat, and rehydrate steadily afterwards. Knowing your own sweat rate through a simple weighing test lets you adjust these guidelines to your case.
