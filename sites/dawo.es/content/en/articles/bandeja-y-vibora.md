There comes a moment in every padel player's development when they stop smashing every high ball as if it were the last. It is when they discover that padel is not won by brute force, but by holding the net and forcing the opponents into mistakes. That is when two shots appear that mark the difference between a beginner and an intermediate player: the **bandeja** and the **víbora**.

Both are overhead shots played with spin, and both are used to answer lobs without losing the net. But they have different aims and different techniques. In this article we explain what they are, how to play them and when to use each one.

## Why not smash everything

In padel, opponents at the back try to drive the pair away from the net with lobs. If you answer every lob with a powerful smash, two things happen:

- Many badly executed smashes end up in the net or out.
- The smashes that do go in, unless they are powerful enough to leave the court, bounce off the back glass and give the opponent a comfortable ball to counter-attack.

That is why experienced players save the smash for balls they can turn into a winning point and use the bandeja or the víbora for the rest. That way they hold their position at the net, which is where the advantage lies in padel.

## The bandeja

The bandeja is an overhead shot with slice, played with a shorter and more controlled movement than the smash. Its name — Spanish for "tray" — comes from the position of the racket, which is held during the preparation as if you were carrying a tray.

### The aim

The bandeja does not try to win the point. It tries to **return the lob safely, without the ball bouncing high off the opponent's glass,** so that you stay at the net. A good bandeja keeps the ball low, near the back wall or the corner, forcing the opponent to defend from an awkward position.

### Step by step

1. **Turn side-on.** As soon as you see the opponent playing a lob, turn your body sideways, with the shoulder opposite your racket pointing towards the net.
2. **Step back.** Move back with side steps or cross steps, without turning your back on the ball. The aim is to hit it slightly in front of your body.
3. **Preparation.** Take the racket back with the elbow high and the face of the racket pointing upwards, like a tray.
4. **Contact.** Hit the ball slightly above your head and a little in front, with a movement from high to low and from outside to inside that gives it slice.
5. **Follow-through.** The racket finishes in front of the body, towards the opposite side. The movement should be fluid, not a whip.
6. **Recovery.** Straight afterwards, move back towards the net to regain your position.

### Where to aim it

The most common targets are the back wall near the corner, or the middle of the court between the two opponents, to create doubt. What matters is that the ball arrives low and does not leave an easy bounce.

## The víbora

The víbora — "viper" — is a more aggressive development of the bandeja. It is hit with stronger side spin and slice, often a little more to the side of the body, and with more speed.

### The aim

The víbora tries to **put the opponent in trouble.** Thanks to the spin, after bouncing and hitting the side or back glass the ball comes out very low and close to the wall, which makes it very hard to return. It does not always win the point outright, but it usually forces a weak reply.

### How it differs from the bandeja

| Aspect | Bandeja | Víbora |
|---|---|---|
| Intention | Hold the net safely | Apply pressure and force a mistake |
| Speed | Moderate | Higher |
| Spin | Slice | Slice and side spin, stronger |
| Contact point | In front and slightly above the head | Somewhat more to the side and often lower |
| Risk | Low | Medium |

### Execution

The preparation is very similar to the bandeja's, but at contact the arm moves faster and the racket "brushes" the ball from the side, with a more diagonal movement. The body goes with the shot with a more pronounced turn. It is a demanding shot for the shoulder and elbow, so it is best to master the bandeja first.

## When to use each one

A simple way to decide:

- **A lob that forces you a long way back or catches you badly placed:** a safe bandeja.
- **A short or mid-height lob, with good position:** a víbora to apply pressure.
- **A very short lob, near the net and with good position:** a smash to finish the point.

During the match, watch how the opponents defend. If they struggle with balls that come out close to the side glass, a víbora into that area will be a very effective weapon.

## Common mistakes

1. **Not turning side-on.** Hitting front-on forces you to make the whole movement with your arm and costs control.
2. **Hitting the ball behind your head.** If you do not move back far enough, the ball gets past you and the shot loses precision. Better to take one more step back.
3. **Trying víboras before mastering the bandeja.** The víbora takes more technique and coordination.
4. **Looking for power from the wrist.** Power should come from turning the body and the arm, not from flicking the wrist, which also strains the elbow.
5. **Forgetting to recover.** After the shot you must get back to the net. A good shot followed by staying back loses all its value.

## Drills for practising them

- **Bandejas from mid-court:** a partner feeds mid-height lobs and you practise the movement, aiming the balls deep, near the corner.
- **Bandeja and back to the net:** after each shot, touch the net with your racket and set yourself again. That makes the recovery automatic.
- **Víboras to a marked area:** put an object near the side glass and try to make the ball bounce close to it.
- **Conditioned game:** in a practice match, points can only be won with shots from the net, which forces you to defend lobs with bandejas.

## Looking after your shoulder and elbow

Overhead shots demand a lot from the shoulder. Before playing, spend a few minutes on shoulder mobility and gentle activation exercises with a resistance band. Off court, strengthening the shoulder and back muscles reduces the risk of problems. If you feel persistent pain in your shoulder or elbow when playing these shots, see a physiotherapist before it becomes an injury.

## Patience as a strategy

Learning the bandeja and the víbora takes time and lots of repetition. At first you will miss more than with a brute-force smash. But as you master these shots you will notice that you win many more points without taking risks, simply because you and your partner stay at the net and the opponents wear themselves out trying to move you. In padel, whoever controls the net controls the match, and the bandeja is the key that keeps it locked.

## Frequently asked questions

### At what level should I learn the bandeja?

As soon as you have the basic back-court shots and the volley, and you start playing matches in which opponents lob regularly. For many players that happens after a few months of play. There is no need to wait until you are advanced: the sooner you add the bandeja, the sooner you stop losing the net with every lob.

### Is the bandeja the same as a weak smash?

No. A weak smash is a flat or topspin shot without enough power, which usually bounces high off the glass and leaves the opponent an easy ball. The bandeja has slice, which makes the ball bounce low and come out little from the glass. The difference lies in the spin and the intention: the bandeja controls, the weak smash gives the point away.

### What do I do if the lob is too good for a bandeja?

If the lob gets past you and you cannot hit it comfortably overhead, the sensible thing is to move back, let the ball bounce and come off the back glass, and defend it from the back. Trying a forced bandeja from a bad position usually ends in a mistake. In those situations your partner should drop back with you so as not to leave gaps.

### How do you coordinate the bandeja with your partner?

While you move back to play the bandeja, your partner should stay alert near the net, slightly towards the middle, to cover a possible quick reply from the opponents. After the shot, you both go back to the net position. Calling the ball with one word ("mine") stops you both going for the same ball.

### Does the víbora work on slow courts?

On slow courts, with artificial grass carrying more sand, or on cold and damp days, the ball bounces less and the víbora is even more effective, because the ball comes out very low from the glass. On fast courts and on hot days, adjust the direction and depth so that the ball does not come too far out from the glass.
