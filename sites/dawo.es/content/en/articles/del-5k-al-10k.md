Ten kilometres is the next big step for many runners who can already run five comfortably. It is a distance that demands a little more consistency and planning, but it is still very accessible: with three or four sessions a week and some patience, most healthy amateur runners can complete it in two or three months.

The biggest risk at this stage is not falling short, but overdoing it. Doubling the distance means almost doubling the time your muscles, tendons and bones spend absorbing impact, and those tissues adapt more slowly than your heart and lungs. That is why this plan puts the emphasis on gradual progression, on the long run at an easy pace and on strength work as a complement.

## The starting point

This plan is designed for anyone who meets these conditions:

- You can run five kilometres without stopping, without difficulty, at a comfortable pace.
- You have been training regularly three days a week for at least a month.
- You have no active aches or injuries.

If you are not there yet, consolidate the five kilometres first. Skipping stages usually costs you dearly.

## The rule of cautious progression

A guideline widely used by coaches is not to increase your weekly volume by more than ten per cent from one week to the next. It is not an exact scientific law, and some studies suggest that the risk depends more on sudden one-off increases than on a particular figure. But as practical guidance it works well: avoid big jumps in your total weekly kilometres or minutes.

Just as important is including **recovery weeks**: every three or four weeks, cut your volume by twenty to thirty per cent so that your body can absorb the work.

## The structure of the week

The plan combines three types of session:

1. **Easy runs**, at a conversational pace. They are the foundation of your training and should make up most of your running time.
2. **One quality session** a week, with moderate changes of pace: stretches at a pace somewhat faster than easy, with jogging recoveries.
3. **The long run**, which gradually gets longer until it slightly exceeds the expected length of the race.

If you have time, you can add a fourth very easy, short run, but it is not essential. Three sessions done well are enough to complete ten kilometres.

## The eight-week plan

Each session starts with a ten-minute progressive warm-up and ends with five minutes of very easy jogging or walking.

| Week | Quality | Easy run | Long run |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 × 3 min at a brisk pace, 2 min jog | 30 min | 6 km |
| 2 | 4 × 4 min at a brisk pace, 2 min jog | 30 min | 7 km |
| 3 | 3 × 6 min at a brisk pace, 2 min jog | 35 min | 8 km |
| 4 | 6 × 2 min at a brisk pace, 90 s jog | 30 min | 6 km (recovery) |
| 5 | 3 × 8 min at a brisk pace, 2 min jog | 35 min | 9 km |
| 6 | 2 × 12 min at a brisk pace, 3 min jog | 40 min | 10 km |
| 7 | 4 × 5 min at a brisk pace, 2 min jog | 35 min | 11 km |
| 8 | 4 × 3 min at a brisk pace, 2 min jog | 25 min | 10 km (goal) |

"Brisk pace" means an effort you can keep up for several minutes, at which you can no longer talk comfortably but are not at your limit. For many runners it roughly matches the pace at which they could run a 10K.

Week 7 includes an eleven-kilometre run, slightly longer than the race. Running it at an easy pace gives you confidence: on the day of your goal you will know that the distance is not a problem.

## The long run, step by step

The long run is the session that prepares you most for the distance. These tips help you get the most out of it:

- **Always at a conversational pace.** If you are at your limit by the end of the run, you set off too fast.
- **Choose pleasant routes with few hills**, especially at first.
- **Plan your water** if you are going to run for more than an hour or it is hot.
- **Take it easy in the afternoon.** A good meal and some rest help you recover.

If aches appear on a long run, cut it short and do not try to make up the lost kilometres the next day.

## Strength, the runner's insurance policy

As you increase the distance, muscle fatigue sets in later in the run, and with it the changes in technique that overload your joints. A simple strength programme, twice a week, reduces that risk. You can do it at home in twenty or thirty minutes:

- **Squats**, 3 sets of 10 to 15 repetitions.
- **Alternating lunges**, 3 sets of 8 to 10 per leg.
- **Glute bridges**, 3 sets of 12 to 15.
- **Calf raises** with straight and bent knees, 3 sets of 15.
- **Front and side planks**, 3 sets of 20 to 40 seconds.

Do these exercises on easy-run days or on non-running days, never just before the quality session. As you get stronger, you can add weight with dumbbells or a loaded rucksack.

## Signs that you are going too fast

Paying attention to these signs prevents many injuries:

- **Pain that lasts more than two or three days** or always appears in the same spot.
- **Tiredness that does not go away** after a rest day.
- **Worse sleep or appetite.**
- **Easy sessions repeatedly feel hard.**

If you notice several of these signs, take a recovery week earlier than planned. Losing a few days of training does not put your goal at risk; an injury does.

## The most common injuries at this stage

As you increase the distance, some aches become more common: pain on the outside of the knee, Achilles tendon trouble, pain in the sole of the foot or on the inside of the shin. In most cases they are linked to increasing the volume too quickly. If a pain does not improve within a few days, see a physiotherapist or a sports doctor; the sooner it is dealt with, the easier it is to sort out.

## Everyday eating

A ten-kilometre plan does not need a special diet. A varied diet based on vegetables, fruit, pulses, wholegrains, fish, eggs and olive oil covers your needs perfectly. Before the long run, a light breakfast with carbohydrates, such as bread, fruit or oats, provides energy. After demanding sessions, a meal that includes protein and carbohydrates helps you recover.

## Race day

If your goal is a 10K fun run, the strategy is simple:

1. **Start calmly.** The first two kilometres should feel easy. The excitement of the start pushes you to run faster than is wise.
2. **Keep a steady pace** between kilometres three and seven. Concentrate on your breathing and a regular cadence.
3. **Decide at kilometre eight.** If you feel good, pick up the pace slightly. If not, hold it.
4. **Enjoy the finish.** The last few hundred metres of a fun run, with the crowd cheering, are one of the best moments in amateur sport.

Do not try anything new on race day: no new shoes, no new breakfast and no gels you have not used before in training.

## After the ten kilometres

After the race, take an easy week with short, gentle runs. Then you can decide on the next step: improving your time over the distance, preparing for a half marathon or simply enjoying running without a specific goal. They are all valid options.

Going from five to ten kilometres is above all a lesson in patience. Those who respect the progression, sleep well and work on their strength reach the finish line in one piece and eager for more.

## Frequently asked questions

### What do I do if I miss a week or two of the plan?

If you miss a week because of a trip, a cold or work, pick up the plan again by repeating the last week you completed. If you miss two or more, go back two weeks. Do not try to make up the lost kilometres by adding them to the following weeks: it is a sure way to overload yourself. If you were ill with a fever, wait until you feel completely well and start with very easy runs.

### Can I run ten kilometres with only two sessions a week?

It is possible, but harder, and the progression needs to be slower. With two sessions, one of them should be the long run and the other an easy run with some gentle changes of pace. Adding a strength or cycling session helps to make up for it.

### Do I need gels or special drinks for ten kilometres?

For a ten-kilometre race, which most amateurs complete in under seventy minutes, you do not usually need any gels. It is enough to arrive well hydrated and having eaten breakfast. If the race is in hot weather, use the drinks stations to take a few sips of water.

### What is a good time for a first 10K?

The best time is the one you achieve while finishing feeling good. Some runners do their first 10K in under fifty minutes and others complete it in seventy or eighty, and all of them have achieved their goal. Use that first race as a reference for planning the next ones, not as an exam.
