Every January, gyms fill up and parks fill with new runners. By March, a good many of them have disappeared. It is not a lack of willpower: motivation is a resource that rises and falls, and anyone who relies on it alone to train ends up giving up when it falls. People who keep exercising for years are not more motivated than everyone else; they have built habits and surroundings that make it easy to carry on even on days when they do not feel like it.

This article brings together practical strategies, based on what we know about the psychology of habits, for staying physically active all year round.

## Motivation and habit are not the same thing

**Motivation** is the desire to do something. It is powerful at first, but unstable: it depends on your mood, tiredness, the weather and a thousand other factors.

A **habit** is a behaviour that is repeated almost automatically in a particular context. When exercise becomes a habit, you no longer have to decide every day whether to do it: it is simply part of your routine.

So the goal is not to be motivated all the time, but to **turn exercise into a habit** that works even when motivation is low.

## Goals that help

### Specific, realistic goals

"Getting fit" is a wish; "walking for thirty minutes three days a week for the next month" is a goal. Specific, measurable, achievable goals are easier to meet and to assess.

### Process goals, not just outcome goals

An outcome goal is "running 10 kilometres in November" or "losing five kilos". A process goal is "running three times a week" or "doing strength training on Tuesdays and Thursdays". Process goals depend only on you and give you a sense of achievement every week.

### Intermediate targets

Breaking a big goal into small stages keeps up the feeling of progress. Each stage completed boosts your confidence.

## Design your surroundings

Many decisions depend more on your surroundings than on willpower:

- **Get your kit ready the night before**, where you can see it.
- **Leave your trainers by the door.**
- **Choose a gym or facility near home or work.** Distance is one of the main barriers.
- **Put exercise in your diary** like any other appointment, with a time and place.
- **Reduce the obstacles**: if training in the morning means getting up very early, maybe lunchtime would work better.

## Link exercise to an existing routine

An effective strategy for creating habits is to link the new behaviour to something you already do every day: "after dropping the children at school, I go for a twenty-minute walk", "when I get home from work, I change and do my strength routine". The existing routine acts as a reminder.

## The never-miss-twice rule

Everyone misses the odd session. What matters is not never failing, but making sure one miss does not turn into a streak. A simple rule: **you can skip one session, but not two in a row**. If you cannot train one day, do something the next, even a cut-down version.

## Minimum sessions

On days with little time or little desire, a minimum session keeps the habit alive: ten minutes of walking, a short run round the block, five minutes of exercises at home. Often, once you start, you end up doing more. And if not, at least you have kept the routine going.

## Enjoy it

In the long run, it is very hard to keep up an activity you do not enjoy. Some ideas:

- **Find the activity you like**, not the one that is supposed to be best. If you hate running, try swimming, padel, cycling, dancing or climbing.
- **Vary it**: alternating activities stops it becoming monotonous.
- **Do it in pleasant places**: a park, the beach, the mountains.
- **Use music or podcasts**, if they help.

## Company

Training with other people makes you more likely to stick with it:

- **A training partner** you meet at a set time makes it harder to skip a session.
- **A group or club**: running groups, mountaineering clubs, padel or amateur football teams.
- **Group classes**, with a timetable and an instructor.
- **Shared challenges**: training for a race or a walking route with friends.

## Record your progress

Seeing how far you have come is motivating. A training diary, an app or a simple calendar on the fridge where you tick off each session lets you see your consistency. Record how you feel as well: sleeping better, having more energy or climbing stairs without getting tired are as valuable as the kilometres.

## Rewards

Rewards can help to consolidate a habit, especially at first: a special breakfast after the Sunday long run, new trainers when you have kept it up for a month. Over time, the feeling of wellbeing after training becomes the reward itself.

## "If… then" plans

A simple technique, fairly well supported by research on habits, is to anticipate obstacles and decide in advance what you will do when they come up. Write it as a sentence: "if it rains on Tuesday, I will do my strength routine at home"; "if I leave work late, I will walk for twenty minutes when I get home instead of going to the gym"; "if I wake up tired, I will just do the minimum session". When the obstacle arrives, the decision has already been made and you do not have to bargain with yourself at the worst possible moment.

## With young children or changing shifts

When time is short and unpredictable, it helps to change your approach: instead of looking for long sessions on fixed days, fit in short blocks of ten or fifteen minutes wherever they go, such as a brisk walk while the children are in the park, a few strength exercises before your shower or some stairs at work. ## Difficult times of year

### Winter

Short days, cold and rain. Some strategies: train at lunchtime to make the most of the daylight, have an alternative at home or at the gym, wear suitable clothing and set maintenance goals.

### Summer

Heat and holidays. Train early, make the most of the water, switch activities and accept that your volume will drop.

### Busy periods at work or life changes

During these periods, scale your goal down instead of abandoning it: fewer sessions, shorter ones, but keep in touch with exercise.

### After an injury or illness

Coming back is hard. Start small, do not compare yourself with your previous level and celebrate each step.

## When motivation disappears

If you have gone weeks without wanting to train, ask yourself:

- **Am I bored?** Change your activity or your route.
- **Am I tired?** You may need more rest, or you may be building up fatigue.
- **Are my goals realistic?** Adjust your expectations.
- **Am I going through a bad patch?** Gentle exercise can help your mood, but if you notice persistent sadness or anxiety, seek professional support.

## A plan for the first eight weeks

The first few weeks are the most fragile. This outline helps you get through them:

1. **Weeks 1 and 2:** choose a single activity and two fixed days. Short sessions, of twenty to thirty minutes, easier than you think you can manage. The only goal is to turn up.
2. **Weeks 3 and 4:** add a third day or make the sessions a little longer. Start recording what you do.
3. **Weeks 5 and 6:** bring in some variety: a different activity one day a week, a new route, a group class.
4. **Weeks 7 and 8:** set a process goal for the next two months and, if you feel like it, a specific challenge: a fun run, a mountain route, a friendly tournament.

At the end of the eight weeks, look back at your calendar. If you have done most of the sessions, the habit is under way. If not, adjust the plan rather than giving up: perhaps the days you chose were not the right ones or the sessions were too long.

## What you tell yourself

The way we talk to ourselves affects consistency. Phrases such as "I didn't do anything right today" or "I'll never be consistent" are discouraging. Swapping them for more realistic ones helps: "today I did a short session, and that counts", "I missed one day this week, but I did three". It is not about fooling yourself, but about valuing what you do manage.

## Common mistakes

1. **Starting too hard** and ending up exhausted or injured.
2. **Relying on motivation alone.**
3. **Setting all-or-nothing goals.**
4. **Comparing yourself with others**, especially on social media.
5. **Giving up after one slip.**

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to form a habit?

It varies a lot between people and behaviours. Some studies suggest it can take anything from a few weeks to several months. What matters is repetition in a stable context.

### Is it better to train in the morning or the evening?

The best time is the one you can keep up. Training early, before the day gets complicated, works for some people; for others, the evening is better.

### Are apps and watches useful?

They can help you record your progress and stay motivated, but they are not essential.

## In short

The key to keeping up exercise all year round is not endless motivation, but well-built habits: realistic goals, surroundings that make training easy, routines linked to other routines, minimum sessions for difficult days, activities you enjoy and company. With that foundation, exercise stops being a daily struggle and becomes part of your life.
