On amateur football pitches you often see the same scene: players arrive with no time to spare, change in a hurry, kick the ball around a few times and start the match. Ten minutes later, someone grabs the back of their thigh after a sprint. It is one of the most common injuries in football, and one of the most closely linked to a lack of preparation.

The warm-up is not a formality. It is how you prepare muscles, joints and nervous system for a sport that combines sprints, braking, changes of direction, jumps and kicks. Structured warm-up programmes, such as the one FIFA developed for injury prevention, have been shown in studies with amateur teams to reduce injuries noticeably when they are done regularly. This article sets out a fifteen-minute routine inspired by those principles, designed to be done before every match or training session.

## What a good warm-up should achieve

- **Raise body and muscle temperature,** which improves tissue elasticity and the speed of contraction.
- **Wake up the cardiovascular system** gradually.
- **Prepare the joints** for the ranges of movement in the match.
- **Activate the key muscles** in injury prevention: glutes, hamstrings, adductors, calves and trunk.
- **Wake up coordination and reflexes.**
- **Focus** on the match.

## The structure

| Phase | Content | Time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Easy running with mobility | 4 min |
| 2 | Strength, balance and jumps | 6 min |
| 3 | Faster running and changes of direction | 3 min |
| 4 | Ball: progressive passing and shooting | 2 min or more |

If you have more time, extend the ball phase.

## Phase 1: easy running with mobility (4 minutes)

Run across the width of the pitch and back, doing each exercise on one leg of the journey:

1. **Easy jog,** to start.
2. **Hip out:** while jogging, lift the knee and turn it outwards, alternating legs.
3. **Hip in:** the same, but taking the knee from outside to inside.
4. **Side-stepping,** facing one way on the way out and the other on the way back.
5. **Running backwards** with short steps.
6. **Knees to chest** while jogging.
7. **Heels to bottom** while jogging.

If you are with teammates, doing it in pairs, crossing and touching shoulders at some point on each run, adds an element of coordination.

## Phase 2: strength, balance and jumps (6 minutes)

This is the most important part for prevention and the one most often skipped.

### Front plank

On your forearms and toes, with your body straight. Hold for 20 to 30 seconds. If it is easy, lift one leg at a time for a few seconds.

### Side plank

On one forearm and the side of your foot, with your hips raised. 20 to 30 seconds each side.

### Hamstrings: the assisted Nordic curl

Kneeling, with a partner holding your ankles, lean forwards slowly keeping your body straight, braking with the backs of your thighs for as long as you can, and catch yourself with your hands as you fall. Three to five repetitions. It is a demanding exercise: if you have never done it, start with few repetitions and a short range. If you have no partner, replace it with single-leg glute bridges.

### Squats

Ten controlled squats, with your knees in line with your feet, not caving inwards.

### Walking lunges

Ten lunges, five on each leg, lowering the back knee close to the ground.

### Single-leg balance

On one leg, with the knee slightly bent, hold your balance for 20 to 30 seconds. To make it harder, pass a ball from one hand to the other or to a partner. Repeat on the other leg.

### Jumps

- **Vertical jumps:** five jumps, landing softly on the balls of your feet, with your knees bent and aligned.
- **Single-leg side hops:** five each side, landing steadily.

A controlled landing, with the knee aligned over the foot, is key to preventing knee injuries.

## Phase 3: faster running (3 minutes)

1. **Build-ups:** run across the pitch accelerating to seventy or eighty per cent of your top speed. Jog back. Repeat twice.
2. **Running with changes of direction:** run four or five steps, brake on the outside leg and change direction. Repeat several times to each side.
3. **Bounding (wide skipping):** cover a stretch lengthening your stride and lifting the knee.
4. **One or two short sprints** of ten to fifteen metres, at almost full speed, at the end of the warm-up.

## Phase 4: the ball (2 minutes or more)

- Short passes in pairs, with both feet.
- Directional first touches.
- A few medium-range shots, building up in intensity.
- Goalkeepers do their own warm-up: catching, progressive dives and movement.

Do not start shooting hard before doing the earlier phases. Powerful shots when cold are a frequent cause of quadriceps and adductor injuries.

## Why hamstrings get injured so often

The hamstrings are the muscles at the back of the thigh. In a sprint they work in a very demanding situation: they brake the leg swinging forwards just before the foot touches the ground, lengthening while contracting. If they are cold, tired or lack strength, that moment is especially delicate. That is why the typical injury happens in a flat-out run, often at the end of a half or in the first minutes if you have not warmed up.

The Nordic exercise in phase 2 trains exactly that ability to brake with the muscle lengthened. Done regularly during the week, and not just in the warm-up, it is one of the best-supported measures for reducing this kind of injury.

## An example with little space

If the pitch is occupied by the previous match and you only have the touchline or a corridor by the changing room, you can adapt the routine:

1. Mobility on the spot: knees to chest, heels to bottom, hip and ankle circles.
2. Planks, squats, lunges and single-leg balance, which need no space.
3. Gentle jumps on the spot.
4. Short runs up and down the touchline, increasing the speed.
5. As soon as the pitch is free, a few minutes of passing and one or two sprints.

What matters is not skipping the strength and balance part and not starting the match without having run at something like match speed first.

## Static stretching: before or after?

Long static stretches — holding a position for thirty seconds or more — just before an explosive effort can slightly reduce power and speed. That is why dynamic movements take priority in the warm-up. Static stretching makes more sense after the match or in separate sessions, if it helps you feel more comfortable.

## When it is cold

In winter, the warm-up should be a little longer and more gradual. Wear warm clothing at first and take it off bit by bit. If the match starts late or there is a long break, get going again with a few minutes of jogging and some mobility exercises before playing again.

## Half-time

During the break, the muscles cool down. Before the second half, spend a couple of minutes jogging, doing a few changes of direction and a short sprint or two. It is especially important for substitutes who come on cold: they should warm up for a few minutes before going on.

## Making it a team habit

In amateur football, the warm-up rarely fails for lack of knowledge: it fails because nobody organises it. It works much better when the team agrees to meet twenty-five minutes before the match, someone takes charge — the captain, the coach or a senior player — and the same sequence is always repeated. After a few weeks the routine becomes automatic and nobody argues about it. A printed list of the exercises kept in the kit bag helps so that it does not depend on one person's memory.

## The cool-down

When the match ends, do not just slump on the bench. Five or ten minutes of very easy jogging or walking, followed by water and something to eat, help the body return to normal. If you find them helpful, it is a good moment for gentle stretches of the calves, hamstrings, adductors and hips.

## The work during the week

The warm-up reduces the risk, but it works no miracles if the body is not prepared. If you only play one match a week, add one or two strength sessions during the week, with exercises for the hamstrings, glutes, adductors and trunk. The combination of a structured warm-up and strength work is the best protection for the amateur footballer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Isn't fifteen minutes too much for a one-hour match?

It is a small investment compared with the weeks lost to an injury. If time is very short, prioritise the strength and balance phase and the build-up runs.

### Does it work for futsal?

Yes, with adaptations: shorter runs and more emphasis on changes of direction and on the ankles.

### And for children?

Yes. There are versions adapted for children, with more playful exercises, which have also proved useful in reducing injuries.

## In short

Fifteen minutes of structured warm-up, with mobility, strength, balance, build-up runs and the ball, prepare the body for what football demands. Doing it before every match is one of the simplest and most effective ways of carrying on playing without injury.
