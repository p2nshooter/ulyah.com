Few complaints are as common among amateur padel players as pain on the outside of the elbow. It starts as a slight ache after a match, then it appears when you pick up a bag or turn a door handle, and in the most persistent cases it even hurts to shake hands. Its popular name is "tennis elbow", although today it probably affects more padel players than tennis players.

The medical term is **lateral epicondylitis** or, more precisely, **lateral elbow tendinopathy**. This article explains what it is, why it is so common in padel and what you can do to reduce the risk. As always, if you already have pain, an assessment by a doctor or a physiotherapist is the first step.

## What happens in the elbow

On the outside of the elbow there is a small bony bump called the lateral epicondyle. The tendons of several forearm muscles that extend the wrist and fingers attach there. Every time you grip the racket hard, absorb a shot or steady your wrist on a volley, those muscles work and pull on their attachment.

When the load on those tendons exceeds their ability to adapt for a period of time, changes appear in the structure of the tendon, along with pain. Although for years people spoke of "inflammation", today it is understood mainly as a problem of tendon overload, which explains why complete rest does not usually solve it and why well-dosed exercise is part of the treatment.

## Common symptoms

- Pain on the outer side of the elbow, which may spread down the forearm.
- Pain when gripping objects firmly, lifting something with the palm facing down or turning the wrist.
- Pain when pressing on the epicondyle.
- A feeling of weakness when squeezing.

If the pain appears after a direct blow, or if there is tingling in the fingers, a significant loss of strength or marked swelling, see a doctor: it could be a different problem.

## Why it is so common in padel

Several factors typical of amateur padel come together:

### How much you play

Many players go from one to four matches a week in a short space of time, especially when they get hooked. The tendon has no time to adapt to that increase.

### Technique

Some technical habits increase the load on the wrist extensors:

- **Hitting with the wrist** instead of using your body and your whole arm.
- **Being late on the ball**, hitting it behind your body, which forces you to compensate with your wrist.
- **Volleys with a floppy wrist** that absorb the impact in the forearm.
- **Poorly executed overhead shots**, especially víboras and forced smashes.

### Equipment

- **Hard, heavy or head-heavy rackets** transmit more vibration and take more strength to control.
- **A very thin or very thick grip** makes you squeeze harder than necessary.
- **Old or flat balls** produce harsher impacts.

### Age and fitness

It is most common between the ages of thirty-five and fifty-five. Tendons lose some of their ability to adapt with age, and many players in that age group start playing without ever having done any strength work for their forearms.

## How to prevent it

Prevention works on several fronts. None of them guarantees anything on its own, but together they greatly reduce the risk.

### 1. Increase how much you play little by little

If you are starting out or coming back after a break, do not jump straight to playing four days a week. Build up gradually and leave rest days between matches.

### 2. Review your technique

A few lessons with a coach can correct the habits that overload the elbow most: preparing earlier, hitting in front of your body, using your legs and the rotation of your trunk, and keeping your wrist firm but not rigid on volleys.

### 3. Choose the right racket

If you are prone to elbow trouble, go for light rackets with a low balance and a soft core, which absorb vibration better. Adjust the thickness of the handle with overgrips so that you can hold the racket without squeezing too hard.

### 4. Strengthen your forearm

Strength work increases the tendon's ability to take load. A few simple exercises, two or three times a week:

- **Wrist extension with a light dumbbell**, with your forearm resting on a table and your palm facing down. Raise your hand and lower it slowly, over about three seconds. Three sets of fifteen repetitions.
- **Wrist flexion**, the same but with your palm facing up.
- **Pronation and supination** with a dumbbell held at one end, turning your forearm slowly.
- **Squeezing a soft ball** or a grip roller.
- **Shoulder and shoulder-blade exercises** with resistance bands, because a weak shoulder makes the arm compensate with the elbow.

The slow lowering phase, called the eccentric phase, is especially useful for tendons. The load should be moderate: the exercise may cause slight discomfort, but not severe pain.

### 5. Warm up before playing

Mobility for the wrists, elbows and shoulders, gentle exercises with a resistance band and a gradual knock-up prepare the tissues for the effort.

### 6. Look after your rest

Sleeping well and spacing out your matches let the tendon recover between sessions.

## If you already have pain

Some general guidelines, always pending an assessment by a professional:

- **Do not ignore the pain.** Carrying on playing with increasing pain usually drags the problem out for months.
- **Reduce the load**, not necessarily to zero. Often it is enough to play less, less intensely or to avoid the shots that hurt most.
- **Keep moving.** Complete rest weakens the tendon and makes the return harder.
- **See a physiotherapist**, who can design a progressive exercise programme suited to your case.
- **Be wary of miracle solutions.** Elbow straps can bring some people relief during play, but they do not solve the problem unless the load is adjusted and the tendon strengthened.

Lateral elbow tendinopathy usually improves with conservative treatment, although it sometimes takes patience: it is not unusual for full recovery to take several months.

## The pain traffic light: a guide to dosing

Many physiotherapists use a simple scale to adjust the load in tendinopathies. Score your pain from 0, none, to 10, the worst imaginable, during play or exercise and on the following morning:

| Pain | Colour | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 2 | green | the load is right; you can keep it or increase it little by little |
| 3 to 5 | amber | acceptable if you are back to your usual level the next morning; do not increase the load |
| Over 5, or worse the next day | red | reduce the load in the next session and seek advice if it happens again |

It is general guidance, not a diagnosis: your physiotherapist can adapt it to your case. Its great strength is that it turns a vague feeling into a figure you can base decisions on.

## Off the court too

The tendon does not distinguish between padel and the rest of your day. Many people with tennis elbow also overload it at work or at home: hours at the mouse and keyboard with the wrist bent back, tools that make you squeeze, heavy bags carried with the palm facing down. A few simple habits help: carrying bags with your palm facing up or close to your body, using a mouse and keyboard that let you rest your wrist in a neutral position, taking short breaks to move your hands and sharing gripping tasks between both arms.

## Getting back to playing

When the pain has eased and you can do the strength exercises without discomfort, return to the court gradually:

1. Start with gentle rallies from the back, without hard volleys or overhead shots.
2. Add gentle volleys and then controlled bandejas.
3. Play short, friendly matches before going back to competition.
4. Keep up the strength exercises even when you no longer have any pain.

If the pain comes back at any step, go back to the previous one for a few days.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is tennis elbow the same as golfer's elbow?

No. Golfer's elbow affects the inside of the elbow, where the muscles that flex the wrist attach. It is less common in padel, but it can appear, especially in players who put a lot of load into topspin forehands.

### Are injections a solution?

That is a medical decision. In some people they can relieve pain in the short term, but they do not replace exercise or correcting the causes. Discuss the options with your doctor.

### Do I have to give up padel for good?

Hardly ever. The vast majority of players go back to playing normally after adjusting the load, their technique and their equipment, and keeping up a strength programme.

## In short

Tennis elbow in padel appears when the load on the forearm tendons grows faster than their capacity. Playing with a technique that uses your body, a suitable racket, a sensible progression and a little forearm strength are the best ways to keep enjoying padel without pain.
