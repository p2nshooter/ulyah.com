Almost everyone has had it: two days after going back to the gym, after a hike with a lot of downhill or after the first match of the season, going down the stairs becomes a small ordeal. Muscle soreness — *agujetas*, as it is called in Spain — is one of the most universal experiences in sport and, at the same time, one of the most surrounded by myths. Is it lactic acid crystals? Can it be cured with sugary water? Do you need to stretch to avoid it? Is it a sign that the workout was a good one?

This article explains what we really know about muscle soreness, why it appears and what can be done to ease it.

## What muscle soreness is

In science it is known as **delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS).** Its features are quite recognisable:

- It appears 12 to 24 hours after exercise.
- It peaks between 24 and 72 hours.
- It fades gradually over a few days, usually in less than a week.
- It comes with stiffness, tenderness to the touch and a temporary loss of strength.
- It affects the muscles that have worked, in a diffuse way.

## The lactic acid myth

For decades it was said that soreness was caused by "lactic acid crystals" building up in the muscles. That idea does not hold up:

- The lactate produced during hard exercise is cleared from the blood and muscles within an hour or two, long before soreness appears.
- No lactate crystals form in the muscles.
- What is more, soreness appears mostly after exercise that does not produce much lactate, such as running downhill.

That is why remedies such as drinking sugary water to "dissolve the crystals" have no basis at all.

## Why it really happens

Current explanations point to a combination of factors:

- **Micro-damage to the muscle fibres,** especially in exercises with eccentric contractions, in which the muscle lengthens while producing force, as when going downhill or lowering the weight in a squat.
- **A local inflammatory repair response.**
- **Greater sensitivity of the nerve endings** in the muscle and the connective tissue around it.

Soreness is, essentially, part of the process by which a muscle adapts to a new or more intense stimulus.

## Which exercises cause it

Soreness is worse when:

- **The exercise is new,** or you have not done it for a while.
- **Eccentric contractions dominate:** going down slopes or stairs, the lowering phase in strength exercises, jumps with landings, braking.
- **Volume or intensity rises suddenly.**

A classic example: someone who usually runs on the flat goes on a mountain route with a long descent. The next day their quadriceps are very sore, even though they are fit.

## The protective effect

One of the most interesting features of muscle soreness is that, after a first session that causes it, the muscle is partly protected: repeating the same exercise a few days or weeks later causes much less soreness. This is known as the **repeated bout effect.**

That is why the best way to avoid severe soreness is to **bring in new exercises gradually:** a first gentle session builds protection for the ones that follow.

## Is it a good sign?

Not necessarily. Being sore shows that the stimulus was new or more intense than usual, but it is not a measure of how effective the training was. You can make progress in strength and fitness without being sore after every session. In fact, chasing soreness all the time usually means you are training too irregularly or with sudden jumps in load.

## How to ease it

There is no remedy that makes it disappear at once, but a few things help you cope:

### Gentle movement

Low-intensity activity, such as walking, easy cycling or swimming, usually eases the pain temporarily while you do it. Keeping moving is better than complete rest.

### Massage

Massage can reduce the feeling of pain for a few hours. Self-massage with a foam roller, at moderate pressure, has a similar effect.

### Heat

A warm shower or bath can ease the feeling of stiffness.

### Cold

Some people feel relief with cold packs or cold-water immersion. The effect is moderate.

### Sleep and food

Sleeping well and eating enough protein and energy help the muscle repair itself.

### Painkillers

Some people turn to anti-inflammatories. It is best not to overuse them without medical advice, as they have side effects and do not speed up recovery. Talk to a health professional before taking them regularly.

### Stretching

As explained in the article on [when stretching helps](/articulos/estiramientos-cuando-sirven/), the evidence shows that stretching before or after exercise has a very small or no effect on soreness. You can do it if it feels good, but do not expect it to make the soreness go away.

## Can I train while sore?

It depends on how sore you are:

- **Mild soreness:** you can train normally, perhaps a little less hard. The warm-up often eases it.
- **Moderate soreness:** better a gentle session, or work other muscle groups.
- **Severe soreness:** put active recovery first and avoid demanding efforts with the affected muscles, because strength and coordination are temporarily reduced and the risk of injury may rise.

## When it is not soreness

Some muscle pains are not DOMS and need attention:

- **Sudden, sharp pain during exercise,** like a stab, which may point to a muscle tear.
- **Pain in one specific spot,** with swelling or bruising.
- **Pain that does not improve** after a week.
- **Very severe muscle pain with marked swelling, clear weakness or dark urine,** especially after an extreme effort far beyond what you are used to. It may be a sign of rhabdomyolysis, an uncommon but potentially serious condition that needs urgent medical attention.

## Soreness by sport

Each activity has its "hot spots":

- **Hiking and trail running:** the quadriceps after long descents, and the calves after steep climbs.
- **Football and padel at the start of the season:** the adductors, hamstrings and calves, because of changes of direction, braking and sprints.
- **The gym:** the muscles worked in new exercises, especially after sessions with a lot of lowering.
- **Skiing:** the quadriceps, because of the sustained bent position and constant braking.
- **Dance or aerobics classes:** the calves and glutes, especially if they include jumps.

Knowing these spots lets you get ahead: for example, adding a few squats and lunges with a slow lowering phase in the weeks before a mountain route, or a few gentle change-of-direction sessions before the first match of the season.

## Coming back after a break without suffering

The time you are most likely to be sore is when you come back after a holiday, an injury or a summer without training. A three-week progression avoids most of the pain:

| Week | Gym | Team sports or running |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Half your usual sets, with weights that leave three or four reps in reserve | Two short, easy sessions, with no sprints or sharp braking |
| 2 | Two thirds of your usual volume, slightly more demanding weights | Three sessions, with some controlled changes of pace and direction |
| 3 | Usual volume and loads, if you feel good | Normal training and the first match or race |

Even if it seems too little at first, that first easy week triggers the protective repeated bout effect: in the following weeks you can train normally without spending three days going down the stairs sideways.

## How to prevent severe soreness

1. **Increase the load gradually,** especially when starting a new sport or after a break.
2. **Bring in eccentric exercises little by little:** the first descents, squats or jumps, with low volume.
3. **Keep it regular:** training consistently reduces soreness compared with training in bursts.
4. **Cool down** and do active recovery after hard efforts.

## Common mistakes

- **Chasing soreness as proof of a good workout.**
- **Going back to the gym after months off with the same routine as before.**
- **Doing a very hard session on the first day of a new sport.**
- **Mistaking an injury for soreness** and ignoring pain that is not normal.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why am I sore if I am fit?

Because soreness depends on how new the stimulus is, not just on general fitness. A fit runner can be sore after a strength session or a mountain descent.

### Does sugary water help?

No. It is a remedy based on the mistaken idea of lactic acid crystals.

### Do children get sore muscles?

They can, although they usually recover quickly. The same progression guidelines work for them.

### Do creams and gels help?

Cooling or warming gels and creams with painkillers can ease the discomfort for a while, but they do not speed up muscle repair. If you want to use them, ask at the pharmacy, read the leaflet and do not apply them to wounds or before going in the sun if the product advises against it.

## In short

Muscle soreness is the muscle's response to a new or more intense stimulus, especially with eccentric contractions. It is not lactic acid, it does not necessarily mean the workout was better, and it is prevented with progression and regularity. Gentle movement, good sleep and patience are the best way through it.
