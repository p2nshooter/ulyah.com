Running is one of the simplest and most effective ways to stay fit, but it also has a less pleasant side: a large proportion of runners suffer some kind of injury over the course of a year. Most are not serious, but they force you to stop, they are frustrating and, if they are not treated properly, they can keep coming back.

The good news is that almost all amateur running injuries have a common, well-known origin: **doing more than your body is ready to handle at that moment**. Sudden increases in kilometres, too many hard sessions in a row or coming back after a break as if no time had passed. Understanding the most common injuries helps you recognise the first signs and act before they get complicated.

This article is for general information. If you are in pain, the right person to assess it is a doctor, a physiotherapist or a podiatrist.

## Why runners get injured

Each time your foot hits the ground, your body takes a force several times its own weight. In a thirty-minute run, that happens thousands of times. Muscles, tendons and bones adapt to that load and get stronger, but they need time. When the load grows faster than the ability to adapt, overuse injuries appear.

The factors that come up most often are:

- **Training errors**: increasing volume or intensity too quickly, not resting enough or suddenly changing surface or footwear.
- **Previous injuries**: a previous injury that did not heal properly is one of the strongest risk factors.
- **Lack of strength** in the hips, thighs, lower legs and trunk.
- **Not enough rest and sleep.**

Other factors, such as the shape of the foot or the type of footstrike, play a less clear role than was thought years ago.

## Patellofemoral pain syndrome ("runner's knee")

It is one of the most common injuries. It shows up as a diffuse pain around or behind the kneecap, which usually gets worse going down stairs, running downhill or after sitting for a long time with the knees bent.

**Why it appears:** the cartilage and tissues of the joint between the kneecap and the thighbone take more load than they can tolerate, often because of rapid increases in volume, a lot of downhill running or weakness in the hip and thigh muscles.

**What helps:** temporarily reducing the load, especially downhills, and strengthening the quadriceps and glutes. A physiotherapist can guide a specific exercise programme. Slightly increasing your running cadence reduces the load on the knee.

## Medial tibial stress syndrome (shin splints)

This is pain along the inner edge of the shinbone, in its lower third, typical of people who are starting to run or who greatly increase their volume. At first it hurts when you start running and eases as you warm up; if it progresses, it hurts throughout the run and even at rest.

**Why it appears:** the bone and the tissues around it take repeated load beyond their ability to adapt.

**What helps:** reducing your running volume, replacing some sessions with non-impact activities such as cycling or swimming, and returning gradually. It is important to distinguish it from a stress fracture: if the pain is very localised at one point on the bone, hurts when pressed or appears even when walking, see a doctor.

## Plantar fasciitis

It is felt as pain in the sole of the foot, near the heel, especially with the first steps in the morning or on getting up after sitting. It usually improves as you move and gets worse after prolonged efforts.

**Why it appears:** overload of the plantar fascia, a band of tissue that supports the arch of the foot. It is linked to increases in volume, spending a long time on your feet and tightness in the calf muscles (gastrocnemius and soleus).

**What helps:** reducing the load, exercises to strengthen the foot and calf muscles, and stretches for the sole and the calves. It usually improves with conservative treatment, although it can take several weeks or months. A podiatrist or physiotherapist can assess whether some kind of support would help.

## Achilles tendinopathy

The Achilles tendon connects the calf muscles to the heel. When it is overloaded, pain and stiffness appear at the back of the ankle, worse in the morning or when you start running.

**Why it appears:** increases in volume, a lot of running on hills, sudden changes to shoes with a lower drop or to a forefoot strike, and a lack of calf strength.

**What helps:** the tendon responds well to progressive loading. Calf raises, done slowly and with control, are the basis of most rehabilitation programmes. Complete rest is not usually the best solution; the right approach is to adjust the load with a professional's help.

## Iliotibial band syndrome

It causes pain on the outside of the knee, which usually appears after running for a while and can force you to stop. It is common on long runs and downhills.

**Why it appears:** irritation where the iliotibial band, a band of tissue running down the outside of the thigh, passes over the side of the knee. It is linked to increases in volume and to weakness in the hip muscles.

**What helps:** temporarily cutting down long distances and downhills, and strengthening the abductors and glutes. Stretching on its own usually has a limited effect.

## Stress fractures

These are small cracks in the bone caused by repeated loading. They can appear in the shinbone, the metatarsals of the foot or elsewhere. The pain is localised, increases with activity and may persist at rest.

They are more common in people who greatly increase their volume in a short time, who eat less energy than they use or who have low bone density. **If you suspect a stress fracture, stop running and see a doctor.** Carrying on training can make it worse.

## How to prevent injuries

No method completely eliminates the risk, but these habits reduce it considerably:

1. **Progress little by little.** Avoid sudden increases in your weekly kilometres or minutes.
2. **Alternate your efforts.** Do not do two hard sessions in a row. Most of your training should be easy.
3. **Include recovery weeks** every three or four weeks.
4. **Train your strength** twice a week, with exercises for the glutes, thighs, calves and trunk.
5. **Get enough sleep.** Sleep is when the body repairs itself.
6. **Eat enough.** A prolonged energy deficit increases the risk of bone injuries.
7. **Change footwear or surface gradually.**
8. **Listen to the early signs.** An ache that lasts more than two or three days deserves attention.

## What to do when pain appears

When an ache does not go away, the most useful thing is to **reduce the load**, not ignore it. Some general guidelines:

- If the pain is mild, does not get worse during the run and has gone by the next day, you can reduce your volume and intensity and see how it develops.
- If the pain increases during the run, changes the way you run or makes you limp, stop.
- Keep fit with activities that do not hurt: cycling, swimming, the cross-trainer.
- See a professional if the pain persists for more than one or two weeks, if it is very localised or if it appears at rest.

## Getting back to running after an injury

The return should be gradual. A common approach is to start by alternating running and walking, on flat ground, every other day, and to increase the running time only if there is no pain the next day. It is better to have a physiotherapist guide you through this process, especially after tendon or bone injuries.

Impatience is the most common cause of relapses. A few more weeks of caution can save months on the sidelines.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do insoles prevent injuries?

Not in general. Insoles can help in specific cases, such as some kinds of foot pain or significant differences between the two legs, but there is no evidence that every runner needs them to avoid injury. If a podiatrist recommends them after an assessment, introduce them gradually, just like new shoes.

### Ice or heat?

Cold can relieve the pain of a recent niggle for a while, and heat usually feels good on stiff or overloaded areas. Neither significantly speeds up healing: what really changes the outcome is adjusting the load and strengthening the area. Use whichever brings you relief, without applying it directly to the skin.

### Does body weight make a difference?

Yes. The greater your weight, the greater the load on your joints, tendons and bones with each stride. Heavier people can run perfectly well, but they should progress more calmly, alternate running and walking for more weeks and combine running with non-impact activities such as cycling or swimming.

## A final thought

Most running injuries are not bad luck, but the body's way of saying that the load has moved faster than the adaptation. Training patiently, strengthening your body and respecting rest is not just a way of avoiding injuries: it is the way to keep running for many years.
