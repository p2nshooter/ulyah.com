Anyone who has played football on different pitches knows it: playing on wet natural grass is not the same as playing on an artificial pitch in the middle of August or on a hard-packed dirt pitch. Each surface has its own grip, its own way of making the ball bounce and its own way of punishing your joints. And each one calls for a different type of boot.

Choosing the right boots is not a matter of fashion or copying a professional footballer. It is a matter of performance and, above all, of injury prevention. A sole that grips too much can lock your foot during a turn and strain your knee; one that grips too little makes you slip. This article explains which type of boot suits each surface and how to make the right choice.

## Types of sole

Football boots are classified by the type of ground they are designed for. The labels usually show a set of abbreviations in English:

### FG: firm ground (natural grass)

FG boots have moulded studs, usually plastic, either conical or bladed. They are designed for natural grass in good condition on firm ground. They are the most common type on the market.

### SG: soft ground

SG boots have longer studs, often metal or mixed, and fewer of them. They are used on very soft or muddy natural grass, where the stud needs to sink into the ground. They are not suitable for hard ground or artificial grass.

### AG: artificial grass

AG boots have more numerous, shorter and often hollow studs, designed to spread the pressure and stop the foot sticking in artificial grass. They are the best choice for most seven-a-side and eleven-a-side synthetic grass pitches.

### TF: turf or carpet

TF boots, also called astro or multi-stud boots, have a sole with lots of small rubber studs. They are designed for short-pile artificial grass or hard surfaces. They are a good option for older artificial pitches and for hard dirt surfaces.

### IN or IC: indoor

A flat rubber sole, for indoor futsal courts. They are no good on grass or dirt.

## Why you should not use FG boots on artificial grass

It is one of the most common situations in amateur football: a player buys FG boots because they are the flashiest and wears them on their league's artificial pitch. They work, but they are not ideal.

Artificial grass grips more than natural grass, and FG studs, long and few in number, dig further into the rubber and sand infill. That can lock your foot during turns and changes of direction, increasing the load on your knee and ankle. What is more, the more abrasive surface wears the studs down quickly.

Many manufacturers offer hybrid FG/AG versions that work reasonably well on both surfaces, but if you nearly always play on artificial grass, AG boots are the most sensible choice.

## Dirt pitches

There are still dirt pitches in Spain, especially in regional leagues and in rural areas. Dirt is usually hard and sometimes uneven. TF or multi-stud boots, with lots of small rubber studs, give good grip and cushioning, and spread the pressure better than long studs, which can be uncomfortable and increase the risk of twisting an ankle.

## Size and fit

The fit of a football boot is different from that of a running shoe. You are looking for a snug fit that lets you feel the ball and stops your foot moving around inside the boot, but without being so tight that it hurts.

Some guidelines:

- **Try them on with the football socks** you usually wear, which are thicker than everyday socks.
- **Leave little room at the toe**: half a finger's width at most. A boot that is too big lets your foot slide inside and causes blisters.
- **Pay attention to the width.** Lasts vary a lot between brands and models. If you have wide feet, look for models with a roomier last.
- **The heel should be held firmly.**

Leather boots mould themselves a little to your foot with use; synthetic ones hardly change. Bear that in mind when choosing the fit.

## Materials

- **Natural leather**, such as kangaroo leather or calfskin: soft, comfortable, adapts to the foot and gives a good feel for the ball. It needs more care and can absorb water.
- **Synthetic materials**: lighter, more water-resistant and easy to clean. They have become much more comfortable.
- **Boots with a "sock"**, that is, an elastic collar that comes up over the ankle: they feel snug, although they do not protect the ankle against sprains.

## Boots for amateur players

Top-of-the-range boots use very light materials and designs aimed at maximum performance, but they tend to be less durable. For an amateur who plays once or twice a week, mid-range boots offer a very reasonable balance of comfort, durability and price. What matters most is that the sole suits your surface and that the boot is comfortable.

## Boots for children

In youth football, feet grow fast and it is tempting to buy boots one or two sizes too big "so that they last". It is a mistake: a big boot lets the foot move inside, causes blisters and makes ball control worse. Leave a centimetre of room at the toe at most and check the size every few months. For the youngest children, Velcro fastenings make it easier for them to put their boots on themselves. And if the child plays on different pitches during the week, multi-stud boots are usually the most versatile and safest option. Hand-me-down boots are fine as long as the studs and sole still keep their shape and there are no open seams that rub.

## Socks and insoles

Socks matter too. Football socks, thicker and longer, protect the skin from the shin pads; many players also wear socks with grip dots on the sole, which reduce the foot sliding inside the boot. If you use custom insoles, take them with you when you try on new boots: they take up space and can change the right size.

## Care and maintenance

- **Clean off the mud after every match** with a brush and lukewarm water.
- **Do not dry them next to a radiator or in direct sun**: heat warps the materials and weakens the glues. Stuff them with newspaper to absorb the moisture.
- **Loosen them when you take them off**, without treading on the heel.
- **Check the wear on the studs.** Very worn studs lose grip and increase the risk of slipping.

## Shin pads

Although they are not part of the boot, shin pads are essential. They protect the shinbone from knocks and are compulsory in official competitions. Choose some that cover the shin well and do not move during play.

## Footwear and injuries

Several common problems among amateur footballers are linked to footwear:

- **Ankle sprains and knee injuries during turns**, when the sole grips too much and the foot stays locked while the body keeps turning. It is more likely with long studs on firm surfaces or artificial grass.
- **Overload in the sole of the foot and the heel**, with very stiff-soled boots and little cushioning on hard ground.
- **Blisters and damaged toenails**, from the wrong size or from wearing new boots for the first time in a long match.
- **Pain in the metatarsals**, when the studs are concentrated under weight-bearing areas and the ground is hard.

It is no coincidence that many injuries appear when players change surface without changing boots, for example when moving from a grass pitch to a dirt one in a summer tournament. If you notice new aches in your feet or knees after changing footwear or pitch, consider your boots as a possible cause.

## How to break in new boots

New boots need a period of adaptation, especially leather ones:

1. **Wear them at home first** for a while, with your playing socks, to check that there are no pressure points.
2. **Take them to a light training session** before a match.
3. **Alternate with your old ones** for a week or two if possible.
4. **Protect any rubbing areas** with plasters if you notice that some part pinches at first.

Wearing new boots straight away in a ninety-minute match is an almost certain way to end up with blisters.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use the same boots on every pitch?

If you play on very different surfaces, ideally you should have two pairs: one for natural grass and another for artificial grass or dirt. If you can only have one pair, choose according to the surface you play on most often.

### How often should you replace your boots?

It depends on how much you use them and on the surface. On artificial grass, soles wear out faster. When the studs are very worn, the boot is damaged or it starts to hurt, it is time to replace it.

### Do expensive boots prevent injuries?

Not necessarily. What reduces the risk is using the right sole for the surface, the correct size and, above all, good physical preparation.

## In short

Before buying boots, ask yourself where you are going to play. For firm natural grass, FG; for soft, muddy ground, SG; for artificial grass, AG; for hard surfaces or dirt, TF; for indoor courts, an indoor sole. With the right sole and a good fit, you will play more comfortably and more safely.
