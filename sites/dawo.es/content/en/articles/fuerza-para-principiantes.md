For a long time, strength training was associated with bodybuilders and gyms full of mirrors. Today we know that it is one of the most valuable tools for anyone's health, whatever their age or sport. The World Health Organization's recommendations include, as well as aerobic activity, at least two days a week of muscle-strengthening activities that work the major muscle groups.

Strength improves your ability to handle everyday tasks, protects your joints, helps to maintain bone mass, reduces the risk of injury in other sports and improves body composition. And, contrary to what many people believe, you do not need to spend hours in the gym or lift enormous weights to notice its benefits.

This article explains the five basic movements on which almost any well-designed strength routine is based. Mastering them is the best starting point.

## Think in movements, not muscles

Many gym programmes are organised by muscle: one day chest, another back, another legs. For a beginner, it is more useful to think in **movement patterns**, the fundamental actions the body performs in daily life and in sport:

1. **Squat:** bending the hips and knees to go down and come back up.
2. **Hip hinge:** tilting the trunk forward from the hips, with a neutral back.
3. **Push:** moving a weight away from the body, forwards or upwards.
4. **Pull:** bringing a weight towards the body, from in front or from above.
5. **Carry and trunk stability:** carrying weight and resisting unwanted movement.

A routine that includes one exercise from each pattern works the whole body in a balanced way.

## 1. The squat

The squat is the movement of sitting down and standing up. We do it dozens of times a day without thinking, and training it improves the strength of the quadriceps, glutes and adductors.

### How to do it

- Feet shoulder-width apart or slightly wider, toes turned slightly out.
- Go down by taking your hips back and down, as if you were about to sit on a chair.
- Your knees follow the direction of your feet, without caving inwards.
- Your back stays in a neutral position, with your chest open.
- Go down as far as you can while keeping good technique and come up by pushing the floor away.

### Progression

1. Squat to a chair or a bench: you sit down and stand up.
2. Unsupported bodyweight squat.
3. Squat holding a dumbbell or kettlebell in front of your chest, called the goblet squat.
4. Later on, the barbell squat, preferably learnt under a coach's supervision.

## 2. The hip hinge

The hinge is the movement of leaning forward by bending at the hips, not the back. It is the movement we use to pick something up from the floor correctly, and it works the glutes, hamstrings and back muscles.

### How to do it

- Feet hip-width apart, knees slightly bent.
- Push your hips back, as if you wanted to close a door with your bottom, while your trunk tilts forward.
- Your back stays straight and neutral throughout the movement.
- Return to the starting position by driving your hips forward and squeezing your glutes.

### Progression

1. Hinge with a broomstick along your back, touching your head, upper back and sacrum. If the stick loses any of those points of contact, your back is rounding.
2. Glute bridge on the floor.
3. Romanian deadlift with dumbbells.
4. Deadlift with a kettlebell or a barbell.

## 3. The push

Pushing exercises work the chest, shoulders and triceps. They divide into horizontal pushing, forwards, and vertical pushing, upwards.

### Basic exercise: the press-up

- Hands slightly wider than your shoulders.
- Your body forms a straight line from head to heels.
- Lower your chest towards the floor with your elbows at about 45 degrees to your trunk, without flaring them out completely.
- Come up by pushing the floor away.

### Progression

1. Press-ups with your hands against a wall.
2. Press-ups with your hands on a table or a bench.
3. Press-ups on your knees.
4. Full press-ups.
5. Dumbbell bench press and overhead press.

## 4. The pull

Pulling exercises work the back and biceps. They are essential for balancing out the effects of spending many hours sitting with your shoulders rounded forward.

### Basic exercise: the row

- With a dumbbell, rest one hand and one knee on a bench, with your back straight.
- Pull the dumbbell towards your hip, taking your elbow back and drawing your shoulder blade towards your spine.
- Lower it under control.

### Progression

1. Row with a resistance band fixed to a door.
2. Dumbbell row.
3. Inverted row under a sturdy table or with rings.
4. Band-assisted pull-ups and, later on, pull-ups.

## 5. Carrying and trunk stability

The trunk is not just for doing sit-ups. Its main job is to stabilise the spine and transfer force between the legs and arms.

### Basic exercises

- **Front plank:** resting on your forearms and toes, body straight, for 20 to 40 seconds.
- **Farmer's walk:** walk holding a heavy dumbbell or kettlebell in each hand, with your body upright, for 20 to 30 metres.
- **Single-arm carry:** the same, but with weight in one hand only, which forces your trunk to resist leaning to the side.

## A starter routine

Two or three times a week, on non-consecutive days:

| Exercise | Sets | Repetitions |
|---|---|---|
| Goblet squat or squat to a chair | 3 | 8–12 |
| Dumbbell Romanian deadlift or glute bridge | 3 | 8–12 |
| Press-ups (in the variation that suits your level) | 3 | 6–12 |
| Dumbbell or band row | 3 | 8–12 per side |
| Farmer's walk | 3 | 20–30 metres |
| Front plank | 3 | 20–40 seconds |

Rest for one to two minutes between sets. Start with a weight that lets you complete every repetition with good technique, leaving two or three repetitions "in the tank".

## How to progress

The key principle of strength training is **progressive overload**: to keep improving, the body needs a slightly greater stimulus over time. You can progress in several ways:

1. Add repetitions within the suggested range.
2. When you complete every set at the top of the range, increase the weight slightly.
3. Move on to a harder variation of the exercise.
4. Control the lowering phase better by making it slower.

Do not try to progress on everything at once or every week. With consistency, the gains come.

## Your first few weeks, session by session

So that the routine does not stay on paper, it helps to know what to expect from each stage:

| Weeks | Goal | How to do it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 and 2 | learn the movements | two sessions, two sets per exercise, very comfortable weights and attention to technique |
| 3 and 4 | gain confidence | three sessions, three sets, weights that leave two or three repetitions to spare |
| 5 to 8 | start progressing | apply double progression: more repetitions first, then more weight |

Write down the weights and repetitions at each session: that way you will know exactly what to try next time. The article on [sets and reps](/articulos/series-y-repeticiones/) explains how to adjust these variables as you advance, and if you prefer to train without leaving home, the guide to [training at home with no equipment](/articulos/entrenar-en-casa-sin-material/) offers a complete alternative.

## Technique comes first

At first, the priority is learning to do the movements well. Filming yourself, using a mirror or, better still, having a few sessions with a qualified coach helps a lot. Correct technique lets you progress for years with little risk of injury.

## Common myths

- **"Strength training makes you huge."** Gaining a lot of muscle mass takes years of specific training and a diet designed for it. For most people, the result is a stronger, more toned body, not a bulky one.
- **"Women should not lift weights."** Strength training is just as beneficial for women as for men, including protecting bone mass.
- **"Lifting weights is bad for your joints."** Done well and progressively, strength training protects the joints.
- **"You have to train to exhaustion."** It is not necessary. Leaving a repetition or two in the tank is enough to progress and reduces the risk of injury.

## Recovery

Muscles get stronger during the rest between sessions. That is why it is a good idea to leave at least one day between workouts that train the same muscle groups, get enough sleep and eat enough protein, found in pulses, eggs, fish, meat, dairy or nuts.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to go to a gym?

No. With a set of adjustable dumbbells, a resistance band and your own body weight you can train every pattern at home. A gym offers more variety of equipment, but it is not essential.

### Strength or cardio?

Both. Aerobic activity and strength training complement each other. Ideally, combine them over the week.

### How long until I see results?

Many people feel stronger in the first few weeks, largely because their coordination improves. Visible changes in body composition usually take a few months of consistency.

## In short

Squat, hinge, push, pull and carry. Five movements, two or three sessions a week and patient progression are enough to build a base of strength that improves health and performance in any sport. Start small, look after your technique and let time do its work.
