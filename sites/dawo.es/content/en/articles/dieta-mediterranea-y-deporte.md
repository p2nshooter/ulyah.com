Whenever healthy eating comes up, the Mediterranean diet appears again and again as the reference. It is not a recent fashion: it is the traditional eating pattern of the countries around the Mediterranean, Spain among them, and it has been studied for decades for its link with a lower rate of cardiovascular disease and better general health. UNESCO recognised it as Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, not just for its foods but for its way of eating: sharing the table, cooking at home and making the most of seasonal produce.

For anyone who does sport, the Mediterranean diet has an extra advantage: it naturally provides the energy, nutrients and variety that an active body needs. This article explains its principles and how to adapt it to the life of an amateur athlete.

## What the Mediterranean diet is

Rather than a diet with strict rules, it is an **eating pattern** with these features:

- **Plenty of plant foods:** vegetables, fruit, pulses, cereals, nuts and seeds.
- **Olive oil**, especially extra virgin, as the main fat for cooking and dressing.
- **Fish and seafood** often, several times a week.
- **Eggs, poultry and dairy**, above all yoghurt and cheese, in moderate amounts.
- **Red meat and processed meats**, rarely.
- **Water** as the main drink.
- **Seasonal, local produce**, with little processing.
- **Home cooking** and shared meals, with time to eat.

The Mediterranean diet pyramid also includes daily physical activity and rest as part of the lifestyle.

## Why it suits sport

### Good-quality carbohydrates

Bread, rice, pasta, potatoes, pulses and fruit provide the carbohydrates that the body uses as its main fuel during exercise. In the traditional Mediterranean diet, these foods are present at every meal.

### Varied proteins

Pulses, fish, eggs, dairy and, to a lesser extent, meat provide good-quality protein for muscle recovery. Combining pulses with cereals, as in lentils with rice, provides complete plant protein.

### Healthy fats

Olive oil, nuts and oily fish provide unsaturated fats and omega-3 fatty acids, linked to good cardiovascular health.

### Vitamins, minerals and antioxidants

The abundance of fruit, vegetables and pulses covers the vitamins and minerals that matter for athletes, such as potassium, magnesium, iron and vitamin C, as well as plant compounds with antioxidant properties.

### Hydration

Many Mediterranean dishes, such as gazpacho, salmorejo, soups, vegetable stews and fresh fruit, contribute to hydration, which is especially valuable in summer.

## How to adapt it if you train

The Mediterranean diet is an excellent base, but anyone who trains regularly can adjust a few things:

### Adjust the amounts to your activity

On days of long or intense training, it is a good idea to increase your portions of carbohydrate-rich foods: a little more bread, rice, pasta or pulses. On rest days, portions can be somewhat smaller.

### Spread your protein

Including a source of protein at each main meal helps recovery: eggs at breakfast, pulses or fish at lunch, yoghurt as an afternoon snack, fish or eggs at dinner.

### Plan meals around training

As another article in this section explains, a carbohydrate-rich meal two or three hours before training and a complete meal afterwards help you perform and recover. Mediterranean dishes are perfect for this: a pasta salad before an afternoon of padel, a plate of lentils after a morning on the bike.

## An example weekly menu

This is only a guide. Amounts should be adapted to each person.

| Day | Lunch | Dinner |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Lentils with vegetables and rice; salad; fruit | Baked hake with potatoes; tomato salad |
| Tuesday | Pasta with tomato, tuna and olives; fruit | Courgette omelette; bread; yoghurt |
| Wednesday | Chickpeas with spinach; bread; an orange | Grilled salmon with sautéed vegetables |
| Thursday | Rice with chicken and vegetables; salad | Gazpacho or vegetable soup; scrambled eggs with mushrooms |
| Friday | Beans with vegetables; wholemeal bread; fruit | Grilled sardines; pisto (Spanish ratatouille); bread |
| Saturday | Seafood paella; salad | A hearty salad with fresh cheese, walnuts and pulses |
| Sunday | Roast chicken with potatoes and peppers | Fish soup; bread; fruit |

Breakfasts and snacks can include wholemeal bread with tomato and olive oil, fruit, yoghurt, nuts, oats or eggs.

## Four quick dishes for after training

Mediterranean cooking does not have to be slow. These ideas take less than twenty minutes and combine carbohydrates, protein and vegetables:

1. **Warm chickpea salad:** tinned cooked chickpeas, tomato, cucumber, onion, a hard-boiled egg, tuna or fresh cheese, olive oil and lemon. With bread, a complete meal.
2. **Pasta with vegetables and sardines:** wholemeal pasta, sautéed courgette and pepper, a tin of sardines in oil and a little parsley.
3. **Stir-fried rice with egg and vegetables:** use rice cooked the day before, stir-fry it with chopped vegetables and add a scrambled egg at the end.
4. **Wholemeal toast with tomato, avocado and egg**, with a piece of fruit and a yoghurt.

Keeping cooked pulses, tinned fish, pasta, rice, crushed tomatoes and nuts in the cupboard lets you prepare balanced meals even on busy days.

## Eating out without leaving the pattern

Many athletes eat out every day because of work. The set lunch menu (*menú del día*), so common in Spain, can be a good ally if you choose wisely:

- **As a first course**, pulses, salads, grilled vegetables, gazpacho or vegetable soup.
- **As a main course**, grilled or baked fish or meat, rather than fried or battered.
- **As a side**, boiled or baked potatoes, rice or salad, instead of chips every day.
- **For dessert**, fruit or yoghurt more often than sweets.
- **To drink**, water.

It is not about giving up the dishes you like, but about making your choice resemble traditional food on most days.

## A Mediterranean store cupboard for athletes

With a well-stocked cupboard, eating this way is quick even in busy weeks:

- **Carbohydrates:** rice, pasta, oats, couscous and good bread, often wholegrain.
- **Pulses:** dried for weekends and cooked in jars for days when you are in a hurry.
- **Easy proteins:** eggs, plain yoghurt, fresh cheese, tinned tuna, sardines and mackerel.
- **From the freezer:** fish, chopped vegetables and some fruit for smoothies.
- **Fats:** extra virgin olive oil and unsalted nuts.
- **Fresh seasonal produce:** vegetables and fruit from the market, bought once or twice a week.
- **For flavour:** garlic, onion, crushed tomatoes, spices and herbs.

With these ingredients you can make complete dishes for before or after training in a few minutes.

## Myths and nuances

- **"The Mediterranean diet is fattening because it has lots of oil and bread."** Olive oil is high in calories, so it is best used in moderation, but as part of a pattern rich in vegetables and combined with physical activity, the Mediterranean diet is linked to good weight control. The problem is not bread, but excess and ultra-processed products.
- **"What we eat in Spain today is the Mediterranean diet."** Not always. Consumption of ultra-processed foods, pastries and sugary drinks has risen, and consumption of pulses and vegetables has fallen in many homes. Going back to traditional dishes is the best way to recover it.
- **"Wine is an essential part of it."** Although wine is part of the tradition, there is no need to include alcohol. For health and sporting performance, the less alcohol the better.

## Practical tips

1. **Cook pulses at least three times a week.** A jar of cooked chickpeas lets you make a meal in ten minutes.
2. **Always keep fruit in sight** and take it with you as a snack.
3. **Use extra virgin olive oil** for dressing and cooking, in moderation.
4. **Plan fish** two or three times a week, including oily fish: sardines, mackerel, anchovies, salmon.
5. **Shop at markets** and choose seasonal produce.
6. **Cut down on ultra-processed foods**: pastries, cured meats, sugary drinks, ready meals.
7. **Eat sitting down and without rushing** whenever you can.

## Common mistakes athletes make

- **Focusing only on protein** and neglecting vegetables, fruit and pulses.
- **Regularly replacing meals with bars and shakes.**
- **Avoiding bread and pasta** out of fear of carbohydrates, which usually means less energy for training.
- **Skipping breakfast** on morning training days.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it suitable for vegetarian athletes?

Yes. The Mediterranean diet is very rich in plant foods, and pulses, nuts, eggs and dairy make it possible to cover protein needs. Vegans need to pay special attention to vitamin B12 and, in some cases, to other nutrients, with a professional's advice.

### Do I need supplements if I follow a Mediterranean diet?

Most amateur athletes do not. Some situations, such as iron or vitamin D deficiency, need a medical assessment.

### Can I follow it if I often eat out?

Yes. The *menú del día* in Spain usually includes pulses, vegetables, fish and fruit. Choosing your dishes well lets you keep to the pattern.

## In short

The Mediterranean diet offers everything an active body needs: good-quality carbohydrates, varied proteins, healthy fats, vitamins and minerals, and a way of eating that is enjoyable and sustainable. For the amateur athlete, there is no need to invent anything new: simply go back to traditional cooking and adjust the amounts to your activity.
