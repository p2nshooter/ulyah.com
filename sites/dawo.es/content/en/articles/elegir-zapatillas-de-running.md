Walking into a sports shop or a specialist website to buy running shoes can be overwhelming. There are hundreds of models, new technologies with eye-catching names appear every season and prices range from reasonable to hard to justify. On top of that, there is plenty of contradictory advice: that you need a stability shoe for overpronation, that it is best to run with little cushioning, that carbon plates change everything.

Research on sports footwear gives a fairly reassuring conclusion: for most amateur runners, **the most important criterion is comfort**. Shoes that feel comfortable from the very first moment are very probably the right ones for you. From there, a few technical aspects help to fine-tune your choice.

## Comfort as the first filter

A well-known approach proposed by biomechanics researchers suggests that runners choose their footwear based on how comfortable it feels, because the body tends to prefer whatever lets it move with its natural pattern. Beyond the details, the idea is simple and practical: if a shoe bothers you in the shop, it will not get better with the kilometres.

When you try shoes on, pay attention to these sensations:

- **Your foot should not feel squashed** anywhere, especially at the toes and over the instep.
- **The heel should be held** without lifting when you walk or jog.
- **There should be no seams or overlays that rub.**
- **Your footstrike should feel stable** when you run a few metres, without the impression that your foot is rolling to one side.

If the shop allows it, jog a little in them. If you are buying online, check the returns policy first and try the shoes on at home on a clean surface.

## The right size

One of the most common mistakes is buying running shoes in the same size as your everyday shoes. During a run your foot swells slightly and slides forward inside the shoe with each stride, especially on downhills. That is why it is best to leave some room at the front:

- **About a finger's width** between your longest toe and the end of the shoe.
- **Try them on in the afternoon**, when your feet are slightly more swollen.
- **Wear the socks you run in.**

Black toenails after a long run are usually a sign of a shoe that is too short or a poor fit.

Width matters too. Some people with a wide forefoot feel uncomfortable in narrow models, even if the length is right. Some brands offer wide-fit versions of their most popular models.

## Cushioning: more is not always better

Cushioning is the midsole's ability to absorb part of the impact. Today there are shoes with very little cushioning, the so-called minimalist shoes, and others with very thick, soft soles.

Scientific evidence has not shown that more cushioning means fewer injuries. What we do know is that the body adjusts the way it lands according to the surface and the footwear. With a very soft shoe, we sometimes land harder without realising it.

For a beginner, medium cushioning is usually the most sensible choice. Minimalist shoes need a very gradual adaptation and are not recommended for starting out. Highly cushioned shoes can be comfortable for long, easy runs, but some people find them unstable.

## Drop: the difference in height between heel and toe

The drop is the difference in height, in millimetres, between the heel and the toe area. Traditional shoes usually have a drop of 8 to 12 millimetres; others have 4 or 6, and some, zero.

A low drop shifts a little more load onto the Achilles tendon and calves; a high drop, a little more onto the knee. Neither is better in absolute terms. If you have been running with a particular drop for a while and have no aches, there is no reason to change it. If you want to, the change should be gradual, alternating the new shoes with the old ones for several weeks.

## Pronation: what it is and how much it matters

Pronation is the natural inward roll of the foot after the heel lands. It is a normal shock-absorbing mechanism. For years runners were classified as "overpronators", "neutral" and "supinators", and they were recommended shoes with more or less reinforcement to control that movement.

More recent studies have cast doubt on whether assigning footwear by foot type reduces injuries. That does not mean that stability shoes are no use to anyone: some people feel more comfortable in them, and that is what counts. But you do not need a gait analysis to buy your first running shoes. If you have a history of injuries or recurring aches, a podiatrist or a specialist physiotherapist can give you individual advice.

## The ground you run on

The type of surface matters too:

- **Tarmac and park paths:** road running shoes, with a smoother, more durable rubber outsole.
- **Mountains and trails:** trail shoes, with deeper lugs for grip, a tougher sole and often toe protection. On tarmac, those lugs wear quickly and are less comfortable.
- **Athletics track:** road shoes are perfectly fine for training.

If you mix surfaces, road shoes with a slightly more robust sole can cope with dirt paths without any problem. It is only worth having a specific pair if you often go out into the mountains.

## Carbon plates and racing shoes

In recent years shoes with stiff plates in the midsole, combined with very responsive foams, have become popular. They have helped to improve times in elite races. However, they are designed for running fast and are expensive and less durable than a training model. For an amateur runner who is just starting, they add nothing essential. If you become interested in them one day, keep them for races and train in conventional shoes.

## How long shoes last

There is no exact number of kilometres, but many runners replace their shoes somewhere between 600 and 1,000 kilometres. It depends on the runner's weight, the surface and the model. Some signs that they have reached the end of their useful life:

- The outsole is worn down to the foam.
- The midsole has deep creases and feels "dead".
- You start to notice aches after running that you did not have before.

A useful trick is to note the date you bought them and keep a rough count of the kilometres. If you run a lot, alternating two pairs also lets the foams recover between uses.

## Looking after your shoes

- Do not put them in the washing machine or dry them next to a radiator; heat warps the foams and adhesives.
- If they get wet, take out the insole and stuff them with newspaper.
- Use them only for running. Walking around in them all day wears them out for no reason.

## A practical summary

1. Choose for comfort from the very first minute.
2. Leave a finger's width at the front and try the size in the afternoon.
3. To start with, go for medium cushioning and a conventional drop.
4. Choose a road or trail sole according to where you will run.
5. Be wary of promises that a particular model will prevent all injuries.
6. Replace your shoes when they are worn out, not when the new model comes out.

Shoes are important, but none of them replaces progressive training, rest and strength. A good pair of shoes is one that lets you forget about them while you run.

## Socks and insoles

Socks are part of your footwear too. A technical sock, without thick seams and made of a fabric that wicks sweat away, reduces chafing and blisters, especially on long runs or in hot weather. Cotton socks hold moisture and increase friction.

As for insoles, the ones that come with the shoe are enough for most runners. If you have recurring foot problems, a podiatrist can assess whether you need custom insoles. It is not a good idea to buy generic "corrective" insoles without an assessment first, because they can change the way you run when you do not need them.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a gait analysis to buy running shoes?

It is not essential to start with. Most amateur runners can choose well by going on comfort. A biomechanical analysis can be useful if you have recurring injuries or aches that training does not explain, and in that case it is best carried out by a health professional.

### Can I use running shoes at the gym?

For strength exercises with heavy loads, such as squats or deadlifts, a very cushioned sole reduces stability. For those sessions, a shoe with a flat, firm sole is preferable. For cardio classes or aerobic machines, running shoes are fine.

### Is it better to buy in a shop or online?

A shop lets you try several models and sizes, which is especially useful the first time. Once you know the model and size that suit you, buying online can be more convenient. Bear in mind that brands change their lasts from one year to the next, so it is worth trying them on again when the model is updated.

### Do more expensive shoes prevent injuries?

There is no evidence that price is linked to a lower risk of injury. Price usually reflects lighter materials, more responsive foams or more elaborate designs, but what really protects you is progressive training, rest and strength.
