"How many sets and how many repetitions do I have to do?" It is probably the most asked question by anyone who starts strength training. And the most honest answer is: it depends on your goal, your level and how much time you have. Fortunately, research in sports sciences has clarified this issue a lot in recent years, and today we can give practical and simple guidelines.

This article explains the basic variables of strength training, how to combine them depending on what you are looking for, and how to avoid the most common mistakes.

## The basic variables

- **Repetition:** a complete performance of an exercise, for example lowering and raising a squat.
- **Series:** a group of repetitions in a row, without rest.
- **Load:** the weight that is used.
- **Rest:** the time between sets.
- **Volume:** The total amount of work, usually measured in sets per muscle group per week.
- **Effort intensity:** How close you finish each set to not being able to do one more repetition, that is, to muscle failure.
- **Frequency:** how many times per week you train the same muscle group or movement.

## Repetitions and objectives

For years a very rigid division was repeated: from 1 to 5 repetitions for strength, from 8 to 12 to gain muscle and from 15 onwards for "endurance." Today we know that reality is more flexible:

- **To gain maximum strength**, high loads and low repetitions, approximately 3 to 6, are especially effective, because strength is in part a skill: the body learns to lift heavy loads by lifting them.
- **To gain muscle mass**, it has been seen that you can grow with a wide range of repetitions, from about 6 to 30, as long as the sets end close to failure. The intermediate ranges, from 6 to 15, are usually the most practical: they do not require maximum loads and are not so long that general fatigue prevents finishing the series.
- **For muscular endurance**, longer sets, with light loads, are useful.

For a beginner with general health and fitness goals, a range of **8 to 12 repetitions** is a good starting point: it allows you to learn the technique with moderate loads and produces improvements in strength and muscle mass.

## What weight to use

The right weight is what allows you to complete the planned repetitions with good technique, finishing close to failure but not there. A very useful tool to measure this is **reps in reserve**: how many more reps you could have done at the end of the set.

- **3–4 in reserve:** moderate effort, suitable for learning the technique.
- **1–2 in reserve:** high effort, suitable for most work sets.
- **0 in reserve:** muscle failure. It can be used occasionally in safe exercises, but it is not necessary and increases fatigue.

If you finish a set of 10 reps feeling like you could have done ten more, the weight is too light. If you don't get to 8 with good technique, it's too heavy.

## How many series

### By exercise

Between 2 and 4 series per exercise is normal. For beginners, 2 or 3 sets are usually enough.

### Per muscle group per week

Research suggests that weekly volume influences muscle mass gains, with benefits increasing up to a point. A practical guide:

- **Beginners:** about 6 to 10 sets per week per muscle group produce good improvements.
- **Intermediate:** between 10 and 20 weekly series per muscle group.
- **Above that**, the additional benefits are smaller and the risk of not recovering well increases.

For a beginner, two full body sessions per week with 3 sets of each exercise more than cover these figures.

## How much rest between sets

- **Large, heavy exercises**, such as squats or deadlifts: 2 to 3 minutes.
- **Smaller exercises**, such as dumbbell rows or lateral raises: 1 to 2 minutes.

For years it was thought that little rest was better for gaining muscle. Today we know that rests that are too short reduce performance in subsequent sets. If you're short on time, you can alternate exercises for different muscle groups, such as a squat and push-ups, resting while you work the other group.

## Frequency: how many days per week

Training each muscle group **twice a week** seems more effective than doing it once with the same total volume. For most people, this is achieved with:

- **Two or three full body sessions** per week, or
- **Four sessions divided** into upper body and lower body, for those who have more time and experience.

## Execution speed

Controlling the descent phase, in about two or three seconds, improves technique and safety. The uphill phase can be done with the intention of going fast, although with heavy loads the actual speed will be slow. There is no need to obsessively count seconds; Just avoid "dropping" and bouncing.

## The progression

To continue improving, the stimulus must increase over time. A simple way is **double progression**:

1. Choose a rep range, for example 8 to 12.
2. Start with a weight that allows you to do 8 repetitions in all sets.
3. Each session, try to add one or two repetitions.
4. When you complete 12 repetitions on all sets, increase the weight slightly and start again from 8.

This method is simple, works for a long time, and prevents weight gain too soon.

## Example routine for beginners

Two or three days a week:

| Exercise | series | Repetitions | In reserve |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goblet squat | 3 | 8–12 | 2–3 |
| Romanian deadlift | 3 | 8–12 | 2–3 |
| Dumbbell bench press | 3 | 8–12 | 2 |
| Dumbbell row | 3 | 8–12 per side | 2 |
| Backward lunge | 2 | 8–10 per leg | 2–3 |
| Side plank | 2 | 20–30 s per side | — |

## The approximation series

Before the work series of the large exercises, it is advisable to do one or two approach series. They don't count towards the volume, but they prepare the joints, rehearse the technique and allow you to check how you feel that day. A simple scheme for a squat that you are going to work with at 40 kg:

| Series | Weight | Repetitions |
|---|---|---|
| Approach 1 | single bar or 20 kg | 8 to 10, very controlled |
| Approach 2 | about 30kg | 4 to 5 |
| Work Series | 40kg | those planned |

For small exercises at the end of the session, it is usually enough to start directly with the working weight, because the muscles are already warm.

## How to target training

A simple registration is the most effective and cheapest progress tool. A notebook or a note on your mobile phone with one line per exercise is enough:

- **Date and fiscal year.**
- **Weight and repetitions of each series**, for example: "Dumbbell row, 16 kg: 12, 11, 10."
- **Reruns in reserve** from the latest series, or a note about how you've been feeling.

When you start the next session, look back at what you did last time and try to improve it a little: one more repetition, or the same work with better technique.

## If you only have half an hour

With little time, prioritize exercises that work more muscles at once, such as squats, Romanian deadlifts, presses, and rows, and reduce them to two sets per exercise. Alternating upper and lower body exercises allows you to rest one while you work the other without lengthening the session. Three short, well-done sessions each week go a long way than one long session every now and then. ## Frequent errors

1. **Change your routine every week.** Progression requires consistency in the exercises for several weeks.
2. **Always train to failure.** It generates fatigue without proportional benefits.
3. **Rest too little** between sets of large exercises.
4. **Increase the weight while sacrificing technique.**
5. **Do not record training.** Without recording weights and repetitions it is difficult to know if you are progressing.

## Download weeks

Every six to eight weeks of continuous training, some people benefit from a lighter week, reducing the number of sets or the weight. It helps to recover accumulated fatigue and come back stronger. For beginners, it is not always necessary if the volume is moderate, but it is appropriate if you notice persistent fatigue or stagnation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it better to do many repetitions with little weight to "define"?

The "definition" depends above all on reducing body fat, something that is achieved with diet and general physical activity. To maintain and gain muscle while losing fat, it is advisable to continue training with loads that require real effort.

### How long should a session last?

Between 40 and 60 minutes is enough for most people. The important thing is the quality of the work, not the duration.

### Can I gain strength over 50 years old?

Yes. Older people respond very well to strength training, with significant improvements in strength, muscle mass and autonomy.

## In summary

To start: two or three full body sessions per week, 2 to 3 sets per exercise, between 8 and 12 repetitions, finishing one or three repetitions from failure, resting as necessary and gradually increasing the repetitions and weight. With these simple guidelines, strength and muscle will come, and they will do so sustainably.
