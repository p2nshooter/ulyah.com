Swimming is one of the most complete exercises that exist: it works the entire body, improves cardiovascular capacity, has little impact on the joints and can be practiced at any age. However, many adults never learned to swim well, or they did as children and haven't been back in the pool in decades. For them, entering a swim lane surrounded by swimmers coming and going can be intimidating.

The good news is that it is never too late to learn or to return. Municipal swimming pools throughout Spain offer courses for adults of all levels, from people afraid of water to those who want to perfect their technique. This article explains how to get started.

## If you are afraid of water

Fear of water is more common than it seems among adults, often due to a bad experience in childhood. It is not an insurmountable obstacle, but it should be approached with patience:

- **Start in the area where you stand.** Feeling the ground gives security.
- **Look for a course for adult beginners**, with instructors accustomed to working on fear.
- **Get familiar with the water little by little:** walk, stick your face in, blow bubbles, float holding on to the edge.
- **Learn to float** before swimming. Discovering that the body floats with lungs full of air greatly changes the relationship with water.
- **Do not compare yourself** with other students. Each person has their own rhythm.

## Breathing, the key to everything

Most adults who "drown" while swimming do not have a fitness problem, but rather a breathing problem. On land we breathe automatically; In the water, breathing must be coordinated with movement and face position.

Some exercises to learn:

1. **Bubbles:** Standing in the low area, take air through your mouth, put your face in the water and release the air slowly through your nose and mouth, making bubbles. Repeat many times.
2. **Rhythmic breathing:** Holding the edge, alternate a breath out of the water with an exhalation inside, continuously.
3. **Head turn:** In the front crawl position, turn your head to one side to take a breath, without lifting it, and put your face back in to release the air.

The most common mistake is holding your breath underwater. The air must be released into the water so that, when you turn your head, you only have to inhale.

## Body position

In the water, swimming well starts with a good position:

- **Body as horizontal as possible**, like a board.
- **Head aligned with the trunk**, looking at the bottom of the pool in front crawl, not forward.
- **High hip.** If the head rises, the hips and legs sink and slow down.

Practicing the slide, pushing off the wall with your arms stretched out in front, helps you feel this position.

## The first styles

### Crawl

It is the fastest and most efficient style, and the most common in swimming pools. Combine alternating strokes, continuous kick and lateral breathing. It is the most difficult to coordinate at first because of breathing.

### Back

You swim face up, which makes breathing easier. It's a good style for beginners who feel overwhelmed with their face in the water. You have to watch the direction so as not to collide with the cork or the wall.

### Fathom

It is the style that many people learned as children. It allows you to swim with your head out of the water, although technically you breathe in each cycle by lifting your head. The breaststroke kick can load the knees if not done right.

### Butterfly

It is the most technically and physically demanding style. It is not necessary to swim regularly.

To begin with, the most practical thing is usually to combine backstroke and breaststroke while learning the front crawl.

## The basic material

- **Comfortable swimsuit** that does not move.
- **Cap**, mandatory in most pools.
- **Glasses** that fit well without being tight.
- **Flip flops** to move around the facilities.
- **Board and pull-buoy**, which are usually available at the pool, to practice legs and arms separately.
- **Short fins**, useful for improving position and kick.

## Pool etiquette

Swimming in a shared street has its rules, which facilitate coexistence:

- **Choose the appropriate street at your pace**: many swimming pools mark slow, medium and fast streets.
- **Swim on the right** of the street, in a circle, going one way and returning the other.
- **To overtake**, do so on the left and only if there is space, preferably in the central part of the length.
- **If someone wants to pass you**, when you reach the wall, move around the corner to let them pass.
- **Do not stand in the middle of the street** or block the wall.
- **Shower before** entering the water.

## A six-week starter plan

For someone who already floats and moves, even if it is with difficulty. Two or three weekly sessions of 30 to 45 minutes.

| Week | Main content |
|---|---|
| 1 | Bubble breathing, sliding, legs with board: 8 × 25 m with rest |
| 2 | Legs with board and fins, back: 8 × 25 m; lateral breathing attached to the edge |
| 3 | Crawl with fins: 6 × 25 m; back: 4 × 25m |
| 4 | Front crawl without fins, breathing every 2 or 3 strokes: 6 × 25 m; breaststroke: 4 × 25 m |
| 5 | Crawl: 8 × 25 m and 2 × 50 m; breaks of 30 to 45 seconds |
| 6 | Crawl: 4 × 50 m and 1 × 100 m; back or breaststroke to recover |

Rest as much as you need between lengths. At first it is normal to take long pauses; As the weeks go by, the breaks shorten themselves.

## Benefits of swimming

- **Low-impact aerobic exercise**, suitable for overweight people, joint problems or recovering from injuries.
- **Strengthening** of the back, shoulders and trunk.
- **Improved mobility.**
- **Relaxing effect** for many people.

Since swimming does not involve impact or weight bearing, it is advisable to combine it with strength exercise and weight-bearing activities, such as walking, to take care of bone health.

## Measure your progress

In a 25-meter pool, four lengths are 100 meters. Counting lengths by heart is more difficult than it seems when you concentrate on breathing, so many swimmers count on their fingers, with the pieces of an abacus placed on the edge, or with a sports watch. Two simple pieces of information allow you to see how you are improving: the total distance of each session and the time it takes you to swim 100 meters at a comfortable pace. Write them down every week. The normal thing is to first increase the distance you can swim in a row and, later, lower the time per 100 meters.

## Take care of your skin, hair and ears

Chlorinated water dries out the skin and hair. Shower with fresh water and mild soap when you get out, apply moisturizer, and wear a hat to protect your hair. Dry your ears well with the towel, tilting your head: the moisture left in the canal favors external otitis, the so-called "swimmer's ear." If you have ear discomfort or pain, consult your doctor before returning to the water. Flip-flops and good drying between the toes prevent fungus, which is common in locker rooms and common showers.

## Up ahead: open water

Swimming in the sea, a lake or a swamp is very different from swimming in a pool: there are no walls to rest on, the water can be cold, there are currents and waves, and orientation is complicated. If you feel like taking the plunge, start with organized groups or clubs with supervision, swim close to the shore and parallel to it, use a brightly colored swimming buoy so you can be seen and always respect the beach flags: green, swimming allowed; yellow, caution; red, bathing prohibited. And never swim alone.

## Frequent errors

1. **Raise your head** to look forward in front crawl, which sinks your legs.
2. **Hold your breath** underwater.
3. **Kick from the knee** instead of the hip.
4. **Wanting to swim many lengths in a row** from the first day.
5. **Do not wear glasses**, which makes it difficult to put your face in the water comfortably.

## Precautions

- If you have heart disease, respiratory disease or epilepsy, consult your doctor before starting.
- Don't swim alone in open water without experience: the sea, rivers and swamps have very different currents, temperatures and depths than a pool.
- Hydrate yourself: you also sweat in water.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it better to learn in a course or on my own?

A course greatly accelerates learning and avoids acquiring technical errors that are difficult to correct later. For people afraid of water, it is especially recommended.

### How long will it take me to swim 100 meters straight?

It depends on the starting point, but with two or three weekly sessions, many adults achieve it in one or two months.

### Does swimming lose weight?

Swimming consumes energy like any aerobic exercise and can be part of a weight control plan, combined with proper nutrition.

## In summary

Learning to swim or returning to the water as an adult is possible and very rewarding. Starting with breathing and floating, choosing an appropriate course, respecting the rules of the street and progressing little by little turns the pool into a place of well-being. And once you learn it, swimming is a lifelong skill.
