There are joints that go unnoticed until they fail. The hip and the ankle are two of them. We spend many hours sitting, with our hips flexed and our ankles almost immobile inside our shoes, and little by little we lose range of motion without realizing it. The problem appears when we ask them for something else: a deep squat, an uphill run, a change of direction in a game or simply bending down to pick up something from the ground.

The lack of mobility in these two joints does not usually hurt in itself, but it forces other areas to compensate. A stiff ankle can cause the knee to pull inward when going down; A hip with little mobility can cause the lower back to curve excessively. This article explains why they matter, how to check your mobility, and what exercises help improve it.

## Mobility is not the same as flexibility

It is worth clarifying two terms that are often confused:

- **Flexibility** is the ability of a muscle to lengthen. It is measured, for example, by how far the hands reach when trying to touch the feet.
- **Mobility** is the ability to move a joint along its path in an active and controlled manner. It includes flexibility, but also strength and control in those positions.

In sport, mobility is most important: it is not enough to be able to reach a position if you do not have the strength or control to move in it.

## The ankle: dorsiflexion

The key ankle movement for sport is **dorsiflexion**: bringing the knee forward over the foot, with the heel supported. It is the gesture that occurs when descending into a squat, climbing stairs, running uphill or braking.

When dorsiflexion is limited, the body looks for alternatives: lifting the heel, turning the foot outward, or bringing the knee inward. Several investigations have linked reduced dorsiflexion with some knee and ankle problems in athletes.

### Knee to wall test

1. Stand barefoot in front of a wall, with one foot in front.
2. Place your toe about four inches from the wall.
3. Bring your knee forward to touch the wall, without lifting your heel and without your knee going inward.
4. If you touch it easily, move your foot further away and repeat.

As a guideline, many people with good mobility touch the wall with their foot about ten centimeters or more. The most useful thing is to compare both sides: a clear difference between one ankle and another deserves attention, especially if you have had sprains.

## The hip: a range in all directions

The hip is a ball and socket joint that moves in many directions: flexion, extension, internal and external rotation, abduction and adduction. In sports, the following are especially important:

- **The flexion**, necessary for the squat and to raise the knee when running.
- **The extension**, which allows you to bring the leg backwards in the stride. Spending many hours sitting tends to shorten the hip flexors and limit it.
- **Rotation**, essential in changes of direction and in racquet sports.

### Deep squat test

Stand with your feet shoulder-width apart and lower yourself into as deep a squat as possible, with your heels planted and your back reasonably straight. Look at:

- If the heels rise, the ankle is probably limiting.
- If the back rounds too much before reaching the bottom, the hips may be limiting.
- If the knees turn inward, there may be a lack of mobility or control.

### Hip extension test

Lie face up on the edge of a high bed or bench, hug one knee to your chest and let the other leg hang down. If the thigh of the hanging leg is not horizontal or overhanging, the hip flexors may be shortened.

## A ten minute daily routine

Mobility improves with regularity rather than intensity. Ten minutes a day, or before training, gives better results than an hour once a week.

### For the ankle

1. **Knee to wall:** From the test position, bring your knee toward the wall and back, 10 to 15 repetitions per side, without straining.
2. **Lung Swing:** In a low lunge position, with your back knee on the ground, bring your weight toward your front knee while keeping your heel supported. 10 slow repetitions per side.
3. **Heel raises with slow descent on a step:** 2 sets of 10 to 12, lowering the heel below the step in about three seconds.

### For the hip

4. **Lord Hip Flexor Stretch:** Back knee supported, squeeze the glute of that leg and bring the hips forward slightly. Hold for 30 to 45 seconds per side, breathing deeply.
5. **Hip rotations in 90-90 position:** Sitting on the floor with one leg in front and one to the side, both knees at 90 degrees, rotate your legs to change sides without using your hands if you can. 8 to 10 changes.
6. **Assisted Deep Squat:** Hold onto a door frame or post and lower yourself into a deep squat, keeping your heels supported. Stay 30 to 60 seconds, moving gently from side to side.
7. **Active butterfly:** sitting with the soles of your feet together, try to bring your knees closer to the ground with your muscles, without pushing with your hands. 10 repetitions.

### To integrate

8. **Stride with rotation:** take a stride forward, rest the hand opposite the front foot on the ground and rotate the trunk, bringing the other arm towards the sky. 5 per side.

## Mobility requires strength

Gaining range of motion without gaining strength in that range has limited effect. Therefore, in addition to mobility exercises, it is advisable to include strength exercises that work in wide ranges:

- Deep squats with light weight.
- Long strides and backward strides.
- Bulgarian squat.
- Heel raises with full range.

The combination of mobility and strength is what really translates to sport.

## Before or after training

- **Before:** dynamic mobility exercises, in motion, as part of the warm-up. They prepare the joints without subtracting power.
- **After or on days off:** Maintained stretches and longer mobility work, if they help you feel looser.

## The role of footwear and daily life

High-heeled shoes limit ankle dorsiflexion over time. Spending some time barefoot at home, walking on varied surfaces, and getting out of your chair every hour to move around are small habits that help maintain mobility.

## What to prioritize according to your sport

- **Runners:** ankle dorsiflexion and hip extension, which allow an efficient stride and reduce knee compensations.
- **Football, paddle tennis and racquet sports:** hip rotation and adductor mobility, highly required in changes of direction and lateral strides.
- **Cyclists:** hip extension, because the position on the bicycle keeps the hip flexed for hours.
- **Strength in the gym:** hip flexion and dorsiflexion, essential for a deep squat with heels supported.

## Mobility during the office day

If you work seated, a few minutes spread throughout the day are worth more than a long session on the weekend. Get up every hour and do one of these gestures: ten standing heel raises, a hip flexor stretch resting one knee on a chair, some hip rotations seated crossing one ankle over the opposite knee or half a minute in a deep squat holding yourself to the table while you wait for the coffee to be made. They are small habits that prevent rigidity from accumulating.

## Measure your progress

Repeat the knee-to-wall test every month, noting the centimeters on each side, and take a profile photo of yourself in the deep squat under the same conditions. Comparing the figures and the photos will tell you if the routine works and which side you need to insist on more.

## When to consult

If you notice a very marked limitation on one side compared to the other, pain when moving, blockages or a feeling that the joint is "stuck", consult a physiotherapist or doctor. Some limitations have structural causes that are not resolved with stretching, and others require specific treatment.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long will it take me to notice improvements?

Many people notice changes in their feeling of looseness within a few weeks of daily practice. Measurable improvements in range of motion usually require a little more time and consistency.

### Is it normal to have one side stiffer than the other?

Yes, it is common, especially if you have had injuries on one side or if your sport is asymmetrical. Put some more work into the more limited side.

### Can I do the routine every day?

Yes. Gentle mobility exercises can be done daily without a problem, as long as they do not cause pain.

## In summary

An ankle that allows the knee to move forward and a hip that moves freely in all directions are the basis of a good squat, efficient running and confident changes of direction. Ten minutes a day of mobility, combined with strength in wide ranges, is enough to notice the difference in any sport.
