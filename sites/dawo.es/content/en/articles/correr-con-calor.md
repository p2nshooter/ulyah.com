In much of Spain, summer brings temperatures that easily pass thirty-five degrees in the afternoon and nights when the thermometer barely drops below twenty-five. For runners, those weeks are a challenge: the same route that felt easy in spring becomes an endurance test, your heart rate soars and your legs seem to weigh twice as much.

It is not just a feeling. Heat changes the way your body works during exercise, and understanding it helps you train sensibly, without losing fitness and, above all, without putting your health at risk. This article explains what happens when you run in the heat, how to adapt and which warning signs must never be ignored.

## What happens to your body when you run in the heat

When you run, your muscles produce a lot of heat. To avoid overheating, your body sends more blood to the skin and produces sweat, which cools the surface of the body as it evaporates. In mild temperatures, this system works efficiently.

When it is hot, the system comes under strain:

- **Blood is shared between the muscles and the skin.** Your heart has to beat faster to meet both demands. That is why, at the same speed, your heart rate is higher in summer.
- **You lose more water and salts in your sweat.** If they are not replaced, your blood volume falls and the effort becomes even harder.
- **Humidity makes things worse.** When the air is very humid, sweat evaporates less well and stops cooling you. Thirty degrees on the Mediterranean coast with high humidity can be more demanding than thirty-five inland with dry air.

The practical result is that performance drops. It is normal to run quite a lot more slowly in summer at the same perceived effort. Trying to keep up your spring paces is one of the most common and most dangerous mistakes.

## Choosing the right time

The most effective measure against the heat is the simplest: **go out when it is coolest**. In summer, that usually means first thing in the morning, shortly after sunrise, before the ground has soaked up the heat of the day.

Late evening, even when the sun is already low, can be deceptive: the tarmac and buildings give off heat stored up over hours, and in many cities the temperature barely falls until well into the night. If you have no other option, look for shaded routes, parks with trees or areas by the water.

Avoid the middle of the day, roughly between midday and five in the afternoon, especially on days with a high-temperature warning. Checking the forecasts and warnings of the State Meteorological Agency (AEMET) before planning your week is a good habit.

## Acclimatising takes a few days

Your body adapts to heat if it is exposed to it gradually. During the first week or two of intense heat, it learns to sweat earlier and more, and to maintain its blood volume better. This process is known as acclimatisation.

To encourage it safely:

- **Reduce the volume and intensity** during the first few hot days. Run for less time and more slowly.
- **Build up gradually** the length of your runs over one or two weeks.
- **Do not seek out the heat on purpose** to "get used to it sooner". Acclimatisation comes by itself if you train regularly in summer at the coolest times.

People coming back from a holiday somewhere cool, or returning to exercise after a break, lose part of that adaptation and should start again cautiously.

## Run by feel, not by pace

In summer, the pace on your watch loses its value as a reference. It is much more useful to go by perceived effort or by heart rate.

A practical rule: **run at the effort the session calls for, even if the pace is slower**. If a session calls for an easy run, it should feel easy, even if that means running thirty or forty seconds per kilometre slower than usual. For quality sessions, such as intervals or changes of pace, consider doing them on cooler days or reducing the number of repetitions.

## Hydration before, during and after

Staying well hydrated does not mean drinking litres of water at once, but keeping well hydrated throughout the day and replacing what you lose through exercise.

### Before running

Start your run well hydrated. Drink normally the day before and, one or two hours before running, have a glass or two of water. A simple indicator is the colour of your urine: pale yellow usually means you are well hydrated.

### During the run

For runs of under an hour at an easy pace, many people do not need to drink during exercise if they are well hydrated at the start, although in very hot weather it is advisable to carry water. For longer runs or in high temperatures:

- Carry a handheld bottle or a belt with bottles, or plan a route with water fountains.
- Take small sips regularly, guided by your thirst.
- On runs of more than an hour with a lot of sweating, a drink with mineral salts helps to replace sodium.

### After running

Drink steadily over the following hour. If you have sweated a lot, meals with a little salt or an isotonic drink help to replace the electrolytes you have lost.

An important detail: drinking too much water, far more than you lose, can also be dangerous in very long efforts, because it dilutes the sodium in your blood. For an amateur runner on normal runs it is rare, but it confirms the idea that your guide should be thirst and real need, not a fixed amount.

## Clothing and sun protection

- **Light, technical, light-coloured clothing** that helps sweat to evaporate.
- **A cap or visor** to protect your head and face from direct sun.
- **Sunglasses** if you run in bright light.
- **Sweat-resistant sun cream** on your face, neck, shoulders and arms, applied a few minutes before you go out.

Wetting your head and neck with water during the run brings immediate relief and helps to get rid of heat.

## Warning signs: when to stop

Heat can cause serious problems. It is important to know the warning signs and not try to "tough it out" when they appear:

- **Severe muscle cramps.**
- **Dizziness, feeling faint or blurred vision.**
- **A bad headache**, nausea or vomiting.
- **Very hot skin**, whether dry or flushed, or shivering with goose pimples despite the heat.
- **Confusion, disorientation** or strange behaviour.

If you notice any of these symptoms, stop immediately, find shade, drink cool water if you can swallow without difficulty and cool your body down with water. If the symptoms do not improve quickly, or if confusion appears, it is an emergency: call 112. Heatstroke is a potentially serious condition that needs urgent medical attention.

If you are running with someone and you notice that they are staggering, talking incoherently or have stopped sweating, do not leave them alone and call for help.

## Who should take more care

Some people tolerate heat less well and should be especially careful:

- Older people and those with heart, lung or kidney conditions.
- People taking certain medicines, such as some diuretics or blood-pressure drugs.
- People who are just starting to train or returning after a long break.
- People who are significantly overweight.

If you are in any of these groups, ask your doctor how to adapt your physical activity during the hot months.

## Check the warnings before you go out

The State Meteorological Agency (AEMET) publishes maximum-temperature warnings with a colour code — yellow, orange and red — according to the risk in each area. Checking them the evening before, together with the hourly forecast, helps you decide whether to get up earlier, shorten the session or swap it for an indoor alternative. On orange or red warning days, the sensible thing is not to make intense efforts outdoors.

## If you run at dusk

Running as the sun goes down is a good way to avoid the heat, but you need to be visible: light-coloured clothing, reflective garments or bands, and a small head torch or chest light if the route has dark stretches. Choose familiar, well-lit routes with little traffic, and let someone know where you are going to run.

## Alternatives on the hardest days

There are days when the most sensible thing is not to run outdoors. Instead of missing your training, you can:

- **Swim**, an excellent aerobic activity that also cools you down.
- **Run on a treadmill** in an air-conditioned gym.
- **Do a strength session** at home or in the gym.
- **Swap the run for an early-morning bike ride**, which creates a bit of a breeze.

Skipping a session because of extreme heat will not cost you your fitness. Heatstroke, on the other hand, can keep you away from sport for weeks.

## Summer as a time for maintenance

Many runners use the summer to maintain their aerobic base with easy runs and save the hardest training and personal-best goals for the autumn, when the fun-run calendar gets going. It is a sensible strategy: the heat makes every session cost more, and with kinder temperatures in September and October your body responds strongly.

Running in summer is possible and can be very enjoyable if you respect the timing, listen to your body and forget your watch for a few weeks. The priority is always to get home safely.
