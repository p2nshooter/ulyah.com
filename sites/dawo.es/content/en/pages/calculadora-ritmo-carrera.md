Pace — the minutes it takes you to cover a kilometre — is the most useful reference for an amateur runner: it helps you plan your training, set off at the right speed in a race and see how you are progressing. This calculator works it out from any distance and time, and estimates how long you would take over other distances.

## The sums

**Pace (min/km) = time ÷ distance**

**Speed (km/h) = distance ÷ time in hours**

To estimate the time over another distance we use Riegel's formula, a classic reference: **T2 = T1 × (D2 ÷ D1)^1.06**. The exponent reflects the fact that the longer the distance, the more the pace slows.

## An example

Someone who completes their first 5 km in 32 minutes runs at 6:24 per kilometre, about 9.4 km/h. These are the values the calculator opens with.

| Distance | Estimated time | Pace |
|---|---|---|
| 5 km | 32:00 | 6:24 /km |
| 10 km | 1:06:43 | 6:40 /km |
| Half marathon | 2:27:12 | 6:59 /km |
| Marathon | 5:06:55 | 7:16 /km |

## How to use the result

- **For the race:** start a little slower than the estimate for the first two kilometres and speed up only if you feel good.
- **For training:** easy runs should be clearly slower, at a conversational pace; our guide to [running 5 km non-stop](/articulos/correr-5-km-sin-parar/) explains how to combine them with intervals.
- **To progress:** repeat the test every few weeks on the same route and in similar conditions.

## The limits of the estimate

The formula assumes you have trained for each distance. A good 5K does not guarantee a good marathon if you have not done long runs: over long distances the estimate is usually optimistic for runners with little volume. Heat, hills and wind also slow your pace for the same effort; our guide to [running in the heat](/articulos/correr-con-calor/) explains by how much. To step up in distance, see [from 5K to 10K](/articulos/del-5k-al-10k/).
