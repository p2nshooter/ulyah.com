Few travel experiences equal that of experiencing a great Latin American festival: thousands of dancers in dazzling costumes, bands that play for days, streets covered in flowers or colored sawdust, altars full of candles and food for the dead. The holidays are the time when a city shows what it is: its history, its faith, its mix of indigenous, African and European cultures, and its way of celebrating.

But traveling during the holidays also has its practical side: accommodations that sell out months in advance, prices that multiply, streets closed, crowds and, sometimes, services closed. And many of these celebrations have a deep religious or community meaning that the visitor must respect. In this guide you will see the best-known festivals in the region, how to plan your trip, how to navigate the crowds, and how to participate respectfully.

## Some of the great parties

The dates of many celebrations change every year, especially those linked to the religious calendar, such as carnival and Holy Week. Always confirm them in official sources.

| Party | Place | Approximate time | What distinguishes it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rio de Janeiro Carnival | Brazil | February or March, before Lent | parades of samba schools and blocos in the streets |
| Carnival of Salvador and Olinda | Brazil | February or March | electric trios in Salvador, frevo and maracatu in Olinda and Recife |
| Barranquilla Carnival | Colombia | the days before Ash Wednesday | recognized by UNESCO as intangible heritage |
| Oruro Carnival | Bolivia | February or March | the diablada and other dances, also recognized by UNESCO |
| Festival of the Virgin of Candelaria | Puno, Peru | february | thousands of dancers and musicians from the highlands |
| Holy Week | Antigua Guatemala, Popayán and many cities | March or April | processions and, in Antigua, carpets of sawdust and flowers |
| Inti Raymi | Cusco, Peru | June 24 | celebration of the sun of Inca origin, recreated in Sacsayhuamán |
| June festivities | Brazil, especially the Northeast | june | forró music, bonfires and corn meals |
| Guelaguetza | Oaxaca, Mexico | july | dances from the regions of Oaxaca |
| national holidays | Chile, Mexico, Peru and others | according to each country | fairs, music, food and parades |
| Day of the Dead | Mexico | November 1 and 2 | altars, illuminated cemeteries and offerings |

In addition, each town celebrates its patron saint with its own festivals, often as intense as the big ones, but much less known to tourists.

## Plan: book well in advance

- **Accommodation:** During big holidays, hotels fill up months in advance and prices go up a lot. Book early and with a cancellation policy.
- **Transportation:** flights and buses are also sold out. Consider arriving a few days early and staying a few days after.
- **Tickets:** Some parades and shows have stands or boxes with tickets. Buy them from official channels to avoid fraud.
- **Program:** The holidays usually have several days of activities. Consult the official program to choose the main moments.

## What to expect on the street

- **Crowds:** many streets are completely filled. Define meeting points with your group and agree on what to do if they get separated.
- **Streets closed:** Traffic is diverted and taxis cannot always arrive. Go on foot or by public transport and allow more time for any trip.
- **Noise:** bands, speakers and fireworks for hours. If you are sensitive to noise, bring earplugs.
- **Games with water and foam:** in some carnivals, such as in parts of Bolivia, Ecuador or Peru, it is tradition to get wet with water or foam. Wear clothing that can get wet and protect your phone.
- **Services closed:** Banks, museums and shops may close on main days. Have cash and food on hand.

## Safety in the crowd

- **Take the minimum:** a copy of the document, some cash and your phone safely stored. Leave the rest at the accommodation.
- **Use a fanny pack or interior pockets** and the backpack in front.
- **Hydrate and protect yourself from the sun:** many festivals are in summer or at altitude, such as Candelaria or Inti Raymi. More information in the guide on [altitude sickness](/bitacora/mal-de-altura-soroche/).
- **Moderate alcohol** and do not accept drinks from strangers.
- **Return to accommodation in a group** and on busy streets, or by trusted transportation.

More tips in the guide on [safety in large cities](/bitacora/seguridad-en-grandes-ciudades/).

## Respect: many holidays are sacred

Behind the color and music, many celebrations have a deep religious or spiritual meaning:

- **Holy Week Processions:** are acts of faith. Be silent when the images pass, do not cross in front of the procession and do not step on the carpets of sawdust and flowers in Antigua Guatemala before the procession passes.
- **Day of the Dead:** cemeteries are places of family remembrance. Visit them discreetly, without invading the graves or photographing the families without permission.
- **Patronal dances:** for many dancers they are a promise or a devotion. Treat them with the respect they deserve.
- **Andean ceremonies:** in celebrations such as Inti Raymi or offerings to Pachamama, follow the instructions of the organizers.

The guide on [community tourism and cultural respect](/bitacora/turismo-comunitario-y-respeto-cultural/) expands on these ideas.

## Participate, not just watch

In some festivals visitors can join:

- **Carnival blocos in Brazil:** are groups that walk the streets with music, and most are open to everyone.
- **Comparsas and dance groups:** in some carnivals you can parade with certain groups after registering in advance and getting the costume.
- **Fairs and inns:** during national holidays people dance, eat and celebrate in spaces open to the public.

Find out in advance how to do it and respect the rules of each group.

## The parties that do not appear in the guides

Each town in the region has its calendar of patron saint festivals, agricultural fairs, anniversaries and harvest celebrations. They tend to be more intimate than the big carnivals, with fewer tourists and more local life. To find them:

- **Consult the tourist office** or the municipality of the area you are going to visit.
- **Ask at your accommodation** and at the markets what is being celebrated on those dates.
- **Check the cultural calendars** published by many regional governments.

At these festivals, hospitality is usually enormous, and also the importance of behaving like a guest: say hello, ask before entering ritual spaces, and gratefully accept what is offered to you.

## What to wear to a big party

- Comfortable, closed-toe shoes: you will spend many hours standing in the crowd.
- Light clothing or a coat depending on the weather, and something that can get wet at carnivals with water.
- Sunscreen, hat and water.
- Earplugs.
- A waterproof bag for your phone and money.
- A copy of the document and the contact of your accommodation noted on paper.

## Eat and drink at the party

The festivities are also a gastronomic festival: dishes that are only prepared on those dates, such as bread of the dead in Mexico, the corn meals of the June festivities or the typical sweets of each patron saint. Choose busy stalls and freshly prepared food, as explained in the guide on [safe street food](/bitacora/comida-callejera-segura/).

## Photograph the party

- **Ask permission** before photographing dancers, musicians or families up close.
- **Do not interrupt** parades or processions to get a photo.
- **Respect the prohibitions** of using drones in areas with crowds.
- **Enjoy without a camera too:** Some of the best experiences happen when you stop looking at the screen.

## Frequent errors

- **Book accommodation at the last minute.**
- **Carrying valuables** into the crowd.
- **Step on the carpets** of a procession or cross in front of it.
- **Treating religious ceremonies** as a spectacle.
- **Do not foresee** which banks and businesses may close.
- **Drink excessively** and lose sight of the group.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it worth traveling to the carnival even if it is expensive?

If you are interested in experiencing the party, yes. If you prefer to explore the city calmly, visit museums and pay less, it is better to choose other dates.

### How do I know when the carnival is each year?

The carnival is celebrated the days before Ash Wednesday, which changes every year according to the religious calendar. Check the official dates in advance.

### What do I do if I lose my group in the crowd?

Go to the meeting point you agreed upon before leaving and wait there. Have the name and address of your accommodation written down on paper, in case your phone runs out of battery or the network becomes saturated, which is common at big parties.

### Are the holidays safe for traveling with children?

Many have family activities during the day. At times of greater crowds and at night, it is advisable to be extra careful or choose other times of the celebration.

## In summary

The great Latin American holidays, from Carnival to the Day of the Dead, are unique experiences, but they require planning: book accommodation and transportation well in advance, buy tickets through official channels, and review the program. On the street, wear the minimum, define meeting points, hydrate and moderate alcohol. Respect the religious and community meaning of many celebrations, ask permission before photographing and, when possible, participate rather than just watch.
