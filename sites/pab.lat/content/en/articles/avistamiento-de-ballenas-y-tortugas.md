Seeing the back of a humpback whale emerge from the water, hearing the blow of a right whale a few meters from the coast or silently contemplating how a sea turtle digs its nest in the sand in the moonlight are some of those experiences that change the way you look at the ocean. Latin America has some of the best places in the world to experience them, in the Pacific, the Caribbean and the Atlantic.

These encounters depend on wild animals that travel thousands of kilometers and that, in many cases, are threatened. Therefore, how you observe it matters as much as where. In this guide you will see what species can be seen, in what places and approximate times, how to choose a responsible excursion, what rules to follow in the water and on the beach and what mistakes to avoid so as not to become a nuisance for the animals you came to admire.

## Whales: where and when

The seasons vary each year and depending on the area, so these dates are indicative. Confirm them with local operators before traveling.

## The great migrations of the humpback whale

Humpback whales spend part of the year feeding in cold, food-rich waters, and travel thousands of kilometers to tropical waters to mate and give birth. Those that reach the coasts of Colombia, Ecuador and Panama come from Antarctica; those that reach Mexico and the Caribbean, from the northern seas. Costa Rica is one of the few places that receive specimens from both hemispheres at different times of the year.

During the season it is possible to see jumps, fin and tail strikes, and mothers with their babies. The males also emit complex songs that can sometimes be heard with hydrophones from boats. To better understand the Pacific coast, consult the guide to the [South American Pacific coast](/bitacora/costa-del-pacifico-sudamericano/).

## The gray whale in Baja California

Every winter, gray whales travel one of the longest migrations of any mammal to reach the coastal lagoons of Baja California Sur, such as Ojo de Liebre or San Ignacio, where their calves are born. Some of these lagoons are part of a sanctuary declared world heritage. The visits are made in small authorized boats, with quotas and delimited areas, and the curiosity of some whales is famous, as they approach the boats on their own. Even so, the rule is clear: they are the ones who decide to approach, never the boat.

## Southern right whale from the coast

On the Valdés Peninsula, in Argentine Patagonia, southern right whales arrive every year in the protected gulfs to reproduce. They can be seen on excursions from Puerto Pirámides and, at certain times, even from the coast, in places such as the beaches near Puerto Madryn. The world heritage-listed peninsula is also one of the few places where orcas deliberately come to shore to hunt sea lions at certain times of the year.

In southern Brazil, the coast of Santa Catarina is another meeting point for this species, which is often observed from viewpoints on land.

## The whale shark

Despite its name, the whale shark is a fish, the largest in the world, and feeds on plankton. In Mexico it is observed in Caribbean waters, near Holbox and Isla Mujeres, approximately in summer, and in the bay of La Paz, in Baja California Sur, at another time of the year. It is also seen in Utila, in Honduras. In many places swimming near them is allowed with strict rules: small groups, minimum distance, prohibition of touching them and using flash, and a limited number of boats.

## How to choose a responsible excursion

- **Authorized operators:** In many countries, birdwatching excursions require permits from environmental authorities.
- **Naturalist guides:** A good guide explains the behavior of the animals and enforces the rules.
- **Suitable boats:** in good condition, with vests for everyone and radio.
- **Small groups** and few boats around the same animals.
- **Respect for the rules:** be wary of anyone who promises to see whales "up close" or claims that you will be able to touch them.
- **Without impossible guarantees:** the animals are wild; a serious operator does not guarantee sightings.

## Observation rules in the water

The specific rules change from one country to another, but they share principles:

- **Minimum distance** between the boat and the animals, greater when there are mothers with babies.
- **Slow approach,** from the side or behind and parallel, never from the front or cutting its path.
- **Engines at minimum** or in neutral near the animals.
- **Limited time** of observation with each group of whales.
- **No chases:** If the animals move away or submerge, they are let go.
- **Do not swim with whales** where prohibited, which is most places.
- **No drones** where they are not allowed.

## sea turtles

Seven species of sea turtles inhabit the world's oceans, and most of them nest or feed on Latin American coasts. They all face threats: bycatch in fishing nets, egg poaching, plastic pollution, loss of beaches due to construction and artificial light, which disorients hatchlings.

| Place | Country | What can be seen |
|---|---|---|
| Tortuguero | Costa Rica | green turtle spawning, especially between July and October, and leatherback turtles in another season |
| Ostional | Costa Rica | olive ridley sea turtle arrivals, with thousands of females nesting at once |
| The Brush | Mexico | olive ridley turtle arrivals on the coast of Oaxaca |
| Las Baulas National Marine Park | Costa Rica | leatherback turtle nesting, the largest in the world |
| Praia do Forte | Brazil | sea turtle conservation center open to the public |
| Akumal | Mexico | green turtles feeding in seagrass beds |

Arrivals are an almost unique phenomenon in the world: on certain nights, thousands of olive ridley turtles arrive at the same beach at the same time to lay their eggs.

## View a spawn with respect

Watching a nesting turtle is a privilege, and also a time when the animal is very vulnerable. On many beaches, nighttime visits are only allowed with authorized guides.

- **Follow the guide** and wait for him to tell you when to approach; This is usually when the turtle is already laying its eggs.
- **No white lights or flashes:** the guides use dim red light, which bothers the animals less.
- **Dark clothing** and silence.
- **Do not stand in front of the turtle** or touch it.
- **Keep your distance** and don't block their path back to the sea.

## Breeding and releases

If you see hatchlings coming out of a nest, do not touch them or take them to the sea: the journey through the sand is important for their orientation. Keep the lights off, because they can become disoriented and walk towards land.

Some centers organize hatchling releases with visitors. Choose only those from recognized conservation programs that follow scientific protocols, and be wary of activities that allow you to manipulate the hatchlings or take photos with them.

## What to bring

- Waterproof and windproof jacket: it is usually cooler at sea and splashes.
- Sunscreen, hat and sunglasses for day outings.
- Binoculars, very useful from boat and from the coast.
- Anti-sickness medication, if needed, taken in advance.
- Dark clothing and comfortable shoes for night visits to spawning beaches.
- A waterproof bag for your phone and camera.

## Photography without disturbing

- **On the boat,** use bursts and high shutter speeds to capture jumps and tails.
- **Keep the horizon straight** and be patient: whales usually come out again near where they dived.
- **On spawning beaches,** no flash or white lights are allowed. Often the best option is to put the camera away and just observe.

## Tourism that helps

Many turtle and whale conservation projects in the region operate thanks to the collaboration of coastal communities, which found in responsible tourism an alternative to the capture or looting of eggs. Choosing local guides and accommodations committed to conservation helps sustain that change. We see it in the guide on [community tourism and cultural respect](/bitacora/turismo-comunitario-y-respeto-cultural/).

## Frequent errors

- **Choose operators without permits** who get too close to the whales.
- **Use flash or flashlights** on spawning beaches.
- **Touch or pick up baby turtles** for a photo.
- **Travel out of season** hoping to see whales.
- **Swimming with animals** where prohibited.
- **Buy tortoiseshell products** or tortoise shell, the trade of which is illegal.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it safe to see whales from a boat?

With authorized operators that respect distances and rules, yes. The main risks are dizziness, the sun and the state of the sea.

### Is seeing whales guaranteed?

No. In the middle of the season, the odds are high in the best places, but the animals are wild and no one can guarantee it.

### Can I see turtles without a guide?

On many nesting beaches, night access is regulated and only permitted with licensed guides. Find out about the rules of each place.

### What is the best time to see whales in South America?

It depends on the species and the place. In the Pacific of Colombia and Ecuador, humpbacks arrive approximately between July and October; in the Valdés peninsula, the southern francas, approximately between June and December.

## In summary

Latin America offers extraordinary encounters with humpback, gray, right and blue whales, whale sharks and sea turtles. Check the approximate seasons of each species and place, choose authorized operators with naturalist guides and small groups, and respect distances, times and speeds. On nesting beaches, follow the guide, use only red light, do not touch the turtles or hatchlings, and support local community conservation projects.
