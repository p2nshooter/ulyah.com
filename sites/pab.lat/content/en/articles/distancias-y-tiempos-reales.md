One of the most common mistakes when planning a trip to Latin America is looking at the map and thinking that two cities are "close." On paper, three or four hundred kilometers seems like a morning's trip. In reality, that same stretch can include a mountain range, dozens of curves, road works, a police checkpoint, rain that causes a landslide, and leaving a big city at rush hour. Result: the trip takes twice as long as planned and the itinerary falls apart.

Understanding why real times differ so much from theoretical ones is key to putting together an enjoyable itinerary. In this guide you will see how big the region really is, what factors lengthen trips, some reference routes, when it is best to fly and when to go by land, and how to plan with a margin that avoids races and missed connections.

## A continent bigger than it seems

Flat maps tend to visually shrink regions near the equator, causing many travelers to underestimate the size of Latin America. Some data helps to size it:

- **Brazil** is larger than the contiguous continental United States.
- **Chile** extends more than four thousand kilometers from north to south.
- **Argentina** is one of the ten largest countries in the world.
- **The Pan-American Highway** connects the continent from north to south, but it is interrupted between Panama and Colombia in the Darién Gap, an area of ​​jungle without a road that it is not advisable to attempt to cross by land. Between both countries you travel by plane or by sea.

## Why maps are deceiving

Map applications calculate times based on the distance and theoretical maximum speed of each road. They do not always take into account:

- **The relief:** in the mountains, curves and slopes greatly reduce speed, especially for buses and trucks.
- **The state of the road:** potholes, sections of gravel, works and detours.
- **Weather:** rain, fog, snow or landslides.
- **Urban traffic:** Leaving or entering a big city can take hours.
- **The stops:** the buses stop to pick up passengers, eat and change drivers.
- **Borders and controls:** immigration procedures, customs and police or health controls.
- **Social unforeseen events:** strikes, road blockades and demonstrations.

## Some reference routes

| Journey | Approximate distance by road | Usual bus time | Why does it take |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lima to Cusco | more than a thousand kilometers | about twenty hours or more | crossing of the Andes with many curves and high altitude |
| Bogota to Medellin | about four hundred kilometers | about nine hours or more | mountain road with heavy truck traffic |
| Quito to Cuenca | about four hundred and fifty kilometers | about nine hours or more | Andean route with curves and fog |
| Mexico City to Oaxaca | about four hundred and sixty kilometers | about six or seven hours | highway, but with a slow exit from the capital |
| Santiago to Mendoza | about three hundred and sixty kilometers | between six and eight hours or more | high mountain pass and border control |
| Peace to Uyuni | more than five hundred kilometers | about eight to ten hours | highlands, often by night bus |

These are indicative times under normal conditions. With rain, construction, lines at the border or traffic, they can take a long time. Check the schedules and durations of the transport companies and the recent opinions of other travelers.

An extreme example helps to understand the scale: between Buenos Aires and Ushuaia there are about three thousand kilometers by road, and as Tierra del Fuego is not linked by land to the rest of Argentina, the trip includes crossing Chilean territory and crossing the Strait of Magellan by ferry.

## Mountains and curves

In the Andes, the roads go up and down thousands of meters with routes full of curves. A bus can take hours to travel sections that seem short in a straight line. Additionally:

- **Vehicles lose power** at altitude and move slowly on climbs.
- **Passengers may get dizzy** from curves or experience altitude sickness on overpasses. We explain it in the guide on [altitude sickness](/bitacora/mal-de-altura-soroche/).
- **Passing is difficult** behind slow trucks on two-lane roads.

## Rain, landslides and snow

In the rainy season, landslides, known in the Andes as landslides or huaicos, can cut off roads for hours or days. In the high mountain passes between Argentina and Chile, snow forces traffic to be closed in winter. In the highlands, summer rains can also disrupt roads. Check the status of the routes on the day of travel and have an alternative plan.

## Big cities and traffic

Leaving Mexico City, São Paulo, Bogotá, Lima or Buenos Aires at rush hour, on a long weekend or on the last day of a vacation can take much longer than expected. If you can, travel at times of lower traffic and also calculate the time to get to the bus terminal or the airport, which are often far from the center.

## Borders and controls

A border crossing can be resolved in minutes or take several hours, depending on the crossing, the day and the number of passengers. In some countries there are also police, military or health checkpoints on the road. Always calculate a margin and read the guide on [crossing borders by land](/bitacora/cruzar-fronteras-por-tierra/).

## Stoppages and blockades

In several countries in the region, transport strikes, protests and road blockades are part of political and social life. They can last hours or days and leave travelers stranded. To reduce its impact:

- **Follow local news** and ask at your accommodation before leaving.
- **Check with transportation companies,** who usually know if there are closed routes.
- **Have days to spare** in the itinerary, especially before an international flight.
- **Do not attempt to cross a blockade** on your own or argue with those maintaining it.

## Plane, bus or train: compare door to door

To decide how to get around, compare the total door-to-door time, not just the length of the main journey:

| Option | Time that is often forgotten |
|---|---|
| airplane | transfer to the airport, advance notice for boarding, possible delays and transfer to the destination |
| Bus | arrival at the terminal, stops en route and transfer from the arrival terminal |
| Train | few frequencies and connections with other transport; We see it in the guide about [tourist trains] (/bitacora/trenes-turisticos/) |

As a general rule, for journeys longer than ten or twelve hours by land, an internal flight usually saves a day of travel. For shorter journeys, the bus allows you to see the landscape and avoids transfers to airports. The guide on [long distance buses](/bitacora/buses-de-larga-distancia/) explains how to choose a company and category.

## Night buses: advantages and limits

Night buses are very popular in the region because they save a night's accommodation and take advantage of the day. But they have limits:

- **You don't see the landscape,** which on some routes is half of the trip.
- **You arrive tired,** sometimes at dawn, to a city you don't know.
- **On some routes** it is recommended to avoid night trips for safety reasons; ask the local people.

If you arrive early in the morning, book your accommodation the night before so you can rest when you arrive.

## Daylight and time zones

- **Near the equator,** the days last approximately twelve hours all year round and it gets dark early, around six or seven in the afternoon. Plan your journeys by land to arrive with light.
- **In Patagonia,** in summer, the days are very long, and in winter, very short. We see it in the guide on [Patagonia: climate and wind](/bitacora/patagonia-clima-y-viento/).
- **Time zones:** some border crossings involve a time change, and large countries like Brazil or Mexico have several time zones. Not all countries apply daylight saving time, so check the local time when crossing.

## How to plan with margin

1. **Start from the map estimate** and add a generous margin, greater in the mountains, gravel and rainy season.
2. **Contrast with local sources:** transportation companies, accommodations and recent travelers.
3. **Consider the travel day as a lost day** for other activities, especially on long journeys.
4. **Avoid chaining many days of transfer in a row:** intersperse days of rest.
5. **Leave at least one day of margin** before an international flight if you are coming from a remote area or a route at risk of interruptions.
6. **Have a plan B:** an alternative bus, a flight or an extra night on the road.

Remember that traveling slower is not only more relaxed: it is also usually cheaper, as we explain in the guide on [realistic travel budget](/bitacora/presupuesto-de-viaje-realista/).

## If you drive

If you go by car, the calculation is even more important, because in addition to time, fatigue counts. Drive during the day, take breaks every two hours, do not plan too long days and consider that on gravel or mountains your average speed will be very low. More tips in the guides on [driving in Latin America](/bitacora/conducir-en-america-latina/) and [scenic road routes](/bitacora/rutas-escenicas-por-carretera/).

## Frequent errors

- **Blindly trust the time given on the map.**
- **Plan activities** on the same day of a long trip.
- **Chain transfers** without days off.
- **Leave the return to the capital** for the same day as the international flight.
- **Do not check the status of the routes** during the rainy or snowy season.
- **Forget transfer time** to terminals and airports.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much margin should I add to the map times?

There is no fixed rule. On highways in good condition, the margin may be small; On mountain, gravel or rainy season roads, it is advisable to be very generous. Always contrast with the times indicated by the transport companies.

### Is it better to fly or go by bus?

It depends on the distance and your priorities. For very long journeys, the plane saves days; For medium journeys, the bus allows you to see the landscape and is usually cheaper.

### What do I do if a lockdown leaves me stranded?

Stay calm, follow the transport company's instructions, look for local information and, if necessary, reorganize the itinerary. Having days of margin is the best protection.

### Can I cross from Panama to Colombia by land?

There is no road between the two countries, and the Darién jungle is a dangerous area that is not recommended to cross. The usual way to travel between them is by plane or by sea.

More guides for traveling by land in the section [Road Routes](/destinos/rutas-por-carretera/).

## In summary

In Latin America, distances are deceiving: mountains, curves, gravel, rain, traffic, borders, controls and blockades mean that land trips take much longer than the map indicates. Compare times with local sources, compare door to door between plane, bus and train, travel during daylight and consider time zones. Above all, leave room: fewer destinations, days of rest between transfers and at least one day off before an international flight.
