To get to know the cuisine of a Latin American country, it is not necessary to reserve a table at a famous restaurant. It is enough to go out on the street at lunchtime or at dusk: al pastor tacos spinning on the top, arepas on the griddle, freshly fried empanadas, anticuchos smoking on the grill, pupusas, choripanes, tamales, elotes, salteñas or fruit juices that you have never tried before. Street food is part of daily life, the work of thousands of families and the identity of each city.

Many travelers avoid it for fear of getting sick, missing out on an essential part of the trip. Others eat everything without precautions and spend two days in the hotel. Between both extremes there is a reasonable path: choose the positions well, know the most delicate foods and know what to do if your stomach protests. In this guide you will see how to do it.

## Signs of a good position

| Signal | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Lots of local clientele | The food rotates quickly and people come back because they trust |
| Food cooked to order | heat kills most microorganisms |
| Clean surfaces and utensils | indicate care in handling |
| Clean water for washing or disposable dishes | reduces contamination of plates and cutlery |
| Whoever collects does not touch the food, or uses gloves or tongs | money passes through many hands |
| Ingredients protected from the sun and insects | prevents damage or contamination |

A line of workers, students and families is usually the best possible recommendation. If one stall is empty at rush hour while the one next to it is full, there is a reason.

## The safest and most delicate

**Safer:**

- foods that are fried, roasted or boiled and served very hot,
- freshly made breads, tortillas and doughs,
- fruits that are peeled in front of you.

**More delicate:**

- **sauces and dressings** that spend hours at room temperature,
- **salads and raw vegetables** washed with water that you do not know if it is drinkable,
- **raw fish and seafood** or barely marinated, if the position does not inspire confidence,
- **unpasteurized dairy,** such as some fresh cheeses or artisanal dairy drinks,
- **reheated food** or food that has been exposed for a long time.

It is not about prohibiting everything delicate, but about choosing more carefully where to eat it. A ceviche at a busy market stall, prepared with fish of the day, is not the same as one that has been in the sun for hours.

## Water, ice and juices

- **Water:** in many places in the region it is not recommended to drink tap water. Ask and, when in doubt, drink bottled or treated water.
- **Ice:** In many cities, business ice is purchased from factories that use purified water. In rural areas or very improvised positions, this may not be the case. Ask, and if you're not sure, ask for the drink without ice.
- **Natural juices:** They are delicious and nutritious. The risk is in the water that is added and in cleaning the blender. Choose clean, busy stalls, or order juices without added water.

## Every meal has its time

Street food follows the rhythm of the city, and some dishes only appear at certain times or certain days:

- **Very early:** tamales, atoles, breads, coffees and breakfasts for those who go to work.
- **Midday:** Lunch stalls with dishes of the day, often near offices and markets.
- **Mid-afternoon:** sweets, fried foods and hot drinks, at the time that in some countries is called eleven or elevens.
- **Night:** grills, anticuchos, tacos and snacks that light up when the sun goes down.
- **Weekends:** long preparation dishes, such as some barbecues and broths, which many stalls only offer on Saturdays or Sundays.

Asking "what is being eaten here at this time?" It is a good way to discover the best of each place.

## If you are vegetarian or vegan

There are many meatless options on the street: arepas with cheese, bean or cheese pupusas, humitas, vegetable tamales, corn, fruits and juices. But it is worth asking, because some ingredients of animal origin are not seen: beans or dough prepared with lard, meat broths in vegetable soups or fried foods made in the same oil as the meat. Learn to say "no meat," "no butter," or "does it have chicken broth?"

## How to order and pay

- **Watch how the locals do it:** in some stalls you order and pay at the end; In others, you pay first and receive a turn.
- **Have cash in small bills,** although more and more stalls accept electronic payments. We see it in the guide on [money, cards and change](/bitacora/dinero-tarjetas-y-cambio/).
- **Question about spiciness:** "is it very spicy?" is a common and well-received question. Sauces are usually served separately.
- **"To take away" or "to eat here"** changes the packaging and sometimes the price.
- **Tipping** at street stalls is usually not mandatory, although rounding up is appreciated.

## Allergies and intolerances

If you have any allergies, street food requires more care, because the ingredients are not always visible:

- **Learn to say it:** "I'm allergic to peanuts," "I can't eat gluten," "I don't eat shellfish."
- **Bring a card written in Spanish** with your allergy and its severity.
- **Ask about hidden ingredients:** some sauces and moles contain nuts or seeds; Many fried foods use the same oil for everything.
- **Carry your emergency medication** if you have a serious allergy.

## Traveler's diarrhea: what to do

Even with care, sometimes the stomach does not adapt to the change in food, water and schedules. Traveler's diarrhea is usually mild and passes in a few days.

- **Hydrate:** the most important thing is to replace fluids and salts. Oral rehydration salts, sold in pharmacies, are the most effective.
- **Eat lightly:** rice, soups, bread, banana, while you recover.
- **Rest** and avoid alcohol.

**Seek medical attention** if there is a high fever, blood in the stool, vomiting that prevents drinking, signs of dehydration, if symptoms last more than a few days, or if the person who is sick is a young child, an elderly person, or someone with a chronic illness. Before taking medications to stop diarrhea, consult a doctor or pharmacist. More information in the guide on [traveler's health](/bitacora/salud-del-viajero-y-vacunas/).

## Eat with respect

- **Respect the lines** and turns.
- **Do not ask for close-up photos** while the seller is working without asking first.
- **Bring your own container or cutlery** if you want to reduce the use of plastics.
- **Buy from local vendors:** Street food sustains many families.
- **Leave the trash in its place** and don't litter the street.

## A street food route

A good way to start is to combine a market and its surroundings. The guide to [traditional markets](/bitacora/mercados-tradicionales/) explains how to navigate them. Many cities also offer guided walking food tours, a convenient way to try various stalls with someone who knows the best.

## Frequent errors

- **Avoid all street food** and miss out on local cuisine.
- **Choose empty stalls** at rush hour.
- **Abusy sauces and raw materials** in the first days.
- **Drinking tap water** without knowing if it is drinkable.
- **Do not carry rehydration salts** in the first aid kit.
- **Do not report an allergy** out of embarrassment.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it safe to eat on the street in Latin America?

In general yes, if you choose wisely: busy stalls, hot and freshly made food and be careful with water and ice. Millions of people eat on the street every day.

### How long does it take for the stomach to get used to it?

It varies. Many travelers prefer to start the first days with cooked and hot dishes and gradually try sauces, raw foods and dairy products.

### Is it advisable to carry something for the stomach in the first aid kit?

Yes: oral rehydration salts, hand sanitizer gel, and any medications prescribed by your doctor for the trip. With this you resolve most minor discomforts while you decide if you need a consultation.

### Are fruit juices safe?

They tend to be in clean and busy positions. If you have a sensitive stomach, ask for juices without water or ice, or whole fruits that you can peel.

## In summary

Street food is one of the best ways to get to know a country. Choose stalls with a lot of local clientele, freshly cooked food and good hygiene, and be more careful with sauces that have been in the sun for hours, raw materials, seafood and unpasteurized dairy products. Ask about water and ice, learn to explain your allergies, and bring rehydration salts. If diarrhea occurs, hydrate, eat lightly, and seek medical attention for fever, blood, or symptoms that do not improve. And eat with respect: lines, turns and trash in your place.
