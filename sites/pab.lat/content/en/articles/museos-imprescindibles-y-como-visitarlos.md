Before climbing a pyramid, touring a colonial center or traveling to an Andean community, few things help as much as spending a few hours in a good museum. The museums of Latin America keep some of the most extraordinary collections in the world: gold pieces from pre-Hispanic cultures, Mexican sculptures weighing several tons, Andean textiles thousands of years old, paintings from the viceroyalty, murals that changed the history of art and contemporary works that dialogue with all that past.

Visiting them well, however, requires some strategy. Many are closed one day a week, some require reservations in advance and almost all of them are so large that it is impossible to see them all without ending up exhausted. In this guide you will see some of the most notable museums in the region, what types of museums should be included in the trip and how to plan your visit to truly enjoy it.

Hours, prices and exhibitions change frequently, and some museums temporarily close for construction. Always check the official information before you go.

## Archeology and native cultures

Archeology museums are the best gateway to the deep history of the region. They allow you to see up close pieces that cannot be exhibited in archaeological sites, understand the chronology of different cultures and make sense of what you will see later on the ground.

- **In Mexico,** the National Museum of Anthropology organizes its rooms by regions and cultures, from the Olmecs and Teotihuacan to the Mayans and the Mexicas. It is so big that many visitors return for a second day.
- **In Colombia,** the Gold Museum shows how pre-Hispanic societies worked with gold and other alloys for ritual and symbolic purposes, not as simple wealth.
- **In Peru,** museums such as the Larco in Lima or the Sipán in Lambayeque bring together ceramics, textiles and metalwork from cultures such as the Mochica, the Nazca, the Chimú or the Inca.
- **In Chile,** the Museum of Pre-Columbian Art brings together pieces from almost the entire continent in a very careful route.

Many archaeological sites have their own on-site museum, ideal for visiting before the tour. We explain it in the guide on [archaeological sites](/bitacora/sitios-arqueologicos/).

### Human remains and sacred objects

Some museums exhibit mummies or human remains, such as those of the Llullaillaco children in Salta, which are displayed under strict conservation conditions. They are people, not curiosities: observe with respect and respect the prohibition of photography when it exists. In recent years, several museums in the region have reviewed how they display these remains and sacred objects, often in dialogue with indigenous peoples.

## Art: from the viceroyalty to muralism and the contemporary

- **Vice-regal art:** churches, convents and museums of colonial art, such as those in Quito, Cusco, Potosí or Puebla, house paintings, sculptures and altarpieces from schools that mixed European techniques with local views and materials. The Quito school and the Cusco school are two of the best known.
- **Mexican Muralism:** In the first half of the twentieth century, artists such as Diego Rivera, José Clemente Orozco, and David Alfaro Siqueiros painted enormous murals on public buildings to tell the history of the country. Many can be seen for free in palaces and buildings in central Mexico City.
- **Modern and contemporary art:** museums such as the MALBA in Buenos Aires, the MASP in São Paulo, whose building suspended over a large square is an icon of architecture, or Inhotim in Minas Gerais, where the works are distributed among gardens and pavilions, show the strength of Latin American art.

If you are also interested in the architecture of the museums themselves, the guide on [architecture for travelers](/bitacora/arquitectura-para-viajeros/) gives tips for looking at the buildings.

## Memory museums

Several countries in the region have created museums and memory sites dedicated to the victims of dictatorships and armed conflicts. Among them are the Museum of Memory and Human Rights in Santiago, the Place of Memory in Lima or the ESMA Site of Memory Museum in Buenos Aires, a former clandestine detention center, registered as a world heritage site. They are intense visits that help to understand recent history. Go early, be silent in the spaces that ask for it and keep in mind that for many families they are places of mourning.

## House museums and community museums

Not everything is in the great national museums. Some of the most special memories come from small places:

- **House museums of artists and writers,** such as Frida Kahlo's Blue House in Mexico City or Pablo Neruda's houses in Chile.
- **Community museums,** managed by towns and communities, which tell their story in their own words. In Oaxaca, for example, there is a network of community museums in different towns.
- **Thematic museums** dedicated to textiles, music, photography, mining or gastronomy.

In addition to enriching the trip, these visits directly support the communities. We see it in the guide on [community tourism and cultural respect](/bitacora/turismo-comunitario-y-respeto-cultural/).

## Plan the visit

1. **Check the closing day:** many museums in the region close on Mondays, although not all.
2. **Look at free days:** Many public museums offer free admission some day of the week or month. In some countries, this free service applies only to residents or nationals.
3. **Book when necessary:** Popular museums and temporary exhibitions can sell out, especially on weekends.
4. **Ask about discounts:** students, teachers and seniors usually have reduced rates by presenting a document.
5. **Choose the best time:** first thing in the morning there are fewer people; Weekends and holidays are filled with local families.
6. **Calculate time:** two or three hours are usually enough for an intense visit; Beyond that, the concentration drops a lot.

## Inside the museum

- **Choose your rooms:** Review the floor plan when you enter and decide what really interests you. It is better to see five rooms well than to rush through twenty.
- **Use audio guides or guided tours:** many museums offer them in several languages, sometimes included in the ticket.
- **Leave your backpack in the storage room:** In many museums, large backpacks, umbrellas or drinks are not allowed in the rooms.
- **Photography without flash:** Flash damages pigments and textiles, and is usually prohibited. Some museums do not allow photography in certain rooms.
- **Take breaks:** The cafeteria or the museum garden are good places to rest and regain energy.
- **Do not touch the works** or lean on display cases, even if they seem resistant.

### Museum fatigue

There is even a name for the fatigue that appears after a while of looking at works and reading posters: museum fatigue. To avoid it, alternate very dense rooms with lighter ones, read only the texts that interest you, sit down every once in a while and don't try to see everything. If the museum is huge, consider splitting your visit over two days.

## With children

Many museums in the region have workshops, family tours and interactive rooms, and some cities have museums designed especially for children. Some ideas to make the visit work:

- **Choose a few rooms** and turn them into a game: look for animals, colors or characters in the works.
- **Visit in the morning,** when they have more energy.
- **Combine the museum with a park** or an outdoor activity.

## Museum nights and special events

Several cities organize museum nights, with late-night openings, free admission or special activities. In Buenos Aires there is a night once a year in which dozens of museums open until dawn, and in Mexico City there are periodic night sessions. They are a great experience, although with a lot of people. Check the city's cultural agenda during your stay.

## Museums and historical centers

Many museums are in historic buildings in city centers: old palaces, convents or colonial mansions. Combining them with a walk through the center is a way to take advantage of the day; We see it in the guide on [touring a historic center](/bitacora/recorrer-un-centro-historico/).

## Frequent errors

- **Arrive on a Monday** without checking the closing day.
- **Try to see an entire large museum** in one visit.
- **Do not reserve** museums with limited spaces, such as the Blue House.
- **Use flash** or photograph where prohibited.
- **Go with a large backpack** without having the luggage.
- **Leaving the museums for the end of the trip,** when they would have helped understand everything else.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much time do I need at the National Museum of Anthropology?

For a complete visit, at least half a day. With less time, choose some rooms, such as the Mexica, the Mayan or the Teotihuacan, and come back another day if you feel like it.

### Are museums expensive?

In general, prices are affordable, and many public museums have free days. Private museums and some house museums may cost more.

### Can I take photos?

In many museums yes, without flash or tripod. Some prohibit photographing certain rooms or pieces, such as human remains or temporary exhibitions. Respect the signs.

### Is it better to go to the museum before or after visiting an archaeological site?

Before, if you can: it will give you context to understand what you will see. But returning later also has value, because you will recognize places and objects.

## In summary

The museums of Latin America hold treasures of archaeology, viceregal art, muralism, contemporary art and recent memory. Include them at the beginning of the trip to better understand what you will see later. Check the closing days, the free days and if you need to reserve, arrive early, choose a few rooms and rely on guides or audio guides. Leave your backpack in the storage room, do not use flash, take breaks to avoid museum fatigue and respectfully visit the spaces dedicated to memory and indigenous peoples.
