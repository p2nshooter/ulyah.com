Few travel experiences impress as much as arriving in the Andes: the Cusco plaza at dawn, Lake Titicaca under an almost violet sky, the Uyuni salt flat to the horizon. But many of those places are more than 3,000 meters above sea level, and the body notices it. Headache, tiredness, shortness of breath when climbing stairs or nights of poor sleep are common signs in the first days. In the Andes it is called **soroche**; in medical books, acute mountain sickness.

In most cases it is mild and passes in one or two days. But in some people it can evolve into serious, life-threatening forms. The good news is that much of the problems can be avoided with a well-thought-out itinerary and some simple precautions. In this guide you will see at what altitude the risk begins, how to plan acclimatization, which symptoms are normal and which require going down immediately, and what is true about coca tea and medications.

## At what height does it appear

Altitude sickness can appear from around **2500 meters**, and becomes more common the higher and faster you climb. Some very visited destinations:

| Destination | Approximate altitude |
|---|---|
| Bogota, Colombia | 2600m |
| Quito, Ecuador | 2850 m |
| Sacred Valley, Peru (Urubamba) | 2870 m |
| Cusco, Peru | 3400m |
| Huaraz, Peru | 3050m |
| La Paz, Bolivia | 3600m |
| El Alto, Bolivia, and its airport | around 4000 m |
| Puno and Lake Titicaca | 3800m |
| Uyuni, Bolivia | 3650m |
| Tatio Geysers, Chile | more than 4200 m |

It does not depend on age or physical condition: highly trained people can suffer from it and other sedentary people cannot. What has the most influence is **the speed of ascent** and the altitude at which you sleep.

## The three forms of altitude sickness

### Acute mountain sickness

It is the most common way. Appears between a few hours and a day after arriving:

- headache,
- tiredness and weakness,
- nausea or lack of appetite,
- dizziness,
- disturbed sleep.

It usually improves with rest, hydration and without increasing more until the symptoms disappear.

### High altitude cerebral edema

It's an emergency. It is recognized by **confusion, severe drowsiness, difficulty walking in a straight line or strange behavior**. Requires immediate descent and medical attention.

### High altitude pulmonary edema

It's also an emergency. It produces **shortness of breath even at rest, persistent cough, great fatigue with the slightest effort** and, sometimes, bluish lips or foamy sputum. Requires immediate descent, oxygen and medical attention.

**Golden rule:** if symptoms worsen despite rest, or any sign of cerebral or pulmonary edema appears, **don't wait: go down**. Descending a few hundred meters usually produces a notable improvement.

## An itinerary that helps you acclimatize

The best prevention is to give the body time. The recommendations of mountain medicine societies coincide in some guidelines:

- **Above about 3000 meters, do not increase your sleeping altitude by more than about 500 meters per day.**
- **Take a day of rest every three or four days** of ascent.
- **Climb during the day and sleep lower** when possible: It's the "climb high, sleep low" principle.

In practice, some useful strategies:

- **In Peru:** When arriving in Cusco from the coast, many travelers first go down to the Sacred Valley, about 500 meters lower, and go up to Cusco a few days later.
- **In Bolivia:** Those who arrive by plane to El Alto land at about 4000 meters. It is a good idea to plan one or two quiet days before any demanding excursion.
- **In Ecuador and Colombia:** Quito and Bogotá are good acclimatization stopovers before climbing volcanoes or higher moors.
- **By land:** Traveling by bus or car from lower areas allows for a more gradual adaptation than a direct flight.

## The first days at altitude

- **Go slowly:** walk calmly, avoid stairs in a hurry and leave demanding excursions for after the second or third day.
- **Drink water regularly.** The air at altitude is dry and you lose a lot of water when you breathe. Dehydration worsens the discomfort.
- **Avoid alcohol** the first few days: it makes acclimatization difficult and disturbs sleep.
- **Eat light,** with more carbohydrates and fewer heavy meals.
- **Protect yourself from the sun:** ultraviolet radiation is much more intense at altitude. Wear sunscreen, glasses and a hat.
- **Bundle up in the evening:** the nights can be very cold even if the day is sunny.

## Sleep at altitude

The first nights at altitude are usually light sleep. It is common to wake up several times or notice irregular breathing, with brief pauses followed by deeper breaths. In general, it is a normal response of the body that improves with acclimatization. It helps to have an early and light dinner, sleep with an extra blanket, keep the room somewhat ventilated and avoid sleeping pills without medical indication, because they can depress breathing.

## Children at altitude

Children can also suffer from altitude sickness, but sometimes they do not know how to explain what they feel. Pay attention if a toddler is more irritable than normal, eats less, vomits, sleeps poorly, or plays much less than usual. In that case, do not continue climbing and consult a doctor. With babies and small children it is advisable to plan even more gradual ascents and speak to the pediatrician first.

## Coca tea: tradition and limits

In Peru and Bolivia, coca tea is a traditional and legal drink offered in many hotels to relieve the discomfort of altitude. Many people find it comforting, but it does not replace good acclimatization or prevent severe forms.

Two important warnings:

- **Do not carry coca leaves or coca products** when leaving the country: in many countries they are prohibited and can be considered a crime.
- **Coca consumption can appear in drug controls** at work or in sports competitions.

## Medications

There are medications that help prevent altitude sickness in people at risk or on rapid ascent itineraries. **They must be indicated by a doctor**, who will assess your history, allergies and interactions. Consult before the trip, ideally at a travel medicine center. Do not self-medicate with drugs purchased without a prescription or recommended by other travelers.

Common pain relievers can relieve mild headache pain, but should not be used to "keep going" while symptoms persist.

## Who should consult before traveling

Seek medical advice before traveling to altitude if:

- you have heart or lung diseases, such as heart failure or COPD,
- you have sickle cell anemia or other blood diseases,
- you are pregnant,
- you travel with babies or small children,
- you already had high-altitude cerebral or pulmonary edema,
- you take medications regularly.

In high altitude destinations, travel insurance with good medical coverage, which includes assistance and transportation in case of emergency, is especially important. ## Frequent errors

- **Fly from sea level and take a demanding excursion** on the same day of arrival.
- **Continue climbing with symptoms** of altitude sickness.
- **Confuse serious symptoms** with simple fatigue.
- **Drink alcohol the first night** at altitude.
- **Forget sunscreen** because it's cold.
- **Take coca leaves back** home.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does altitude sickness last?

Mild symptoms usually improve in one to three days if you do not continue climbing. If they worsen or serious signs appear, descend and seek medical attention.

### Does being in good physical shape prevent soroche?

No. Good physical fitness does not protect against altitude sickness, and sometimes leads some people to push themselves too soon. What helps the most is to go up gradually.

### Is hotel oxygen useful?

Oxygen can relieve symptoms and is part of the treatment of severe forms, but it does not replace descent when there are warning signs. Use it for relief and consult a doctor if symptoms persist.

## In summary

Altitude sickness can appear from around 2500 meters and depends mainly on the speed of ascent. Plan a gradual itinerary, without increasing the altitude at which you sleep above 3000 meters by more than about 500 meters per day, and with rest days. The first few days go slowly, drink water, avoid alcohol and protect yourself from the sun and cold. If you experience confusion, difficulty walking, or shortness of breath at rest, go down immediately and seek medical attention. Consult a doctor before the trip if you have previous illnesses and do not carry coca leaves when leaving the country.
