The chocolate that is eaten all over the world today was born in America. Long before becoming a tablet, chocolate or dessert, cocoa was a ceremonial drink, offering, medicine and even currency. Its seeds traveled from the Amazon rainforest to Mesoamerica, from there to Europe and then to the rest of the planet. For those who travel through Latin America, following the trail of cocoa is a delicious way to learn about history, tropical landscapes and communities that have been cultivating it for generations.

A visit to a cocoa farm allows you to see the pod hanging from the trunk, taste the sweet pulp that surrounds the seeds, understand why fermentation is so important and prepare your own chocolate. In this guide you will see where cocoa comes from, what varieties exist, where it is grown, what you learn on a farm, what traditional drinks and dishes use it and how to taste and buy good chocolate.

## An Amazonian fruit with thousands of years of history

Cocoa, whose scientific name is *Theobroma cacao*, which in Greek means something like "food of the gods", is native to the upper Amazon basin, in northwest South America. Archaeological investigations in southern Ecuador found traces of its use in vessels from the Mayo-Chinchipe culture about five thousand years old, which places some of the oldest known uses in that region.

From there, cocoa reached Mesoamerica, where the Olmecs, Mayans and Mexica made it a central element of their culture:

- **Ceremonial drink:** It was prepared with water, corn, chili, spices or flowers, and beaten until it formed foam, which was highly valued.
- **Offering and symbol:** appeared in rituals, banquets and celebrations of the elite.
- **Currency:** among the Mexica, cocoa beans were used to pay tributes and make exchanges.

After the arrival of the Spanish, cocoa crossed the Atlantic, was sweetened with sugar and gradually transformed into the chocolate we know.

## The varieties: criollo, forastero and trinitario

| Group | Features | Where is it located |
|---|---|---|
| Creole | aromatic and delicate, with little bitterness; plants more sensitive to diseases | Mesoamerica, Venezuela and other areas of northern South America |
| Outsider | more resistant and productive, with a more intense flavor; basis of much of the world's production | Amazon basin and, today, also West Africa |
| Trinitarian | hybrid between Creole and foreigner, emerged on the island of Trinidad; combines aroma and resistance | many producing regions of America |

In addition to these large groups, there are highly appreciated local varieties. In Ecuador, for example, the cacao called Nacional is famous for its floral notes. Geneticists have described many more groups than these three classics in recent years, but the traditional classification remains useful to the traveler.

## Where is it grown today?

| Country | Known regions | What stands out |
|---|---|---|
| Ecuador | coast and Amazon | one of the largest producers of fine aroma cocoa |
| Peru | Piura, San Martín, Cusco and other areas | cooperatives and highly valued cocoas of origin |
| Colombia | Santander, Huila, Arauca and other regions | growing production and rural projects |
| Venezuela | central coast and other areas | long-famous Creole cocoas |
| Mexico | Tabasco and Chiapas | Mesoamerican tradition and cocoa farms |
| Guatemala, Nicaragua and Costa Rica | low and humid areas | small producers and indigenous communities |
| Dominican Republic | north and east of the country | large producer of organic cocoa |
| Brazil | southern Bahia and the Amazon | the so-called cocoa coast and systems under forest shade |
| Bolivia | Amazon | wild cocoa harvested in the jungle |

Cocoa grows in warm, humid climates, approximately between twenty degrees north and south latitude. That's why farms are usually in tropical lowlands, with heat, humidity and frequent rain.

## Visit a cocoa farm

Many farms, cooperatives and communities offer tours that follow the entire process:

1. **The tree:** the flowers and ears grow directly from the trunk and thick branches. Cocoa usually grows in the shade of taller trees, in agroforestry systems that conserve part of the forest.
2. **The harvest:** The ripe ears are carefully cut by hand so as not to damage the tree.
3. **The opening:** inside each ear there are dozens of seeds covered by a sweet white pulp.
4. **Fermentation:** the seeds with their pulp are left to ferment for several days in wooden boxes or covered with leaves. It is a decisive step: there the chocolate aromas begin to form.
5. **Drying:** the seeds are dried in the sun or in dryers for days, stirring them frequently.
6. **Roasting:** dried beans are roasted to develop flavor.
7. **Hulling and milling:** The husk is removed and the grain pieces, called nibs, are ground into a paste.
8. **Chocolate making:** The paste is mixed with sugar and sometimes cocoa butter or milk, refined and tempered so that it has shine and a clean break.

At the end of many visits you can grind roasted beans in a metate or in a manual grinder and prepare a drink or a small tablet. Ask beforehand how long the tour lasts and if it includes a tasting or workshop.

## The pulp: the flavor that few know

Those who try the fresh pulp that surrounds the seeds for the first time are usually surprised: it is sweet, acidic and aromatic, with a flavor reminiscent of tropical fruits and not at all like chocolate. In some areas it is used to prepare juices, ice cream or jams. If you visit a farm during harvest season, ask to try it.

## Drinks and dishes with cocoa

| Preparation | Where | What is it |
|---|---|---|
| Water or milk chocolate | Mexico and Central America | cocoa bars with sugar and cinnamon, dissolved and beaten with a wooden grinder |
| Champurrado | Mexico | thick corn atole with chocolate |
| Tejate | Oaxaca, Mexico | cold corn and cocoa drink with a characteristic foam |
| Pozol | Tabasco and Chiapas, Mexico | corn and cocoa dough drink, refreshing in the heat |
| Santafereño chocolate | Bogota, Colombia | hot chocolate served with cheese and bread; the cheese is dipped into the cup |
| Mole | Mexico | complex sauces; some, like certain versions of mole poblano, include chocolate among many ingredients |

Trying these preparations in traditional markets and kitchens is a way to understand that cocoa was not always sweet: for centuries it was drunk more spicy than sugary. ## How to taste chocolate

Tasting chocolate is similar to tasting wine or coffee, and you learn quickly:

1. **Look:** a good dark chocolate has a uniform shine and an even color, without whitish spots.
2. **Listen:** when you break it, it should break with a clean snap.
3. **Smell:** Rub it a little with your fingers and look for aromas of fruit, flowers, nuts, spices or toast.
4. **Let it melt:** Put a piece on your tongue without chewing and watch how the flavor changes.
5. **Compare:** try chocolates from different origins with the same percentage; The differences are surprising.

Whitish spots do not always indicate that the chocolate is in poor condition: they are usually due to changes in temperature or humidity, which affect its appearance and texture but do not make it dangerous.

## Read the label

- **Percentage of cocoa:** indicates how much of the total weight comes from cocoa, adding the cocoa mass and cocoa butter. 70 percent means the rest is mostly sugar.
- **Ingredients:** A good dark chocolate usually has few: cocoa, sugar and sometimes cocoa butter. If other vegetable fats appear instead of cocoa butter, the quality is usually lower.
- **Origin:** Single-origin tablets indicate the country, region, and even the farm, like in specialty coffee.
- **From bean to bar:** some producers control the entire process, from the purchase of the bean to the final chocolate, and usually explain it on the label.

## Buy and transport chocolate

- **Buy on the farm, from cooperatives or from local chocolate shops,** so that more of the price remains in the region.
- **Be careful with the heat:** in the tropics, chocolate melts quickly. Store it in the coolest part of your luggage, away from the sun, and don't leave it in a car.
- **Nibs and cocoa powder** travel better than tablets and make a good souvenir.
- **Raw or fresh grains:** health rules in many countries restrict the entry of seeds and unprocessed plant products. Processed chocolate is usually allowed, but always check the rules in your country. We explain it in the guide on [crossing borders by land](/bitacora/cruzar-fronteras-por-tierra/).

## A fairer cocoa

The price of cocoa on the international market changes a lot, and small producers usually receive a small part of the final value of the chocolate. In addition, the crop has suffered serious diseases: the so-called witch's broom, for example, hit the plantations in southern Bahia, Brazil, hard since the late 1980s. As a visitor, you can contribute in several ways:

- **Choose visits organized by cooperatives or communities,** not just by intermediaries.
- **Buy direct** when possible.
- **Ask how it is grown:** Shade systems, which conserve native trees, help biodiversity.
- **Value the work:** an artisanal chocolate costs more because behind it there are many hours of manual work.

## Cocoa and coffee on the same trip

In several countries, cocoa- and coffee-growing areas are relatively close, although at different altitudes: cocoa prefers low, warm lands, and quality coffee prefers mountain slopes. Combining both routes allows us to compare two tropical crops with surprisingly similar processes, such as fermentation and drying. ## Frequent errors

- **Visit a farm without asking** if there is a harvest or activity on that date.
- **Leave the chocolate in the sun** or in the car in hot weather.
- **Carrying raw grains** without reviewing customs rules.
- **Confusing high percentage with better quality:** The origin and process also matter.
- **Buy only in airport stores,** when local chocolate shops offer more variety and better prices for producers.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is cocoa the same as cocoa?

In many Spanish-speaking countries, "cacao" is used for the tree, the fruit and the grain, and "cocoa" for the powder that remains after extracting part of the butter. Uses change depending on the country and industry.

### When is cocoa harvested?

In many regions there is a main harvest and a minor harvest throughout the year, depending on the climate of each area. Ask the farm what you can see on the date of your trip.

### Is chocolate with a higher percentage more bitter?

In general, yes, because it contains less sugar. But a good high percentage chocolate can be very aromatic and balanced, without aggressive bitterness.

### Do you need to know about chocolate to enjoy a visit?

No. The visits are designed for all audiences and are also ideal for families with children.

## In summary

Cocoa was born in the Amazon, it was a sacred drink and currency in Mesoamerica and today it is cultivated in much of Latin America. Visit a farm or cooperative to see the entire process, from cob to fermentation, drying, roasting and tableting, and taste the fresh pulp if it's in season. Discover traditional drinks such as tejate, pozol or Santafereño chocolate, learn to taste and read labels, and buy directly from producers, protecting the chocolate from heat and respecting customs rules.
