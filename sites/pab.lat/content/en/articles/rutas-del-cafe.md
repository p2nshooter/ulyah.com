Latin America produces a good part of the coffee drunk in the world. Brazil is the largest producer on the planet, Colombia has a coffee landscape recognized as a world heritage site and highly appreciated high altitude coffees are grown in the mountains of Central America, Mexico, Peru and Bolivia. For those who travel, coffee is much more than a drink: it is a way to get to know mountain landscapes, colorful towns, producer families and a culture that sets the pace of the day.

A coffee route combines nature, history and flavor. You walk among coffee plantations, learn how a red cherry becomes your breakfast cup, taste freshly roasted coffee, and understand why two coffees from the same region can taste so different. In this guide you will see the main coffee regions, when to visit them, what you learn on a farm, how coffee is drunk in each country and how to buy a good bean to take away.

## The great coffee regions

| Country | Known regions | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| Colombia | Coffee Axis: Caldas, Quindío, Risaralda and north of the Valley | colorful villages, farms with accommodation and the Cocora valley with its wax palms |
| Guatemala | Antigua, Huehuetenango, Atitlán, Cobán | coffee plantations on the slopes of volcanoes and mountains |
| Costa Rica | Tarrazú and Central Valley | farms organized for visits and high-altitude cafes |
| El Salvador | Flower Route | coffee towns and food festivals |
| Honduras and Nicaragua | Copán, Marcala, Matagalpa, Jinotega | cooperatives and mountain landscapes |
| Panama | Boquete | high altitude farms, known for highly appreciated varieties |
| Mexico | Chiapas, Veracruz and Oaxaca | towns like Coatepec and a long tradition of high altitude coffee |
| Peru | Chanchamayo, Cusco, Cajamarca, Amazonas | high altitude coffees from small producers |
| Bolivia | Yungas | subtropical slopes near La Paz |
| Brazil | southern Minas Gerais, São Paulo, Espírito Santo | large areas and enormous production |

## The Coffee Cultural Landscape of Colombia

It is perhaps the best-known coffee route in the region. UNESCO inscribed it as a world heritage site for the way in which generations of families adapted cultivation to steep mountains and created a culture of their own, with towns with colorful architecture, traditional farms and a strong sense of community. Towns like Salento or Filandia, cities like Manizales, Pereira and Armenia, and the Cocora valley, with the towering wax palms, Colombia's national tree, are part of the most common routes. In many towns, classic jeeps are still used as rural transportation.

## When to visit

- **In harvest season,** you will see the pickers at work and the coffee mill in full activity. In many areas of Central America and Mexico the main harvest occurs approximately between the end of the year and the first months of the following year; In Colombia there is usually a main harvest and a secondary harvest throughout the year, depending on the area.
- **Outside of the harvest,** the farms also receive visitors and explain the process, with less activity but more tranquility.
- **The mountain climate** is usually temperate, with frequent rains at some times. Wear a light jacket.

Ask the property what you can see on the date of your trip.

## What you learn on a farm

A good visit goes all the way to coffee:

1. **Cultivation:** varieties, height, tree shade and plant care. Many producers grow coffee under shade, which favors biodiversity.
2. **The Harvest:** In quality coffee, the ripe cherries are picked by hand, one by one.
3. **Processing:**
   - **Washing:** The pulp is removed and the grain is fermented and washed before being dried. It usually gives clean and bright coffees.
   - **Natural:** the whole cherry is dried in the sun. It usually gives sweeter and fruitier coffees.
   - **Honey or honey:** The skin is removed but part of the mucilage is left when it dries. Gives intermediate profiles.
4. **Drying:** on patios, raised beds or dryers, until the appropriate humidity is reached.
5. **Threshing:** the dry husk is removed and the green coffee remains, ready for export or roasting.
6. **Roasting:** transforms the green bean into the aromatic coffee we know.
7. **Cupping:** the professional way of tasting and evaluating coffee, smelling and sipping each sample.

Many farms end the visit with a tasting. It is time to compare coffees from different processes and roasts.

## How coffee is drunk in each country

| Place | How to ask or how to take |
|---|---|
| Colombia | a tinto is a black coffee, often small and served throughout the day |
| Mexico | Café de Olla is prepared with cinnamon and piloncillo, traditionally in a clay pot. |
| Brazil | cafezinho is a small, strong and often sweet coffee, a symbol of hospitality |
| Argentina, Uruguay and Chile | the cut is a coffee with a little milk |
| Venezuela | There is talk of black, brown or milk coffee depending on the proportion of milk |
| Central America | morning filtered coffee accompanies breakfast in many homes |

Trying coffee the way the locals drink it is part of the experience, even if you prefer another way at home.

## Prepare coffee like on the farm

On many farms in the region, coffee is still prepared with simple methods that are worth learning:

- **Fabric filter:** In Costa Rica the chorreador is used, a wooden support with a cloth bag through which hot water is poured. Similar methods exist in other countries.
- **Coffee pot:** the coffee is cooked briefly with water, cinnamon and piloncillo and strained before serving.
- **Manual and press filtering methods:** widely used in specialty coffee shops throughout the region.

Some general guidelines that baristas usually recommend: use freshly ground coffee, hot but not boiling water, and an approximate proportion of one part coffee to fifteen to seventeen parts water, adjusting to your taste.

## A day in a coffee region

A typical plan combines a visit to a farm in the morning, when the weather is usually more stable and there is activity at the mill, with a lunch of local cuisine, a walk through the town in the afternoon and a tasting or coffee at a local roastery at the end of the day. If you stay several days, alternate large and small farms: you will see very different ways of producing.

## Buy coffee to go

- **Buy on the farm or from cooperatives,** so that a greater part of the price reaches the producers.
- **Prefers coffee beans,** which preserves its aroma better.
- **Look at the roast date:** the more recent, the better.
- **Read the label:** region, altitude, variety and process tell you a lot about the flavor.
- **Bags with valve** protect the coffee and let the gases escape.
- **Check your country's customs rules**: Roasted coffee is usually allowed, but unroasted green coffee may have restrictions. We see it in the guide on [travel documents](/bitacora/documentos-de-viaje/).

## Responsible coffee tourism

- **Choose farms and cooperatives** that pay fair prices and treat their workers well.
- **Respect the work:** during the harvest, the pickers work intensely; do not interrupt or photograph without permission.
- **Stay in rural accommodation** owned by the producing families themselves.
- **Buy direct** when possible.

The guide on [community tourism and cultural respect](/bitacora/turismo-comunitario-y-respeto-cultural/) expands on these ideas.

## Frequent errors

- **Visit only a tourist farm** without knowing the real work of the producers.
- **Buy ground coffee** that loses aroma quickly.
- **Do not ask about the harvest** before traveling.
- **Go without shelter** to mountain regions.
- **Carrying green coffee** without checking customs rules.
- **Photographing collectors** without asking permission.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best coffee in Latin America?

There is no single answer: each region has different profiles depending on altitude, climate, variety and process. It is best to try several and find out which one you like best.

### Do you need to know about coffee to enjoy a route?

No. The visits are designed for all audiences, and learning from scratch is part of the experience.

### Can you stay on a coffee farm?

Yes, in many regions there are farms with rooms or cabins for visitors, a quiet way to get to know rural life.

## In summary

Latin America offers coffee routes in Colombia, Central America, Mexico, Brazil and the Andes, each with its own landscapes and flavors. Visit a farm to walk through the entire process, from growing and harvesting to processing, roasting and tasting, and ask what you can see on your travel date. Taste coffee the way the locals drink it, buy beans and from producers or cooperatives, check customs rules, and travel with respect for the work of those who grow it.
