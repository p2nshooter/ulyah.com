The beaches of Latin America are among the most beautiful in the world, from the turquoise Caribbean to the big waves of the Pacific. But the sea is not a pool. Every year drownings occur on tourist beaches, and many of them have the same cause: a rip current that drags bathers out to sea who did not recognize it or who tried to swim against it until they were exhausted.

The good news is that almost all of these accidents can be avoided with basic information: knowing how to read the sea, understanding the flags, swimming near lifeguards and knowing what to do if the current catches you. In this guide you will see how to identify a rip current, how to get out of it, what the flags mean, what other risks there are on the shore and how to care for children in the water.

## What is a rip current

Waves push water toward the shore. That water has to return to the sea, and it does so through narrow channels where a current is concentrated away from the beach. That's a rip current, also called a rip current.

Some key information:

- **It doesn't sink you:** it keeps you away from the shore. The danger is in fatigue and panic when trying to swim back against it.
- **Can be very fast,** faster than a trained swimmer.
- **It appears on beaches with waves,** both in the Pacific and the Atlantic and in the Caribbean, and can change location with the tide.
- **It is usually narrow:** a few meters on the sides the water can be calm.

## How to recognize it from the sand

Before entering the water, observe the sea for a few minutes. A rip current can look like:

- **A strip where the waves do not break,** between areas where they do break.
- **Darker water,** because it is deeper.
- **Foam, algae or sand** that advance out to sea.
- **Rough or rippled water** in a narrow strip.

If in doubt, ask the lifeguards where it is safe to swim. They know the daily behavior of their beach.

## If the current drags you

1. **Keep calm.** The current is not going to sink you.
2. **Do not swim directly to shore** against the current.
3. **Swim parallel to the beach** until you exit the current channel, and then return diagonally towards the shore.
4. **If you can't swim, float** and let the current lose strength; many weaken beyond the wave zone.
5. **Ask for help** by raising one arm and shouting.

## If you see someone in trouble

- **Immediately notify the lifeguards** or call the country's emergency number.
- **Do not jump into the water** without a float or preparation: many victims are people who were trying to rescue another.
- **Throw something that floats** like a board, an empty cooler, or a life preserver.
- **Keep your eyes on the person** to indicate their position to rescuers.

## After a rescue

If a person comes out of the water after swallowing water or almost drowning:

- **Call emergencies** even if she seems recovered.
- **Check if he is breathing.** If he is not breathing and you know how to do it, start CPR until help arrives.
- **Cover it** and keep it at rest.
- **Seek medical evaluation:** some respiratory complications may appear hours later.

A basic first aid course is one of the best investments for anyone who travels to the beach often.

## Tides: the sea changes during the day

The tide modifies the depth, the strength of the currents and the width of the beach. On some Pacific coasts, such as Panama and Colombia, the difference between high and low tide can be several meters: a wide beach in the morning can be almost covered in the afternoon, and an accessible rock can be isolated. Check tide tables, usually published by hydrographic services or tourist offices, before walking along the coast or exploring pools and caves.

## Before going to the beach: a quick list

1. Find out if the beach has lifeguards and what hours.
2. Check the state of the sea, tides and weather alerts.
3. Write down the country's emergency number.
4. Bring water, shade, sunscreen, and shoes for rocky areas.
5. Agree with your group on a visible meeting point in the sand.
6. Bring life jackets if you travel with children who don't swim well.

## What do the flags mean?

Many beaches use flags to indicate the state of the sea. The system may vary between countries and even between beaches, but the usual thing is:

| Flag | Usual meaning |
|---|---|
| Green | favorable conditions, bathe with normal caution |
| Yellow | caution, moderate waves or currents |
| Red | danger, bathing not recommended or prohibited |
| Double red or black | beach closed to bathing in some countries |
| Purple or violet | presence of dangerous marine fauna, such as jellyfish |

Always respect the flag, even if you see other people in the water. If there are no flags or lifeguards, take extreme caution.

## Swim near lifeguards

On beaches with lifeguard service, also called lifeguards or lifeguards, swim in the area they monitor and on their schedule. Outside of that area and that time, no one will see you if you have problems. Many drownings occur early in the morning or in the evening, when there is no longer any surveillance.

## Shore waves and sloping beaches

On some beaches, especially on the Pacific, the waves break strongly directly on the shore. They can knock a person over and slam them into the sand. To avoid injuries:

- **Don't dive headfirst** into unfamiliar waters or into waves that break at shallow depths.
- **Do not turn your back on the sea** on the shore.
- **Be careful with children and older people** on steep beaches.

The beaches of the South American Pacific also have cold waters and intense waves, as we explain in the guide on the [South American Pacific coast](/bitacora/costa-del-pacifico-sudamericano/).

## Other risks in the water

- **Stripes:** On sandy bottoms, shuffle your feet when walking in the water so they move away instead of stepping on them.
- **Jellyfish or jellyfish:** if there are warnings, avoid swimming. If you are stung, get out of the water and seek help at the lifeguard station.
- **Hedgehogs:** wear water shoes in rocky areas.
- **River mouths:** can have strong currents and murky waters.
- **Rocks and reefs:** waves can drag you against them.
- **Sun and heat:** dehydration and heatstroke are also beach risks. More information in the guide on [traveler's health](/bitacora/salud-del-viajero-y-vacunas/).

## Children in the water

- **Always keep an eye on them,** without distractions like the phone, even if there is a lifeguard.
- **Drowning is usually silent:** a child in trouble does not scream or splash, he sinks.
- **Wear life jackets** suitable for children who do not swim well; Inflatable floats are not safety devices.
- **Choose quiet beaches** and shallow areas.
- **Assign an adult watchman in turns,** who knows that their only task at that time is to watch the children in the water. In large groups, everyone thinks someone else is watching.

## Alcohol and the sea do not combine

Alcohol reduces reflexes, perception of danger and physical resistance. A large portion of adult drownings involve alcohol. If you're going to bathe, don't drink beforehand.

## Frequent errors

- **Enter the water without observing the sea** for a few minutes.
- **Swim against the current** until you are exhausted.
- **Shower outside of lifeguard hours.**
- **Ignore the red flag** because others bathe.
- **Plunge headfirst** into shore waves.
- **Go to rescue without a float.**

## Frequently asked questions

### Do Caribbean beaches have rip currents?

They can have them, especially with waves or bad weather, although many Caribbean beaches protected by reefs are calmer. The rule is the same: observe, ask and respect the flags.

### How do I know if I can swim enough for the sea?

Swimming in a pool does not prepare you for the open sea, with waves, currents and cold water. If you don't have experience, stay where you stand and close to the lifeguards.

### Is it safe to bathe at night?

It is not recommended. At night there are no lifeguards, it is difficult to see the currents, waves and rocks, and if someone is in trouble no one will see them in time. Enjoy the sea at night from the shore.

### What do I do with my things while I bathe?

Take the bare minimum to the beach and don't leave valuables in the sand. More tips in the guide on [safety in large cities](/bitacora/seguridad-en-grandes-ciudades/), which also apply to tourist areas.

## In summary

A rip current doesn't sink you: it pushes you away from the shore. Learn to recognize it by the waveless strip, the darker water and the receding foam. If you get caught, stay calm, swim parallel to the beach or float and call for help. Respect the flags, swim near the lifeguards and on their schedule, do not dive into shore waves, watch out for rays and jellyfish, always watch the children and do not mix alcohol with the sea. If someone is in trouble, let them know and throw something that floats before taking the risk.
