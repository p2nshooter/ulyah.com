Latin America is a land of volcanoes. From central Mexico to southern Chile, hundreds of cones, calderas and craters are part of the landscape, history and daily life. There are volcanoes that can be seen from the capitals, such as Popocatépetl from Mexico City or Pichincha from Quito, volcanoes that can be climbed in a morning, others that require high mountain technique and some that, at night, light up the sky with their explosions.

Visiting a volcano is an unforgettable experience, but also an activity that demands respect. An active volcano can change behavior in a short time, emit dangerous gases or throw rock fragments, and many are located at high altitudes. In this guide you will see some of the most visited volcanoes in the region, how to check their level of activity, what risks to know, when you need a guide and what to bring.

## The ring of fire

Much of the Pacific coast of America is part of the so-called Pacific Ring of Fire, where the collision between tectonic plates produces earthquakes and volcanoes. That's why there are so many volcanoes in Mexico, Central America and along the Andes, from Colombia to Patagonia. Some have been asleep for centuries; others erupt frequently and are constantly monitored.

## Before you go: check the activity

Each country has institutions that monitor their volcanoes and publish alert levels. Before planning your visit, and again the day before, review your information:

| Country | Surveillance institution |
|---|---|
| Mexico | National Center for Disaster Prevention, CENAPRED |
| Guatemala | National Institute of Seismology, Volcanology, Meteorology and Hydrology, INSIVUMEH |
| El Salvador | Ministry of Environment and Natural Resources |
| Nicaragua | Nicaraguan Institute of Territorial Studies, INETER |
| Costa Rica | Volcanological and Seismological Observatory, OVSICORI, and National Seismological Network |
| Colombia | Colombian Geological Survey |
| Ecuador | Geophysical Institute of the National Polytechnic School |
| Peru | Geophysical Institute of Peru and INGEMMET |
| Chile | National Geology and Mining Service, SERNAGEOMIN |
| Argentina | Argentine Mining Geological Service, SEGEMAR |

Alert levels are often expressed with colors, such as green, yellow, orange and red. Always respect exclusion zones and closures: they are not a formality. In Popocatépetl, for example, access to the summit has been prohibited for decades due to its activity.

## The dangers of an active volcano

- **Gases:** Volcanoes emit gases such as sulfur dioxide, which irritate the eyes and respiratory tract. People with asthma, heart or respiratory problems, pregnant women, and children should avoid the edges of craters with emissions. If you experience burning, coughing, or difficulty breathing, move away from the crater.
- **Projectiles:** in volcanoes with explosive activity, thrown rocks can fall some distance from the crater. That is why security perimeters are established.
- **Pyroclastic flows:** avalanches of hot gas, ash and rock that descend at enormous speed. They are the deadliest danger and the main reason for exclusion zones.
- **Lahars:** flows of mud and debris that form when water, melted snow or rain drag volcanic material along the channels.
- **Hot, unstable ground:** fumaroles and steam zones can cause burns, and the edges of craters can crumble.

## Altitude and climate

Many volcanoes exceed 4,000 meters, and some, such as Cotopaxi or Chimborazo in Ecuador, approach or exceed 6,000. At these heights, the air has much less oxygen and the risk of altitude sickness is high. Acclimatize for several days before attempting an ascent, as we explain in the guide on [altitude sickness](/bitacora/mal-de-altura-soroche/).

The weather in the highlands changes quickly. The mornings are usually clearer, and in the afternoon clouds and storms form in many areas. Ascents to large snow-capped volcanoes usually begin at dawn to reach the summit early, when the snow is firmer and the weather is more stable.

## Guided hikes and technical climbs

It is convenient to distinguish between two types of experiences:

- **Hikes:** trails to viewpoints, crater lagoons or accessible edges, which do not require technique but do require good physical fitness and, often, acclimatization.
- **Technical ascents:** summits with glaciers or snow, where crampons, ice axes, rope and high mountain knowledge are needed.

In many volcanoes, going with a guide is mandatory, at least for the summit or to get closer to the crater. Even if it isn't, an experienced local guide knows the terrain, knows how to read the weather and volcano activity, and carries safety equipment. Choose guides accredited by park authorities or mountain guide associations, check the condition of the equipment they rent and ask what they will do if conditions worsen. On volcanoes with emissions, such as Villarrica, guides usually wear gas masks for the final section.

## Acatenango and the Fuego volcano

One of the most popular volcanic hikes in the region is the climb to Acatenango, near Antigua Guatemala, to spend the night in front of the Fuego volcano, one of the most active in Central America, and see its explosions in the dark. It is an extraordinary, but demanding experience:

- **Strong slope:** you climb many meters along steep paths and volcanic sand.
- **Intense cold:** at night, the temperature in the camp can drop below zero. Wear real warm clothes, a hat and gloves, and check the quality of the sleeping bag.
- **Agencies:** compare what they include, how many guides they have per group and what condition the tents and bags are in.
- **Fire Activity:** respects approach restrictions. In 2018, an eruption of the Fuego volcano caused pyroclastic flows that caused tragedy in nearby communities.

## Easily accessible volcanoes

You don't have to be a mountaineer to see a volcano up close. Several have viewpoints accessible by vehicle or with short walks:

- **Masaya, in Nicaragua,** allows you to look into the crater from a viewpoint; At some times it has been possible to see the glow of the lava. The residence times are short due to the gases.
- **Irazú and Poás, in Costa Rica,** have viewpoints over their craters. In Poás, visits have been made by reservation, in shifts and with limited time, and the park closes when activity increases.
- **Quilotoa, in Ecuador,** offers a view of its turquoise lagoon from the edge of the crater, a few steps from the town.
- **The Quito cable car** climbs the slopes of Pichincha to about 4000 meters, with views of the city and the volcanoes.

## Hot springs and other experiences

Volcanic areas also offer more relaxed experiences:

- **Hot springs:** in Baños de Agua Santa, in Ecuador, in the south of Chile or in Costa Rica, among many other places. Respect the warnings about water temperature, hydrate and do not bathe in natural sources without information.
- **Board descent:** in Cerro Negro, in Nicaragua, you descend by sliding over the volcanic sand. Use the protective equipment provided and control your speed.
- **Mud volcanoes:** near Cartagena, in Colombia, the so-called Totumo volcano allows you to bathe in warm mud. It is not a lava volcano, but a phenomenon of mud and gases.
- **Volcanic cities:** Arequipa, in Peru, is largely built with ashlar, a white volcanic stone that gives it its name of the White City, under the gaze of the Misti.

## What to bring

- Hiking boots or shoes with good soles, suitable for sand and loose rock.
- Clothing in layers, waterproof jacket, hat and gloves.
- Sunglasses with UV filter and high sunscreen: the sun is very intense at altitude.
- Sufficient water and energetic food.
- Headlamp for early morning outings.
- Bandana or tubular collar to protect you from dust and ash.
- Walking sticks, very useful on volcanic sand descents.

## Respect the volcano and its surroundings

- **Do not enter closed areas** or go down into craters.
- **Do not throw objects** into craters or lagoons.
- **Do not take rocks** from protected areas.
- **Respect local beliefs:** For many communities, volcanoes are beings with names and personalities, and are part of their worldview.
- **Take your trash away,** including organic trash.

## Lessons from history

The history of the region remembers the power of volcanoes. In 1985, an eruption of Nevado del Ruiz, in Colombia, generated lahars that buried the city of Armero and caused the death of more than twenty thousand people. In 1943, in a crop field in Michoacán, Mexico, Paricutin was born, which in a few years buried two towns; The tower of the church of San Juan Parangaricutiro still appears among the lava. These stories are not intended to scare, but rather to explain why alerts and exclusion zones should be taken seriously.

## Frequent errors

- **Do not check the alert level** before the visit.
- **Attempt a high ascent** without acclimatization.
- **Underestimate the cold** on overnight hikes in the mountains.
- **Approach the crater** with asthma or breathing problems.
- **Hire guides without accreditation** or adequate equipment.
- **Ignore exclusion zones** to get a photo.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it dangerous to visit an active volcano?

It can be if alerts and exclusion zones are not respected. With up-to-date information, accredited guides, and respect for closures, many visits to active volcanoes are done with controlled risks.

### Do I need experience to climb a volcano?

It depends on the volcano. Many hikes only require good physical fitness and acclimatization. Glacier summits, such as Cotopaxi or Villarrica, require technical equipment and a guide.

### What is the best time to visit a crater?

In the morning, in general, when there are usually fewer clouds. In volcanoes with visible activity, like Fuego, the show is better at night, always from safe areas.

### Can I see lava?

In some volcanoes, and only in certain periods of activity. There are no guarantees: it depends on the behavior of the volcano, which no one can predict exactly.

## In summary

The volcanoes of Latin America, from Mexico to Patagonia, offer everything from accessible viewpoints to technical ascents to more than six thousand meters. Before you go, check the alert levels at surveillance institutions and respect closures and exclusion zones. Know the dangers of gases, projectiles, pyroclastic flows and lahars, acclimatize to the altitude, hire accredited guides when necessary and bring warm clothing, sun protection and water. With information and respect, a volcano can give you one of the best memories of the trip.
