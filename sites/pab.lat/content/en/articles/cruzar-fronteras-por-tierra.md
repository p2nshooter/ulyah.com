Crossing a border by land is one of the most characteristic experiences of a long trip through Latin America. From Lake Titicaca to the border between Peru and Bolivia, from the high mountain pass between Mendoza and Santiago, from the bridges over the Uruguay River or from the Central American passes where in a few hours you can change country, currency and accent. It is also a time that can generate nerves: lines, forms, baggage checks and, sometimes, people trying to take advantage of the traveler's confusion.

With a little preparation, most crossings go smoothly. In this guide you will see how a border crossing works, what documents to bring, how to prepare for health and customs controls, what you need if you cross with a vehicle, what to keep in mind about schedules and closures and how to avoid the most common scams.

## How a border crossing works

In general, crossing involves three steps:

1. **Departure from the country you are in:** The immigration authority registers your departure, often with a stamp in your passport or an electronic record.
2. **Entry to the destination country:** the immigration authority of the new country registers your entry and grants you a period of stay.
3. **Customs and health control:** Luggage and what you are carrying are checked, especially food, products of animal or plant origin, cash and merchandise.

In some steps, both countries have their offices in the same building, in what is called integrated control. In others, the offices are separated by several kilometers of no man's land.

**Always make sure your entry is recorded.** If there is no proof that you entered legally, you may face fines or problems when leaving the country.

## Documents

- **Passport or identity document,** depending on what the destination country accepts for your nationality. We see it in the guide on [travel documents](/bitacora/documentos-de-viaje/).
- **Immigration card or form,** if the country provides it upon entry. Save it: you may need it when you leave.
- **Exit ticket or itinerary,** which some countries may request.
- **Authorizations for minors,** if they travel with only one parent or without them.
- **Vehicle documents,** if you cross by car.

## Regional agreements

Some agreements facilitate passage between neighboring countries. For example, between Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras and Nicaragua there is an agreement that allows many foreign visitors to circulate between the four countries for a single period of stay. In South America, the Mercosur and Andean Community agreements allow many of their citizens to cross with their identity document. The conditions depend on your nationality, so check how they apply to you.

## Fees and cash

Some countries charge entry, exit or border crossing fees, especially at certain borders in Central America. Bring cash in whatever currency they accept, often US dollars or local currency, and always ask for official receipt. Find out beforehand if there are reliable ATMs or exchange offices; In many borders there are not. More information in the guide about [money, cards and change](/bitacora/dinero-tarjetas-y-cambio/).

## Health controls: declare food

Many countries protect their agriculture and livestock with strict border controls. It is common that it is prohibited to enter:

- fresh fruits and vegetables,
- meats, sausages and dairy products,
- seeds, plants, flowers and soil,
- honey and other products of animal origin.

Some countries check luggage with scanners and trained dogs, and fines for failing to declare can be high. **When in doubt, declare.** The most practical thing is to consume or throw away fresh food before reaching the control.

## Hours and closures

- **Not all steps operate 24 hours a day.** Check hours before leaving.
- **High mountain passes:** some Andean crossings are temporarily closed due to snow or wind, especially in winter. The authorities publish the status of the passes; check it the same day.
- **Holidays and high seasons:** long lines may form. Arrive early.
- **Arrive in daylight:** Crossing at night and getting to an unknown border town without transportation can be complicated.

## Cross by bus

On international buses, all passengers get off for immigration procedures and, many times, for luggage inspection. Keep your documents close at hand, don't lose sight of your belongings and don't get back on the bus without having completed all the paperwork. More tips in the guide on [long distance buses](/bitacora/buses-de-larga-distancia/).

## crossing with a vehicle

- **Own car:** you will need documents proving ownership, valid insurance in the destination country and, in many cases, a **temporary import permit** for the vehicle, which is processed at the border or online. Some countries require specific insurance for foreign vehicles, and Mercosur countries use a common civil liability insurance to circulate between them.
- **Rental car:** you will need **written authorization from the agency** to leave the country, which not all of them grant and which usually has an additional cost.
- **When leaving the destination country,** cancel the vehicle's temporary permit: if you don't, you may have problems and fines.

The guide on [driving in Latin America](/bitacora/conducir-en-america-latina/) expands information on insurance and documents.

## How to avoid scams

- **Use only official windows** and ask for proof of any payment.
- **Be wary of intermediaries** who offer to do the paperwork for you in exchange for money: they are almost always unnecessary.
- **Be wary of anyone who presents themselves as an official** without uniform or identification in areas outside the offices.
- **Exchange money only what is necessary** and compare rates before accepting.
- **Never carry other people's packages** across the border, even if they ask you nicely. Laws against drug trafficking are very severe throughout the region.

## Cross with pets

Traveling with a dog or cat between countries requires planning weeks in advance. Typically, a recent **veterinary health certificate**, the **current rabies vaccine** and, in some cases, deworming and official documents from the health service of the country of departure are requested. Requirements change from country to country and are updated frequently, so check with the official animal health service of the destination country before leaving and bring all original papers.

## If you are denied entry

It is rare, but it can occur due to incomplete documents, having exceeded previous stays or not meeting a requirement of your nationality.

- **Remain calm and polite.** Arguing or raising your voice never helps.
- **Respectfully ask the reason** and what document or requirement is missing.
- **Never offer money** to resolve the situation: in addition to being illegal, it can greatly aggravate the problem.
- **Contact your country's consulate** if you need guidance.
- **Have an alternative plan,** such as returning to the previous city or rearranging the route.

## Ready for crossing day

- Passport or valid document, with paper copies and on the phone.
- Immigration card of the country you are leaving, if they gave it to you.
- Cash in small bills for taxes and transportation.
- Fresh food consumed or discarded before the control.
- Vehicle documents and authorizations, if you cross by car.
- Pass schedule and route status reviewed that same day.
- Address of your accommodation on the other side and how to get there.

## border towns

Border towns are usually places of passage, with a lot of commercial movement. Keep your things close, do not show valuables and plan your transportation to continue your trip without delays. If you must spend the night, choose accommodation with good references. More ideas in the guide on [safety in large cities](/bitacora/seguridad-en-grandes-ciudades/).

## Frequent errors

- **Leaving a country without checking out** or entering without checking in.
- **Carry fruits or meats** without declaring them.
- **Arrive at a closed mountain pass** without having checked its condition.
- **Pay intermediaries** for free procedures.
- **Crossing with a rented car** without authorization.
- **Planning the crossing at the end of the day** without knowing how to continue.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does a land border crossing take?

It depends on the pace, the day and the means of transportation. It can go from a few minutes to several hours in high season or on buses with many passengers. Always calculate a wide margin, as we explain in the guide on [real distances and times](/bitacora/distancias-y-tiempos-reales/).

### Will my luggage be checked?

It is possible, especially in health and customs controls. Have your luggage organized and declare what is appropriate.

### Can I cross walking?

In many steps yes. Find out how to continue the journey on the other side, because sometimes you have to take a taxi or a local bus to the nearest town.

### What happens if I lose my immigration card?

It depends on the country. In some cases it is enough to inform it when leaving; In others you may have to request a duplicate or pay a fine. Keep it next to your passport and photograph it as soon as it is given to you.

## In summary

A land border crossing involves registering the exit from one country, the entry into the other and passing customs and health controls. Bring the appropriate document for your nationality, keep the immigration card and make sure your entry is registered. Have cash for possible fees, declare or discard fresh food, check schedules and the status of mountain passes, and arrive in daylight. With a car, you have valid insurance, the temporary permit and the authorization of the agency if it is rented. Only use official windows and never transport other people's packages.
