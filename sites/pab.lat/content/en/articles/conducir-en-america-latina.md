Touring Latin America by car is one of the freest ways to travel. It allows you to stop at an unexpected viewpoint on the Carretera Austral, take a detour to a town in the Mexican mountains, explore the Uruguayan coast at your own pace or cross the Argentine Puna on roads where no bus passes. But driving in another country involves documents, insurance, regulations, driving customs and road conditions that are important to know before turning the key.

This guide does not replace the traffic regulations of each country, which you should consult in official sources, but it helps you prepare the essentials: what papers to bring, how to rent without surprises, what the roads are like, how to manage fuel and tolls, what to do at a police checkpoint and what precautions to take to arrive safely.

## Driving documents

- **Current driver's license** from your country.
- **International driving permit:** some countries require it for foreign drivers and in others it is recommended. It is processed in your country, normally through the automobile club or transit authority, before traveling.
- **Passport or identification document.**
- **Vehicle documents:** the rental contract or, if it is your car, the registration card or title.
- **Mandatory insurance:** Many countries require traffic accident insurance to circulate, with names such as SOAT in Colombia, Peru or Bolivia, or SOAP in Chile. Rental cars already include it; If you travel with your vehicle, find out which one you need.

More information about documents in the guide on [travel documents](/bitacora/documentos-de-viaje/).

## Rent a car without surprises

1. **Book in advance** in high season and in remote destinations, where supply is limited.
2. **Check what insurance includes:** damage to the vehicle, theft, civil liability and the amount of the deductible you would pay in the event of an accident.
3. **Know the hold on the credit card:** many agencies block an amount as a guarantee.
4. **Inspect the car before you leave:** Take photos and video of the body, tires, windshield and interior, and note each damage on the contract.
5. **Check spare tire, jack and tools.**
6. **Ask about the fuel policy** and mileage.
7. **If you plan to cross a border,** you will need written authorization from the agency, which is not always granted and usually has an additional cost.

In gravel or high mountain areas, a vehicle with greater ground clearance or four-wheel drive may be necessary. Ask what roads the contract allows: some agencies prohibit driving on certain sections.

## What are the routes like?

The road network in the region is very varied:

| Route type | What to expect | Precautions |
|---|---|---|
| Toll Highways | good condition, several lanes | Bring cash or the accepted means of payment |
| National two-lane routes | trucks, buses and overtaking | patience, only overtake with full visibility |
| mountain roads | sharp curves, slopes, precipices | low speed, be careful with landslides |
| Gravel or dirt roads | loose stones, dust, potholes | low speed, tires in good condition |
| urban streets | heavy traffic, motorcycles, pedestrians | constant attention and secure parking |

Some typical elements of the region's routes:

- **Speed bumps:** in Mexico they are called speed bumps; in Colombia, police lying down. They can appear without signs at the entrance to towns.
- **Animals on the road:** cows, horses, dogs, llamas or guanacos, especially in rural areas and at night.
- **Pedestrians and cyclists** on the shoulders, even on fast routes.
- **Landslides and cuts** in the rainy season in mountain areas.

## Don't drive at night

It is one of the most repeated recommendations by those who know the routes of the region: **avoid driving at night** outside the cities. Lighting is poor, there are animals and pedestrians on the route, some vehicles circulate without lights and it is more difficult to see potholes and landslides. Plan the stages to reach your destination in daylight.

## Fuel

- **In remote areas, the seasons may be far apart:** In Patagonia, the highlands, or the Atacama Desert, fill up whenever you can.
- **Names change:** Gasoline is called naphtha in Argentina and benzine in Chile, and diesel has different names depending on the country. Always confirm what fuel your car uses before charging.
- **Payments:** some rural stations only accept cash.

## Tolls

Many highways have tolls, called casetas in Mexico. Some accept cash and card; others work with electronic devices or online payments. Ask the rental agency how it works on your route and bring cash in local currency just in case.

## Police checks

It is common to find checkpoints on the route. To cross them without problems:

- **Keep calm and be courteous.**
- **Have documents on hand:** license, international permit, vehicle documents and insurance.
- **If you are informed of a violation,** ask that the fine be issued in writing with the agent's information, and pay it through official channels.
- **Do not offer or accept informal payments.**

## Rules that should be known

- **Seat belt** for all occupants.
- **Children's seats:** many countries require them for small children. Ask the agency for one when booking.
- **Alcohol:** several countries have zero tolerance or very low limits. The safest rule is not to drink anything if you are going to drive.
- **Lights on during the day** on certain routes or countries.
- **Speed ​​limits** in kilometers per hour, which can change abruptly at the entrance to towns.
- **Phone:** Use while driving is prohibited or restricted in many countries.

## Height and mountains

In the Andean passes, the engine loses power and the driver can feel the effects of the altitude. Rest, hydrate and do not drive if you have symptoms, as we explain in the guide on [altitude sickness](/bitacora/mal-de-altura-soroche/). On long descents, use low gears to avoid overheating the brakes.

## Navigation and communication

- **Download offline maps:** There is no phone signal on many routes.
- **Do not blindly trust GPS times:** in mountain, gravel or construction areas, journeys usually take much longer. We see it in the guide on [real distances and times](/bitacora/distancias-y-tiempos-reales/).
- **Notify someone of your route** when traveling through isolated areas.

## Breakdowns and emergencies

- **Have the assistance phone number** of the agency or insurance on hand.
- **Take water, some food, a coat and a flashlight** in the car.
- **Some countries have public roadside assistance services;** find out if they exist on your route.
- **In the event of an accident,** mark the location, call emergencies and do not move the injured unless there is immediate danger.

## A well-planned route stage

Before each day of travel, go over this quick list:

1. **Distance and real time** of the section, adding stops and margin for unforeseen events.
2. **State of the route:** works, cuts, weather and, in the mountains, snow or landslides.
3. **Fuel:** where the stations are and if it is convenient to fill up before leaving.
4. **Daylight arrival time** and accommodation reserved if you are traveling in high season.
5. **Water, food, shelter and first aid kit** in the car.
6. **Downloaded maps** and someone who knows your route.

With this routine, unforeseen events become anecdotes instead of problems.

## Driving in big cities

The capitals of the region usually have intense traffic, motorcycles that sneak between cars and special rules, such as traffic restrictions depending on the license plate number on certain days or times in some cities. Find out when you arrive and, if you are only going to get around the city, consider leaving the car parked and using public transportation, taxis or apps.

## Park safely

Use secured parking, especially at night and in large cities. Do not leave objects visible inside the car. In many places there are informal car sitters; It is customary to leave them a small tip.

## Frequent errors

- **Driving at night** outside of cities.
- **Do not inspect the car** and its damages when renting it.
- **Leaving without enough fuel** in remote areas.
- **Trust GPS times** in the mountains.
- **Cross a border** without authorization from the agency.
- **Drink alcohol** before driving.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need an international driving permit?

It depends on the country. Some require it and others accept the foreign license for a time. As it is easily processed in your country, it is advisable to take it.

### Can I cross to another country with a rental car?

Sometimes yes, with written authorization from the agency, insurance valid in the other country and an additional cost. We explain it in the guide on [crossing borders by land](/bitacora/cruzar-fronteras-por-tierra/).

### What insurance do I need if I travel with my own car?

One that is valid in each country you visit. Some countries require specific insurance for foreign vehicles at the border. Check with your insurer and the destination authorities.

## In summary

It has a valid license, international permit if applicable, vehicle documents and mandatory insurance. When renting, check coverage, deductible and damages, and ask for authorization if you will cross borders. Be prepared for bumps, animals, gravel and mountain curves, avoid driving at night and fill the tank in remote areas. Have cash for tolls, be courteous at checkpoints, and demand written fines. Use seat belts and child seats, do not drink alcohol, download offline maps and calculate realistic times.
