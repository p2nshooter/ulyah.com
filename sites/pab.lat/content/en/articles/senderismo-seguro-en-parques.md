Walking through a national park is one of the best ways to get to know Latin America. A trail can take you to a turquoise lagoon in the Cordillera Blanca, to the base of the granite towers of Patagonia, to a waterfall hidden in the jungle of Costa Rica, to a mist-covered wasteland in the Ecuadorian Andes or to a viewpoint over the Chapada Diamantina canyon. They are experiences that cannot be bought with a ticket: they are gained step by step.

But the mountains, the jungle and the moor have their own rules. Each year, park rangers and rescue teams tend to visitors who got lost, ran out of water, underestimated the cold, or left too late. Almost all of these problems are avoided with preparation. In this guide you will see how to choose an appropriate walk, what to bring, how to calculate times, what risks to know depending on the environment, what to do if you get lost and how to walk without leaving a trace.

## Some very visited parks

| park | Country | Keep in mind |
|---|---|---|
| Torres del Paine | Chile | strong wind, reservations required to spend the night on long circuits |
| Los Glaciares, El Chaltén sector | Argentina | very popular day trails, changeable weather |
| Huascaran | Peru | high-altitude hikes, such as Laguna 69, which require acclimatization |
| The Boxes | Ecuador | moorland with frequent fog and cold, near Cuenca |
| Tayrona | Colombia | heat, humidity and temporary closures throughout the year |
| Corcovado | Costa Rica | very remote jungle; Entry is done with a certified guide |
| Diamantina Veneer | Brazil | canyons, waterfalls and multi-day treks |
| Iguazu | Argentina and Brazil | short walkways and trails, very hot and humid |

Each park's rules change frequently: hours, spaces, reservations, fees, and trail closures. Always check official information from the park administration before going.

## Choose a walk to suit you

A well-chosen walk is the first safety measure. Before deciding, look at four facts:

1. **Total distance,** round trip.
2. **Unevenness,** that is, how many meters you go up and down. A short trail with a lot of climbing can be harder than a long, flat one.
3. **Maximum altitude:** above about 2500 meters, the body performs less until it acclimatizes, as we explain in the guide on [altitude sickness] (/bitacora/mal-de-altura-soroche/).
4. **Type of terrain:** walkways, dirt paths, loose stones, mud, river crossings or sections with chains.

Be honest with your physical condition and that of the group. The walk is marked by the slowest person, not the fastest.

## Calculate times with margin

A classic mountain reference, Naismith's rule, calculates about one hour for every five kilometers on flat terrain, plus one hour for every six hundred meters of ascent. It's just an orientation: the altitude, the heat, the mud, the weight of the backpack and the stops for photos lengthen the times a lot. Always compare with the times indicated by the park and add a margin.

- **Leave early:** it gives you plenty of daylight, more pleasant temperatures and, in the mountains, more chances of good weather.
- **Set a return time** and respect it even if you have not reached the destination.
- **Keep in mind the limit hours** for entering some trails, which exist precisely so that no one walks at night.

## Sign up and tell someone

In many parks you have to register when entering or inform the park rangers of the trail you are going to walk. It is not a procedure: it is what allows them to search for you if you do not return. Additionally:

- **Leave your plan to someone you trust:** trail, departure time and expected return time.
- **Write down the emergency number** of the country and the telephone number of the park administration.
- **Don't count on phone signal:** in many of the parks there is no coverage. On remote routes, a satellite communication device can make a difference.

## What to carry in your backpack

| Element | why |
|---|---|
| Enough water | Dehydration makes you tired, dizzy and increases the risk of accidents |
| energy food | nuts, dehydrated fruit, sandwiches or bars |
| Layers of clothing | thermal layer, coat and waterproof and windproof jacket |
| Hat, sunglasses and sunscreen | the sun is intense at altitude and in open areas |
| Headlamp | in case the walk takes longer than expected |
| Downloaded map and external battery | the phone is useful, but the battery runs out |
| Basic first aid kit | blister dressings, pain relievers, bandages and your medications |
| Whistle | can be heard much further away than the voice |
| Garbage bags | everything that enters the park comes back to you |

Also wear tested shoes before the trip: wearing new boots on a long walk is the surest way to end up with blisters. The guide on [luggage for various climates](/bitacora/equipaje-para-climas-variados/) helps you combine clothing for different climates on the same trip.

## Water: how much and from where

Calculate at least half a liter for every hour of walking in temperate conditions, and much more in heat, high elevation or altitude. Drink in sips and regularly, without waiting until you are thirsty.

Water from rivers and streams can appear clean and still contain microorganisms that cause digestive problems. If you are going to recharge on the road, treat it with a filter, water purification tablets or by boiling it. Ask the park rangers if there are safe water points.

## Risks depending on the environment

### Mountain and height

- **Cold and wind:** The temperature drops with altitude and the wind increases the feeling of cold. Wear a coat even if you go out in the sun.
- **Thunderstorms:** in many mountain areas, especially in the rainy season, storms usually form in the afternoon. If you hear thunder, get off ridges and peaks, stay away from isolated trees and metal structures, and do not take shelter under an exposed rock overhang.
- **Hypothermia:** severe shaking, clumsiness and confusion are warning signs. Seek shelter, change wet clothes, dress warmly, and drink something warm.

The guide on [Patagonia: climate and wind](/bitacora/patagonia-clima-y-viento/) expands on how to walk in one of the most changeable climates on the continent, and the one on [visiting volcanoes](/bitacora/visitar-volcanes/) addresses the risks inherent to those mountains.

### Jungle and tropical forest

- **Heat and humidity:** Sweating is intense and dehydration comes quickly. Walk slowly and drink frequently.
- **Mud and roots:** The trails are slippery, especially after the rain. Wear shoes with good soles.
- **Insects:** use repellent, long clothing and check your body when finished for ticks.
- **Floods:** An easy river to cross in the morning can become dangerous after heavy rain upstream. Do not cross if the water rises, runs strongly or reaches above the knee.

More information in the guide about [travel to the Amazon](/bitacora/viajar-a-la-amazonia/).

### Moorland, desert and open areas

- **Fog:** On moors and highlands, fog can erase the trail in minutes. Follow the markings, don't go off the path and, if you lose the reference, stop.
- **Intense sun:** without shade, the risk of sunstroke is high. Protect your head and neck.
- **Thermal amplitude:** in the desert and the highlands, the midday heat can give way to very cold nights.

## Fauna: respect and distance

In most parks, the biggest risk with wildlife is for the animals themselves: being fed, disturbed or run over. Observe them from a distance, do not give them food and keep yours tightly closed.

In tropical areas there are poisonous snakes. Accidents are rare if you follow a few guidelines: walk on the trail, watch where you step and where you put your hands, wear closed-toed shoes and a flashlight if you walk at dusk. If someone is bitten, keep them calm and still, remove rings or watches from the affected limb and seek medical attention as soon as possible, because the treatment is done in a health center. Do not cut the wound, suction out the poison, or apply tourniquets or ice.

If you are interested in wildlife, the guide on [bird watching](/bitacora/observacion-de-aves/) explains how to enjoy it without disturbing it.

## If you get lost

Getting lost is usually not serious if you react well. An easy guideline to remember:

1. **Stop.** Continuing to walk aimlessly tends to take you further away.
2. **Think.** When was the last time you saw a mark or sign?
3. **Observe.** Look at the map, the surroundings, the position of the sun and what time it is.
4. **Plan.** If you are sure, retrace your steps to the last known landmark. If you are not, stay where you are, in a visible place.

If you must wait, dress warmly, ration water, make yourself visible with colorful clothing and use the whistle: three short repeated signals are a recognized distress call in the mountains. If whoever is waiting for you knows your plan, the search will start where it should.

## Guides: when to hire one

In some parks and trails, going with a guide is mandatory. In others, it is simply a good idea: on journeys of several days, on poorly marked routes, in dense jungle or if you have no experience. A local guide knows the terrain and the climate, knows what to do in the event of an unexpected event, and shows you things you wouldn't see on your own. Choose guides accredited by the park or tourism authorities, and agree in advance on the route, duration and what is included in the price.

## Walk without a trace

The parks suffer from the number of visitors. A few practices, known internationally as "leave no trace" principles, help preserve them:

- **Plan and prepare** so as not to improvise on the ground.
- **Walk on marked trails** and do not take shortcuts, which cause erosion.
- **Take all your trash,** also fruit peels and food scraps.
- **Leave what you find:** stones, flowers, feathers or seeds stay in the park.
- **Light fire only where permitted,** or better yet, don't do it.
- **Respect the fauna** and do not feed it.
- **Respect other visitors:** turn down the volume and give way on climbs.

For physiological needs, use the park's bathrooms. If there are none, stay at least sixty meters away from rivers, lakes and trails, and carry the paper in a bag.

## Frequent errors

- **Leave late** and walk back at night.
- **Carry little water** or not have a way to make it drinkable.
- **Go with cotton clothing** that, when wet, cools the body.
- **Brand new shoes** on a long walk.
- **Do not register** or notify anyone about the tour.
- **Continue climbing** in bad weather or after the return time.
- **Take shortcuts** off the trail.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I hike alone?

On popular, marked trails and with other visitors, many people do it without problem. On remote, long or high mountain routes, it is much safer to go accompanied or with a guide. If you go alone, always leave your plan with someone and check in at the park.

### What footwear do I need?

For simple trails, trekking shoes with good soles are enough. For loose stone terrain, mud or treks with a heavy backpack, boots that support the ankle provide more stability.

### How much water should I bring?

For reference, at least half a liter per hour in temperate weather, and more in heat or altitude. Find out if there are water spots on the road and how to treat it.

### What do I do if a storm starts?

Get off high, exposed areas, stay away from isolated trees and metal objects, and wait in a lower area for it to pass. If the storm surprises you near the trailhead, the wisest thing to do is to turn around.

## In summary

Choose walks according to your physical condition, looking at distance, gradient, altitude and terrain, and calculate the times with a margin. Leave early, set a return time, check in at the park, and leave your plan with someone. Bring water and a way to make it drinkable, food, layers of clothing, flashlight, downloaded map, first aid kit and whistle. Know the risks of each environment, from storms in the mountains to floods in the jungle, and if you get lost, stop and think before moving. Hire accredited guides when necessary and walk without a trace so that the parks remain what we came for.
