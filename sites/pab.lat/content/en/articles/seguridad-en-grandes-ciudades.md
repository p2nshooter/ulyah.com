Mexico City, Bogotá, Lima, Santiago, Buenos Aires, São Paulo or Rio de Janeiro are some of the most vibrant cities in the world: extraordinary museums, neighborhoods full of life, top-notch cuisine and a cultural offer that does not end in a trip. Millions of visitors visit them every year without any problem. But they are also enormous metropolises, with marked inequalities and crimes that, for the most part, affect those who are distracted: the theft of a phone, a purse ripped off on a corner or a well-rehearsed scam.

The good news is that almost all of these risks are greatly reduced with a few habits. It is not about traveling with fear, but with the same attention that you would have in any big city in the world. In this guide you will see how to choose where to stay, how to get around, how to protect your phone and your money, what scams you should know about, how to enjoy the night, what to do in the event of an earthquake and how to act if you are robbed.

## Put it in perspective

Most incidents suffered by visitors are **crimes of opportunity**: someone sees a phone in their hand, an open backpack or a wallet on the table and takes advantage of a second of distraction. Violence against tourists is much less frequent and is usually concentrated at specific times and places, which the inhabitants know well.

Therefore, the best source of information is the local people: your host, the accommodation staff or a guide. Ask which areas to avoid and at what times, how to get to a neighborhood or what transportation to use at night. Also check the travel recommendations published by your country's government, which are updated frequently.

## Choose where to stay

The neighborhood where you sleep determines a good part of the trip.

- **Look for areas with street life,** shops, restaurants and good lighting, well connected to transportation.
- **Read recent reviews** that mention what the area is like at night.
- **Check arrival:** If you land at night, book the transfer or be clear about how to get there with safe transportation.
- **Upon arrival, ask the accommodation** for a map or recommendations on where to walk.

The guide on [choosing safe accommodation](/bitacora/elegir-alojamiento-seguro/) expands on what to check before booking.

## Getting around the city

### Taxis and apps

- **Use official taxis,** those ordered by phone or from the accommodation, or transportation applications, which record the trip, the driver and the car.
- **At airports and terminals,** hire transportation at authorized counters, not with whoever meets you at the door.
- **Check your car's license plate and model** before getting in when using an app.
- **Share the journey** with someone you trust from the application.
- **Avoid hailing taxis on the street at night** in cities where residents advise against it.

In some cities, so-called express kidnappings have been recorded, in which a person is held for a while to force them to withdraw money from ATMs. Using registered transportation greatly reduces that risk.

### Metro and urban buses

The metro and rapid bus systems in many capitals are efficient and economical. At peak times, however, they fill up, and crowds are ideal terrain for pickpockets.

- **Carry your backpack in front** and keep your phone stored in full cars.
- **Be alert when getting on and off,** which is when robberies usually occur.
- **Avoid peak congestion hours** if you carry luggage.
- **Some metros have exclusive carriages or sectors** for women and children at certain times, such as Mexico City.

### on foot

Walking is the best way to get to know a city. Walk with a sure step, like someone who knows where you are going, consult the map inside a shop or a cafe instead of in the middle of the sidewalk and, if a street is empty or you don't like it, turn around. More ideas in the guide about [touring a historic center](/bitacora/recorrer-un-centro-historico/).

## The telephone: the most coveted object

In almost all big cities, the phone is the most stolen thing. In some, like Buenos Aires or Bogotá, there are even words for the outburst from a motorcycle or while passing, such as motochorro or raponazo.

- **Do not walk with your phone in your hand** along the edge of the sidewalk.
- **Do not leave it on the table** on terraces and outdoor cafes.
- **Use wired headphones or a single earbud** to continue hearing what's going on around you.
- **Activate remote lock and backup** before traveling, and write down the device's IMEI number.
- **Consider carrying a simpler secondary phone** for everyday use.

## Bags, backpacks and wallets

- **Crossbody bag on the front,** with the zipper closed.
- **Backpack in front** in crowds and public transportation.
- **In restaurants,** loop the bag strap over your leg or chair; do not hang it on the backrest.
- **Spread the money:** some in your wallet for the day, the rest in an inside pocket or in your accommodation.
- **Do not display jewelry, expensive watches** or large cameras hanging around your neck in crowded areas.

## Scams and distraction tricks

| Situation | How it works | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| The stain | someone "tells you" that your clothes were dirty and offers to clean you up while an accomplice takes your things | walk away, check your belongings, and clean up later in a safe place |
| The fake police | a person claiming to be an agent asks to check your wallet or money "for counterfeiting" | asks for identification and suggests going to a police station; a real police officer won't ask you for your cash on the street |
| Help at the cashier | someone offers to help you with the machine or distracts you while you type the password | refuses help and cancels the operation if anyone approaches |
| The fallen coin | someone drops something near you so you duck or look | Hold your belongings and continue on your way |
| The taxi without a meter | the price is "decided" upon arrival | Agree on the price beforehand or use official apps and taxis |

None of these situations require violence: they work because the traveler is distracted. Knowing that they exist is the best defense.

## ATMs and money

Use ATMs inside banks, shopping centers or guarded places, during the day, and save the money before leaving. Take only what you need for the day and leave the rest in the accommodation's safe. We see it in detail in the guide on [money, cards and change](/bitacora/dinero-tarjetas-y-cambio/).

## the night

The big Latin American cities have an intense nightlife, and enjoying it is part of the trip.

- **Plan how to get back** before leaving: transportation app, local taxi or nearby accommodation.
- **Do not leave your glass unattended** or accept drinks from strangers. In some cities, cases of drinks adulterated with substances that leave the victim disoriented have been reported.
- **If you meet people through dating apps,** meet in public and crowded places, let someone know where you are and don't bring strangers to your accommodation.
- **Moderate alcohol:** Most nighttime problems start when you let your guard down.
- **Carry little money and only one card** when you go out at night.

## Parties and large events

Carnivals, soccer matches, concerts and patron saint celebrations bring together huge, happy crowds that are also taken advantage of by pickpockets. Bring the minimum, agree with your group on a meeting point in case they get separated and locate the exits. The guide on [festivals and carnivals](/bitacora/fiestas-y-carnavales/) expands on these ideas.

## Demonstrations

Protests and marches are frequent in many capitals and are usually peaceful, but they can lead to clashes, street closures and station closures. Avoid getting close, don't take photos in the midst of tension, and follow local news. In some countries, the law also restricts the participation of foreigners in political activities.

## Earthquakes and other natural risks

Many large cities in the region, such as Mexico City, Santiago, Lima, Quito or Bogotá, are in seismic zones. Modern buildings are usually built with anti-seismic standards, and the population is accustomed to drills.

- **Upon arrival, identify the exits** and safe areas of your accommodation.
- **During an earthquake,** crouch, take cover under sturdy furniture or protect your head, and hold on until it passes. Don't run into the street while it's shaking.
- **On the coast,** if you feel a strong and prolonged earthquake, move away from the sea to high areas and follow the tsunami evacuation signs.
- **Pay attention to alerts:** Mexico City, for example, has a seismic alert system with speakers in the street.

## Digital security

- **Avoid banking on public Wi-Fi networks** or use a secure connection.
- **Do not post your location in real time** on social networks; Share the photos later.
- **Save digital copies** of your passport and documents in a secure location in the cloud.

## If they rob you

1. **Do not resist.** No object is worth more than your integrity.
2. **Go to a safe place** and ask for help.
3. **Block cards and phone** from another device or by calling your bank and your operator.
4. **Make a police report:** you will need it for insurance. In several cities there are police stations or tourist police units that serve visitors.
5. **If you lost your passport,** contact your consulate to process a travel document. We explain it in the guide on [travel documents](/bitacora/documentos-de-viaje/).
6. **Notify your insurance** and keep all receipts. More information in the guide about [travel insurance](/bitacora/seguro-de-viaje/).

## Emergency numbers

| Country | Main number |
|---|---|
| Mexico, Costa Rica, Panama, Ecuador, Uruguay | 911 |
| Colombia | 123 |
| Peru | 105, police |
| Chile | 133, police; 131, ambulance |
| Argentina | 911, police in many cities; 107, medical emergencies |
| Brazil | 190, police; 192, ambulance; 193, firefighters |

Numbers may vary by city or change over time. Confirm them when you arrive and save them on your phone along with your insurance assistance and your consulate.

## Frequent errors

- **Walk with the phone in your hand** along the road.
- **Hang your bag on the back of the chair** in restaurants.
- **Get into unregistered taxis** at airports or at night.
- **Accept help from strangers** at ATMs.
- **Carry all the money and all the cards** with you.
- **Resist theft.**

## Frequently asked questions

### Are the big cities of Latin America dangerous?

Each city is different, and within each one there are very different neighborhoods. Most visitors do not experience any incidents. The most common risks are theft of opportunity, which is prevented with simple habits and local information.

### Is it better to use transportation apps or taxis?

Both options are good if they are safe: official taxis ordered by phone or from the accommodation, or applications that record the journey. The least advisable thing is to get into unmarked vehicles that approach you on the street.

### Can I use the phone on the street?

Yes, with common sense: check it in sheltered places, don't carry it in your hand next to the sidewalk, and keep it in crowds and on public transportation.

### What do I do if I feel followed?

Go into a busy shop, restaurant or hotel, ask the staff for help and wait there. If necessary, call a taxi or app from within.

## In summary

Large Latin American cities can be enjoyed with simple habits: stay in lively and well-connected areas, use official taxis or applications, take care of your phone and bag, be wary of distractions and use safe ATMs. At night, plan your return and watch your drink. Avoid protests, learn what to do in the event of an earthquake and protect your data. If you are robbed, do not resist, block cards, file a report and notify your consulate and your insurance. And above all, ask the locals: no one knows their city better.
