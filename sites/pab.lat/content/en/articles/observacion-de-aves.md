A hummingbird that stops in the air in front of a flower, a quetzal with a very long tail perched on an aguacatillo tree, a flock of red macaws that arrives at dawn on a clay wall in the Amazon, a condor that hovers without moving its wings over an Andean canyon or thousands of pink flamingos in a lagoon in the highlands. For those who enjoy nature, Latin America is the place in the world with the most birds, and observing them becomes a wonderful way to travel.

You don't need to be an expert to enjoy it. With binoculars, some patience, and the help of a good local guide, any traveler can see dozens of species in a single day. In this guide you will see why the region is so rich in birds, what species and destinations stand out, when to go, what equipment to bring, how to start if you are a beginner and what ethical rules to respect so as not to disturb the birds.

## Why the region is a bird paradise

The Neotropical region, which encompasses Latin America and the Caribbean, is home to approximately one-third of the world's known bird species. Several factors explain it:

- **The Andes mountain range**, which creates in a short distance an enormous variety of climatic zones, from low jungles to paramos.
- **The Amazon,** the largest tropical forest on the planet.
- **The isolation of mountains and islands,** which gave rise to species that only exist in one place, called endemics.
- **Migratory routes:** Millions of North American birds spend the winter in the region.

Colombia, Peru, Brazil and Ecuador are among the countries with the most bird species in the world, and on global counting days, such as Global Big Day, countries like Colombia and Peru are usually among those that record the most species in a single day.

## When to go

- **All year round:** in tropical areas there are birds to see at any time.
- **Migrant season:** From approximately October to April, many North American birds join the resident species in Mexico, Central America, the Caribbean and northern South America.
- **Dry season:** Trails are firmer and easier to navigate, although in some places the rainy season coincides with more reproductive activity.
- **Early hours of the day:** Birds are most active at dawn and in the early hours of the morning. In the afternoon there is usually another, shorter peak of activity.

## Basic equipment

- **Binoculars:** the essential equipment. Many observers choose eight or ten times magnification models with objectives of around forty-two millimeters, which offer good luminosity in the forest.
- **Field guide or applications:** Illustrated guides for each country and identification applications by image or by song help a lot. Download the region's data before traveling, because there is usually no signal in the countryside.
- **Notebook:** writing down what you see helps you remember and learn.
- **Clothes in neutral colors** that attract little attention.
- **Waterproof jacket,** because in the cloud forests and jungle it rains often.
- **Repellent, sunscreen and hat.**
- **Camera with zoom,** if you are interested in photography.

## How to start if you are a beginner

1. **Start with the common birds** in the garden of your accommodation or a park: learning to recognize them will give you a base.
2. **Look before searching the guide:** Note the size, beak shape, colors, behavior and song.
3. **Learn families:** Recognizing a hummingbird, a tanager, a woodpecker or a raptor is the first step.
4. **Go out with a local guide** at least once – you'll learn more in a morning than you will in weeks on your own.
5. **Be patient:** Sometimes, standing still next to a tree with fruits gives more results than walking a lot.

## Local guides and reservations

In the region there are many guides specialized in birds, often members of local communities who know every song and every corner of the forest. Hiring them is the best way to see difficult species while supporting rural economies that depend on conservation. Many private and community reserves charge an entrance fee which helps protect the forest. The guide on [community tourism and cultural respect](/bitacora/turismo-comunitario-y-respeto-cultural/) expands on this idea.

## Feeders and feeding stations

In many reserves and lodges there are hummingbird feeders with sugar water, or tables with fruit for tanagers and other birds, which allow you to see them up close and photograph them. Although they are a great opportunity, they must be well managed: frequent cleaning, adequate proportions and without replacing natural food. Do not feed birds on your own during walks.

## The ethics of the observer

- **The well-being of the birds comes first,** above the photo or adding a species to your list.
- **Do not approach the nests** or photograph them closely: stress can cause the parents to abandon them.
- **Use song recordings very sparingly** to attract birds, or do not use them at all, especially during the breeding season and with threatened species. In some reserves it is prohibited.
- **No flash** with nocturnal birds, such as owls or nictibians.
- **Walk the trails** and respect private property.
- **Be silent:** in addition to disturbing less, you will hear much more.

## bird photography

The best photos usually come with patience, not with chases. Find a place with good light, near a tree with fruit, a feeder or a clearing, and wait. Use high shutter speeds to freeze motion, and keep in mind that light is poor in the forest. Share your photos without revealing the exact location of nests or very sensitive species.

## Birds in the city

You don't have to leave the city to get started. The wetlands of Bogotá, the Villa swamps in Lima or the Costanera Sur ecological reserve in Buenos Aires, among many other urban spaces, host hundreds of species. They are a good way to take advantage of a free morning before traveling to the countryside.

## Citizen science

Observation recording platforms allow anyone to contribute their bird lists to databases used by scientists and conservationists around the world. Recording what you see, with the date and place, turns your trip into a small contribution to the knowledge of birds in the region.

## Health and safety

- **Altitude:** in places like the Colca Canyon or the highland lagoons, acclimatize beforehand, as we explain in the guide on [altitude sickness] (/bitacora/mal-de-altura-soroche/).
- **Jungle:** use repellent, long clothing and closed shoes, as we explain in the guide on [traveling to the Amazon](/bitacora/viajar-a-la-amazonia/).
- **Departures at dawn:** bring a headlamp and notify your accommodation of your route.
- **Trails:** Follow the guidelines in the guide on [safe hiking in parks](/bitacora/senderismo-seguro-en-parques/).

## Frequent errors

- **Go out to observe at midday,** when the birds are less active.
- **Wear brightly colored clothing** and speak loudly.
- **Abusing singing recordings.**
- **Get closer to the nests** for a photo.
- **Never hire a local guide,** who multiplies what you will see.
- **Do not download applications and maps** before reaching areas without signal.

## Frequently asked questions

### What binoculars do I need to get started?

Eight-fold binoculars with objectives of about forty-two millimeters are a very versatile option. The important thing is that they are comfortable, bright and that you always have them at hand.

### What is the best country for bird watching?

Colombia, Peru, Ecuador, Brazil and Costa Rica are excellent destinations, each with its specialties. Costa Rica offers a lot of infrastructure in a small space; Colombia and Peru, enormous diversity.

### Can I watch birds without a guide?

Yes, especially the common species. But a local guide will help you find difficult species and learn their songs.

### Is it an activity for children?

Yes. With suitable binoculars and short outings, children usually have a lot of fun looking for hummingbirds, parrots or toucans.

## In summary

Latin America is home to about a third of the world's birds, with such emblematic species as the condor, the quetzal, hummingbirds, macaws and highland flamingos. Choose destinations such as the cloud forests of Ecuador and Costa Rica, the Peruvian Amazon, the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta or the Pantanal, go out at dawn, bring binoculars and downloaded applications and hire local guides. Observe ethically: away from the nests, without abusing recorded songs, without flash and in silence.
