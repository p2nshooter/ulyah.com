Few regions in the world have a cuisine as diverse as Latin America. On the same trip you can have an arepa for breakfast in Bogotá, a ceviche for lunch in Lima, a salteña snack in La Paz and a barbecue for dinner in Buenos Aires, and each of those dishes tells a different story: that of the native peoples who domesticated corn and potatoes, that of the conquerors and immigrants who arrived later, that of the enslaved African people who brought techniques and ingredients, and that of each region that adapted all of this to its climate and its products.

Trying regional dishes is one of the best ways to get to know a country. In this guide you will see what ingredients America contributed to the world, what dishes to try in each country, which ones are only prepared on certain dates, how the names of foods change from one country to another and how to order and eat like the locals.

## Ingredients that America gave to the world

Many foods that today seem universal were born in America and spread throughout the planet after the sixteenth century:

- **Corn:** the basis of the diet of Mesoamerica and much of the Andes.
- **Potato:** domesticated in the Andes, where thousands of varieties are grown today.
- **Tomato, chili or chili, beans, pumpkins and avocado.**
- **Cocoa and vanilla.**
- **Quinoa, amaranth, yucca, peanuts and many tropical fruits.**

Visiting an Andean or Mexican market is the best way to see that diversity, as we explain in the guide on [traditional markets](/bitacora/mercados-tradicionales/).

## A meeting kitchen

Over the centuries, ingredients and techniques from other parts of the world were added to this native base:

- **Europe:** wheat, rice, beef and pork, dairy, citrus and onion, as well as techniques such as frying and baking.
- **Africa:** palm oil, okra, the use of coconut and many preparations from the Caribbean, the Pacific coast of Colombia and Ecuador and the northeast of Brazil.
- **Asia:** in Peru, Chinese immigration gave rise to Chifa cuisine and Japanese immigration gave rise to Nikkei cuisine.
- **Middle East:** in Mexico, Lebanese immigration inspired Arab tacos and, according to the most widespread explanation, the trompo of tacos al pastor.
- **Italy:** in Argentina and Uruguay, pastas, pizzas and customs such as eating gnocchi on the 29th of each month.

## Kitchens recognized as heritage

UNESCO has recognized some culinary traditions of the region as intangible cultural heritage, including traditional Mexican cuisine and the practices associated with Peruvian ceviche. In Brazil, the profession of the acarajé sellers of Bahia is protected as national heritage. Beyond the recognition, each country has regional cuisines that are worth the trip on their own.

## Seasonal and festive dishes

Some dishes are only found at certain times of the year:

- **Chiles en nogada, in Mexico:** stuffed chiles covered with walnut and pomegranate sauce, typical of Puebla, especially between August and September.
- **Fanesca, in Ecuador:** thick soup of tender grains and cod, prepared at Holy Week.
- **Colada morada and guaguas de pan, in Ecuador:** for the Day of the Dead, at the beginning of November.
- **Hallacas, in Venezuela:** the emblematic dish of Christmas.
- **Locro, in Argentina:** closely associated with national holidays, such as May 25.
- **Tamales and bread of the dead, in Mexico:** in celebrations such as the Day of the Dead.

Also be careful with words that change meaning: in Mexico, a cake is a sandwich, while in other countries it is a cake; and the Mexican tortilla is a piece of corn dough, while in the Southern Cone it usually refers to a potato tortilla with egg.

## How to order and eat like a local

- **Lunch is the main meal** in many countries. The menu of the day, which goes by different names, such as Comida Corrida in Mexico, Corrientazo or Executive Lunch in Colombia or Prato Feito in Brazil, usually includes soup, main course and drink at a good price.
- **Times change:** in Argentina, Uruguay and Chile you have a late dinner, often after nine at night; In other countries you have dinner before.
- **"Bon appetite":** in many places it is customary to say it to those who are eating when entering or leaving a dining room.
- **Ask for recommendations:** Ask the waiter what is typical of the house or the region.
- **Eating on the street and in the markets** is part of the experience, with the precautions that we explain in the guide on [safe street food](/bitacora/comida-callejera-segura/).
- **Tipping** varies by country; We see it in the guide on [money, cards and change](/bitacora/dinero-tarjetas-y-cambio/).

## Special diets and allergies

- **Vegetarians:** in almost all countries you will find rice, beans, eggs, cheeses, vegetables and fruits, and in big cities there are more and more vegetarian restaurants. Keep in mind that many soups and beans are made with broth or meat fat; always ask.
- **Gluten-free:** Cuisines based on corn, potatoes and cassava make things easier, but cross-contamination is common.
- **Allergies:** Bring a card written in Spanish or Portuguese that explains your allergy, and ask about the ingredients, especially peanuts, shellfish, and tree nuts.

## Frequent errors

- **Look only for international food** and miss out on local cuisine.
- **Order ceviche at night** in places where the tradition is to eat it at midday, when the fish is freshest.
- **Assume that a dish is called the same** in all countries.
- **Don't ask about the spice** if you can't tolerate it.
- **Ignore seasonal dishes,** which are only available on certain dates.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Latin American food too spicy?

It depends on the country and the dish. Mexican and some Andean cuisine uses a lot of chili or chili, but the spice is often served separately in sauces. In the Southern Cone, the food is usually not very spicy.

### Which country has the best gastronomy?

Every country has extraordinary dishes. Mexico and Peru are famous for the variety of their cuisines, but the best answer is to try a little of everything.

### Is it safe to eat in markets?

In general, yes, choosing stalls with a lot of customers, freshly prepared food and good hygiene.

### How do I find authentic restaurants?

Ask locals where they eat, look for eateries full of locals at lunchtime, and move a couple of blocks away from the more touristy areas.

## In summary

Latin American cuisine combines native ingredients such as corn, potatoes, chili, beans and cocoa with contributions from Europe, Africa, Asia and the Middle East. Each country has emblematic dishes, from Mexican tacos and mole to pupusas, tumba paisa, ceviche, salteñas, asado or feijoada. Look for seasonal dishes, learn how food names change, eat the daily menu for lunch, ask about ingredients if you have any restrictions, and eat where the locals eat.
