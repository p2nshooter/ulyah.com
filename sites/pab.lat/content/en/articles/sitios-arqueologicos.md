Few places in the world concentrate as many traces of ancient civilizations as Latin America. The pyramids of Teotihuacan, the Mayan cities of Tikal, Palenque or Copán, the Inca citadel of Machu Picchu, the ancient Caral, the mud city of Chan Chan, Tiwanaku next to Lake Titicaca, the Lost City in the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta or the moai of Rapa Nui are extraordinary testimonies of people who built cities, observatories and temples with knowledge that still astonishes.

Visiting them is one of the great moments of any trip, but it requires planning. Many have limited spaces, strict schedules and rules to protect fragile structures. Some are in the jungle, others at high altitude, and almost all require walking in the sun. In this guide you will see how to organize the visit, how to choose a guide, what to bring and what rules to respect so that these places continue standing for future generations.

## Tickets and places: buy in advance

At the most famous sites, the number of visitors per day is limited to protect the structures. That means:

- **Buy tickets in advance** on official channels, especially in high season.
- **Respect the schedule** that appears on the ticket: in some places it is not allowed to enter outside the reserved time slot.
- **Choose the circuit or route** when there are several options.
- **Bring the identification document** with which you bought the ticket.

Be wary of resellers and unofficial pages. Conditions, prices and schedules change frequently, so confirm them from official sources before traveling.

## With a guide or on your own

A good guide transforms the visit: he explains what is seen, tells the archaeologists' hypotheses, points out details that would go unnoticed and helps to understand the culture that built the place.

- **Hire accredited guides** by the tourism or culture authority. They usually carry a credential.
- **Agree on the price, duration and language beforehand.**
- **In some places the guided tour is mandatory,** or it is in certain areas.
- **If you go on your own,** read about the site first or use the official information available.

## How to read an archaeological site

At first, many ruins look like a group of stones that is difficult to interpret. Some keys help to organize it:

- **Plazas:** were meeting, market and ceremony spaces, and usually organized the rest of the site.
- **Temples and pyramids:** In Mesoamerica, many pyramids served as the base of temples and were built in stages, one on top of the other.
- **Palaces and residential complexes:** show how elites and sometimes common people lived.
- **Ball games:** elongated fields with inclined walls, typical of Mesoamerica, linked to rituals and political life.
- **Terraces or platforms:** in the Andes, they stepped the slopes to cultivate and control erosion.
- **Depots and canals:** food warehouses and water systems that reveal a great capacity for organization.

Ask the guide about the function of each space and how the site was dated: understanding the context completely changes the visit.

## Combine several sites in one trip

Visiting two great sites in a row, under the sun and with long transfers, can exhaust anyone. Alternate sites with days of rest, towns, markets or museums, and leave the most important ones for when you are already acclimatized and rested. You will enjoy each place more.

## Photography at the sites

Early light is best for photographing stones and reliefs. Tripods, large stabilizers, and commercial recording require permission in many places. Photograph with respect, without climbing walls or invading closed areas, and remember that in sacred places there may be ceremonies that should not be photographed.

## Heat, jungle and height

- **Arrive early:** first thing in the morning it is less hot, there are fewer people and the light is more beautiful.
- **Sun and heat:** in places like Chichén Itzá, Teotihuacan or Chan Chan there is almost no shade. Bring a hat, sunscreen and plenty of water.
- **Jungle:** in Tikal or Palenque there is humidity, insects and irregular paths. Use repellent and closed shoes.
- **Height:** Machu Picchu is about 2,400 meters and Tiwanaku is more than 3,800. Acclimatize beforehand, as we explain in the guide on [altitude sickness] (/bitacora/mal-de-altura-soroche/).
- **Rain:** In the rainy season, some trails become slippery and certain accesses may be closed.

## What to bring

- Printed ticket or on the phone, and identification document.
- Enough water and something to eat, if allowed.
- Hat, sunglasses and sunscreen.
- Comfortable and closed footwear, with good sole.
- Lightweight raincoat in the rainy season.
- Repellent in jungle areas.
- A small backpack: Some places do not allow large backpacks.

## Rules to protect heritage

- **Do not climb structures** where it is prohibited, nor sit on old walls.
- **Do not touch reliefs, paintings or sculptures:** the grease from your hands damages them.
- **Do not take stones or fragments,** no matter how small they may be.
- **Walk only on marked trails.**
- **Do not use drones** without authorization: in most places they are prohibited.
- **Do not leave trash** and respect food regulations.
- **Do not buy supposed archaeological pieces:** their sale is illegal in many countries and most are fakes.

## Sacred places and living towns

Many of these sites are not just ruins: they remain sacred places for indigenous peoples today. The Lost City, for example, is in the territory of towns in the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, which close it to visitors at certain times of the year. Respect the ceremonies, community instructions and access restrictions. The guide on [community tourism and cultural respect](/bitacora/turismo-comunitario-y-respeto-cultural/) expands on these ideas.

## Astronomical phenomena and crowds

Some sites attract crowds on special dates, such as the equinoxes, when sunlight creates visual effects on certain buildings. If you want to experience them, prepare for a lot of people and arrive very early. If you prefer tranquility, avoid those dates.

## With children or people with reduced mobility

Big sites involve walking several kilometers, often with uneven steps and no shade. With children, bring water, food and patience, and consider the visit as an adventure with breaks. For people with reduced mobility, find out in advance about accessible routes: some places have adapted sections, but many do not.

## Frequent errors

- **Do not buy tickets on time** in high season.
- **Arrive at noon** to places without shade.
- **Climb prohibited structures** for a photo.
- **Hire non-accredited guides** without agreeing on conditions.
- **Ignore height** in Andean sites.
- **Buy supposedly archaeological pieces.**

## Frequently asked questions

### How far in advance do I buy the entrance to Machu Picchu?

In high season, it is advisable to do it weeks or months in advance, and even earlier if you want to do the Inca Trail, whose permits sell out quickly. Check the conditions on the official channels.

### Can you climb the pyramids?

It depends on the site and the regulations in force, which change to protect the structures. In many important sites, such as Chichén Itzá, climbing is prohibited.

### Is it better to visit in the dry or rainy season?

The dry season offers firmer trails and clearer skies, but also more visitors. The rainy season brings greener landscapes and fewer people, with the risk of downpours, mud and some closures. Choose based on your tolerance for weather and crowds.

### How much time do I need at a large archaeological site?

For the largest ones, like Tikal or Teotihuacan, at least half a day; many people dedicate a full day. For smaller sites, two or three hours may be enough.

## In summary

Buy tickets in advance through official channels, respect the schedules and circuits and consider an accredited guide. Arrive early, protect yourself from the sun, the jungle or the altitude depending on the location, and bring water, appropriate footwear and just enough in a small backpack. Follow the rules: do not climb where it is prohibited, do not touch or take anything, do not use drones without permission and do not buy archaeological pieces. Remember that many sites remain sacred to indigenous peoples and visit them with the respect they deserve.
