Most trips through Latin America pass without any health problems other than a sunburn, a couple of days of stomach upset or the fatigue of the first days at altitude. But the region is huge and diverse: in the same itinerary you can go from a city at 2800 meters to a tropical beach, and from there to the Amazon jungle, where the risks are very different. Preparing in advance allows you to enjoy the trip with peace of mind and react well if something goes wrong.

In this guide you will see when to see a doctor before traveling, what vaccines are usually considered, how to protect yourself from mosquitoes, what precautions to take with water and food, what to pack in your first aid kit, and what to do if you get sick during or after your trip. It is an informative guide: the specific recommendations depend on your health, your itinerary and the current health situation, and only a professional can give you personalized advice.

## Consult a travel doctor ahead of time

The ideal is to make an appointment at a travel medicine center or with your doctor **at least a month before leaving, and preferably between four and eight weeks before.** Some vaccines require several doses or take time to take effect, and others require time to obtain the certificate.

Take to the consultation:

- **Your detailed itinerary:** countries, regions, rural or jungle areas, altitude and time of year.
- **Your vaccination history,** if you have it.
- **Your chronic diseases, allergies and medications.**
- **The planned activities:** walks, diving, contact with animals, volunteering, long trips or stays in remote areas.

If you are pregnant or plan to be, traveling with babies or elderly people, or have a weakened immune system, prior consultation is even more important.

## Yellow fever and the international certificate

Yellow fever is a viral disease transmitted by mosquitoes, present in jungle and transition areas of several South American countries, such as parts of Brazil, Colombia, Peru, Bolivia and Ecuador, and in some areas of Panama, Argentina and Paraguay, among others. There is a very effective vaccine.

- **Some countries require a vaccination certificate** for travelers arriving from countries with risk of transmission, and others recommend it to visit certain regions, such as the Amazon.
- **The international vaccination certificate,** often called the yellow card, is the document that certifies it. It is considered valid from ten days after vaccination.
- **According to the World Health Organization, a single dose protects for life** in most people, although some countries may have their own requirements.
- **Not all people can be vaccinated:** there are contraindications for certain groups, which the doctor will assess.

Requirements change, so check the requirements of each country on your route, including transit requirements, and keep the certificate with your passport. We also mention it in the guide on [travel documents](/bitacora/documentos-de-viaje/).

## Other vaccines commonly considered

| Vaccine | When is it usually considered |
|---|---|
| Routine vaccinations | measles, tetanus and diphtheria, and the others on your country's calendar, up to date for all travelers |
| Hepatitis A | frequent for travelers to the region; It is transmitted through contaminated water and food |
| typhoid fever | trips to rural areas, long stays or when eating out frequently |
| Hepatitis B | long stays, healthcare work, possible medical treatments or tattoos |
| Anger, before traveling | long stays, remote areas far from medical care, contact with animals or cyclists and walkers in rural areas |
| Flu and other respiratory vaccines | according to the season and current recommendations |
| Dengue | There is a vaccine approved in several countries, with specific indications that the doctor must evaluate |

The table is indicative. The professional will decide what is best for you based on your history and your route.

## Mosquitoes: the most important protection

In much of the tropical and subtropical areas of the region, mosquitoes transmit diseases such as **dengue, chikungunya and Zika,** and in some specific areas, **malaria** and **yellow fever.** There are no vaccines for everyone, so avoiding bites is the main prevention measure.

| Disease | Where and when | What is good to know |
|---|---|---|
| Dengue | widespread in tropical and subtropical areas, also in cities | the mosquito that transmits it bites mainly during the day |
| Chikungunya | tropical areas | can cause severe and prolonged joint pain |
| Zika | tropical areas | especially relevant in pregnancy; consult before traveling |
| Malaria | specific areas, especially in the Amazon basin and some rural areas | the risk is low in most tourist destinations; The doctor will assess if you need preventive medication |

**How to avoid bites:**

- **Use repellent** on exposed skin. The most recommended by health authorities contain DEET, icaridin, IR3535 or lemon eucalyptus oil. Follow the instructions on the packaging, especially with children.
- **Apply sunscreen first** and then repellent.
- **Wear light-colored long-sleeved clothing** and long pants at dawn, dusk, and in jungle areas.
- **Sleep under a mosquito net** if the accommodation does not have screened windows or air conditioning.
- **Eliminate standing water** that you may see near your accommodation.

The guide on [traveling to the Amazon](/bitacora/viajar-a-la-amazonia/) expands precautions for the jungle.

## Water and food

Digestive problems are the most common discomfort among travelers. To reduce risk:

- **Water:** in some cities tap water is drinkable; In many other places it is advisable to drink bottled, filtered or boiled water. Ask at your accommodation.
- **Ice:** in trusted places it is usually done with treated water; in street stalls, better to avoid it if you have doubts.
- **Food:** Prefer dishes cooked and served hot, fruit that you peel yourself, and stalls with a high turnover of customers.
- **Hands:** wash them before eating or use disinfectant gel.

We see it in detail in the guide on [safe street food](/bitacora/comida-callejera-segura/).

### If you have diarrhea

In most cases it resolves in a few days. The most important thing is to **hydrate yourself,** ideally with oral rehydration salts, which are sold in pharmacies throughout the region. Consult a doctor if there is a high fever, blood in the stool, vomiting that prevents drinking, signs of dehydration, or if symptoms last more than a few days, and before giving any medication to children.

## Height, sun and heat

- **Altitude:** in cities and Andean places above about 2500 meters, altitude sickness is common. Go up little by little, rest the first day and drink water. We explain it in the guide on [altitude sickness](/bitacora/mal-de-altura-soroche/).
- **Sun:** near the equator and at altitude, ultraviolet radiation is very intense. Wear sunscreen, a hat, and sunglasses, even in cloudy weather.
- **Heat:** On the coast and the jungle, heat and humidity dehydrate quickly. Drink frequently, avoid exertion during the middle hours and pay attention to signs such as dizziness, severe headache or confusion.

## Animals and rabies

Rabies is present in animals in many countries in the region. Avoid touching dogs, cats, monkeys and bats, even if they seem tame. If an animal bites you, scratches you, or licks your wound:

1. **Wash the wound immediately** with plenty of soap and water for about fifteen minutes.
2. **Seek medical attention that same day,** even if the wound seems small and even if you have been vaccinated before: a treatment that is only applied in health centers may be required.

In some rural areas there is also Chagas disease, transmitted by an insect that lives in cracks in precarious homes. The risk to travelers is very low; It is enough not to sleep in adobe buildings in poor condition without a mosquito net.

## The traveler's medicine cabinet

- Your usual medications, in sufficient quantities for the entire trip and a few more days.
- Analgesic and fever reducer.
- Oral rehydration salts.
- Antihistamine for allergies and bites, according to medical indication.
- Dressings, gauze, tape, bandages and disinfectant.
- Special dressings for blisters.
- Repellent and sunscreen.
- Thermometer.
- Medication for motion sickness, if you tend to get motion sickness on buses or boats.
- Hand sanitizer gel.

## Traveling with medications

- **Take the medications in their original container,** with the leaflet.
- **Bring the prescription or a medical report,** with the generic name of the active ingredient, which is how you will find it in pharmacies in other countries.
- **Distribute them** between your carry-on luggage and another bag, in case a suitcase gets lost.
- **Find out** if any of your medications have restrictions in the destination country; some strong pain relievers and certain psychotropic drugs may require special documentation.
- **If you use insulin or other medications that require cold,** plan how to store them for long trips.

## Medical care during the trip

In large cities there are usually private clinics with good care, although with high costs for those who do not have insurance. In rural and remote areas, services may be limited and far away. That is why it is essential to have insurance that covers medical care, hospitalization and evacuation; We explain it in the guide on [travel insurance](/bitacora/seguro-de-viaje/).

- **Save the insurance helpline** on your phone and on paper.
- **Ask at your accommodation** for the nearest clinic or hospital.
- **Pharmacies** are very accessible in the cities and can guide you with minor problems, but they do not replace a consultation for important symptoms.

## after the trip

Some illnesses appear days or weeks after returning. **If you have a fever in the weeks following a trip to the tropics,** see a doctor as soon as possible and tell them where you were: that information guides the diagnosis. If you were in an area with malaria, the consultation is urgent.

## Frequent errors

- **Make a medical appointment a few days before** leaving.
- **Forget the yellow fever certificate** when a country on the route requires it.
- **Use repellent only at night** in dengue areas, when the mosquito bites during the day.
- **Bring medications without a prescription** or original container.
- **Travel without medical insurance** or with one that does not cover evacuation.
- **Do not mention the trip** to the doctor if you get sick when you return.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is any vaccine mandatory to travel through Latin America?

The only one that some countries require in certain cases is that of yellow fever, especially for travelers arriving from countries with risk of transmission. The rest are recommendations that depend on your itinerary. Review the current requirements of each country on the route.

### Can I drink tap water?

It depends on the place. In some cities it is drinkable and in many others it is not recommended for visitors. When in doubt, drink bottled or treated water.

### Do I need anti-malaria pills?

Not in most tourist destinations, because the risk is low or non-existent. In some jungle areas the doctor may recommend them. Always decide with a professional according to your route.

### What do I do if I get bitten by a mosquito and have a fever?

Consult a doctor, especially if you have a high fever, muscle or joint pain, rash, or general discomfort. Avoid self-medicating with anti-inflammatories until you are evaluated, because in the case of dengue they may not be appropriate.

## In summary

Consult a travel doctor a month or more in advance, with your itinerary and vaccination history. Check if any country on your route requires a yellow fever certificate and update the recommended vaccines. Protect yourself from mosquitoes day and night, take care of water and food, respect altitude, sun and heat, and do not touch animals. Travel with a basic first aid kit, your prescription medications, and insurance that covers medical care and evacuation, and check promptly if you have a fever during or after your trip.
