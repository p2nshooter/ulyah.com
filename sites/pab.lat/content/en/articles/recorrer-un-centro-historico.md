Many of the most visited cities in Latin America have at their heart a historic center protected as a World Heritage Site: Quito, Cusco, Cartagena, Antigua Guatemala, Oaxaca, Puebla, Guanajuato, Salvador de Bahía, Ouro Preto, Colonia del Sacramento, Valparaíso, Old Havana or the Colonial City of Santo Domingo, among many others. They are neighborhoods where each street has centuries of history, but also places where people live and work every day.

It's easy to rush through them, jumping from one photo to another, and come back without understanding anything. It is also easy to give them the time they deserve and discover how the city was thought of, why the churches have those facades, what the balconies or squares have to say. In this guide you will see how to plan your visit, what time it is best to leave, how to read the architecture and layout, what to do with guides and museums and how to move respectfully through a neighborhood that is not a set.

## Before you arrive: a little context

An hour of reading before the trip completely changes the experience. Good to know:

- **When and how the city was founded,** and if an indigenous city existed before in the same place, as occurs in Mexico City or Cusco.
- **What earthquakes, fires or wars** transformed it, because they explain many reconstructions.
- **What are its key buildings:** the cathedral, the main convents, the government palace, the most important museums.
- **Which holidays or markets** coincide with your dates. More information in the guide about [festivals and carnivals](/bitacora/fiestas-y-carnavales/).

## The layout: understanding the city from the square

Many cities founded in colonial times follow a checkerboard plan, with straight streets that intersect at right angles around a main square. The Spanish ordinances of the late 16th century established rules for laying out new cities, and their imprint is recognized throughout the continent.

The main square has different names: **Zócalo** in Mexico, **Plaza de Armas** in Peru and Chile, **Plaza de Bolívar** in Colombia and Venezuela, **Plaza Mayor** or **Plaza Grande** in other places. The powers of the time were grouped around it: the cathedral, the town hall or town hall and the seat of government. Starting the tour there allows you to understand the rest of the city.

There are charming exceptions: mining cities like Guanajuato or Ouro Preto adapted to mountainous terrain with winding streets, and Valparaíso climbed its hills with stairs and elevators.

## What time to leave

- **Early in the morning:** the light is soft, there are fewer people, it is less hot and you can see the city waking up: shops that open, markets that fill, people on their way to work.
- **In the middle of the afternoon and at dusk:** the facades are illuminated with a warm light and the squares are filled with life.
- **At noon:** a good time for museums, churches, interior patios and a long lunch, especially in hot cities.
- **At night:** many historic centers are very lively, but it is advisable to find out which areas are safe.

## What to watch: a quick reading guide

| Element | What counts |
|---|---|
| Church facades | styles such as baroque, which in the Andes and Mexico incorporated indigenous and mestizo motifs |
| wooden balconies | in Lima and other cities, closed balconies of colonial origin that allowed you to look without being seen |
| Interior courtyards | the center of life in the mansions, with galleries, fountains and plants |
| Covers and shields | families, religious orders or institutions that occupied each building |
| Mixed walls | in Cusco, Inca stone walls on which colonial buildings were built |
| Facade colors | local traditions and, in some centers, regulated palettes to preserve the set |

## Guides, tours and museums

- **Official accredited guides:** Many cities have guides registered by the tourism authority. They usually identify themselves with a credential. Ask to see it and agree on the price and duration before you start.
- **Voluntary paid walking tours:** are a good introduction on the first day. If you liked it, leave a tip according to the time and quality.
- **City museums:** Many historic centers have a museum that explains their history. Visiting it at the beginning gives context for the rest of the days. We see it in the guide on [essential museums and how to visit them](/bitacora/museos-imprescindibles-y-como-visitarlos/).
- **Times:** Churches and museums may close at noon or one day of the week. Confirm them before.

## Churches: visit with respect

The churches in historic centers are monuments, but also active places of worship.

- **Respect the mass schedules** and do not tour the temple while it is being celebrated.
- **Dress discreetly:** in some temples it is asked to cover your shoulders and not enter with a hat.
- **Ask if photographs are allowed,** and never use flash on paintings and altarpieces.
- **Speak quietly** and do not sit on the altar steps.

## Footwear, heat and height

- **Cobblestones and cobblestones:** Almost all historic centers have irregular stone streets. Wear comfortable shoes with good soles.
- **Steep streets:** in cities like Guanajuato, Valparaíso or Salvador, be prepared to go up and down stairs.
- **Heat:** in Cartagena, Mérida or Havana, organize walks during cool hours, bring water and look for the shade of doorways.
- **Height:** in Cusco, Quito, Potosí or La Paz, walk slowly the first few days. More information in the guide on [altitude sickness](/bitacora/mal-de-altura-soroche/).

## A living neighborhood, not a decoration

The historic centers are also home to families, long-standing businesses and traditional trades. So that tourism keeps them alive and does not empty them:

- **Shop at local businesses,** markets and workshops. We see it in the guide on [traditional markets](/bitacora/mercados-tradicionales/).
- **Do not climb monuments, walls or fountains,** or touch paintings or carvings.
- **Respect the rest of the neighbors** at night.
- **Ask permission before photographing people.**
- **Choose registered accommodations** that comply with local regulations.

## Photograph the historic center

- **The first light and the golden hour** of sunset enhance the facades and avoid the harsh shadows of midday.
- **Look for the details:** Knockers, bars, tiles, stone carvings and doors count as much as great views.
- **Go up to a viewpoint or terrace** to understand the shape of the city and its roofs.
- **Respect the prohibitions** on photography in some museums and churches and do not use drones without authorization: in many protected centers they are restricted.

## With children or elderly people

The historic centers can be enjoyed with the family if the pace adapts. Alternate short walks with stops in squares, parks and cafes, choose interactive museums or ones with activities for children and bring water and something to eat. For people with reduced mobility, cobblestones and slopes can be an obstacle: find out if there are accessible routes, tourist transport or flat pedestrian areas, and plan frequent breaks.

## Security at the center

Historic centers tend to be areas with a lot of police and tourist presence, but they also attract pickpockets. Carry just enough, keep your backpack in front of you, be careful with your phone in busy areas and find out which streets should be avoided at night. More tips in the guide on [safety in large cities](/bitacora/seguridad-en-grandes-ciudades/).

## A plan for the first day

| Moment | Proposal |
|---|---|
| First hour | walk through the main square and its neighboring streets with few people |
| Mid morning | city museum or guided tour |
| Noon | lunch at a local market or eatery |
| First afternoon | churches, convents and interior patios, in the shade |
| sunset | gazebo, terrace or walk through the square when it lights up |
| night | dinner in the neighborhood and return through busy streets |

## Frequent errors

- **Try to see everything in one day** and see nothing calmly.
- **Travel in full sun** in hot cities.
- **Hire non-accredited guides** without agreeing on the price.
- **Entering churches during mass** with cameras and loud voices.
- **Inappropriate shoes** for cobblestones and slopes.
- **Treat the neighborhood like a theme park.**

## Frequently asked questions

### How many days do I need for a historic center?

It depends on its size and your interests. For the largest and richest, like Quito, Cusco or Mexico City, two or three days allow you to see it calmly. For small centers, a full day may be enough.

### Is it advisable to stay within the historic center?

It is comfortable to explore on foot and enjoy early and at dusk, but it can be noisy or more expensive. Staying in a close and well-connected neighborhood is another good option.

### Is a guided tour worth it?

Yes, especially the first day: a good guide gives you keys to understand what you will see later on your own.

## In summary

Prepare yourself with a little history, start in the main square to understand the layout and leave early or at dusk. Learn to read facades, balconies, patios and walls, rely on accredited guides and the city museum and visit the churches with respect. Wear comfortable shoes, take care of the heat or height and remember that the historic center is a living neighborhood: buy local, don't go up to the monuments, take care of your belongings and respect those who live there.
