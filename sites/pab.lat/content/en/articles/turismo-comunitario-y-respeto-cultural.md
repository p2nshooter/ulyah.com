Spending a night in a family's house on an island in Lake Titicaca, learning to dye wool with plants in a Mayan village next to Lake Atitlán, exploring the jungle with a Kichwa guide who knows each medicinal plant or sharing the cuisine of an Afro-descendant community on the Pacific coast: community tourism allows you to get to know Latin America from within, through the people who live there.

But the meeting between travelers and communities is not always fair. There are experiences that turn culture into a decoration, photographs taken without permission, haggling that leaves artisans without profit or visits that interrupt daily life. The difference is made, in large part, by the traveler's own decisions. In this guide you will see what community tourism is, how to recognize a genuine proposal and how to behave respectfully when photographing, shopping, staying with families, sacred places and dealing with children.

## What is community tourism

Community tourism is that organized and managed by the communities themselves, often indigenous peoples, Afro-descendants or peasants, who decide what to show, how to do it and how to distribute the income. Unlike excursions that only "pass" through a town, here the community is the host and protagonist.

Its benefits can be important:

- **Income that stays in place,** for families and for common projects.
- **Valuation of one's own culture,** of language, fabrics, music or cuisine.
- **Incentives to conserve** forests, lakes and coasts.
- **A real exchange** between people, rather than a glance from a bus window.

## How to recognize a genuine proposal

Not everything that is advertised as "community" is. Some useful questions:

- **Who organizes the visit?** The community, a local association or an external agency?
- **How ​​is the income distributed?** A serious proposal usually explains it with transparency.
- **Who are the guides?** Ideally, they should be people from the community.
- **Are there visitor limits** and rules designed by the community itself?
- **Does the experience respect everyday life** or does it seem like a show put on for tourists?

Be wary of quick visits that "show" a community as if it were an attraction, without exchange or clear benefit for its inhabitants.

## Before you go: learn a little

- **Read about the history and culture** of the town you are going to visit: its language, its festivals, its relationship with the land.
- **Learn some words** of greetings and thanks in the local language. The gesture is usually received with smiles.
- **Ask about the rules:** clothing, schedules, places that cannot be entered.
- **Visit a museum** before you go, as we suggest in the guide on [must-see museums and how to visit them](/bitacora/museos-imprescindibles-y-como-visitarlos/).

Some common greetings in native languages:

| Language | Where is it spoken | usual greeting |
|---|---|---|
| Quechua | Andes of Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador and other countries | allillanchu, who asks how are you? |
| Aymara | highlands of Bolivia and Peru | kamisaraki, greeting equivalent to how are you |
| Guarani | Paraguay and neighboring areas | mba'éichapa, who asks how you are |

The shapes change from one region to another; Ask your hosts how they greet people in their community.

## Photography: always ask permission

- **Ask permission before photographing a person,** with words or a gesture. If the answer is no, accept it without insisting.
- **Some people or communities charge** to be photographed; It is your decision, and it is advisable to know it beforehand.
- **Never photograph children** without their parents' permission.
- **Respect the prohibitions** in ceremonies, temples, cemeteries or sacred places.
- **Think about how you post images:** avoid showing people as something exotic; If you can, share the photos with those who appear in them.

## Buy crafts fairly

A handmade textile can take weeks of work; a pottery, days of modeling, drying and firing. When purchasing:

- **Buy directly** from artisans or cooperatives, so that the money reaches those who work.
- **Do not haggle excessively:** in the markets you negotiate, but with kindness and knowing that a small difference for you can be a lot for the seller.
- **Ask about the process:** how long it takes, what materials it uses, what the designs mean.
- **Be wary of industrial products** presented as artisanal.
- **Avoid products made with protected species** such as tortoiseshell, certain feathers or skins.

## Stay with a family

Stays in family homes are one of the richest experiences of community tourism:

- **Adapt to the rhythm of the house:** in the countryside you usually get up early and have dinner early.
- **Eat what they offer you,** except for allergies or restrictions that should be notified in advance.
- **Participate if you are invited** to cook, work in the garden or a family activity.
- **Take care of water and electricity,** which can be scarce.
- **A small gift** from your country or region can be a nice gesture, without it having to be expensive.
- **Respect privacy:** do not enter spaces that have not been offered to you.

## Children, gifts and volunteering

- **Do not hand out candy, money or gifts to children** who approach you on the street or in the communities: it encourages begging and, in the case of candy, dental problems in places without access to the dentist. If you want to help, do so through your school, community, or trusted local organizations.
- **Avoid visits to schools or orphanages** that interrupt classes or make children an attraction.
- **Be careful with short-term volunteering with children:** Organizations specialized in child protection advise against passing visitors working with minors without training or controls.

## Sacred places and ceremonies

Many natural and archaeological places are sacred to the people who live in them: mountains, lakes, caves, stones or ancient cities. Some are closed to visitors at certain times, such as the Lost City in the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, and others have reserved areas. Respect those decisions, even if it means changing your plans. The guide on [archaeological sites](/bitacora/sitios-arqueologicos/) expands how to visit them.

If you are invited to a ceremony, such as an offering to Pachamama in the Andes, participate respectfully and follow the instructions of those who guide it.

Be especially careful with ceremonies with plants such as ayahuasca, which are offered to visitors in some areas of the Amazon. They involve real health risks, especially in combination with certain medications, and serious cases have occurred with unprepared facilitators. If you are interested, educate yourself thoroughly, consult a doctor and be wary of commercial offers. The guide on [traveling to the Amazon](/bitacora/viajar-a-la-amazonia/) includes more recommendations for the jungle.

## Afro-descendant peoples

Afro-descendant culture is an essential part of Latin America, present in music, cuisine, religiosity and daily life in many countries. San Basilio de Palenque, in Colombia, preserves its own Creole language and was recognized by UNESCO as a cultural space of universal value. The Garifuna communities of the Caribbean coast of Honduras, Belize, Guatemala and Nicaragua maintain a language, music and dances that are also recognized as intangible heritage. In Brazil, quilombola communities descend from former refuges of people who escaped slavery.

Visiting them with local guides and supporting their cultural and gastronomic initiatives is a way to learn about a story that is often little told.

## Take care of the environment

- **Carry a reusable bottle** and avoid single-use plastics.
- **Take your garbage** back, especially in rural areas without collection.
- **Use biodegradable hygiene products** when water ends up in rivers or lakes.
- **Follow the trails** and the instructions of the local guides.

If you are interested in nature, the guide on [bird watching](/bitacora/observacion-de-aves/) explains how community guides help conserve forests.

## Words and images

- **Use the names** with which the peoples name themselves: Wayuu, Emberá, Rarámuri, Bribri, Aymara, Kichwa and many others.
- **Avoid stereotypes:** Not all indigenous people wear traditional clothing, nor do they live in the countryside, nor do they speak only their native language.
- **Listen more than you talk:** Respectful questions open conversations; Quick trials close them.

## Frequent errors

- **Photographing people without permission,** especially children.
- **Haggle hard** with artisans for small amounts.
- **Distribute candy or money** to the children.
- **Choose quick tours** that treat a community as an attraction.
- **Ignore closures** of sacred places.
- **Participating in ceremonies with plants** without being informed about the risks.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is community tourism more expensive?

Not necessarily. It is often priced similarly to other options, except that more of the money goes back into the community.

### Do I need to speak the local language?

No. In most places, your hosts will speak Spanish or Portuguese in addition to their language. Learning a few words, however, is a much appreciated gesture.

### Can I tip my hosts or guides?

Yes, if you liked the service. Ask what the custom is and if there is a common community fund.

### How do I know if my visit really helps?

Ask who organizes the experience, how the income is shared, and if the guides are from the community. Genuine proposals usually respond with transparency.

## In summary

Community tourism allows you to get to know Latin America through those who live there, with benefits that stay in the place. Choose proposals managed by the communities themselves, with local guides and transparent income. Learn something about the culture and a few words before you go, ask permission to photograph, buy crafts directly and without excessive haggling, adapt to the rhythm of the families that receive you and do not give gifts to the children. Respect the sacred places, inform yourself well before any ceremony and remember that you are traveling as a guest.
