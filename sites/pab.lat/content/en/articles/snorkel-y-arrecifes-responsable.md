Showing your face into the water for the first time on a reef is an experience that few forget. Suddenly a world of corals appears in the shape of a brain, a fan or a deer's horn, schools of colorful fish, a turtle that comes up to breathe or a stingray that glides over the sand. Latin America has some of the best places on the planet to experience it, from the great reef of the Mexican and Central American Caribbean to the Galapagos Islands, the San Andrés archipelago or the crystalline rivers of Brazil.

To enjoy it you don't need to be a diver or have experience: just a mask, a tube, some fins and some basic notions are enough. But reefs are fragile and threatened ecosystems, and each visitor can help protect them or contribute to damaging them without realizing it. In this guide you will see where to snorkel, what equipment to use, how to float without touching anything, what sunscreen to choose, how to behave around wildlife, what risks to be aware of, and how to choose a responsible operator.

The Mesoamerican reef system, which extends about a thousand kilometers from the north of the Yucatan Peninsula to the Bay of Honduras islands, is the largest barrier reef in the Western Hemisphere and one of the great natural treasures of the region.

## Why reefs need care

A coral looks like a rock or a plant, but it is a colony of small animals, polyps, that build a skeleton of calcium carbonate. Microscopic algae live in their tissues that provide them with food and much of their color. Many corals grow very slowly, often only a few millimeters or a few centimeters per year, so a fin swipe can destroy in a second what took decades to form.

Reefs are also much more than a beautiful landscape:

- **They protect the coasts,** because they stop the force of the waves and reduce beach erosion.
- **They are nurseries for fish,** many of them important for fishing in local communities.
- **They support tourism** for thousands of families throughout the region.

Today they face serious pressures: the increase in sea temperature causes episodes of **bleaching**, in which the coral expels its algae and remains white and weakened; They are also affected by diseases, pollution, excess nutrients arriving from the coast and physical damage caused by anchors, boats and visitors. A responsible snorkel does not solve all that, but it avoids adding another problem.

## The basic equipment

- **Mask:** should seal well. To try it on, place it on your face without adjusting the strap and inhale gently through your nose: if it sticks alone, it is your size. Hair trapped under the rim or a mustache can cause leaks.
- **Tube or snorkel:** A simple model with a comfortable mouthpiece is enough. Some have a valve that makes it easy to expel the water.
- **Fins:** They help you move forward with little effort and without moving your arms. They should be firm, without squeezing or rubbing.
- **Lycra t-shirt or short suit:** protects from the sun, small bites and cold, and reduces the amount of sunscreen you need.
- **Flotation vest:** highly recommended if you do not swim proficiently, and mandatory in some marine parks.

**Against fogging,** new masks usually need an initial cleaning that removes the film left from manufacturing; Afterwards, a specific anti-fog or a few drops of mild baby shampoo, rinsed well, usually work.

If you rent the equipment, check that the mask has no cracks, that the mouthpiece is clean and in good condition, and that the fins are your size. If you are going to snorkel for many days, bringing your own mask and snorkel is more hygienic and comfortable.

### A note on full face masks

Masks that cover the entire face have become popular because they allow you to breathe through the nose, but some models have raised doubts due to the possible accumulation of carbon dioxide and the difficulty in removing them quickly in case of a problem. Several operators do not allow them. If you are inexperienced, the traditional mask and tube combination is the most proven option.

## Before entering the water

1. **Find out about the conditions of the day:** waves, wind, currents and visibility. If there are flags on the beach, respect them; We explain it in the guide on [rip currents and flags](/bitacora/corrientes-de-resaca-y-banderas/).
2. **Listen to the guide's talk:** he explains where to swim, what areas to avoid and how to get back to the boat.
3. **Never go in alone.** Agree with your partner how to communicate and always stay close.
4. **Practice in calm water** if this is your first time: learn to breathe through the tube with your face in the water before walking away.
5. **Avoid alcohol** before swimming and enter the water hydrated and rested.
6. **Watch the tide:** At low tide, some reefs are just a few centimeters from the surface and are easy to hit.

## Technique: float without touching

The key to responsible snorkeling is to stay on the surface, in a horizontal position and without touching the bottom.

- **Enter the water through sandy areas** or from the boat, never walking on the reef.
- **Kick slowly from the hips,** with legs almost straight. The sudden movements raise sand that suffocates the corals.
- **Do not stand on the coral** to rest or adjust the mask. If you need to stop, find an area of ​​deep sand, hold on to your life jacket, or get back on the boat.
- **Keep your distance from the bottom,** especially in shallow areas, where a flick of the fin is enough to break a coral.
- **To empty the tube,** blow hard when you come to the surface.
- **If submerged,** gently equalize ear pressure and don't try this if you have a cold.

## Sunscreen and reefs

The tropical sun burns much more than it seems when you are in the water, and the back and the back of the legs are the areas that suffer the most during snorkeling. At the same time, some ingredients in chemical protectants have been associated with damage to corals, and several protected areas and cenotes require biodegradable protectants or outright ask not to use any in the water.

- **Wear clothing with UV protection:** a long-sleeved lycra and, if you want, tights reduce the need for sunscreen to a minimum.
- **Prefer mineral protectors** based on zinc oxide or titanium dioxide, and respect the instructions for each location.
- **Apply it in time,** at least half an hour before entering, so that it does not come off immediately in the water.
- **Comply with local regulations,** which vary from location to location.

## Fauna: looking without touching

- **Do not touch or chase the animals.** Turtles, for example, need to come out to breathe calmly; If you surround them or follow them, you stress them.
- **Do not feed the fish:** it alters their behavior and diet, and is prohibited in many parks.
- **Do not collect shells, starfish or coral,** live or dead. Pulling a starfish out of the water for a photo can kill it.
- **Maintain the distance** indicated by local regulations and always leave animals an escape route.
- **Do not buy coral, tortoiseshell or turtle shell crafts:** their trade is illegal in many countries and fuels the destruction of these species.

In places where turtles feed on seagrass near the shore, access is usually regulated, with delimited areas, small groups and a mandatory guide. More ideas in the guide on [whale and turtle watching](/bitacora/avistamiento-de-ballenas-y-tortugas/).

## Animals that should be known

| animal | Why be careful | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| sea urchins | its spikes dig into feet and hands | do not step on rocky areas without shoes and do not rest your hands |
| fire coral | causes burning and hives when touched | Identify it with the guide and keep your distance |
| Jellyfish | some species produce painful stings | ask if there are jellyfish in the area and wear lycra |
| lionfish | its thorns are poisonous; It is an invasive species in the Caribbean | observe it without getting too close |
| Stripes | They can defend themselves if you step on them | in sandy areas, shuffles when walking |

If you are stung, get out of the water, follow the instructions of the guide or local staff and seek medical attention if the pain is severe, if you have difficulty breathing or an allergic reaction. ## Cenotes and rivers of crystal clear water

Not all snorkeling occurs in the sea. In the Yucatan Peninsula, cenotes are natural freshwater wells connected to underground rivers, with surprising transparency and cool temperatures. They usually ask you to shower before entering to remove sunscreen and repellent, and many do not allow you to use them in the water. Some are open and others are inside caverns, where it is advisable to go with a guide and light.

In Bonito, in the Brazilian state of Mato Grosso do Sul, floating is practiced in crystal-clear rivers full of fish. Visits are organized with reservations and quotas through authorized operators, and the rules usually prohibit sunscreen and repellent in the water and stepping on the bottom of rivers. They are examples of how a system of strict rules can keep a place almost intact despite visitors.

## How to choose a responsible operator

- **Small groups** and a reasonable guide-to-swimmer ratio.
- **Previous talk** about safety and care rules for the reef.
- **Vests available** and equipment in good condition.
- **Boats that are tied to buoys** and do not anchor on the coral.
- **Licenses and permits** from the marine park or local authority.
- **No feeding fish** or encouraging touching the animals for the photo.
- **Radio, first aid kit and a plan** in case anyone needs help.

Be wary of overly cheap offers that fill the boat with people or promise interactions with animals that should be wild. If you are going to move between islands, the guide on [ferries and boats](/bitacora/viajar-entre-islas-ferris-y-lanchas/) helps you choose safe transportation.

## Safety in the water

- **Wear a vest** if you don't swim proficiently: the snorkel does not replace knowing how to swim.
- **Do not move away from the group** or the indicated area; the boats don't always see a swimmer.
- **Wear bright colored clothing** or a signaling buoy to be visible.
- **Be aware of currents:** if you are swept away by one, do not fight head on; swim parallel to the coast or let it carry you and ask for help by raising your arm.
- **Leave on time** if you feel cold, tired, dizzy or cramped.
- **Carry fresh water** in the boat and protect yourself from the sun between one outing and another.

Also check that your insurance covers water activities and evacuations from remote areas; We explain it in the guide on [travel insurance](/bitacora/seguro-de-viaje/).

## The best time

Conditions vary greatly by region. In the Caribbean, visibility is usually good for much of the year, but hurricane season, between June and November, can bring storms and rough seas, as we explain in the guide on [Caribbean hurricane season](/bitacora/temporada-de-huracanes-caribe/). In some months, the arrival of sargassum also affects beaches and waters near the coast; We see it in the guide on [sargassum in the Caribbean](/bitacora/sargazo-en-el-caribe/). In the Galapagos, the water is warmest between approximately December and May, and colder and with more marine life in suspension the rest of the year, when a wetsuit is appreciated.

## Responsible underwater photography

- **Secures the camera to your wrist** so you don't have to chase it if it slips away.
- **Do not lean on the bottom** to frame or use long poles near the coral.
- **Keep your distance** from animals: a good digital zoom is better than an invasive close-up.
- **Enjoy without a screen too:** Sometimes the best way to remember a reef is to simply observe it.

## Frequent errors

- **Stand on the coral** to rest or adjust the mask.
- **Use lots of chemical sunscreen** instead of UV protective clothing.
- **Snorkel alone** or get away from the group.
- **Feed fish** or touch turtles and starfish.
- **Entering the water without knowing the currents** or the state of the sea.
- **Hire operators who anchor on the reef** or fill the boat with people.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to know how to swim to snorkel?

It is highly recommended. With a vest you can float safely in calm waters, but knowing how to swim gives you autonomy and confidence if something goes wrong. If you don't swim easily, choose quiet places and always go with a guide.

### What is the difference between snorkeling and diving?

In snorkeling you swim on the surface breathing through a tube, without special training. Tank diving allows you to go deeper, but requires a course and certification.

### Can I snorkel with children?

Yes, in places with calm and shallow waters, with a vest, a mask of your size and always under the supervision of an adult in the water.

### Is it true that touching coral damages it?

Yes. Rubbing the skin, sand raised by the fins or simple support can damage the polyps and open the door to infections. It is best not to touch anything.

## In summary

Latin America offers some of the best places in the world for snorkeling, from the Mesoamerican reef to the Galapagos, San Andrés or the rivers of Bonito. Use a mask that seals well, snorkel, fins, lycra and a vest if you need to, learn about currents and tides, and never swim alone. It floats horizontally, without touching or stepping on the coral, without feeding fish or chasing animals, and prefers clothing with UV protection to excess sunscreen. Choose operators who work with small groups, moor to buoys and respect the rules of each park: this way the reefs will still be there for those who come after.
