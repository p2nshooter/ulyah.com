The Caribbean has a reputation as paradise all year round, and to a large extent it is: warm waters, sun and white sand beaches. But between June and November comes the Atlantic hurricane season, and many travelers doubt: is it worth traveling in those months, with lower prices and fewer tourists, or is it too risky? What happens if a storm forms right during the holidays?

The answer depends on the destination, the month, and how flexible your plan is. Most days of the season pass without hurricanes in a given location, but when one arrives, it can force flights to be canceled, beaches to close, or coastal areas to be evacuated. In this guide you will see when the season is and what its peak is, which areas are most and least exposed, how to follow official alerts, what to take into account with insurance and what to do if a storm approaches during your trip.

## When is the season

| Basin | Official season | Busiest months |
|---|---|---|
| Atlantic, Caribbean and Gulf of Mexico | June 1 to November 30 | from mid-August to the end of October |
| Northeast Pacific, Pacific coast of Mexico and Central America | May 15 to November 30 | from July to September, approximately |

Outside of these dates storms may form, but it is rare. In-season, June and July are typically quieter than September, the historically busiest month in the Atlantic.

## What areas are most exposed

The risk is not the same throughout the Caribbean:

- **Most exposed:** the Antilles, the Yucatan Peninsula with Cancun and the Riviera Maya, the Gulf of Mexico, the Bahamas and the Caribbean coast of northern Central America.
- **Pacific coast of Mexico and Central America:** also has its own cyclone season.
- **Less exposed:** the southern tip of the Caribbean, such as Aruba, Bonaire and Curaçao, and the Caribbean coast of Colombia and Venezuela, which rarely receive the direct impact of a hurricane due to their latitude, although they can experience rain and waves from distant storms.

This does not mean that the southern Caribbean is free of bad weather, but it does mean that the probability of a hurricane is lower.

## Advantages and disadvantages of traveling in season

**Advantages:**

- lower prices on accommodation and flights in many destinations,
- fewer tourists on beaches, ruins and restaurants,
- green vegetation and lush landscapes.

**Disadvantages:**

- risk of a storm affecting your trip, greater at the peak of the season,
- more rain, often in the form of afternoon showers,
- high heat and humidity,
- On some coasts, it coincides with the arrival of sargassum, as we explain in the guide on [sargassum in the Caribbean](/bitacora/sargazo-en-el-caribe/).

## What is the weather like in season, day by day

Most days of the season there are no storms nearby, and the weather follows a fairly typical pattern for the tropics: sunny mornings, increasing heat and humidity, and heavy but brief showers in the afternoon or evening. Some ideas to take advantage of it:

- **Plan beach, ruins and nature excursions in the morning.**
- **Reserve the afternoons** for museums, naps, swimming pools or indoor activities.
- **Protect yourself from mosquitoes:** Rain multiplies breeding sites, and dengue and other diseases circulate in many areas. Use repellent and light, long-sleeved clothing at dusk.
- **Hydrate more than usual:** The humid heat makes you sweat a lot.

## What to bring if you travel in season

- A lightweight raincoat or folding umbrella.
- Quick-drying clothing and sandals that will not be damaged by water.
- Insect repellent and sunscreen.
- A waterproof bag for documents, phone and money.
- A charged external battery and a small flashlight.
- Digital copies of your documents and the telephone numbers of your insurance, your airline and your consulate.

## How to follow alerts

- **United States National Hurricane Center,** which issues warnings for the Atlantic and Northeast Pacific, also in Spanish.
- **National meteorological services** of each country.
- **Civil Protection** or the local emergency authority, which gives evacuation orders.
- **Your accommodation and your airline,** which communicate changes and contingency plans.

Two key terms in the official notices:

- **Watch:** Storm or hurricane conditions are **possible** in the area in the coming days.
- **Notice:** conditions are **expected** in the area. It's time to act.

## Hurricanes and storms: the scale

Hurricanes are classified from 1 to 5 based on sustained wind speed. A tropical storm has lower winds than a hurricane, but can bring very intense rain and flooding. Don't underestimate a "only" tropical storm or a low-category hurricane: water usually causes more damage than wind.

The deadliest danger on the coast is the **storm surge**, a wind-driven rise in sea level that floods low-lying areas. That is why coastal evacuation orders must always be followed.

## Travel insurance

- **Contract the insurance when booking,** not when the storm already has a name: many policies exclude events that were already known when contracting.
- **Check what it covers:** cancellation, trip interruption, delay expenses, additional accommodation and medical assistance.
- **Read the fine print** about weather events and evacuations.

More information in the guide about [travel insurance](/bitacora/seguro-de-viaje/).

## Book flexibly

- **Refundable rates or with free changes** on flights and accommodation.
- **Hurricane Policies:** Some hotels and airlines offer changes or refunds if a storm affects the destination. Ask before booking.
- **Itineraries with margin,** without tight connections at the end of the trip.
- **Solid and well-prepared accommodations,** with emergency plans. We see it in the guide on how to [choose secure hosting](/bitacora/elegir-alojamiento-seguro/).

## If a storm approaches during your trip

1. **Follow official instructions** and those of your accommodation staff.
2. **Contact your airline:** They often offer free changes before the storm arrives. If you can leave earlier, consider doing so.
3. **Have a basic kit:** water, food that does not need cooking, medications, flashlight, charged batteries and some cash.
4. **Store your documents** in a waterproof bag.
5. **Don't go to the beach** to watch the surf: waves and currents are very dangerous before and during the storm. Read the guide on [rip currents and flags](/bitacora/corrientes-de-resaca-y-banderas/).
6. **During the storm, stay indoors,** away from windows.
7. **Then,** wait for authorities to say it's safe to go out: there may be downed wires, flooding, and contaminated water.

## With children or elderly people

If you are traveling with small children, elderly people, or someone with health problems, flexibility is even more important. Choose accommodations with good services and easy access to medical centers, carry enough medication for a few extra days, and have a clear plan to leave early if a storm is announced. In these cases, traveling outside the peak of the season is usually the quietest option.

## Islands and ferries

In season, inter-island ferries and boats may be suspended due to bad weather at short notice. If your plan includes several islands, leave days of margin. More information in the guide about [traveling between islands](/bitacora/viajar-entre-islas-ferris-y-lanchas/).

## Frequent errors

- **Travel at the peak of the season** with an itinerary without flexibility.
- **Contract insurance when the storm already has a name.**
- **Ignore evacuation orders.**
- **Go watch the waves** at the beach during a storm.
- **Do not have cash or charged batteries** when bad weather is announced.
- **Thinking that the entire Caribbean has the same risk.**

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best month to travel to the Caribbean in season?

June and early July are typically quieter than the peak of August to October. If you want to reduce your risk even further, consider destinations in the southern Caribbean or travel during the off-season.

### Can you travel in September?

Yes, many people do it without problems, but it is the busiest month. Travel with flexible reservations, insurance contracted in advance and attentive to the forecasts.

### How far in advance do you know that a hurricane is coming?

Official forecasts typically track the formation and likely path of a storm several days in advance, allowing time to change plans. Still, the intensity and course can vary, so it is a good idea to check the warnings several times a day when an active system is nearby.

### What about cruises?

Shipping companies often change routes to avoid storms. Review the conditions of your reservation regarding itinerary changes.

## In summary

The Atlantic hurricane season runs from June 1 to November 30, with its peak between mid-August and late October; the Northeast Pacific begins on May 15. The risk is highest in the Antilles, Yucatan and the Gulf of Mexico, and lowest in the extreme south of the Caribbean. If you travel in season, book flexibly, purchase insurance when booking, follow official alerts, distinguish between watch and warning, follow evacuation orders and stay away from the beach when a storm approaches.
