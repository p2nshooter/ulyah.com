Walking through a Latin American city is to travel through several centuries of history in a few blocks. In Cusco, Inca stone walls support colonial houses; in Mexico City, a baroque cathedral stands next to the ruins of the Mexica Templo Mayor; in Buenos Aires, a European-inspired theater coexists with modernist buildings; and in Brasilia, the entire city is a manifesto of twentieth-century architecture. Buildings are documents that tell who was in charge, what was believed, how people lived and what materials were on hand.

You don't have to be an architect to enjoy them. With some simple keys, a façade that seemed only "pretty" begins to tell a story: the indigenous motifs hidden among baroque saints, the reason why all the squares look alike or why certain houses have closed wooden balconies. In this guide you will see how to read the major stages of the region's architecture and what to look for in each one.

## Before the Europeans: stone, adobe and orientation

Pre-Hispanic cultures built cities, temples and observatories with their own techniques:

- **Mesoamerica:** the pyramids served as the base of temples and were expanded in stages, covering the previous one. In Teotihuacan, the slope and deck system is characteristic, which alternates inclined planes and vertical panels. The Mayans used the so-called Mayan vault or false arch, formed by stones that advance from each side until closing at the top.
- **The Andes:** Inca masonry is famous for its carved stone blocks that fit together without mortar, such as the famous twelve-angle stone in Cusco. The trapezoidal doors, windows and niches are an easy-to-recognize Inca signature, and their inclination helps with stability against earthquakes.
- **The Peruvian coast:** Cultures such as the Chimú built enormous adobe cities, such as Chan Chan, with walls decorated with reliefs of fish, birds and nets.

Many ancient buildings were oriented according to the movement of the sun or stars. The guide to [archaeological sites](/bitacora/sitios-arqueologicos/) explains how to explore them.

## The city in checkerboard

If you have ever been surprised by how much the central squares of cities as different as Antigua Guatemala, Bogotá, Lima or Santiago look alike, there is a reason: most of the cities founded by the Spanish followed a common model, the grid or checkerboard, which the Crown regulated in the ordinances of 1573.

- **In the center,** a main square or parade ground.
- **Surrounding,** the cathedral or main church, the town hall or town hall and the houses of the most powerful neighbors.
- **From the square,** straight streets that form regular blocks.

Knowing this helps you orient yourself: in almost any colonial city, the main square is the best starting point. We see it in the guide on [touring a historic center](/bitacora/recorrer-un-centro-historico/).

## Churches and convents

The churches and convents were the great architectural projects of the colonial era, and in them you can see the evolution of styles:

- **Sixteenth century:** In Mexico, large convents were built with wide atriums, open chapels to celebrate mass before indigenous crowds, and small chapels in the corners of the atrium, called posas.
- **Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries:** The Baroque filled the facades and interiors with movement, columns, sculptures and gold. Churches such as the Compañía de Jesús in Quito or the Rosario chapel in Puebla dazzle with their golden interiors.
- **The Mexican ultra-baroque:** is recognized by the stipes, columns that taper downward like inverted pyramids, on facades so ornate that they leave almost no free space.
- **The Andean mestizo baroque:** in cities such as Arequipa, Potosí or the towns of Lake Titicaca, indigenous stonemasons carved facades where, along with saints and angels, mermaids, tropical fruits, flowers, monkeys or figures from the Andean world appear.

## How to read a baroque facade

Many colonial church facades function as a stone altarpiece. To read them:

1. **Look for the horizontal levels,** called bodies, and the vertical streets, separated by columns.
2. **Note the type of columns:** smooth, spiral-shaped, spirally twisted, or stipes.
3. **Identifies the main saint,** usually in the center, who is usually the patron of the temple.
4. **Look for local details:** fruits, animals, faces or symbols that do not appear in European baroque.
5. **Look up:** bell towers, tiled domes and finials are usually the most original.

Visit churches with respect: dress appropriately, remain silent and avoid visiting them during masses.

## Caribbean Strengths

To protect the ports from pirates and rival powers, the Spanish Crown fortified the main cities of the Caribbean. The walls of Cartagena de Indias, the castles of Morro in Havana and San Juan in Puerto Rico, the walls of Campeche or the forts of Portobelo in Panama form one of the most impressive sets of military architecture in America. Many are world heritage sites and can be visited. Note the arrowhead-shaped bulwarks, designed to cover all shooting angles.

## Houses, patios and balconies

The urban colonial house used to be organized around one or several patios, which provided light, freshness and privacy. From the street, a sober façade; inside, galleries, plants and fountains.

- **The closed wooden balconies,** very characteristic of Lima, allowed you to look at the street without being seen, an inheritance of Mudejar traditions.
- **The open balconies with flowers,** typical of Cartagena or many Caribbean cities, helped ventilate the houses in the heat.
- **The mansions converted into museums,** hotels or cultural centers allow you to see these spaces from the inside.

Remember that many colonial houses are still private homes: do not enter patios without permission.

## Colonial Brazil

Portuguese Brazil developed its own baroque. In Salvador de Bahía, the Pelourinho neighborhood brings together churches with interiors covered in gold and colorful facades. In Minas Gerais, the wealth of gold financed cities such as Ouro Preto, where the sculptor and architect Antônio Francisco Lisboa, known as Aleijadinho, author of the soapstone prophets of the Congonhas sanctuary, worked.

## Nineteenth century: republics, theaters and iron

After independence, the new republics sought a modern image, often inspired by France, Italy or England:

- **Neoclassical buildings** for conferences, universities and banks, with columns and pediments.
- **Large opera theaters** such as the Teatro Colón in Buenos Aires or the Teatro Amazonas in Manaus, built in the middle of the jungle with the wealth of rubber.
- **Iron architecture:** markets, train stations and pavilions with iron structures, often manufactured in Europe and assembled in America.
- **Palaces and avenues** inspired by the Paris of Baron Haussmann, especially in Buenos Aires and Mexico City.

## Twentieth century: art deco and modernism

The twentieth century turned Latin America into a laboratory of modern architecture:

- **Art Deco:** buildings with geometric lines and stylized decoration in cities such as Havana, Mexico City or Montevideo.
- **UNAM University City,** in Mexico City, with buildings covered in murals and mosaics, declared world heritage.
- **Luis Barragán,** Mexican architect who worked with walls of intense colors, water and light; His house and studio in Mexico City are world heritage.
- **Brasilia,** the capital planned by Lúcio Costa, with buildings by Oscar Niemeyer, inaugurated in 1960.
- **The University City of Caracas,** the work of Carlos Raúl Villanueva, which integrates architecture and art.
- **Eladio Dieste,** Uruguayan engineer who created vaults and undulating brick walls, like in the church of Atlántida.
- **Contemporary architecture:** Several Latin American architects have received the Pritzker Prize, considered the most prestigious in the profession, and cities like Medellín became known for urban transformation projects, such as their library parks and cable cars in hillside neighborhoods.

## Traditional and popular architecture

Beyond the great monuments, the popular architecture says a lot about each region:

- **Palafitos de Chiloé,** wooden houses on stilts next to the sea.
- **Adobe houses** from the highlands, with thick walls that conserve heat.
- **Bahareque and guadua** in the coffee region of Colombia, techniques with cane and earth very adapted to earthquakes.
- **Quincha** on the coast of Peru, another cane and mud technique with good seismic behavior.
- **Colorful wooden houses** in the Caribbean and on the Atlantic coast of Central America.
- **Malocas** in the Amazon, large communal houses made of wood and palm.

## Tips for looking and photographing

- **Look up:** balconies, cornices, bell towers and domes often go unnoticed.
- **Take a guided tour:** In many cities there are tours focused on architecture.
- **Interior visit:** museums, libraries, markets and cultural centers operate in historic buildings.
- **Choose the light:** The first hour of the morning and the last hour of the afternoon highlight the reliefs of the facades.
- **Respect the regulations** for photography in churches and public buildings, and the privacy of those who live in historic homes.

The guide on [essential museums and how to visit them](/bitacora/museos-imprescindibles-y-como-visitarlos/) includes some museums housed in emblematic buildings.

## Frequent errors

- **Look only at the main monument** and not the city that surrounds it.
- **Enter churches during mass** as if it were a museum.
- **Ignore modern architecture** to only look for the colonial.
- **Enter without permission** in patios of private houses.
- **Climb historic walls or structures** for a photo.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which city is best to see colonial architecture?

There are many: Cartagena, Antigua Guatemala, Puebla, Oaxaca, Quito, Cusco, Potosí, Sucre, Salvador de Bahía or Ouro Preto, among others. Each one has its character.

### Where can you see modern Latin American architecture?

Mexico City, Brasilia, São Paulo, Caracas, Bogotá and Montevideo have important works from the twentieth century, many of them visitable.

### Can you enter churches?

In general, yes, respecting worship times, dressing appropriately and remaining silent. Some charge admission or are only open during certain hours.

### Do you need to know about architecture to enjoy it?

No. With some basic clues and curiosity, any walk becomes more interesting.

## In summary

The architecture of Latin America is an outdoor history lesson: pre-Hispanic stone and adobe techniques, checkerboard colonial cities with their main plaza, mestizo baroque churches with indigenous motifs, Caribbean fortresses, houses with patios and balconies, theaters and markets from the 19th century, and a 20th century full of modern works. Learn to read facades, look up, visit interiors and respect both places of worship and private homes.
