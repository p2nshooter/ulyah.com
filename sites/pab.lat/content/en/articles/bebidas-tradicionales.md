To get to know a country, sometimes it is enough to accept what they offer you to drink. A mate shared in a plaza in Montevideo, a cold chicha morada with lunch in Lima, an agua de jamaica in a market in Oaxaca, a red coffee mid-morning in Manizales or an iced tereré in the heat of Asunción tell as much about the local culture as a museum. Behind every drink are native ingredients, ancient techniques, social rituals, and often centuries of history.

Latin America has an extraordinary variety of drinks: herbal infusions, fermented corn preparations, fruit juices that almost no one knows about outside the region, distillates from agave, sugar cane and grapes, and high-altitude wines. In this guide you will see the most representative ones, how and where to try them, what customs accompany them and how to enjoy them safely.

## Mate and his relatives

### The mate ritual

In Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay and southern Brazil, mate is much more than a drink: it is a gesture of hospitality and a way to spend time together. It is prepared with yerba mate in a container, also called mate, and drunk with a metal bulb. One person, the cebador, fills the mate with hot water and passes it to each person in turn.

Some customs that should be known:

- **The water should not boil:** too hot burns the herb and makes the flavor bitter.
- **Do not move the bulb:** it becomes clogged and disturbs the primer.
- **Drink the entire contents** before returning it, without keeping the mate for too long.
- **Saying "thank you"** usually means that you don't want any more.

In the south of Brazil they drink a version called chimarrão, with very green and finely ground herb.

### The tereré

In Paraguay, the heat turned mate into a cold drink: tereré, which is prepared with ice water or juices and often with crushed medicinal herbs, known as yuyos. Their traditional practices and knowledge are recognized by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage. It is common to see groups of friends or coworkers sharing the thermos at any time of the day.

### Coca tea

In the Andes of Peru and Bolivia, coca leaf infusion is an everyday drink, which tradition associates with relieving the discomfort of altitude. It is legal and very common in those countries, but keep two things in mind: you can test positive for drugs, and taking coca leaves or their derivatives to other countries is prohibited in many of them. Consume it there and do not carry it in your luggage. We see it in the guide on [altitude sickness](/bitacora/mal-de-altura-soroche/).

## Corn drinks

Corn, the basis of the diet of many American cultures, is also drunk.

| Drink | Where | What is it |
|---|---|---|
| chicha de jora | Andes of Peru and Bolivia | fermented drink of sprouted corn, of pre-Hispanic origin |
| purple chicha | Peru | non-alcoholic drink of boiled purple corn with fruit and spices, served cold |
| Atole | Mexico and Central America | hot, thick drink of masa or corn flour, of many flavors |
| Champurrado | Mexico | atole with chocolate |
| purple colada | Ecuador | thick drink of purple corn flour with fruits and spices, typical of the Day of the Dead |
| Pozol and roof | southern Mexico | corn and cocoa drinks |

In some Andean towns, houses that sell homemade chicha are recognized by a flag or an object hanging on the door. Chicha de jora has alcohol, although in varying degrees depending on the fermentation. More about pozol and tejate in the guide on [cacao and chocolate] (/bitacora/cacao-y-chocolate/).

## Fresh waters and fruit juices

In the markets and stalls of Mexico, fresh waters, prepared with water, fruit or flowers and some sugar, are the most common way to cool off: hibiscus, made with hibiscus flower, tamarind, horchata rice with cinnamon or lemon with chia. In Central America, horchata sometimes has morro seeds, and in southern Ecuador, in Loja, the word horchata designates a reddish infusion of many aromatic herbs.

Natural juices are another of the great pleasures of the region, with fruits that are rarely found outside of it:

| Fruit | Where is it common | Flavor |
|---|---|---|
| Lulo or naranjilla | Colombia and Ecuador | acidic and refreshing, between citrus and tropical |
| passion fruit | the entire tropical region | acidic and very aromatic |
| Soursop | tropical areas | sweet and creamy |
| Andean blackberry | Andes | sweet and sour |
| tree tomato | Andes | acidic, with notes of tomato and fruit |
| camu camu | Amazon | very acidic, rich in vitamin C |
| Acai | Brazil | earthy; It is mostly taken as frozen pulp |

Order juices with treated water or milk, or in trusted places, and be careful with ice in dubious stalls, as we explain in the guide on [safe street food](/bitacora/comida-callejera-segura/).

## Coffee and chocolate

Coffee is part of everyday life in much of the region, from Colombian red to Brazilian cafezinho or Mexican café de Olla, and hot chocolate has ancient roots. Both have their own guides: [coffee routes](/bitacora/rutas-del-cafe/) and [cocoa and chocolate](/bitacora/cacao-y-chocolate/).

## Agave drinks: pulque, mezcal and tequila

In Mexico, agave, or maguey, gave rise to some of the country's most emblematic drinks.

- **Pulque:** fermented drink from the sap of the maguey, aguamiel, consumed since pre-Hispanic times. It is white, thick and with an acidic flavor, and is eaten plain or cured with fruits in traditional pulquerías.
- **Mezcal:** distillate that is made with different species of agave, whose heart is traditionally cooked in earthen ovens, which gives it smoky notes. Oaxaca is its best-known region.
- **Tequila:** a type of mezcal made with blue agave in a delimited region, mainly in the state of Jalisco. The agave landscape and the old Tequila facilities are world heritage.

Tequila and mezcal are usually drunk in small sips, to appreciate their aromas, rather than in one gulp.

## Cane and grape distillates

| Drink | Where | What is |
|---|---|---|
| Cachaça | Brazil | distilled from fresh sugar cane juice, base of caipirinha |
| Brandy | Colombia, Ecuador and other countries | cane distilled, in Colombia flavored with anise |
| Rum | Caribbean and Central America | distilled from cane or molasses, with a long tradition in Cuba, the Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico, Venezuela, Guatemala or Nicaragua |
| Pisco | Peru and Chile | grape brandy; both countries produce it and consider it part of their identity |
| Singani | Bolivia | distilled from muscat grapes from the high valleys, especially from Tarija |

Many wineries welcome visitors with tours and tastings. If you take a wine tour by car, designate a driver who does not drink.

## Classic cocktails

- **Pisco sour:** pisco, lemon, sugar syrup, egg white and a few drops of bitters.
- **Caipirinha:** cachaça, lime, sugar and crushed ice.
- **Mojito:** rum, mint, lime, sugar and soda, of Cuban origin.
- **Michelada:** beer with lemon, salt and, depending on the recipe, sauces and spices.
- **Canelazo:** hot drink from the Andes of Ecuador and Colombia, with brandy, cinnamon and fruit, ideal for cold nights.

## Drinking customs

- **The toast:** "Cheers!" It is the most common expression throughout the region.
- **The offering to Pachamama:** in Andean areas, it is common to spill a few drops of the drink on the ground before drinking, as a thank you to Mother Earth. If you are invited, you can do so respectfully.
- **Sharing:** in many meetings a bottle or a mate is shared among everyone; Rejecting politely is fine, but do it with a smile.
- **Minimum age:** in most countries it is eighteen years old to purchase and consume alcohol.

## Drink safely

- **Altitude:** At altitude, alcohol takes effect faster and worsens altitude sickness. Moderate the first nights.
- **Liqueurs of dubious origin:** avoid unlabeled, bulk or very cheap drinks in unknown places. Serious methanol poisoning in adulterated beverages has been reported in several countries.
- **Ice and water:** in trusted places they are usually safe; in street stalls, preferably bottled or hot drinks.
- **Behind the wheel:** do not drive after drinking. In several countries in the region, tolerance to alcohol while driving is zero.
- **Do not leave your glass unattended** in bars and parties, as we explain in the guide on [safety in large cities] (/bitacora/seguridad-en-grandes-ciudades/).

## Bring souvenir drinks

A bottle of pisco, mezcal, cachaça or wine is a great souvenir. Wrap it well in checked luggage, never in carry-on if it exceeds the liquid limits, and check how much alcohol your country allows without paying taxes. Remember that the coca leaf and its derivatives are prohibited in many destinations.

## Frequent errors

- **Move the bulb** of the mate or stay with it too long.
- **Take coca leaves** in your return luggage.
- **Drink unlabeled liquor** of unknown origin.
- **Drinking alcohol in excess** in the first days of altitude.
- **Ask for ice at any stall** without knowing where the water comes from.
- **Drink tequila or mezcal in one gulp** without appreciating them.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it rude to refuse a mate?

Not if you do it kindly. But accepting it is a way to integrate, and it is enough to say "thank you" when you no longer want it.

### What is the difference between tequila and mezcal?

Tequila is a type of mezcal that is made with blue agave in a defined region. Mezcal can be made with many species of agave and often has smoky notes from traditional cooking in earthen ovens.

### Does chicha morada have alcohol?

No. Chicha morada is a non-alcoholic purple corn drink. The one that is fermented is chicha de jora.

### Can you drink coca tea without a problem?

In Peru and Bolivia it is legal and very common. But it can test positive for drugs and should not be taken to other countries.

## In summary

The drinks of Latin America tell the story of the region: mate and tereré in the Southern Cone, corn chichas and coca mate in the Andes, fresh waters and pulque in Mexico, tropical fruit juices, agave, cane and grape distillates and high-altitude wines. Try them in markets, pulquerías, wineries and homes, respect the customs that accompany them and drink in moderation, especially at altitude. Avoid liquor of dubious origin, do not drive if you drink and do not carry coca leaves in your return luggage.
