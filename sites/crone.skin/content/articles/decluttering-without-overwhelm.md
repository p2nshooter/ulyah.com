By our fifties and sixties, most of us have accumulated a great deal: furniture from several homes, children's artwork and school reports, kitchen gadgets, books, inherited china, souvenirs, paperwork, clothes in several sizes, and boxes in the loft whose contents are a mystery. Some of it is treasured. Much of it is simply there, taking up space, needing to be cleaned around, and quietly weighing on us.

Decluttering can feel daunting, but it does not have to be a dramatic weekend purge. Done gently, a little at a time, it can be freeing, even enjoyable. It creates a home that is easier to clean and move around in, makes room for the things and activities you love, and saves loved ones from a difficult task in the future. This guide offers a calm, practical approach.

## Why declutter now?

- **Ease and safety.** Fewer objects mean fewer trip hazards, easier cleaning and more space to move.
- **Calm.** Many people find a less cluttered home feels more peaceful.
- **Time and energy.** Less to clean, maintain, search through and organise.
- **Freedom.** Whether you plan to stay put, downsize or travel more, owning less makes life simpler.
- **Kindness to family.** Sorting your own belongings, while you can make the decisions, is a gift to those who might otherwise have to do it.
- **Rediscovery.** Decluttering often uncovers treasures you had forgotten.

## Start with the right mindset

- **Go slowly.** Decades of belongings will not be sorted in a day. Little and often is more sustainable.
- **Be kind to yourself.** Clutter is not a moral failing; it accumulates naturally over a busy life.
- **Focus on what you want to keep**, not only on what to get rid of. The aim is a home filled with things you use and love.
- **Expect emotions.** Objects carry memories. Allow yourself time to remember, laugh or feel sad.

## Begin small

The biggest mistake is starting with the hardest area, such as the loft full of family memorabilia. Instead, begin somewhere small and low-emotion, to build momentum and confidence:

- a kitchen drawer;
- the bathroom cabinet;
- a bookshelf;
- the coat cupboard;
- a handbag.

Set a timer for fifteen or thirty minutes. When it rings, you can stop or continue. Finishing a small area gives a satisfying sense of progress.

## A simple sorting system

Prepare boxes or bags labelled:

- **Keep**: things you use, love and have space for.
- **Donate or sell**: good-condition items others can use.
- **Recycle**: items that can be recycled.
- **Rubbish**: broken, worn-out or unusable items.
- **Elsewhere**: things that belong in another room.
- **Maybe**: items you are unsure about (use sparingly).

For each item, ask:

1. **Do I use it?** When did I last use it?
2. **Do I love it?** Does it bring joy, comfort or meaning?
3. **Do I have space for it?**
4. **Do I have duplicates?**
5. **Would I buy it again today?**
6. **If I let it go, could I replace it easily if needed?**

## Room by room

### Kitchen

- **Clear out expired food** from cupboards, the fridge and the freezer.
- **Reduce duplicates**: multiple sets of measuring cups, dozens of mugs, numerous plastic containers without lids.
- **Let go of gadgets** you rarely use.
- **Keep everyday items** at waist-to-shoulder height for easy reach.
- **Consider how much you actually cook and entertain now.** If you no longer host large family gatherings, you may not need the large serving dishes.

### Living room

- **Books**: keep favourites and reference books; donate the rest or pass them to friends.
- **Films and music**: if you now stream, consider letting go of physical collections, keeping only special items.
- **Ornaments**: display a smaller selection of favourites so each can be appreciated.
- **Cables and old electronics**: recycle responsibly, wiping personal data from devices first.

### Bedroom and wardrobe

Clothes are a big category. Our guide to editing your wardrobe offers a step-by-step method. Keep bedside tables clear for a restful space, and check under the bed.

### Bathroom

- **Discard expired medicines** safely — many pharmacies accept them for disposal.
- **Clear out old toiletries and cosmetics** (see our guide to editing your beauty shelf).
- **Keep towels** to a practical number.

### Spare room, loft, garage and shed

These spaces are often the most cluttered and emotionally charged. Tackle them in stages:

- **Work in short sessions**, ideally with help for heavy lifting.
- **Bring boxes down** a few at a time rather than working in a hot or cramped loft.
- **Check for safety**: ladders, lighting and heavy items need care. Ask for help if needed.
- **Sort tools and garden equipment**, keeping what you use and donating duplicates.

## Sentimental items

These are often the hardest to sort. Some approaches that help:

- **Leave them until last**, when your decision-making skills are stronger.
- **Keep the best, not everything.** Choose a few of your children's drawings, a handful of letters, a favourite piece of a relative's china.
- **Create a memory box** for each family member or period of life, and keep what fits.
- **Photograph items** before letting them go. A photo book of treasured objects can be a beautiful keepsake.
- **Share stories.** Talk to family about the history of objects, and write notes to accompany heirlooms you keep.
- **Offer items to family members**, without pressure. Many younger people have less space and different tastes, so do not be hurt if they decline.
- **Repurpose**: turn a quilt of old clothes, frame a special piece of fabric, or use inherited china every day rather than keeping it in a cupboard.

Remember that memories live in you, not in objects.

## Paperwork

Paperwork accumulates quietly. Sort it into categories: keep, shred, recycle. Keep important documents — such as birth and marriage certificates, wills, property documents, pension and insurance information — together and securely. Shred documents with personal details before recycling. Going paperless for bills and statements can reduce future clutter.

## Letting go well

### Donating

- **Charity shops** welcome clean, good-condition clothing, books, homeware and some furniture. Check what they accept before taking items.
- **Furniture reuse charities** may collect large items.
- **Community groups and online free-sharing networks** connect you with people who need items.
- **Schools, libraries, community centres and animal shelters** may accept specific items.

### Selling

- **Online marketplaces** for furniture, collectables and good-quality items.
- **Auction houses** for antiques, jewellery and collectables; ask for a valuation if you are unsure of an item's worth.
- **Car-boot sales and yard sales** for smaller items.

Selling takes time and effort; decide whether the money is worth it for each item.

### Recycling and disposal

- **Check local recycling rules** for electronics, batteries, paint, textiles and furniture.
- **Use bulky waste collection services** for large items.
- **Dispose of medicines, chemicals and paint** safely, following local guidance.

## When decluttering stalls

Almost everyone hits a point where progress slows. You may feel tired, sad, guilty about waste, or simply bored. A few ideas can help you start again:

- **Change the room.** If the loft feels too much, return to a small, easy area for a quick win.
- **Change the question.** Instead of asking "Should I get rid of this?", ask "If this were lost tomorrow, would I replace it?"
- **Use the box test.** Pack uncertain items in a dated box. If you have not opened it in six months, donate it unopened.
- **Celebrate progress.** Take before-and-after photographs of each cleared space.
- **Think about the people who will benefit.** Knowing that a winter coat will keep someone warm, or that books will be read again, makes letting go easier.
- **Accept "good enough".** A home does not need to look like a magazine. The aim is a space that works for you.

## Organising what you keep

Once you have decided what to keep, give every item a home:

- **Store things where you use them**: tea towels near the sink, gardening gloves by the back door, reading glasses beside your favourite chair.
- **Keep the most-used items within easy reach**, avoiding high shelves and low cupboards that require stretching or kneeling.
- **Use clear, labelled containers** for smaller items.
- **Leave some empty space**: a shelf or drawer that is not completely full is easier to use and keep tidy.

## Getting help

- **Friends and family** can offer practical help and moral support — choose helpers who are patient and respectful of your decisions.
- **Professional declutterers or organisers** can help with large projects or emotional situations.
- **Removal and clearance companies** can take away large quantities of unwanted items; ask what they donate or recycle.

## Keeping clutter away

- **One in, one out**: when something new comes in, something old goes.
- **Pause before buying**: do I need it? Where will it live?
- **Regular mini-sessions**: ten minutes a week keeps clutter under control.
- **Give experiences**, consumables or charitable donations as gifts, and gently suggest the same to family.
- **Handle post immediately**: recycle junk mail, file important papers.

## Decluttering and later life planning

Decluttering is also a practical part of planning ahead, whether or not you intend to move. Some people use the idea of "death cleaning", a phrase popularised in Scandinavia, meaning gradually sorting your belongings so that others are not burdened later. It need not be morbid; many find it liberating and an opportunity to pass on meaningful objects with their stories while they can.

## The bottom line

Decluttering is a gift to yourself and your family. Start small, go slowly, sort with clear questions, treat sentimental items with care, and let go generously through donation, sale and recycling. With each drawer and cupboard cleared, your home becomes lighter, calmer and easier to live in — and filled with things that truly matter to you.
