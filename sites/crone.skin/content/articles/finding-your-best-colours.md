Have you ever put on a top and immediately looked tired, washed out or slightly grey — and then changed into another in a different colour and suddenly looked rested and bright? The difference is often not the style or the fit but the colour. Colours worn near the face reflect onto the skin, and the right shades can make the complexion glow, soften lines and brighten the eyes, while the wrong ones can emphasise shadows and redness.

Many women find that the colours that suited them in their twenties no longer work in their fifties and beyond. Hair may have turned grey or silver, skin tone may have softened or become paler, and eye colour can appear less intense. This is not a loss — it is an invitation to discover a new palette. This guide explains the basics of colour and how to test which shades suit you, without needing a professional consultation.

## Why colour matters near the face

Colour affects how others perceive your skin. When you wear a colour, it casts a subtle tint onto your face and influences how your features look by contrast. A flattering colour can:

- make skin look clearer and more even;
- reduce the appearance of shadows under the eyes;
- make eyes look brighter;
- harmonise with your hair and natural colouring.

An unflattering colour can make skin look sallow, grey, red or tired, and can draw attention to lines and blemishes.

Colour matters most in items worn near the face: tops, dresses, scarves, collars, jackets and coats. Trousers, skirts and shoes affect your overall look but have less impact on your complexion.

## The three key ideas

Most colour systems are built around three characteristics of your natural colouring: **undertone**, **depth** and **contrast**.

### 1. Undertone: warm, cool or neutral

Undertone is the subtle hue beneath the surface of your skin. It does not change with a tan or with redness, though it may soften with age.

- **Warm undertones** have golden, peachy or yellow hints. Warm-toned people often look good in colours with yellow undertones: camel, olive, warm red, coral, rust, cream and gold jewellery.
- **Cool undertones** have pink, rosy or bluish hints. Cool-toned people often suit colours with blue undertones: navy, cool grey, berry, rose, icy blue, pure white and silver jewellery.
- **Neutral undertones** have a balance of both, and many people with neutral undertones can wear a broad range of colours, especially those that are neither very warm nor very cool.

### Ways to estimate your undertone

None of these tests is perfect, but together they give useful clues:

- **The fabric test.** In natural daylight, without makeup, hold a piece of warm, golden fabric (such as camel or mustard) and then a cool fabric (such as icy pink or blue-grey) under your chin. Which makes your skin look clearer and brighter?
- **The jewellery test.** Do you look better in gold or in silver? Gold often flatters warm undertones; silver often flatters cool undertones. Many people with neutral undertones suit both.
- **The white test.** Do you look better in crisp, pure white or in soft cream? Pure white often suits cool undertones; cream often suits warm undertones.
- **The vein test.** Some people look at the veins on the inner wrist: bluish veins may suggest cool undertones, greenish veins warm undertones. This test is popular but unreliable on its own.

### 2. Depth: light, medium or deep

Depth describes how light or dark your overall colouring is — your skin, hair and eyes together.

- **Light colouring** often suits lighter and softer colours, which do not overwhelm the face.
- **Deep colouring** often suits deeper, richer colours.
- **Medium colouring** can usually wear a mix.

As hair turns grey, overall depth often becomes lighter, which is one reason why very dark colours may start to look heavy near the face.

### 3. Contrast and clarity

Contrast is the difference between your hair, skin and eyes.

- **High contrast**, such as dark hair with pale skin, or bright silver hair with deep skin, often suits bold, clear colours and strong combinations such as navy and white.
- **Low contrast**, such as fair hair with fair skin, or soft grey hair with light skin, often suits softer, blended colours and tonal outfits.

Clarity describes whether your colouring looks bright and clear, or soft and muted.

- **Clear colouring** often suits bright, saturated colours.
- **Soft or muted colouring** often suits greyed, dusty or blended colours — such as dusty rose, sage, soft teal and taupe.

## How colouring changes with age

Many women notice that:

- **hair becomes lighter** or grey, reducing contrast;
- **skin may become paler or more uneven**, with more redness or pigmentation;
- **eye colour may appear softer.**

As a result, colours that were once flattering — especially very dark, very bright or very warm shades — may now overpower the face. Many women find that softer, slightly lighter or more muted versions of their favourite colours suit them better now. Others, particularly those with bright silver hair, find that clear jewel tones look wonderful.

Your colours are not fixed for life, so it is worth reassessing from time to time.

## How to test colours at home

You do not need a professional consultation to find flattering colours, though some people enjoy one. A home test works well:

1. **Choose natural daylight**, ideally near a window, without direct sunlight.
2. **Remove makeup** or keep it minimal.
3. **Cover your hair** with a white or neutral towel if it is dyed, or leave it as it is if you plan to keep its natural colour.
4. **Wear a neutral top**, or drape a white towel over your shoulders.
5. **Hold different coloured fabrics under your face** one at a time — clothes from your wardrobe, scarves or even coloured paper.
6. **Look at your face, not the colour.** Does your skin look smoother, brighter and healthier? Do shadows and redness fade? Or does your skin look grey, yellow, red or tired?
7. **Compare pairs** of colours directly: warm red versus blue-red, cream versus white, camel versus grey, coral versus pink.
8. **Take photos** in the same light; it is often easier to judge colours in a picture.

Ask a friend whose opinion you trust to help. Often others notice the difference more readily than we do.

## Building a palette

From your tests, make a list of colours that make you look well. Aim for:

- **Two or three neutrals** for the base of your wardrobe — for example navy, soft grey and cream, or camel, chocolate and olive.
- **Several accent colours** that make you glow.
- **A few colours to avoid** near your face.

Keep a small card or photo of your palette on your phone to use when shopping.

### Example palettes

These are only illustrations; your own tests are the best guide.

- **Warm and soft**: camel, olive, warm taupe, terracotta, peach, moss green, teal, cream.
- **Cool and soft**: soft navy, grey, dusty rose, mauve, lavender, sage, soft white.
- **Cool and clear**: navy, charcoal, pure white, fuchsia, sapphire, emerald, true red.
- **Warm and clear**: chocolate, warm navy, ivory, coral, tomato red, turquoise, golden yellow.
- **Deep**: charcoal, black, deep plum, burgundy, forest green, deep teal.
- **Light**: light grey, stone, powder blue, blush pink, mint, soft aqua.

## What if you love an unflattering colour?

You do not have to give up colours you love:

- **Wear them away from your face**: in trousers, skirts, shoes or bags.
- **Use a flattering colour near your face**, such as a scarf, collar or necklace, to separate the colour from your skin.
- **Adjust makeup**: a touch more blusher or a stronger lip colour can balance a challenging shade.
- **Choose a different version**: a softer, lighter, deeper or warmer version of the same colour may work beautifully.

## Prints and patterns

Colour analysis applies to prints too. A print works best when its main colours come from your palette and its scale suits your frame and features:

- **Small, delicate prints** often suit petite women and softer, low-contrast colouring.
- **Larger, bolder prints** often suit taller women and higher-contrast colouring.
- **Blurred, watercolour-style prints** suit soft, muted colouring.
- **Crisp graphic prints and stripes** suit clear, high-contrast colouring.

Look at a print from a few steps away: if it blends into a single impression that flatters you, it works; if you see the print before you see your face, it may be overpowering.

## Colour and makeup

Your colour palette can also guide makeup choices. Warm undertones often suit peach, coral and warm brown makeup; cool undertones often suit rose, berry and taupe. If you change your hair colour or go grey, revisit your makeup shades. Our guide to makeup for mature skin explains more.

## Colour and confidence

The most important colours are the ones that make you feel wonderful. Colour can lift your mood, express your personality and help you stand out — or blend in when you want to. Use these guidelines as a starting point, not a rulebook.

## The bottom line

Finding your best colours is a simple way to look brighter and more confident every day. Explore your undertone, depth and contrast, test colours near your face in natural daylight, build a palette of neutrals and accents that suit you, and wear the colours you love in clever ways. As your colouring changes with age, your palette can evolve too — and discovering new favourites is part of the pleasure.
