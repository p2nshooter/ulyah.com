Many of us have taken the kind of holiday that leaves us needing another holiday. Seven cities in ten days, early starts, packed itineraries, hours on coaches and a camera full of places we barely remember. We saw a great deal, but we experienced very little.

Slow travel is the gentle alternative. Instead of rushing from sight to sight, you stay longer in fewer places. You settle in, find a favourite café, learn the rhythm of a neighbourhood, and let the place reveal itself. You travel overland when you can, enjoy the journey as part of the holiday, and leave plenty of unplanned time for discovery.

For women over fifty, slow travel has particular appeal. It is easier on the body, kinder to the budget over longer trips, often better for the environment, and far more relaxing. Above all, it offers something that rushed travel rarely does: a genuine sense of having lived, briefly, somewhere else.

## What slow travel means

Slow travel is not a set of rules but an attitude. It usually involves:

- **Staying longer in one place** — a week, a month or even a season, rather than a night or two.
- **Depth rather than breadth** — getting to know one region well, rather than skimming across a continent.
- **Living like a resident** — shopping at markets, cooking some meals, using local transport, joining in with local life.
- **Enjoying the journey** — taking trains, ferries, buses or even walking, rather than always flying.
- **Leaving room for serendipity** — keeping plans flexible enough to follow recommendations, accept invitations and change your mind.
- **Respecting the place** — supporting local businesses, learning a little of the language and culture, and treading lightly.

## The benefits

### More rest, less exhaustion

Packing and unpacking, checking in and out, and constant travel are tiring. With one or two bases, you unpack once, learn where things are, and have energy left for enjoying yourself. Many women find that slow travel suits their pace and avoids the fatigue of whirlwind tours.

### Deeper experiences

When you stay longer, you notice things that short-stay visitors miss: the market on a Thursday, the village festival, the way the light changes in the square in the evening. Shopkeepers start to recognise you. You find the bakery the locals use. You might be invited into a home or a celebration.

### Better value

Longer stays often attract significant discounts on accommodation, especially in rented apartments or cottages. Cooking some meals saves money, and using local transport rather than taxis and tours keeps costs down.

### Kinder to the planet

Fewer flights and more overland travel reduce your environmental impact, and spending money in local, independent businesses supports the communities you visit.

## Choosing where to go

Almost anywhere can be enjoyed slowly, but some places lend themselves particularly well to it:

- **Small cities and market towns** with walkable centres, good cafés and easy day trips.
- **Rural regions** with villages, footpaths, vineyards, lakes or coastline to explore.
- **Islands**, where the pace of life is naturally slower.
- **Regions with good public transport**, so you can explore without driving.

When choosing, think about what you love: art, food, walking, history, gardens, the sea, music. Choose a base with plenty of these within easy reach, and a pleasant everyday environment for the days you simply want to potter.

Consider the season, too. Travelling outside the peak months often means fewer crowds, lower prices and a more authentic experience — though check that the places you want to visit are open, as some seasonal destinations close down in winter.

## Finding the right base

Your accommodation matters even more when you are staying longer. Consider:

- **Apartments or cottages** with a kitchen, a comfortable sitting area and good light. Monthly rates are often much lower than nightly ones.
- **Guesthouses or small family hotels**, where you will be looked after and can ask for local advice.
- **House-sitting or home exchange**, which can provide free or very low-cost accommodation in return for looking after someone's home and pets — popular with older travellers who have flexible time.
- **Location**: within walking distance of shops, cafés and transport, in a neighbourhood that feels safe and pleasant.

Read reviews carefully, and ask practical questions before booking: Is there Wi-Fi? Heating or air conditioning? A washing machine? Stairs or a lift? How far is the nearest shop?

## Living like a local

The heart of slow travel is daily life. Some ideas:

- **Shop at the market** and learn the names of local foods. Try regional specialities and ask stallholders how to cook them.
- **Find your café** and go most mornings. Before long you will be greeted as a regular.
- **Take a class**: cookery, language, painting, dance or crafts. It is one of the best ways to meet people.
- **Use the library, pool or community events**, which are often open to visitors.
- **Learn some of the language.** Even a few phrases make a difference, and locals appreciate the effort.
- **Follow local rhythms** — the long lunch, the evening stroll, the market day.
- **Read about the place**: history, novels set there, local newspapers.

## Day trips and exploring

With a base, you can explore the surrounding area at leisure. Plan a mix of day trips and quiet days. Local buses and trains are often an adventure in themselves. Tourist offices are excellent sources of ideas, as are your hosts and fellow travellers.

Try not to fill every day. Leave days free to wander, read, write, sketch, or simply sit in the square and watch the world go by. Those unplanned days often become the most memorable.

## The journey as part of the holiday

Slow travellers often avoid flying where possible, or combine a single flight with overland travel once they arrive. Train journeys in particular can be a highlight: comfortable seats, room to move, beautiful scenery, a dining car, and arrival in the centre of town rather than at a distant airport. Ferries, river boats and long-distance buses can also be enjoyable.

Overland travel takes longer, but that is the point. The changing landscape, the conversations with fellow passengers and the sense of distance covered all add to the experience.

## Planning a longer trip

If you are considering a stay of several weeks or months, think about:

### Practicalities at home

- **Who will look after your home**, garden, post and pets?
- **Bills and payments**: set up direct debits and online banking.
- **Security**: timer switches, a trusted neighbour, informing your insurer if your home will be empty for an extended period, as policies often have conditions about this.

### Paperwork

- **Passports and visas**: check the rules for the length of stay you are planning, as limits on visitor stays vary between countries and regions.
- **Travel insurance**: many standard policies limit the length of individual trips; check that yours covers the whole period and any pre-existing medical conditions.
- **Healthcare**: understand how to access care where you are going, and take sufficient medication with copies of prescriptions.

### Money

- **Use cards** that do not charge foreign transaction fees, if possible.
- **Budget for longer stays**, including accommodation, food, transport, activities and a contingency fund.
- **Keep some cash** in local currency for markets and small shops.

## A sample slow week

If you are wondering what a slow week actually looks like, here is one possible shape, based on a stay in a small market town:

- **Day one:** arrive, unpack properly, walk the streets around your base, find the nearest bakery and grocer, and have an early night.
- **Day two:** visit the tourist office for maps and ideas, explore the old town on foot, and book a cookery or language class for later in the week.
- **Day three:** market morning — buy fruit, cheese and bread for a picnic, then take a local bus to a nearby village or viewpoint.
- **Day four:** a deliberately quiet day: a long breakfast, a book in the garden or the square, an afternoon rest and an evening stroll.
- **Day five:** your class, followed by lunch with the people you met there.
- **Day six:** a longer day trip by train to a larger town, a famous garden or a museum.
- **Day seven:** return to your favourite places, write postcards, and decide whether to stay on a little longer.

Notice how much empty space there is. That space is not wasted; it is where the unexpected conversations, discoveries and moments of contentment happen.

## Packing for slow travel

Paradoxically, a longer trip does not require much more luggage. With a washing machine or a launderette, you can pack for a week and wash as you go. Choose versatile, comfortable clothes in a coordinated palette, good walking shoes, layers for changing weather, and a few items that make you feel at home: a favourite scarf, a good book, a small photo, a journal.

## Staying connected — or not

Slow travel offers a chance to step back from constant connection. Some travellers enjoy a digital detox; others keep in regular touch with family through video calls. Find the balance that suits you. Many women enjoy keeping a travel journal or blog, or sending postcards, as a way to record and share their experiences.

## Slow travel close to home

You do not need to go abroad to travel slowly. A fortnight in a rented cottage in a region you have never explored, a week on a canal boat, or a month-long house-sit in another part of your own country can all offer the same joys: time, depth and a change of rhythm.

## Coming home refreshed

Women who travel slowly often say they return home rested rather than exhausted, with richer memories and a sense of having truly experienced a place. Many find that the habits of slow travel — noticing, savouring, connecting — come home with them too.

Next time you plan a trip, consider doing less, more slowly. Choose one place, stay longer, and let it unfold around you. You may find that seeing less allows you to experience far more.
