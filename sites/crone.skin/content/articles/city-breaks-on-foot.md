A few days in a beautiful city can be one of the most refreshing ways to travel. There is no long journey to recover from, no complicated itinerary, just streets to wander, buildings to admire, museums to explore, cafés to rest in and the pleasure of discovering somewhere new. City breaks suit many women after fifty particularly well: they can be short, flexible and full of culture, and they can be done alone, with a friend, with a partner or with family.

They can also be tiring if they are planned like a race: too many sights, too much walking on hard pavements, too many late nights. This guide explains how to plan a city break that suits your pace and energy, from choosing the city and the season to picking a well-placed base, shaping a gentle daily rhythm, enjoying museums without exhaustion, eating well, getting around and staying safe.

## Choosing the city

Think about what you enjoy most:

- **art and museums**, such as cities with great galleries and historic collections;
- **architecture and history**, such as old towns with cathedrals, palaces and narrow streets;
- **food and markets**, such as cities known for their cuisine;
- **music and theatre**, such as cities with concert halls and opera houses;
- **gardens and parks**, such as cities with famous green spaces;
- **water**, such as cities on rivers, canals, lakes or the sea.

Also consider practical questions:

- **How far is it?** A short, direct journey leaves more energy for the city itself.
- **How hilly is it?** Some beautiful cities are built on steep hills with many steps; others are flat and easy to walk.
- **How easy is public transport?** Good trams, buses or metros make a big difference.
- **What language is spoken?** Not essential, but it affects how you feel getting around.

## Choosing the season

The same city can feel completely different at different times of year.

- **Spring and autumn** often bring mild weather, fewer crowds and pleasant walking.
- **Summer** can be very hot in some cities, with crowds at popular sights.
- **Winter** can be atmospheric and quiet, with festive markets in some places, but days are shorter and colder.

Avoiding the busiest weeks, such as major holidays and festivals, usually means lower prices and calmer streets, unless the festival is what you want to see.

## Choosing where to stay

Location is the most important decision for a relaxed city break.

- **Stay central**, within walking distance of the sights you most want to see, or close to a good public transport stop.
- **Check the surroundings**: a quiet street near a lively area is ideal; directly above a busy bar may not be.
- **Ask about lifts**, especially in older buildings, and the number of stairs to the room.
- **Look for a comfortable bed and a quiet room**, since good sleep makes everything easier.
- **Consider breakfast included**, which saves searching for somewhere each morning.

A well-placed hotel or apartment lets you return for a rest in the middle of the day, a luxury that transforms a city break.

## A gentle daily rhythm

Many experienced travellers find that a rhythm like this works well:

1. **Morning:** one main activity, such as a museum or a guided walk, while you are fresh and before crowds build.
2. **Late morning:** a café stop.
3. **Lunch:** a relaxed meal, perhaps the main meal of the day.
4. **Early afternoon:** a rest, back at the hotel or in a park.
5. **Late afternoon:** a gentle wander, a market, a garden or shopping.
6. **Evening:** an early dinner, a concert or simply a stroll.

The key is **one or two highlights per day**, not five. You will remember more and enjoy it more.

## Walking the city

Walking is the best way to discover a city, but it needs some planning.

### Shoes and comfort

- **Wear comfortable, broken-in shoes** with good cushioning and grip, especially for cobblestones. Our guide to [comfortable shoes that still look good](/journal/comfortable-shoes-that-look-good/) has ideas.
- **Bring a second pair** to alternate.
- **Carry a small bag** that sits comfortably, ideally across the body.
- **Take water** and a light layer.

### Routes

- **Plan walks by area**, grouping sights that are close together.
- **Use maps to check distances and hills** before setting out.
- **Mix walking with public transport**, trams or river boats, which double as sightseeing.
- **Join a guided walking tour**, which brings streets to life and often goes at a gentle pace. Many cities offer themed walks on history, architecture or food.

### Rest stops

- **Benches, parks and churches** offer free places to sit.
- **Cafés** are ideal for longer breaks.
- **Plan toilet stops** in museums, department stores and cafés.

Our guide to [walking for everyday fitness](/journal/walking-for-everyday-fitness/) explains how to build stamina before a trip.

## Museums without exhaustion

Museums can be wonderful and exhausting in equal measure.

- **Choose one section or a few highlights** rather than trying to see everything.
- **Arrive early** or at quieter times, often weekday mornings or late openings.
- **Book tickets in advance** for popular museums, to avoid long queues.
- **Use the cloakroom** to leave coats and bags.
- **Take a break** in the museum café halfway through.
- **Use audio guides or apps**, which help you focus on key works.
- **Borrow a stool or wheelchair** if the museum offers them, or check for seating in galleries.
- **Ask about discounts** for older visitors, which some museums offer.

Two hours is often plenty before the mind and feet start to protest.

## Eating well

- **Enjoy local specialities**, in markets, bakeries and traditional restaurants.
- **Eat your main meal at lunchtime**, when many restaurants offer set menus at lower prices.
- **Book dinner** at popular restaurants, especially at weekends.
- **Ask locals or hotel staff** for recommendations away from the busiest tourist streets.
- **Carry snacks and water**, to avoid becoming tired and hungry.

If you have dietary needs, learn a few key phrases or carry a card in the local language explaining them.

## Getting around

- **Buy a day pass or travel card** if you will use public transport often.
- **Use taxis or ride-hailing apps** for evening returns or when tired; make sure they are licensed.
- **Check accessibility**: some metro stations have many stairs and no lifts.
- **Consider hop-on hop-off buses or boat tours**, which give an overview of the city without walking.

## Keeping a little room for chance

The best moments of a city break are often unplanned: a small courtyard glimpsed through an open gate, a concert in a church you happened to pass, a conversation with the owner of a bookshop. Leaving gaps in your plans makes room for these discoveries. Try spending one afternoon simply wandering a neighbourhood without a map, sitting in a square and watching daily life, or asking a local where they would go on a free afternoon. These are the moments that make a city feel like yours.

## Travelling solo

City breaks are ideal for solo travel. You can follow your own interests, rest when you like and meet people on tours and in cafés. Our guide to [solo travel for women over fifty](/journal/solo-travel-for-women-over-fifty/) covers planning, safety and enjoying your own company.

## Staying safe

- **Keep valuables secure**, in a zipped bag worn in front, especially in crowds and on public transport.
- **Carry only what you need** for the day.
- **Know how to contact local emergency services**, and keep your accommodation's address on you.
- **Be aware of common scams**, such as distraction tactics in busy areas.
- **Tell someone your plans** if you are travelling alone.
- **Take out travel insurance** that covers your needs, including any health conditions, which you should declare.

## Packing for a city break

Pack light: you will be carrying your bag up stairs and through stations.

- **a few versatile outfits** that mix and match;
- **layers** for changing weather;
- **comfortable shoes** and one smarter pair for evenings;
- **a small umbrella or rain jacket**;
- **any medication**, in your hand luggage, with a list of what you take.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a city break be?

Three or four days is enough for many cities, with time to see highlights and rest. Longer stays allow a slower pace and day trips.

### Is a city break suitable if I cannot walk far?

Yes, with planning. Choose a flat city with good public transport, stay centrally, use taxis, boat tours and hop-on hop-off buses, and check accessibility in advance.

### How do I avoid crowds?

Travel outside peak seasons, visit popular sights early in the morning or late in the day, and explore quieter neighbourhoods.

## The bottom line

A city break is most enjoyable when it is planned around your interests and your pace. Choose a city and a season that suit you, stay centrally in a quiet, comfortable place, and shape each day around one or two highlights with plenty of rest. Walk in good shoes, mix walking with public transport and guided tours, enjoy museums in focused, unhurried visits, and make lunch the main meal. Keep valuables secure, take out suitable travel insurance, pack light, and give yourself permission to sit in a café and simply watch the city go by.
