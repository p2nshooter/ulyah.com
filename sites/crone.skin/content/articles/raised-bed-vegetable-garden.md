There is a particular satisfaction in eating something you have grown yourself: a sun-warmed tomato, a handful of salad leaves picked minutes before lunch, beans that snap with freshness. Many women find that their interest in growing food deepens after fifty, with more time to enjoy the garden and a wish to eat fresh, seasonal produce. Yet traditional vegetable gardening can be hard work: digging heavy soil, kneeling for long periods, bending to weed and harvest. Raised beds change that. By bringing the soil up to you, they make growing food easier on the back, knees and hands, and often more productive too.

This guide explains why raised beds suit so many gardeners, how to choose the right height and position, how to build and fill a bed, what to grow for good results, how to water efficiently, which tools make the work easier, and how to follow a gentle routine through the seasons. If you have back, joint or heart problems, pace yourself and ask your doctor or physiotherapist about safe ways to garden.

## Why raised beds?

- **Less bending and kneeling:** the higher the bed, the less you need to stoop.
- **Better soil:** you fill the bed with good-quality soil and compost, avoiding heavy clay or poor ground.
- **Good drainage:** water drains well, which many vegetables like.
- **Earlier warming:** the soil in raised beds tends to warm up sooner in spring.
- **Fewer weeds:** a defined area is easier to keep tidy.
- **Accessibility:** taller beds can be reached from a stool or even a wheelchair.
- **Neatness:** they bring structure and charm to a garden, even a small one.

## Choosing the height and size

The right height depends on your comfort:

- **Low beds**, around knee height, are cheaper to build and fill but still require some bending.
- **Medium beds**, around thigh height, allow you to sit on the edge and work.
- **Tall beds**, around waist height, can be worked standing up with little bending.

Width matters as much as height. You should be able to **reach the centre of the bed from the side without stretching**. For most people, a bed no wider than about the length of an arm and a half works well if you can reach from both sides; narrower if you can only reach from one side. Length can be whatever suits your space.

Leave **wide, level paths** between beds, ideally wide enough for a wheelbarrow, a garden stool or a walking aid.

## Choosing the site

Most vegetables need **plenty of sun**, ideally at least six hours of direct sunlight a day in the growing season. Look for a spot that is:

- **sunny** and sheltered from strong winds;
- **close to the house** and a water supply, so watering and harvesting are easy;
- **on level ground**, or levelled before building;
- **away from large trees**, whose roots compete for water and nutrients.

If you have only a balcony or patio, raised planters on legs work in the same way. Our guide to [balcony and container gardening](/journal/balcony-and-container-gardening/) explains how to grow in small spaces.

## Building a raised bed

You can buy kits or build your own.

### Materials

| Material | Pros | Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Untreated hardwood or naturally durable wood | attractive, natural | lasts several years; check it is suitable for growing food |
| Recycled plastic or composite boards | long-lasting, low maintenance | can be more expensive |
| Galvanised steel kits | durable, modern look | can heat up in strong sun; check edges are rolled or protected |
| Brick or stone | permanent and beautiful | more expensive and harder to build |

Avoid old wood treated with preservatives that may not be safe for growing food, such as some old railway sleepers. Ask the supplier whether a material is suitable for vegetable beds.

### Practical tips

- **Rounded or capped edges** on tall beds make a comfortable place to sit and lean.
- **Line the base** with cardboard to suppress weeds if building on grass, and add a mesh base if moles or burrowing animals are a problem.
- **Ask for help** with heavy lifting and construction; many communities have garden volunteers or handyperson services.

## Filling the bed

Tall beds need a lot of material, so filling them in layers can save money:

1. **Bottom layer:** in very tall beds, some gardeners use coarse organic material, such as small branches and woody prunings, to fill space. It breaks down slowly over time.
2. **Middle layer:** a mix of garden soil and compost.
3. **Top layer:** at least the depth of a spade of good topsoil mixed with well-rotted compost, where most roots will grow.

Expect the level to **sink** over the first year as organic material breaks down; top it up with compost each season.

## What to grow

Start with easy, productive crops you love to eat:

- **salad leaves** and lettuces, which can be picked a few leaves at a time;
- **radishes**, which grow very quickly;
- **herbs** such as parsley, chives, coriander and basil;
- **dwarf beans** and **peas**, which are productive and easy to pick;
- **courgettes**, which give generously, though one or two plants are plenty;
- **tomatoes**, ideally cordon types supported by canes, in a warm, sunny spot;
- **chard and spinach**, which crop over a long period;
- **strawberries**, which are easy to reach in a raised bed.

Avoid crops that take up a lot of space for a small harvest, such as large cabbages or maincrop potatoes, unless you have plenty of room.

### Make the most of space

- **Sow little and often**: sow a short row of salad every few weeks rather than a whole packet at once, for a steady supply.
- **Grow upwards**: climbing beans, peas and cucumbers on frames save space.
- **Interplant**: fast crops such as radishes between slower ones such as tomatoes.
- **Rotate crops** from year to year to reduce pests and diseases.

## Watering wisely

Raised beds can dry out faster than the ground in hot weather.

- **Water deeply** a few times a week rather than lightly every day, so roots grow deeper.
- **Water in the morning or evening**, when less water evaporates.
- **Mulch** the soil surface with compost, straw or leaf mould to keep moisture in.
- **Collect rainwater** in a water butt, placed close to the beds.
- **Consider a simple drip or soaker hose** on a timer, which saves effort and water.
- **Use a lightweight hose or watering can** with a long spout to avoid carrying heavy loads.

## Tools that spare your body

- **Long-handled tools** reduce bending.
- **Lightweight hand tools** with cushioned grips are easier on the hands.
- **A garden kneeler or stool**, especially the type that converts between kneeling pad and seat.
- **A trolley or wheelbarrow** with two wheels for stability.
- **Gloves** that fit well and protect against thorns and blisters.

## A gentle seasonal routine

- **Late winter and early spring:** top up beds with compost, plan what to grow, sow early crops when the soil warms.
- **Late spring and summer:** sow and plant out, water regularly, support tall crops and harvest often.
- **Autumn:** harvest the last crops, sow green manures or cover the soil with compost or leaves, clear spent plants.
- **Winter:** rest, read seed catalogues, repair beds and tools.

Pace yourself: short sessions, frequent breaks, drinking water and working in cooler parts of the day. Our guide to [creating a low-maintenance garden](/journal/low-maintenance-garden/) offers more ideas for keeping gardening enjoyable.

## Keeping pests in balance

In a small raised bed, a few simple measures usually keep pests under control without harsh chemicals. Fine netting over young brassicas and carrots keeps flying insects off, while collars or barriers around seedlings deter slugs and snails. Hand-picking pests in the evening, encouraging birds and beneficial insects with nearby flowers, and growing strong, well-watered plants all help. Accept a little damage: a few holes in a lettuce leaf are a small price for food grown without sprays.

## Sharing the harvest and the work

Vegetable gardening is wonderful to share:

- **grow with grandchildren**, who love fast crops such as radishes and strawberries; see our guide to [grandparenting with joy](/journal/grandparenting-with-joy/);
- **swap seedlings and surplus produce** with neighbours;
- **join a community garden or allotment association**, a great way to learn and meet people.

## Frequently asked questions

### How deep should a raised bed be?

Many vegetables grow well with the depth of a long spade of good soil, but deeper beds hold more moisture and allow deeper roots. Taller beds also mean less bending.

### Do I need to dig the ground first?

Usually not. Clear large weeds, lay cardboard on grass to suppress it, and build the bed on top.

### What if I can only manage a small bed?

Even a small bed can provide fresh salad, herbs and a few beans and tomatoes through the summer. Start small and expand if you enjoy it.

## The bottom line

Raised beds bring the soil up to you, making vegetable growing easier on the back and knees and often more productive. Choose a height that suits your comfort and a width you can reach across, in a sunny, sheltered spot near the house and water. Build with materials suitable for food growing, fill with good soil and compost, and start with easy crops such as salads, herbs, beans, courgettes, tomatoes and strawberries. Water deeply, mulch, collect rainwater, use tools that spare your body, pace yourself and share the harvest.
