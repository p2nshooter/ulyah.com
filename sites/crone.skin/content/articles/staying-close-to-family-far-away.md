Families spread out. Children move to other cities or countries for work, love or adventure; grandchildren grow up in places we visit only once or twice a year; brothers and sisters retire to the coast or move closer to their own children. For many women after fifty, some of the people they love most live far away. Distance can bring a quiet ache, especially at birthdays and holidays. Yet many families stay remarkably close across hundreds or thousands of miles, through small, regular gestures and a little imagination.

This guide offers practical ways to stay connected: making video and phone calls work well, the lasting power of letters and parcels, creating shared rituals, staying close to grandchildren of different ages, planning visits that everyone enjoys, handling time differences and costs, and looking after your own feelings when you miss people.

## Regular contact matters more than long contact

Many families find that **frequent, short contact** builds closeness better than occasional long calls. A five-minute chat twice a week, a photo of the garden, a message saying "thinking of you" all keep the thread unbroken. Agree on a rhythm that suits everyone, rather than waiting for someone else to call.

## Making calls work

### Video calls

Video calls let you see faces, share moments and even "visit" each other's homes.

- **Choose one simple app** that everyone in the family can use, and stick to it.
- **Ask someone to set it up** on your phone, tablet or computer, and practise together until you feel confident.
- **Use a stand** or prop the device up, so your hands are free and the picture is steady.
- **Sit facing a window or lamp** so your face is well lit, not with a bright window behind you.
- **Use headphones** if hearing is difficult, or turn on captions if the app offers them.
- **Agree regular times**, such as Sunday afternoon or a weekday evening, which makes calls a habit rather than a scramble.

### Making calls interesting

Long silences can creep into calls, especially with children. Some ideas:

- **share something to show**: a new plant, a recipe you are cooking, a photograph;
- **ask specific questions**: "What made you laugh this week?" rather than "How are you?";
- **do something together**: cook the same recipe, watch the same programme and chat afterwards, play a simple game;
- **keep calls short with young children**, whose attention wanders quickly.

### Phone calls and voice messages

Not every call needs video. A phone call while out walking, or a voice message someone can listen to when they have a moment, can feel very personal. Voice messages are especially useful across time zones.

## Letters, cards and parcels

In an age of instant messages, a letter or parcel arriving in the post is a special event.

- **Write letters** to grandchildren, who often treasure them; include drawings, pressed flowers or stickers for younger children.
- **Send postcards** from outings or trips.
- **Post small parcels**: a book you loved, home-baked biscuits, seeds to plant, a knitted hat.
- **Encourage replies** with self-addressed envelopes or a "letter-writing kit" for children.
- **Keep a family journal** that travels between households by post, with each person adding a page.

Our guide to [writing your life story](/journal/writing-your-life-story/) suggests ways to share memories in writing that families often treasure.

## Shared rituals

Rituals give relationships a rhythm across the distance:

- **a weekly call at the same time**;
- **a shared book**: read the same book and talk about it chapter by chapter, which works wonderfully with older grandchildren;
- **a bedtime story** read over video;
- **a "photo of the day"** shared in a family group;
- **marking special days**: lighting a candle at the same time on an anniversary, cooking the same dish on a festival;
- **a family quiz or game night** online.

The best rituals are simple enough to keep going for years.

## Staying close to grandchildren

Every age brings different ways to connect:

| Age | Ideas |
|---|---|
| Babies and toddlers | short video calls with songs and peekaboo; recorded stories they can listen to |
| Young children | reading together over video; drawing the same picture; sending parcels; simple games |
| School-age children | helping with homework or a project; sharing hobbies; letters and postcards |
| Teenagers | messaging in the way they prefer; showing interest in their passions without pushing; occasional one-to-one calls |
| Young adults | treating them as friends; sharing your own stories; visiting them on their own ground |

Respect parents' rules and routines, and agree with them how and when to contact the grandchildren. Our guide to [grandparenting with joy](/journal/grandparenting-with-joy/) explores building close bonds while respecting boundaries.

## Planning visits

Visits are precious, and planning helps them go well.

### Before the visit

- **Discuss dates and length** openly, considering school terms, work and everyone's energy.
- **Agree on expectations**: Will you stay with them or nearby? Will you help with childcare? How much time together, and how much apart?
- **Plan a few special activities**, but leave plenty of unplanned time.
- **Consider costs**, and discuss them frankly; sharing travel costs is common in many families.

### During the visit

- **Fit in with their routines** where you can.
- **Offer help** without taking over.
- **Spend one-to-one time** with each person, if possible.
- **Take photos**, but also put the camera away and simply be present.
- **Take time for yourself**, too; rest makes everyone happier.

## Time zones and costs

- **Use a world clock** on your phone to keep track of time differences.
- **Find overlap windows** when both households are awake and free, such as early morning for one and evening for the other.
- **Use free or low-cost calling apps** over Wi-Fi rather than expensive international calls.
- **Send parcels thoughtfully**: international postage can be expensive, so consider sending smaller items or ordering gifts locally for delivery.

## Sharing everyday life, not just news

Distance tends to turn conversations into news reports: what happened, who said what, what is planned next. What often gets lost is the texture of ordinary life, the small things that make us feel part of each other's days. A few ideas bring it back:

- **share the ordinary**: a photo of your morning coffee in the garden, the view from your walk, the cake that did not rise;
- **ask about routines**: what they had for lunch, what the weather is doing, how the commute was;
- **keep each other company** while doing chores, with a video call running in the background while you both cook or fold laundry;
- **celebrate small wins**: a good school report, a new plant flowering, a finished jigsaw.

These little exchanges can feel trivial, but they are what makes a relationship feel close rather than polite. Over time, they create a sense that you know each other's lives from the inside, even from far away.

## Building your digital confidence

If technology feels daunting, you are not alone, and you do not need to master everything. Ask a family member to write down simple step-by-step instructions for the one or two things you use most, such as answering a video call and sending a photo. Many libraries and community centres run free sessions for people who want to build confidence with phones, tablets and computers. Practise with a friend before an important call, and remember that it is perfectly fine to ask for help again: most families are delighted to spend a few minutes helping someone they love stay in touch.

## When distance hurts

Missing family is natural, and it can be especially hard during holidays, illness or big life events.

- **Acknowledge your feelings.** It is normal to feel sad or lonely at times.
- **Build a life full of local connections** too: friends, neighbours, clubs and activities. Our guide to [starting a club or group](/journal/starting-a-club-or-group/) can help.
- **Plan the next contact or visit**, which gives you something to look forward to.
- **Celebrate in your own way** on special days, perhaps with friends.
- **Talk to someone** if sadness becomes persistent; your doctor or a counsellor can help.

## Practical support from afar

Sometimes family members far away need practical help, or you do:

- **share important contact details** and emergency information;
- **agree on a plan** for illness or emergencies;
- **use online shopping or delivery services** to send meals or essentials;
- **keep a list of local friends and neighbours** who can help each household.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should I call my children and grandchildren?

There is no right answer. Agree on a rhythm that suits everyone, and remember that short, frequent contact often works better than long, rare calls.

### What if my grandchildren seem uninterested on calls?

That is common, especially with young children and teenagers. Keep calls short, do something together, or try letters, parcels and shared interests instead.

### How can I help if a family member far away is unwell?

Stay in touch regularly, offer practical help such as arranging deliveries, coordinate with local friends or relatives, and visit when you can.

## The bottom line

Closeness across distance is built from small, regular gestures. Agree on a rhythm of contact, make video calls easy and interesting, and remember the lasting magic of letters and parcels. Create simple shared rituals, adapt the way you connect to each grandchild's age, respect parents' rules and plan visits with clear expectations and plenty of unplanned time. Manage time zones and costs sensibly, build a full local life of your own and seek support if missing your family becomes overwhelming.
