Sun protection is often presented as a summer holiday chore: a bottle of sunscreen packed for the beach and forgotten for the rest of the year. Yet for skin after fifty, everyday protection is one of the most effective things we can do to keep it comfortable, even-toned and healthy. Years of sun exposure show up as dark spots, uneven texture, fine lines and dryness, and the skin's own defences change with age. The good news is that a few simple habits, built into the morning routine, make a real difference at any age.

This guide explains how sunshine affects mature skin, what the information on sunscreen labels means, how much to use and when to reapply, how to wear sunscreen under makeup comfortably, the role of hats, clothing, shade and sunglasses, and why checking your skin regularly is so important. If you have a skin condition, take medication that makes you sensitive to light, or notice any change in a mole or patch of skin, speak to your doctor or a dermatologist.

## How the sun affects mature skin

Sunlight contains ultraviolet radiation. Two types matter most for the skin:

- **UVB** is the main cause of sunburn.
- **UVA** penetrates more deeply and contributes to skin ageing, such as lines, loss of firmness and pigmentation. It reaches us even on cloudy days and passes through window glass.

Over time, sun exposure is linked to dark spots, rough texture, broken capillaries and skin cancers. Mature skin may also be thinner and drier, and some medications increase sensitivity to sunlight. That is why many dermatologists recommend daily protection rather than protection only on sunny days.

## Reading a sunscreen label

Labels vary between countries, but most include:

- **SPF (Sun Protection Factor):** a measure of protection against UVB. Many health organisations recommend using at least SPF 30 for everyday protection.
- **Broad spectrum or UVA rating:** shows that the product also protects against UVA. Look for a broad-spectrum label or the UVA symbol or rating used in your country.
- **Water resistance:** indicates how long the product keeps working when swimming or sweating, according to testing standards.
- **Expiry date:** sunscreen can become less effective after its expiry date or if it has been stored in heat.

### Mineral or chemical filters?

- **Mineral filters**, such as zinc oxide and titanium dioxide, sit on the skin and reflect and absorb UV. They can suit sensitive skin but sometimes leave a white cast, although modern formulas are much better.
- **Chemical filters** absorb UV and are often lighter and easier to blend.

Both types protect well when used correctly. The best sunscreen is the one you enjoy using every day. If your skin reacts to a product, try a different formula or ask a pharmacist or dermatologist for advice.

## How much and how often

Most people apply far less sunscreen than the amount used in testing, which means they get less protection than the label suggests.

- **Use enough:** a common guide is to apply a generous amount to the face and neck, roughly enough to cover two fingers' length for the face and neck together, and more for other exposed areas. Follow the product's instructions.
- **Do not forget** the ears, the back of the neck, the chest, the backs of the hands and the scalp where hair is thin or parted.
- **Reapply** regularly when you are outdoors for long periods, after swimming, sweating or towelling, as directed on the label. Many guidelines suggest reapplying about every two hours outdoors.
- **Use lip balm with SPF**; lips burn easily and are often forgotten.

## Sunscreen in your daily routine

A simple morning routine might be:

1. cleanse;
2. apply any treatment products, such as serums;
3. moisturise, if your sunscreen is not moisturising enough on its own;
4. apply sunscreen generously;
5. wait a few minutes, then apply makeup.

### Under makeup

- **Choose a sunscreen with a texture you like**: lotion, fluid, cream or gel. Many are made specifically to sit well under makeup.
- **Let it settle** for a few minutes before foundation.
- **Do not rely only on makeup with SPF.** Most people do not apply enough foundation or powder to get the protection stated on the label. Use it as an extra layer, not a replacement.
- **Reapplying over makeup:** sunscreen sprays, mists or powders designed for the purpose can help top up protection during the day, although they may not be as reliable as a full layer of cream. ## Beyond sunscreen: hats, clothing and shade

Sunscreen is only one part of sun protection.

- **Seek shade** during the hours when the sun is strongest, usually around the middle of the day. A useful rule of thumb in many places: if your shadow is shorter than you are, the sun is strong.
- **Wear a wide-brimmed hat** that shades the face, ears and neck. Baseball caps leave the ears and neck exposed.
- **Choose clothing that covers**: lightweight long sleeves, tightly woven fabrics and, if you spend a lot of time outdoors, clothing labelled with a UV protection factor.
- **Wear sunglasses** that offer UV protection according to recognised standards, to protect the eyes and the delicate skin around them. Our guide to [glasses and makeup that work together](/journal/glasses-and-makeup/) has tips on choosing frames.
- **Remember reflection**: water, sand, snow and pale concrete reflect UV, increasing exposure.

## Indoors, in the car and by the window

UVA passes through ordinary window glass, which is why some people notice more sun damage on the side of the face and the arm nearest the window when they drive often. If you spend long periods next to a sunny window, at home, at work or in the car:

- apply sunscreen to the face, neck and backs of the hands before setting off on long drives;
- consider sun-protective films or blinds for windows where you sit for hours, checking local rules for car windows;
- keep a small tube of sunscreen in your bag or glove compartment for top-ups.

You do not need to fear daylight indoors; simply include the exposed areas in your morning routine on days when you will sit by a window for a long time.

## Hands, chest and scalp

These areas often show sun damage first, and are often forgotten.

- **Hands:** apply sunscreen to the backs of your hands after washing them, especially before driving or walking. Our guide to [hands and nails care](/journal/hands-and-nails-care/) offers a gentle routine.
- **Chest and neck:** extend sunscreen down to any skin your neckline leaves exposed.
- **Scalp:** if your hair is thinning or you have a parting, wear a hat or use a sunscreen suitable for the scalp. Our guide to [caring for thinning hair](/journal/caring-for-thinning-hair/) has more ideas.

## Check your skin

Regularly looking at your skin helps you notice changes early. Once a month, in good light, check your face, scalp, neck, arms, hands, legs and feet, and ask someone to help with your back, or use a mirror.

See a doctor promptly if you notice:

- a **new mole** or mark, especially if it looks different from your others;
- a mole that **changes** in size, shape or colour;
- a spot that **bleeds, itches, crusts or does not heal**;
- a patch of skin that is **rough, scaly or tender** and does not go away.

Most changes turn out to be harmless, but skin cancers are easier to treat when found early. Your doctor can examine your skin and refer you to a specialist if needed.

## Sun and vitamin D

Sunlight helps the skin make vitamin D, which is important for bones. Recommendations about sun exposure and vitamin D vary between countries and depend on where you live, your skin type and your health. If you are concerned about your vitamin D levels, talk to your doctor rather than skipping sun protection.

## Travelling to sunny places

When travelling somewhere sunnier than home:

- pack sunscreen, lip balm with SPF, a hat and sunglasses in your hand luggage, following airline rules for liquids;
- protect your skin from the first day, when it is not used to the sun;
- take extra care in the mountains, near water and in snow.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need sunscreen on cloudy days?

Many dermatologists recommend daily protection, because UVA passes through cloud and glass. On dull winter days in some places, levels are lower, but daily use is a simple habit that avoids guesswork.

### Is a higher SPF always better?

Higher SPF offers more UVB protection, but no sunscreen blocks all UV. Applying enough and reapplying matter as much as the number.

### Can sunscreen fade existing dark spots?

Sunscreen helps prevent spots from getting darker and new ones from forming. For existing spots, ask a pharmacist, doctor or dermatologist about suitable treatments.

## The bottom line

Everyday sun protection is one of the kindest habits for skin after fifty. Choose a broad-spectrum sunscreen, many health organisations suggest at least SPF 30, that you enjoy using, apply it generously every morning and reapply when outdoors for long periods. Do not rely on makeup alone, and remember the ears, neck, chest, hands and scalp. Add hats, covering clothing, shade and good sunglasses, take extra care with reflective surfaces and sunny travel, and check your skin every month, seeing a doctor promptly about any new or changing mark.
