Reaching to a high shelf, looking over your shoulder while reversing the car, tying shoelaces, getting in and out of the bath, fastening a necklace behind your neck: everyday life depends on flexibility. As the years go by, many women notice that these movements feel stiffer, especially first thing in the morning or after sitting for a long time. Muscles feel tighter, joints less supple, and movement less fluid.

Regular stretching and mobility work can help keep the body moving comfortably. It does not require special equipment or great flexibility to begin with, and it can be wonderfully relaxing. This guide explains why flexibility matters, how to stretch safely, and offers a simple routine you can do at home.

This article is general information, not medical advice. If you have joint replacements, osteoporosis, back problems, recent injuries, or any condition affecting movement, ask a physiotherapist or doctor which stretches are suitable for you.

## Why flexibility changes with age

Several changes contribute to stiffness:

- **Connective tissues** — tendons, ligaments and the tissue around muscles — can become less elastic with age.
- **Joints** may be affected by wear and conditions such as osteoarthritis.
- **Inactivity** leads to stiffness; muscles and joints adapt to the positions we spend most time in, such as sitting.
- **Posture habits** — hunching over phones, computers or the steering wheel — can tighten the chest and hip flexors.

Flexibility is "use it or lose it". Regular, gentle movement helps maintain the range of motion we need for daily life.

## The benefits of regular stretching

Commonly reported benefits of regular stretching and mobility exercise include:

- **Easier everyday movements**, such as reaching, bending and turning.
- **Less stiffness**, especially in the morning and after sitting.
- **Better posture**, by counteracting the effects of sitting and hunching.
- **Relaxation and stress relief**, through slow movement and deep breathing.
- **Support for other activities**, such as walking, gardening, dancing and strength training.

Health guidelines for older adults emphasise a mix of aerobic activity, strength and balance exercise. Flexibility work complements these and is often included in activities such as yoga, Pilates and tai chi.

## Types of stretching

- **Static stretching**: holding a stretch in a comfortable position for a period, typically 15 to 30 seconds or longer. Best done when muscles are warm, such as after a walk.
- **Dynamic stretching or mobility**: moving gently through a range of motion, such as arm circles, leg swings or shoulder rolls. Useful as a warm-up.

## How to stretch safely

- **Warm up first.** Stretch after a few minutes of gentle movement, such as marching on the spot or a short walk, or after a warm bath or shower.
- **Move slowly and gently.** Ease into each stretch.
- **Stretch to the point of mild tension**, not pain. You should feel a gentle pull, never sharp or stabbing pain.
- **Do not bounce.** Bouncing can strain muscles.
- **Breathe slowly and deeply.** Exhaling as you ease into a stretch helps the muscles relax.
- **Hold static stretches** for around 15 to 30 seconds, and repeat two or three times if comfortable.
- **Stretch both sides** equally.
- **Use support.** Hold onto a chair or wall for standing stretches, and use cushions for comfort on the floor.
- **Be consistent.** A little stretching most days is more effective than an occasional long session.

### Special cautions

- **Osteoporosis**: avoid stretches that involve forceful forward bending or twisting of the spine, and ask a physiotherapist for safe alternatives.
- **Hip or knee replacements**: follow your surgeon's or physiotherapist's guidance about positions to avoid.
- **Back pain**: get advice about which stretches are appropriate.
- **High blood pressure or glaucoma**: avoid positions where your head is below your heart unless advised otherwise.

## A gentle full-body routine

This routine takes about ten to fifteen minutes. Most stretches can be done standing or seated on a chair, which is helpful if getting down to the floor is difficult.

### Neck

1. **Neck tilt**: sit or stand tall. Gently tilt your head, bringing one ear towards the shoulder, until you feel a stretch on the opposite side of the neck. Hold, then switch sides.
2. **Neck turn**: slowly turn your head to look over one shoulder, hold, then turn to the other side.

Avoid rolling the head in full circles if it causes discomfort or dizziness.

### Shoulders and upper back

3. **Shoulder rolls**: roll your shoulders slowly forwards several times, then backwards.
4. **Cross-body arm stretch**: bring one arm across your chest and gently hold it with the other arm, just above the elbow. Hold, then switch.
5. **Upper back stretch**: clasp your hands in front of you, round your upper back gently and push your hands forward, feeling a stretch between the shoulder blades.

### Chest

6. **Doorway or wall chest stretch**: stand beside a wall or in a doorway, place your forearm against it with the elbow at shoulder height, and gently turn your body away until you feel a stretch across the chest. Hold, then switch sides.

This helps counteract hunching.

### Arms and wrists

7. **Triceps stretch**: raise one arm, bend the elbow so your hand reaches down behind your head, and gently support the elbow with the other hand. Skip this if you have shoulder problems.
8. **Wrist stretch**: extend one arm in front, palm up, and gently pull the fingers back with the other hand. Then turn the palm down and gently press the back of the hand.

### Sides

9. **Side stretch**: stand or sit tall, raise one arm overhead and lean gently to the opposite side. Hold, then switch.

### Lower back

10. **Seated forward lean**: sit on a chair with feet flat, and lean forward gently from the hips, letting your hands slide towards your shins. Return slowly. (Avoid if you have osteoporosis, unless advised.)
11. **Knee to chest (lying)**: lying on your back on a bed or mat, bring one knee gently towards your chest, holding behind the thigh. Hold, then switch.

### Hips

12. **Hip flexor stretch**: stand holding a chair, step one foot back, keeping the back leg straight and heel down or lifted, and gently tuck your pelvis under until you feel a stretch at the front of the hip of the back leg. Hold, then switch.
13. **Seated figure-four**: sit on a chair, cross one ankle over the opposite knee, and gently lean forward with a straight back until you feel a stretch in the buttock. Skip if you have had a hip replacement, unless approved.

### Legs

14. **Hamstring stretch**: sit on the edge of a chair with one leg extended in front, heel on the floor and toes up. Sit tall and lean forward slightly from the hips until you feel a stretch at the back of the thigh. Hold, then switch.
15. **Quadriceps stretch**: holding a chair for support, bend one knee and hold your ankle or trouser leg behind you, bringing your heel towards your buttock. Keep knees together. If you cannot reach, loop a towel around your ankle. Hold, then switch.
16. **Calf stretch**: stand facing a wall, hands on the wall, step one foot back and press the heel down, keeping the back leg straight. Hold, then bend the back knee slightly to stretch the lower calf. Switch sides.

### Ankles and feet

17. **Ankle circles**: seated, lift one foot and circle the ankle slowly in each direction.
18. **Toe spreads**: spread your toes wide, then relax.

## Morning and evening mini-routines

### Morning wake-up (five minutes)

- Shoulder rolls.
- Neck turns.
- Side stretches.
- Ankle circles.
- Gentle marching on the spot.

### Evening wind-down (five to ten minutes)

- Chest stretch.
- Hamstring stretch.
- Calf stretch.
- Knee to chest.
- Slow, deep breathing.

## Desk and sofa breaks

Long periods of sitting cause stiffness. Every half hour or so:

- **Stand up and walk around.**
- **Roll your shoulders and stretch your arms overhead.**
- **Turn your head gently.**
- **Do a few heel and toe raises.**

## Stretching in bed and in the bath

On stiff mornings, a few gentle movements before you even get up can make the first steps of the day easier. Lying on your back, slowly circle your ankles, bend and straighten your knees one at a time, gently hug one knee towards your chest, and stretch your arms overhead with a long, slow breath. Then roll onto your side and push up to sitting rather than sitting straight up.

A warm bath or shower is another good moment for gentle mobility, because warmth makes muscles more pliable. Roll your shoulders, gently turn your neck and stretch your fingers and wrists while the water is warm — taking care, of course, to stay steady on wet surfaces and use a grab rail if you have one.

## Tracking your progress

Flexibility improves slowly, so it can be encouraging to note small changes: reaching a little further down your shins, turning your head more easily when reversing the car, or fastening a bra or necklace with less effort. Write a few notes every month, or take a photograph of a stretch from the side, and you are likely to be surprised by how much has changed after a few months of regular practice.

## Combining stretching with other exercise

Stretching works best as part of a balanced routine:

- **Walking or other aerobic activity** for heart health (see our walking guide).
- **Strength training** two or more days a week (see our strength training guide).
- **Balance exercises** several days a week (see our balance guide).
- **Flexibility work** most days, often after other activities.

Classes such as yoga, Pilates and tai chi combine flexibility, strength and balance.

## The bottom line

Flexibility helps keep everyday movements easy and comfortable. A few minutes of gentle stretching most days — after warming up, moving slowly, breathing deeply and never forcing — can ease stiffness, support posture and help you feel more relaxed. Adapt stretches to your body, seek advice if you have health conditions, and enjoy the quiet pleasure of moving well.
