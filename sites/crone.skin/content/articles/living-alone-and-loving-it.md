More women over fifty live alone than ever before. Some arrive there by choice — after children leave, after a divorce, or simply because they have discovered they prefer their own company. Others arrive through bereavement, separation or circumstance, and find themselves facing an empty house they never expected.

However you came to live alone, it can become one of the most liberating chapters of your life. There is the freedom to eat when you like, decorate as you please, sleep diagonally across the bed, keep the radio on all day or enjoy perfect silence. There is space to rediscover who you are when you are not arranging your life around anyone else. And there is the quiet pleasure of a home that reflects exactly you.

Living alone well, however, does take a little thought. It means building routines that keep you well, a social life that keeps you connected, and a few safety habits that give you and your family peace of mind. This guide brings together practical ideas from women who have made solo living work beautifully.

## Alone is not the same as lonely

It is worth saying clearly at the start: living alone and feeling lonely are different things. Many people living with partners or families feel deeply lonely, while many people living alone have rich, connected lives. Solitude — time alone that you have chosen and enjoy — is restorative. Loneliness is the painful feeling that you lack the connection you need.

The aim is not to avoid being alone, but to make sure your time alone is balanced with enough meaningful contact with others. That balance is different for everyone. Some women are happiest with a quiet week and one good lunch with a friend; others need company most days. Getting to know your own needs is the first step.

## If living alone is new to you

If you have recently begun living alone after many years of sharing a home, the adjustment can be significant — especially if it follows a loss. Be patient with yourself. The silence may feel heavy at first. Meals for one may seem pointless. Evenings and weekends can feel long.

Many women find that the first months are the hardest and that things gradually improve as new routines take shape. Allow yourself to grieve what has changed, while also noticing small moments of freedom or pleasure. If you are grieving a partner, bereavement support services and groups can be very helpful, and talking to others who have been through the same experience often brings comfort that friends and family, however loving, cannot.

## Make your home truly yours

One of the joys of living alone is that your home can reflect your tastes alone. Consider:

- **Rearranging rooms** to suit the way you actually live. Perhaps the dining room would make a better studio, library or sewing room. Perhaps the most comfortable chair belongs by the sunniest window.
- **Reclaiming spaces** that were previously shared or dominated by someone else's belongings — sensitively, at your own pace.
- **Surrounding yourself with things you love**: art, books, plants, photographs, colours that make you happy.
- **Creating comfort**: a good reading lamp, a soft throw, a well-made bed, flowers on the table. These are not indulgences; they make daily life pleasant.
- **Thinking ahead**: if you plan to stay for many years, consider whether the home will continue to suit you, and what small changes — better lighting, grab rails, a more accessible kitchen — might help in future.

## Build routines that keep you well

Routines provide structure and help you take care of yourself when no one else is prompting you.

### Mornings

A regular wake-up time, a pleasant breakfast, getting dressed properly even when you are not going out, and opening the curtains to daylight all help to set a positive tone. Many women who live alone find that a short walk first thing — to collect a newspaper, buy bread or simply round the block — makes a big difference to mood and energy, and creates small daily contacts with neighbours and shopkeepers.

### Meals

Cooking for one can feel like a chore, but eating well matters. Ideas that help:

- **Cook once, eat twice or three times.** Make a pot of soup, stew or curry and freeze portions.
- **Keep a well-stocked cupboard** of quick, nourishing basics: eggs, tinned fish, beans and lentils, wholegrain bread, oats, frozen vegetables, cheese, yoghurt and fruit.
- **Set the table**, even for one. Use a nice plate, a napkin, perhaps a candle. Eating mindfully is more satisfying than eating standing up.
- **Invite people to eat with you.** A simple supper for one or two friends is a lovely way to share the cooking and the company.
- **Try a new recipe each week** to keep cooking interesting.

### Evenings

Evenings can be the loneliest time. Plan some of them: a class, a choir, a film with a friend, a phone call with family. Enjoy others at home with deliberate pleasures — a good book, a favourite programme, a hobby, a bath, music. A regular wind-down routine also helps you sleep well.

### Weekends

Weekends can stretch out when friends are busy with family. Plan at least one thing to look forward to: a walk with a group, a market, a gallery, lunch out, a trip to the cinema, gardening, a visit. Many women keep a list of "things to do on a quiet Sunday" for inspiration.

## Stay connected

Social connection is essential to health and happiness. When you live alone, connection needs a little more effort and planning.

### Keep in regular touch

- **Schedule regular calls** with friends and family — a weekly phone call or video chat creates a reliable rhythm.
- **Reply to invitations with a yes** more often than feels natural; you can always leave early.
- **Take the initiative.** Many people are waiting for someone else to suggest meeting. Be the one who does.

### Build weekly commitments

Regular activities with the same people are the most reliable way to build friendship. Consider a walking group, choir, exercise class, book club, volunteering role, faith community, allotment, art class or language course. Seeing the same faces each week makes it easy for acquaintances to become friends.

### Value small contacts

Brief, friendly exchanges — with neighbours, shopkeepers, the librarian, other dog walkers — do more for wellbeing than we often realise. Shop locally, chat to people, learn names. These small connections create a sense of belonging.

### Use technology to your advantage

Video calls, messaging groups and social media can keep you close to people who live far away. Online communities based around interests — gardening, books, crafts, travel — can be friendly and supportive. If you are not confident with technology, many libraries and community centres offer help, and grandchildren are often delighted to teach.

### Consider a companion animal

For many women, a dog or cat brings daily companionship, routine and affection. Dogs also provide a great reason to walk and to chat with other dog owners. Think carefully about the costs, time and long-term commitment before taking on a pet; fostering or helping to walk a neighbour's dog can be good alternatives.

## When loneliness comes

Even with the best plans, loneliness can creep in, especially at holidays, anniversaries, during illness or in winter. It is a normal human feeling, not a personal failure.

If you are feeling lonely:

- **Reach out to one person** — a call, a message, a visit. It often helps more than you expect.
- **Get out of the house**, even briefly. A walk, a coffee in a café, a trip to the library.
- **Do something for someone else.** Helping others is one of the most effective ways to feel connected.
- **Plan ahead for difficult days.** If Christmas, a birthday or an anniversary is likely to be hard, arrange something in advance.
- **Look for local groups and services** for people wanting more company, including befriending schemes run by charities.

If loneliness is persistent, or if you notice signs of low mood such as losing interest in things, sleeping badly or withdrawing from people, speak to your doctor. Support is available, and you deserve it.

## Safety and peace of mind

Living alone safely is mostly a matter of good habits and a few simple precautions.

### At home

- **Fit and test smoke and carbon monoxide alarms** regularly.
- **Have good locks** on doors and windows, and use them.
- **Use a door chain or viewer**, and never feel obliged to let in callers you do not know. Genuine officials will understand if you check their identity by phoning their organisation using a number you find yourself.
- **Keep a torch** by the bed and in the kitchen.
- **Keep emergency numbers** easy to find, along with a list of your medications and key contacts.
- **Reduce trip hazards**: secure rugs, good lighting on stairs, no trailing cables.

### A check-in system

Arrange a simple daily or regular check-in with a friend, neighbour or family member — a morning message, a call, or a sign such as opening certain curtains. Some women set up mutual check-ins with friends who also live alone, which is reassuring for everyone.

### Personal alarms and technology

Personal alarms, worn as a pendant or on the wrist, can summon help at the press of a button. Some detect falls automatically. Smart speakers can make calls by voice. These options are worth considering, particularly if you have health concerns or are prone to falls.

### Trusted contacts

Give a spare key to a trusted neighbour or friend nearby, and make sure someone knows how to reach your family in an emergency.

### Protect yourself from scams

Unfortunately, fraudsters sometimes target people living alone. Be wary of unexpected phone calls, emails, texts or visitors asking for money, personal details or access to your bank account. Banks and official organisations will never ask you to transfer money to a "safe account" or reveal your PIN. If in doubt, hang up, wait a few minutes, and call the organisation using a number you know to be genuine.

## Look after your health

When you live alone, there is no one to notice if you are not quite yourself, so it is important to keep on top of your health:

- **Keep up with regular check-ups**, eye tests, dental visits and screening invitations.
- **Move every day** — walking, gardening, swimming, dancing, a class. Activity supports both physical and mental health.
- **Sleep well**, with a regular routine.
- **Keep alcohol moderate.** It can be tempting to have a glass or two every evening for company; keep an eye on how much you drink.
- **Have a plan for illness**: who would bring shopping or medicines if you were unwell? Agree this in advance.

## Enjoy your freedom

Finally, remember to enjoy the many pleasures of living alone. You can:

- **Follow your own rhythms** — early nights, late breakfasts, a nap in the afternoon.
- **Take up the hobbies you never had time for.**
- **Travel when and where you like**, on your own or with friends.
- **Eat what you fancy**, from a proper roast to toast and soup.
- **Enjoy silence**, or play your favourite music as loudly as you please.
- **Invite people into your home** on your terms.

Many women say that, once they adjusted, living alone became one of the happiest periods of their lives — a time of independence, self-discovery and contentment. With good routines, strong connections and a few sensible precautions, a solo life can be safe, sociable and wonderfully your own.
