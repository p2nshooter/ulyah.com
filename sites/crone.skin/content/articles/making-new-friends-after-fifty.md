When we are young, friendships seem to happen almost by themselves. School, university, early jobs and the busy years of raising children put us in daily contact with people at a similar stage of life. Later, the structures that created those friendships often fall away. Children grow up and leave, colleagues scatter after retirement, friends move away to be near grandchildren, and some relationships end through divorce or bereavement. Many women find themselves in their fifties, sixties or seventies wishing they had more friends nearby — and unsure how to make them.

If that sounds familiar, you are far from alone. Making new friends later in life is entirely possible, and many women describe friendships formed after fifty as some of the most rewarding of their lives: chosen freely, based on shared interests and values rather than circumstance, and free of the competitiveness that sometimes coloured younger friendships. This guide explores why friendship matters, where to meet people, and how to help new connections grow.

## Why friendship matters so much

Friendship is not a luxury. Strong social connections are widely associated with better wellbeing, and public health organisations increasingly recognise loneliness and social isolation as important health concerns, particularly in later life. Friends offer laughter, practical help, emotional support, new ideas and a sense of belonging. They help us through difficult times and make good times better.

Friendships also tend to change in character with age. Many women value depth over number, preferring a few close, trusted friends to a large social circle. Others enjoy a wide network of lighter connections — the neighbour you chat to over the fence, the regulars at the swimming pool, the members of a book group. Both kinds of connection matter.

## Recognising when you want more connection

Loneliness can creep up slowly. You might notice that days pass without a meaningful conversation, that weekends feel long, or that there is no one to call with good news. It is not a sign of failure; it is a signal, like hunger or thirst, that you need something. Life changes such as retirement, moving home, widowhood or children leaving often trigger these feelings, and they are worth taking seriously.

If loneliness is affecting your mood, sleep or health, talk to your doctor or a trusted person. Many communities have services specifically aimed at reducing loneliness, from befriending schemes to social prescribing, in which health professionals connect people with local activities and groups.

## Where to meet new people

The most reliable way to make friends is to see the same people regularly, doing something you enjoy. Shared activity gives you something to talk about and allows friendship to grow naturally over time.

### Classes and courses

- **Adult education courses** in languages, art, history, writing, photography, cookery and much more.
- **Exercise classes**: yoga, Pilates, aqua aerobics, dance, tai chi or walking football and netball.
- **University programmes** for older learners, and organisations that offer lifelong learning groups run by and for retired people.

See our guide to learning something new later in life for ideas.

### Groups and clubs

- **Book groups**, often organised by libraries and bookshops.
- **Walking and rambling groups** (see our walking guide).
- **Choirs and community singing groups**, which are famously welcoming and joyful.
- **Gardening clubs and allotment societies.**
- **Craft groups**: knitting, quilting, sewing and crafting circles.
- **Women's organisations and clubs**, many with long histories and local branches.
- **Bridge, chess, quiz and games clubs.**
- **Faith communities** and their social groups.
- **Cultural societies**: film clubs, local history groups, art societies, theatre groups.

### Volunteering

Volunteering connects you with people who share your values while doing something worthwhile. Many friendships begin over a shared shift at a charity shop, a food bank or a community garden. See our guide to volunteering that suits you.

### Your neighbourhood

- **Say hello** to neighbours and stop for a chat.
- **Join community events**: street parties, fairs, local festivals and clean-up days.
- **Use local cafés, libraries and markets** regularly; familiarity breeds connection.
- **Join neighbourhood groups**, whether in person or online.

### Online communities

Online groups based on hobbies, interests or local areas can lead to real-life meetings. Some organisations and apps help people find friends with similar interests. Take sensible precautions: meet in public places at first and do not share personal or financial information with people you do not know well.

### Travel

Group holidays, walking tours, cultural trips and retreats designed for solo travellers or older adults can lead to lasting friendships. See our guide to solo travel for women over fifty.

## Turning acquaintances into friends

Meeting people is the first step; friendship grows with time and a little courage.

- **Show up regularly.** Familiarity is the foundation of friendship. Attending the same class or group week after week makes conversations easier.
- **Be curious.** Ask questions and listen to the answers. People enjoy talking to someone genuinely interested in them.
- **Share a little of yourself.** Friendship grows through gradual, mutual openness.
- **Suggest a next step.** After a few pleasant conversations, invite someone for a coffee after class, a walk, or a visit to an exhibition. Many people are waiting for someone else to make the first move.
- **Follow up.** Send a message after meeting, or share an article or event you think they would enjoy.
- **Be patient.** Close friendships often take months, even years, to develop. Not every acquaintance will become a close friend, and that is fine.
- **Be reliable.** Keep arrangements and be kind.

## Overcoming shyness and nerves

Walking into a room full of strangers can feel daunting at any age. Some strategies that help:

- **Arrive a little early**, when the room is quieter and conversation easier.
- **Prepare a few questions**: "How long have you been coming here?", "What brought you to this group?", "Have you read anything good lately?"
- **Volunteer for a role**, such as making the tea or welcoming newcomers; it gives you a reason to talk to people.
- **Go with a friend** the first time, if possible.
- **Give it several visits** before deciding whether a group is right for you.
- **Remember that others feel the same.** Many people in any group are hoping someone will speak to them.

## Nurturing existing friendships

While making new friends, do not forget the ones you already have:

- **Reconnect with old friends** you have lost touch with; a message or letter is often warmly received.
- **Keep in touch with distant friends** through calls, video chats and visits.
- **Schedule regular get-togethers**, such as a monthly lunch or weekly walk.
- **Be there in hard times.** Showing up during illness or loss deepens friendships.

## Friendship across generations

Friendships with younger and older people can be enriching. Intergenerational projects, mentoring, volunteering with young people, shared housing schemes and community events all bring generations together. Younger friends can share new perspectives and skills; you bring experience, wisdom and a sense of perspective.

## When friendships change or end

Friendships naturally evolve. Some fade as lives move in different directions; others end after disagreements or because they no longer feel supportive. It is healthy to let go of relationships that are consistently draining or unkind, and to invest your energy in those that nourish you. Grieving the loss of a friendship is natural.

## Friendship after a big life change

Some of the most important moments to reach out are also the hardest. After the death of a partner, many women find that couples they socialised with drift away, not out of unkindness but because they do not know what to say or how to include someone on her own. After a divorce, friendships may divide. After retirement, the daily companionship of colleagues disappears overnight. After a move, there is no familiar face at the shop or the doctor's surgery.

At these times, it helps to be gentle with yourself and to accept that rebuilding a social life takes time. Bereavement groups, widows' and widowers' social clubs, retirement groups and newcomers' clubs exist precisely because these experiences are common. Saying yes to invitations — even when you do not quite feel like it — and setting yourself small, regular commitments, such as a weekly class, can provide structure while new friendships form. Many women describe the friends they made in these periods as among the most understanding and loyal they have ever had.

## Being a good friend

Friendship is built on small, consistent acts of care. Remembering birthdays and important dates, sending a message when someone is unwell, sharing a book or a recipe, offering a lift, celebrating good news and simply listening without rushing to give advice all deepen connection over time. Friendship also involves receiving: letting others help you, sharing your own worries, and accepting invitations. Balanced give and take is what turns pleasant acquaintances into people you can rely on.

## A simple plan to widen your circle

1. List your interests, past and present.
2. Find two or three local groups or classes related to them.
3. Commit to attending each at least three or four times.
4. Speak to at least one new person each time.
5. Invite someone for coffee or a walk.
6. Contact one old friend you have lost touch with.
7. Notice how you feel, and keep going.

## The bottom line

Making friends after fifty takes a little more intention than it did when we were young, but it is entirely possible — and deeply rewarding. Choose activities you enjoy, show up regularly, be curious and open, take the first step towards friendship, and be patient as connections grow. The friends you make now can bring laughter, support and companionship for many years to come.
