You do not need a large garden to enjoy growing things. A balcony, patio, courtyard, doorstep or even a sunny windowsill can become a lush green retreat filled with flowers, herbs and even a few vegetables. For women who have downsized to a flat, who no longer want the work of a full garden, or who simply love plants, container gardening offers all the pleasure of gardening on a manageable scale.

Containers are also wonderfully flexible: you can move them to catch the sun, change displays with the seasons, raise them to a comfortable height and choose exactly the soil each plant needs. This guide explains how to plan, plant and care for a beautiful container garden.

## Assess your space

Before buying anything, look carefully at your space:

- **Light**: how many hours of direct sun does it receive, and when? South- and west-facing spaces (in the northern hemisphere) are usually sunnier; north-facing spaces are shadier. Plants are labelled for full sun, partial shade or shade.
- **Wind**: balconies, especially higher up, can be very windy, which dries plants and can damage them.
- **Weight**: balconies have weight limits. Soil, water and pots are heavy. If in doubt, check with your landlord, building management or a structural professional, and choose lightweight containers.
- **Rules**: some buildings have rules about what you can place on balconies, hanging items over railings or watering that drips onto neighbours.
- **Water access**: how far will you carry water?
- **Your comfort**: will you want to sit out there? How much space do you need for a chair and table?

## Choosing containers

Almost anything can be a container, as long as it has drainage holes.

- **Terracotta**: attractive and breathable, but heavy, dries out faster and can crack in frost.
- **Glazed ceramic**: decorative and holds moisture well, but heavy.
- **Plastic and resin**: lightweight, inexpensive and retain moisture. Many look like stone or terracotta.
- **Fibreglass and lightweight composite**: light and durable, ideal for balconies.
- **Wood**: natural-looking troughs and planters; line them to extend their life.
- **Metal**: stylish but can heat up in sun.
- **Fabric grow bags**: lightweight and inexpensive, good for vegetables.
- **Self-watering containers**: have a reservoir that reduces watering frequency.

Bigger is generally better: large containers hold more compost and water, so plants dry out less quickly and grow better. Use **pot feet** to lift containers slightly, improving drainage and preventing staining on balcony floors. **Saucers** can catch drips but should not be left full of water for long, as roots can rot.

## Compost

- **Use good-quality multipurpose compost** for most plants, or specific composts for particular plants (for example, ericaceous compost for acid-loving plants such as camellias and blueberries).
- **For long-term plantings** such as shrubs, a loam-based compost or a mix with added loam holds nutrients and moisture longer.
- **Do not use garden soil** in pots; it compacts and drains poorly.
- **Add water-retaining granules** if your pots dry out quickly, following instructions.
- **Refresh compost** each year by replacing the top few centimetres, or repot completely every couple of years.

Peat-free composts are widely available and are better for the environment.

## Choosing plants

### For sunny spaces

- **Herbs**: rosemary, thyme, sage, oregano, lavender, basil.
- **Flowers**: geraniums (pelargoniums), petunias, verbena, osteospermum, salvias, marigolds, nasturtiums.
- **Succulents and drought-tolerant plants**: sedums, sempervivums, agave (with care around sharp tips).
- **Grasses**: small ornamental grasses add movement.
- **Edibles**: tomatoes, peppers, chillies, strawberries, dwarf beans.
- **Shrubs**: dwarf olive trees, lavender, compact hebes, small citrus trees (which need protection in cold winters).

### For partial shade

- **Flowers**: fuchsias, begonias, busy lizzies (impatiens), heucheras.
- **Herbs**: mint (best in its own pot as it spreads), parsley, chives.
- **Edibles**: salad leaves, spinach, chard.
- **Shrubs**: hydrangeas, camellias, Japanese maples (in sheltered spots).

### For shade

- **Ferns**, hostas (which may need slug protection), ivy, heucheras, and shade-tolerant grasses.
- **Spring bulbs** such as snowdrops and some daffodils.

### For windy balconies

Choose sturdy, low-growing plants with small or tough leaves, such as lavender, rosemary, grasses and sedums, and use heavy or anchored containers. Screens of trellis or woven panels can shelter plants (check building rules).

## Herbs and edibles

Growing food in containers is rewarding and practical:

- **Herb pots** by the kitchen door or on a sunny windowsill provide fresh flavours.
- **Salad leaves** grow quickly and can be picked as needed; sow small amounts every few weeks for a steady supply.
- **Tomatoes** grow well in large containers in sunny spots; choose compact or bush varieties for small spaces.
- **Strawberries** suit hanging baskets and window boxes.
- **Chillies and peppers** thrive in warm, sunny conditions.
- **Dwarf fruit trees** on suitable rootstocks can grow in large containers.

Edibles need regular watering and feeding; follow seed packet and plant label instructions.

## Designing your display

- **Use the "thriller, filler, spiller" approach**: one tall or striking plant (thriller), bushy plants around it (filler), and trailing plants that spill over the edge (spiller).
- **Group containers** of different heights for impact; group pots also create a microclimate that keeps them moist.
- **Choose a colour scheme**: harmonious shades for calm, or contrasting colours for drama.
- **Repeat plants or pot styles** for a cohesive look.
- **Use vertical space**: wall planters, trellises with climbing plants, tiered plant stands and railing planters (where permitted and securely fixed).
- **Add lighting**: solar lanterns or string lights for evenings.
- **Include seating**: a small bistro set or comfortable chair to enjoy your garden.

## Watering

Containers dry out much faster than garden soil, especially in sun and wind.

- **Check daily in warm weather** by pushing a finger into the compost; water when the top few centimetres feel dry.
- **Water thoroughly** until water drains from the bottom.
- **Water in the morning or evening.**
- **Use self-watering pots**, watering spikes or a simple drip system for holidays.
- **Group pots together** and move them out of strong afternoon sun in heatwaves if needed.
- **Use a lightweight watering can** and fill it only halfway if carrying is difficult.
- **Collect rainwater** if you have space for a small water butt.

## Feeding

Compost contains nutrients that last a few weeks. After that, feed container plants regularly during the growing season with a liquid or slow-release fertiliser, following instructions. Flowering plants and edibles often benefit from a high-potash feed (such as tomato feed).

## Year-round interest

- **Spring**: bulbs such as tulips, daffodils, crocuses and hyacinths; primulas; pansies.
- **Summer**: geraniums, petunias, fuchsias, herbs, tomatoes.
- **Autumn**: heucheras, ornamental grasses, cyclamen, asters.
- **Winter**: evergreens, winter-flowering heathers, violas, small conifers, skimmias.

Evergreen shrubs and grasses provide a permanent structure, with seasonal plants added for colour.

## Caring for containers through winter

- **Move tender plants indoors** or to a sheltered spot.
- **Raise pots on feet** to prevent waterlogging.
- **Wrap pots** with horticultural fleece or hessian to protect roots in very cold weather.
- **Water sparingly** in winter, but do not let pots dry out completely, especially evergreens.

## Comfortable, safe gardening

- **Raise containers** on tables or plant stands to reduce bending.
- **Use lightweight pots** and compost bags you can manage, or ask for deliveries.
- **Use a trolley** or pot mover for heavy containers.
- **Secure pots and hanging items** so they cannot fall from balconies.
- **Keep walkways clear** to avoid tripping.
- **Wear gloves** and wash hands after handling compost.

## Indoor and windowsill gardening

If you have no outdoor space at all, a bright windowsill can still grow a surprising amount. Herbs such as basil, parsley, chives and mint grow well indoors in good light, and pea shoots, cress and microgreens can be harvested within a couple of weeks of sowing. Houseplants add greenery all year: many, such as pothos, spider plants, peace lilies and snake plants, are known for being forgiving of beginners. Group plants together, turn pots regularly so they grow evenly towards the light, and take care not to overwater, which is the most common cause of problems with indoor plants. If you have pets, check that plants are not toxic to cats or dogs before bringing them home.

## Starting small and building up

It is tempting to buy a car boot full of plants on the first sunny weekend of spring. A gentler approach is to start with three or four large containers — perhaps a herb pot, a seasonal flower display and one evergreen shrub — and see how much watering and care they need in your particular spot. Once you know how your balcony behaves in sun, wind and rain, you can add more pots with confidence. Keep a simple notebook or phone note of what you planted, where, and how it performed; it becomes an invaluable guide for the following year.

## Attracting wildlife

Even a small balcony can support wildlife:

- **Choose pollinator-friendly flowers**, such as lavender, salvias, thyme and single-flowered blooms.
- **Add a small water dish** with stones for insects to land on.
- **Hang a bird feeder**, where permitted and if it will not disturb neighbours.

## The bottom line

A balcony, patio or windowsill can become a beautiful, productive garden with the right containers, compost and plants. Match plants to your light and wind conditions, choose large and lightweight pots, water and feed regularly, and plan for colour through the seasons. Container gardening offers all the joys of growing — fresh herbs, colourful flowers, visiting bees — on a scale that is easy to manage and wonderful to enjoy.
