It often happens gradually. The book that was easy to read in the armchair now needs to be held closer to the window. The steps down to the garden seem darker in the evening. Choosing between navy and black socks in the bedroom has become a guessing game. As we get older, our eyes typically need more light to see clearly, and they adapt more slowly when moving between bright and dim spaces. Lighting that seemed perfectly adequate twenty years ago may now leave us squinting, straining or at risk of a stumble.

Good lighting is one of the simplest and most effective ways to make a home safer, more comfortable and more beautiful. This guide explains why lighting needs change, how to layer light in each room, how to choose bulbs by brightness and colour, where to add task lighting, how to light stairs and night-time routes, and how to make the most of natural daylight. If you notice sudden changes in your vision, or difficulty seeing that better light does not help, see an optometrist or doctor.

## Why we need more light as we age

With age, many people find that:

- they need **more light** to read and see fine detail;
- they are **more sensitive to glare**, such as a bare bulb or bright reflections;
- their eyes **adjust more slowly** when moving from a bright room into a dark hallway;
- **contrast** becomes harder to see, for example the edge of a step of the same colour as the floor.

Good lighting addresses all four: enough light, without glare, with smooth transitions and clear contrast.

## Think in layers

Lighting designers talk about three layers of light. Using all three makes a room both practical and inviting.

1. **Ambient light** provides overall illumination, often from ceiling lights or wall lights.
2. **Task light** is focused where you need to see detail: reading, cooking, sewing, applying makeup.
3. **Accent light** adds warmth and atmosphere: a lamp on a sideboard, a light in a bookcase, candles or fairy lights.

A room lit only by a single ceiling light tends to feel flat and leaves shadows exactly where you need to see. Adding lamps at different heights solves both problems.

## Choosing bulbs: brightness and colour

### Brightness

Bulb brightness is measured in **lumens**. The higher the number, the brighter the light. Modern LED bulbs give much more light for less energy than old bulbs, so look at the lumens rather than the watts. If a room feels dim, try:

- a **brighter bulb** in an existing fitting, checking the maximum the fitting allows;
- **more light sources**, such as an extra lamp;
- **dimmer switches**, which let you choose bright light for tasks and softer light for relaxing.

### Colour temperature

The colour of light is measured in kelvins (K):

- **warm white** light has a soft, yellowish glow and suits living rooms and bedrooms;
- **neutral white** light is clearer and works well in kitchens, bathrooms and reading areas;
- **cool white** light is bluish and very crisp; some people find it harsh at home.

Many people find warm or neutral white most comfortable. Using similar colour temperatures within a room keeps it harmonious.

### Colour rendering

Some bulbs show colours more accurately than others. A high colour rendering index (CRI) on the packaging means colours look more natural, which helps with makeup, clothes and cooking.

## Room by room

### Living room

- combine a **ceiling or wall light** with **two or three lamps** at different heights;
- place a **good reading lamp** beside your favourite chair, positioned slightly behind and to the side so the light falls on the page without glare;
- use **dimmers** or smart bulbs for evening relaxation.

### Kitchen

- add **under-cabinet lighting** to light the worktop directly;
- choose **bright, even ceiling lighting**;
- light the **hob and sink** areas well.

Our guide to [a kitchen that works for you](/journal/a-kitchen-that-works-for-you/) offers more ideas.

### Bathroom

- use **bright, even light** around the mirror, ideally from both sides, so your face is lit without shadows; this makes shaving, makeup and skincare much easier;
- make sure lights and switches are **suitable for bathroom use** under local electrical rules;
- consider a **soft night light** for night-time visits.

### Bedroom

- place a **bedside lamp** within easy reach, with a switch you can find in the dark;
- use **wardrobe lighting** so you can see the colours of your clothes;
- consider a **low-level night light** on the route to the bathroom.

### Hallways and stairs

These are among the most important places to light well, because they are where many falls happen.

- **Light the whole staircase evenly**, with no dark steps.
- **Have switches at both the top and the bottom** of the stairs.
- **Consider motion-sensor lights** that switch on automatically.
- **Mark step edges** with a contrasting strip if they are hard to see.
- **Keep a light on** or use a night light in hallways at night.

### Outdoors

- light **paths, steps and entrances** so you can see your way and find your keys;
- **motion-sensor lights** add safety and security;
- **solar lights** along paths can help, though they may be dim in winter.

## Lighting for hobbies

Many of the pleasures of later life involve close work: reading, knitting, sewing, painting, jigsaws, writing letters, crafts. Good light makes them more enjoyable and less tiring.

- **An adjustable task lamp** with a flexible arm lets you place the light exactly where you need it, close to your work and below eye level so it does not shine into your eyes.
- **Daylight-style bulbs** with good colour rendering help when matching yarn, fabric or paint colours.
- **A magnifying lamp**, which combines a light and a lens, can be a great help for very fine work such as embroidery or reading small print.
- **Light from the side opposite your writing or working hand** avoids casting a shadow over your work.

Arrange a favourite hobby corner near a window if possible, with a good lamp for evenings and dull days. Our guide to [learning something new later in life](/journal/learning-something-new-later-in-life/) has ideas for hobbies worth lighting well.

## Light and colour on the walls

Light and decoration work together. Pale, matt wall colours reflect more light and make rooms feel brighter without glare, while dark, glossy finishes absorb light and can create reflections. If a room feels gloomy even with good lamps, a lighter shade of paint may help as much as a new bulb. Contrast also matters for safety: a light switch, door handle or step edge in a colour that stands out from its background is much easier to find, especially in dim conditions.

## Reducing glare

- **Use shades and diffusers** so you do not look directly at bare bulbs.
- **Avoid very shiny surfaces** that reflect light into your eyes.
- **Position lamps** so they light the task, not your eyes.
- **Use blinds or sheer curtains** to soften strong sunlight.

## Making the most of daylight

Natural light is free, beautiful and good for mood.

- **Keep windows clean** and trim plants that block light.
- **Use light, sheer curtains** during the day.
- **Place mirrors** opposite or beside windows to reflect light into the room.
- **Choose lighter wall colours** in darker rooms.
- **Arrange seating** for reading and hobbies near windows.

Daylight also helps many people feel more energetic. Spending time near a bright window, or outdoors, is a simple pleasure worth building into the day. Our guide to [better sleep habits](/journal/better-sleep-habits/) explains how morning light and dim evenings support sleep.

## Smart and easy controls

Modern lighting technology can make life easier:

- **smart bulbs** that you control from your phone or by voice;
- **timers** that switch lights on before you come home;
- **touch lamps** that do not need a fiddly switch;
- **plug-in night lights** with light sensors.

Choose what feels simple to use; technology should reduce effort, not add to it.

## Safety and energy

- **Never exceed** the maximum bulb wattage stated on a light fitting.
- **Have new wiring or fixed lights installed** by a qualified electrician.
- **Replace flickering or damaged fittings.**
- **LED bulbs** use less electricity and last longer, which means fewer bulb changes on stepladders.

## Frequently asked questions

### How bright should my reading lamp be?

Brighter than you might think. Try a lamp with a bright LED bulb and adjust its position until the page is evenly lit without glare.

### Is cool white light better for reading?

Some people prefer neutral white for reading because it feels clearer. Try both and choose what is most comfortable for your eyes.

### What is the single most important lighting change?

For many homes, lighting stairs and hallways well, with switches at both ends and night lights, makes the biggest difference to safety.

## The bottom line

As our eyes change, we need more light, less glare, gentle transitions and clearer contrast. Layer ambient, task and accent lighting in every room, choose bulbs by lumens and a comfortable colour temperature, and add good task lights for reading, cooking and grooming. Light stairs and hallways evenly, with switches at both ends and night lights, and light outdoor paths and entrances. Reduce glare with shades and diffusers, make the most of daylight and use simple controls that make life easier. And if better lighting does not help you see clearly, have your eyes checked.
