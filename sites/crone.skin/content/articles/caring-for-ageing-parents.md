Many women in their fifties and sixties find themselves in the middle of several generations at once: adult children who still need support, perhaps grandchildren, and parents who are growing older and gradually needing more help. Caring for an ageing parent can be an expression of love and gratitude, and it can bring moments of real closeness. It can also be exhausting, worrying and complicated, especially when it has to be balanced with work, family and your own health.

This guide offers practical support for women in this situation: how to begin difficult conversations, how to understand what help your parent needs, how to organise important information and paperwork, where to find practical and professional support, how to share the load with siblings and relatives, and, just as importantly, how to protect your own wellbeing. Rules, services and support differ widely between countries, so check official sources and local organisations where you and your parent live. For medical questions, legal matters and financial decisions, seek advice from qualified professionals.

## Recognising when help is needed

Changes often happen gradually, and it can be hard to know when to step in. Signs that a parent may need more support include:

- difficulty with **everyday tasks**, such as shopping, cooking, cleaning or managing medication;
- **unopened post**, unpaid bills or confusion about money;
- **falls**, unexplained bruises or reluctance to move around;
- **weight loss** or an empty fridge;
- **forgetfulness** that affects safety, such as leaving the cooker on;
- **withdrawal** from friends and activities;
- changes in **personal care** or the state of the home.

Noticing changes does not mean taking over. The aim is to support your parent to live as independently and safely as possible, in the way that matters to them.

## Starting the conversation

Talking about getting older, needing help or planning for the future can feel awkward for both parent and child. Some approaches that help:

- **Choose a calm moment**, not during a crisis.
- **Start with their wishes**: "What matters most to you about staying at home?" or "How would you like things to be if you ever needed more help?"
- **Listen more than you speak.**
- **Respect their right to make their own decisions**, even ones you would not make, as long as they have the capacity to do so.
- **Take it in stages**; you do not need to settle everything in one conversation.
- **Involve other family members** as appropriate, so nobody feels left out or ganged up on.

If your parent finds it hard to talk with you, a trusted friend, doctor or advisor may be able to help start the conversation.

## Understanding the needs

A clear picture of what help is needed makes planning easier. Consider:

- **health**: medical conditions, medications, appointments;
- **daily living**: washing, dressing, meals, cleaning, shopping;
- **mobility and safety**: stairs, bathroom, falls risk, getting out of the house;
- **money and paperwork**: bills, banking, pensions, benefits;
- **social life**: friends, activities, loneliness;
- **emotional wellbeing**: mood, worries, bereavement.

In many countries, health or social services can carry out a **needs assessment**, which can open the door to support services, equipment or adaptations. Ask your parent's doctor or the local authority how to request one.

## Organising information

In an emergency, finding key information quickly is invaluable. With your parent's agreement, gather:

- **contact details**: doctors, pharmacy, neighbours, friends, other relatives;
- **a list of medications** and health conditions;
- **important documents**: identification, insurance, pension and bank information, property documents;
- **passwords and account details**, stored securely;
- **their wishes**, including any written plans for future care.

Keep a copy in a safe place, and make sure trusted people know where it is.

### Legal arrangements

Many countries have legal arrangements that allow a person to choose someone to make decisions on their behalf if they become unable to do so, for example about money, property, health or care. Making these arrangements while your parent is able to decide for themselves can prevent great difficulty later. Ask a solicitor, notary or official advice service about what applies where your parent lives.

## Practical help at home

Small changes can make a big difference to independence and safety:

- **grab rails** in the bathroom and by steps;
- **better lighting**, especially on stairs and at night; our guide to [lighting your home well](/journal/lighting-your-home-well/) explains how;
- **removing trip hazards**, such as loose rugs and trailing cables;
- **a pill organiser** or reminder system for medication, agreed with the pharmacist;
- **a personal alarm** that can call for help after a fall;
- **meal deliveries** or batch-cooked meals in the freezer;
- **regular check-in calls** from you or a neighbour.

Our guide to [a home that ages with you](/journal/a-home-that-ages-with-you/) has many more ideas that work for parents' homes too.

## Finding support

You do not have to do everything yourself. Depending on where your parent lives, support may include:

- **home care services**, for help with personal care or household tasks;
- **day centres** offering company, activities and meals;
- **community and volunteer services**, such as befriending, transport to appointments or shopping help;
- **occupational therapists**, who advise on equipment and adaptations;
- **charities and organisations** for older people or specific conditions, which often provide information, advice and helplines;
- **respite care**, giving family carers a break;
- **financial support**, such as benefits or allowances for older people or carers.

Contact your parent's doctor, the local authority, or national organisations for older people to find out what is available.

## Sharing the load

Caring often falls disproportionately on one person, frequently a daughter. Sharing the load more fairly protects relationships and health.

- **Hold a family meeting**, in person or online, to discuss needs and responsibilities.
- **Divide tasks by strengths and circumstances**: a sibling who lives far away might manage paperwork or online shopping, while someone nearby helps with visits.
- **Be specific**: "Could you take Mum to her appointments on Tuesdays?" works better than "I need more help."
- **Review regularly**, as needs change.
- **Talk about money openly**, including who pays for what, and keep records.

Our guide to [staying close to family far away](/journal/staying-close-to-family-far-away/) offers ideas for involving relatives who live at a distance.

## Looking after yourself

Caring for someone you love can be emotionally and physically draining. Many carers put their own needs last until they are exhausted. Protecting your own health is not selfish; it is what allows you to keep caring.

- **Keep up your own health checks** and appointments.
- **Make time for rest and activities you enjoy**, even if only briefly each week. Our guides to [walking for everyday fitness](/journal/walking-for-everyday-fitness/) and [better sleep habits](/journal/better-sleep-habits/) may help.
- **Stay connected** with friends.
- **Accept help** when it is offered, and ask for it when it is not.
- **Learn about your rights as a carer**, which in some countries include assessments, flexible working or financial support.
- **Join a carers' group**, in person or online, where people understand what you are going through.
- **Set boundaries**: decide what you can and cannot do, and communicate it kindly.

Watch for signs of carer burnout, such as constant exhaustion, irritability, low mood, poor sleep or feeling that you cannot cope. If you notice them, speak to your doctor or a carers' support organisation.

## Working and caring

If you work, caring can be especially difficult to balance:

- **talk to your employer** about flexible hours, leave or other arrangements; some countries have legal rights for carers;
- **use technology**, such as video calls and online banking, to help from a distance;
- **plan for emergencies**, such as who can step in if you cannot.

## When needs become too great

There may come a time when living at home is no longer safe or practical, even with support. Exploring options early, such as sheltered or assisted housing, moving closer to family or residential care, gives your parent more choice and reduces crisis decisions. Involve your parent as fully as possible in every decision, and seek professional advice about costs and options.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I persuade my parent to accept help?

Focus on what they want to keep doing, introduce help gradually, and present it as a way to stay independent. Sometimes advice from a doctor or another trusted person is accepted more easily.

### What if my siblings do not help?

Be specific about what is needed, consider tasks they could do from a distance, and if necessary seek support from mediators or advice services. Look after your own limits, too.

### Where can I find information about services?

Your parent's doctor, the local authority and national organisations for older people and carers are good starting points.

You will find more ideas for meaning and connection in the [Purpose & Connection](/chapters/purpose-and-connection/) chapter.

## The bottom line

Caring for an ageing parent is an act of love that works best with planning, support and boundaries. Notice changes early, talk calmly about your parent's wishes, and build a clear picture of the help they need. Organise key information, and ask about legal arrangements for future decisions while your parent can still make them. Use practical home changes and local services, share responsibilities fairly with siblings and relatives, and look after your own health and wellbeing. Seek professional advice for medical, legal and financial matters, and reach out to carers' organisations when you need support.
