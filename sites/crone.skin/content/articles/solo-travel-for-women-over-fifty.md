There comes a moment for many women when they realise that if they wait for someone to travel with, they may never go at all. A friend cannot get the time off, a partner has no interest in art galleries, the children are busy, or there is simply no one left to ask. And so, a little nervously, they book a trip on their own — and discover one of the great freedoms of later life.

Solo travel after fifty is increasingly popular, and for good reason. You choose where to go, what to see, when to eat and when to rest. You can linger for an hour in front of one painting or skip the museum altogether for a long lunch in the sun. You meet more people, because you are more open to conversation. And you come home with a quiet confidence that you can handle the world on your own terms.

This guide covers everything you need to plan your first — or your next — solo journey with confidence.

## Why travel alone?

Women who travel solo describe many rewards:

- **Complete freedom.** Your itinerary, your pace, your budget, your choices.
- **Deeper experiences.** Without a companion to talk to, you notice more — the details of a street, the conversations around you, the taste of local food.
- **More connections.** Solo travellers are approached more often by locals and other travellers, and are more likely to start conversations themselves.
- **Confidence.** Navigating a new city, solving small problems and enjoying your own company builds real self-assurance.
- **Time to reflect.** Travel alone gives space to think, write, sketch and simply be.

That does not mean every moment is easy. There may be times when you feel lonely, anxious or self-conscious. Those moments usually pass, and planning helps to reduce them.

## Choosing your first destination

For a first solo trip, choose somewhere that will help you build confidence. Good choices often have:

- **A language you speak**, or where English is widely understood.
- **Good public transport**, so you can get around easily without driving.
- **A compact, walkable centre** with plenty to see.
- **A reputation for being safe and welcoming** to visitors.
- **A culture of eating out casually**, with cafés and markets where dining alone feels natural.

Cities with excellent museums, cafés and walkable old towns make wonderful first trips. So do coastal towns with promenades, or rural areas with well-marked walking paths and friendly guesthouses. Some women prefer to start closer to home, with a weekend in a city within their own country, before venturing further.

## Consider a group or structured trip

If the idea of travelling completely independently feels too daunting, there are many options in between:

- **Small-group tours** designed for solo travellers, often with no or reduced single supplements. Many specialise in travellers over fifty.
- **Special-interest holidays** — painting, cookery, walking, photography, language or yoga — where you travel independently but join a group with a shared interest.
- **River and small-ship cruises**, where meals and activities are organised and solo travellers are often seated together.
- **Retreats and courses**, which provide structure and company.

These can be a lovely way to build confidence and meet like-minded people, and many women go on to travel fully independently afterwards.

## Planning and booking

### Accommodation

Where you stay shapes your experience. For solo travellers, consider:

- **Location first.** Choose somewhere central, close to public transport, in a lively area where you will feel comfortable walking back in the evening.
- **Reviews from other solo women.** Look for comments about safety, friendliness and the neighbourhood.
- **Small hotels and guesthouses** where staff get to know you and can offer advice.
- **Single rooms**: ask whether single rooms are available, or look for operators that waive the single supplement.
- **Apartments** give space and a kitchen, but less contact with people; a hotel or guesthouse may suit a first trip better.

### Arrival

Plan your arrival carefully, as this is when travellers often feel most vulnerable:

- **Arrive in daylight** if possible.
- **Know exactly how you will get from the airport or station** to your accommodation — by pre-booked transfer, official taxi rank or public transport.
- **Save the address** of your accommodation in your phone and on paper, in the local language if necessary.

### Pacing

Do not over-schedule. Allow time to rest, to wander and to say yes to unexpected invitations. Many solo travellers find that one main activity per day, with plenty of time for cafés and strolling, is ideal.

## Staying safe

Most solo trips pass without any problems at all, but sensible precautions give you peace of mind.

### Before you go

- **Read official travel advice** for your destination.
- **Buy comprehensive travel insurance**, checking that it covers any pre-existing medical conditions you have.
- **Leave your itinerary** with a friend or family member, with copies of your passport and insurance details.
- **Register for your country's travel alert services**, if available.
- **Carry copies of important documents**, stored separately from the originals and digitally.

### Money and valuables

- **Use a cross-body bag** that zips and sits in front of you, rather than a shoulder bag or backpack in crowds.
- **Carry two cards** and some cash, stored in different places.
- **Use cash machines inside banks** or shops during the day.
- **Leave valuables** in the hotel safe, and avoid wearing expensive jewellery.

### Out and about

- **Walk with purpose**, even when you are not sure where you are going. Step into a shop or café to check your map.
- **Trust your instincts.** If a situation or person makes you uneasy, leave.
- **Be careful with alcohol**, and never leave drinks unattended.
- **Use licensed taxis** or reputable ride apps, and check the car details before getting in.
- **Keep your phone charged**, and carry a small power bank.
- **Learn a few phrases** in the local language, including how to ask for help.

### Keep in touch

Agree a simple check-in routine with someone at home — a message each evening, for example. Location-sharing on your phone can also give family peace of mind.

## Eating alone with confidence

For many women, dining alone is the most daunting part of solo travel. It need not be.

- **Start with lunch.** Lunch alone is very common and relaxed, and often better value.
- **Choose places with counter or bar seating**, where solo diners are the norm and it is easy to chat with staff.
- **Try markets, food halls and cafés**, where eating alone feels completely natural.
- **Bring a book, journal or postcard to write**, if it helps you feel at ease — or simply watch the world go by.
- **Ask for a good table.** You are a paying customer, and deserve a seat by the window, not the one by the kitchen door.
- **Join a food tour or cookery class**, a wonderful way to eat well and meet people.

Many seasoned solo travellers say that, once they got used to it, they came to love eating alone: savouring the food, watching local life and enjoying the freedom to order exactly what they wanted.

## Meeting people

You will be alone only as much as you want to be. Ways to meet people include:

- **Walking tours**, including free tours in many cities.
- **Day trips and excursions** with small groups.
- **Classes**: cookery, painting, dance, language.
- **Staying in guesthouses** with communal breakfasts.
- **Chatting with other travellers** in cafés, on trains and at sights.
- **Online groups and forums** for solo women travellers, where you can share tips and sometimes arrange to meet.

Equally, enjoy your solitude. Time alone in a beautiful place is one of the great luxuries of solo travel.

## Looking after yourself

Travel can be tiring, especially as we get older. Look after your energy and wellbeing:

- **Choose comfortable, supportive shoes.** You will walk more than you expect.
- **Take regular breaks**, and do not feel guilty about a rest in your room in the afternoon.
- **Stay hydrated**, especially in hot weather or on long journeys.
- **Pack medications** in your hand luggage, in original packaging, with copies of prescriptions.
- **Know where to find help**: the nearest pharmacy, the emergency number, your insurer's helpline.

## Handling the wobbles

Most solo travellers experience moments of loneliness, anxiety or homesickness, particularly on the first or second evening. These feelings are normal and usually short-lived. Things that help:

- **Have a plan for evenings**: a concert, a guided walk, a restaurant you are looking forward to.
- **Call or message a friend at home.**
- **Write in a journal**, reflecting on what you have enjoyed.
- **Treat yourself**: a nice meal, a massage, a visit to a beautiful place.
- **Remember why you came**, and remind yourself that you are doing something brave and wonderful.

## Budgeting as a solo traveller

Travelling alone can cost more, mainly because of single supplements and the lack of shared costs. Ways to keep costs down:

- **Look for operators** that waive single supplements or offer solo-friendly pricing.
- **Travel outside peak season**, when prices are lower and places are calmer.
- **Use public transport** and walk.
- **Eat your main meal at lunchtime**, when set menus are often better value.
- **Choose a guesthouse or small hotel** rather than a large international chain.

## Coming home

Many women find that solo travel changes them. They return more confident, more curious and more willing to take on new challenges. Some begin planning their next trip on the flight home.

Your first solo journey does not have to be ambitious. A weekend in a nearby city, a few days by the sea or a short trip to a place you have always wanted to see is enough to begin. With good planning, sensible precautions and an open mind, travelling alone after fifty can be one of the most rewarding adventures of your life — and the start of many more.
