Walking is the most natural exercise there is. It needs no gym membership, no special skills and very little equipment. You can do it almost anywhere, at any pace, alone or with friends, and it fits easily into everyday life — to the shops, to visit a neighbour, around the park or along a coastal path. For many women in their fifties, sixties and beyond, a regular walking habit becomes the foundation of feeling well.

This guide explains why walking is so worthwhile, how much to aim for, how to build up gradually and safely, and how to keep it enjoyable throughout the year. It is general information, not medical advice. If you have a heart condition, high blood pressure, joint problems, diabetes, osteoporosis, have recently been unwell or had surgery, or are unsure whether a new activity is right for you, speak to your doctor or a physiotherapist first.

## Why walking is so valuable

Regular physical activity is widely recognised as one of the most important things we can do for our health at any age. Walking is a particularly accessible form of it. Benefits commonly associated with regular walking include:

- **Heart and circulation.** Brisk walking is a form of moderate aerobic exercise, which supports heart and lung fitness.
- **Weight-bearing activity.** Because you carry your own weight, walking contributes to keeping bones and muscles working, alongside strength training.
- **Mood and stress.** Many people find that walking — especially outdoors and in green spaces — lifts mood, eases stress and helps clear the mind.
- **Sleep.** Regular daytime activity and time outdoors in daylight can help many people sleep better.
- **Joints.** Gentle, regular movement helps many people feel less stiff, although anyone with joint pain should get personalised advice.
- **Social connection.** Walking with friends or groups is a lovely way to stay connected.
- **Independence.** Staying active helps maintain the strength and stamina we need for everyday tasks.

## How much should you aim for?

Health organisations such as the World Health Organization recommend that adults, including older adults, aim for at least 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity a week, or a smaller amount of vigorous activity, plus muscle-strengthening activities on two or more days a week. Older adults are also encouraged to include activities that challenge balance on several days a week.

That might sound like a lot, but 150 minutes is, for example, thirty minutes on five days of the week — and it can be broken into shorter walks of ten minutes or more. Any activity is better than none, and if you are starting from a low level, small increases bring real benefits.

### What counts as moderate intensity?

Moderate-intensity walking is brisk enough that you breathe faster and feel warmer, but you can still hold a conversation. A simple test: you can talk but not sing. If you can sing comfortably, you can probably walk a little faster; if you cannot speak more than a few words, slow down.

### Steps

Many people use step counts as a guide. There is nothing magic about any particular number; the most useful approach is to find your current average and gradually increase it. Phones and simple pedometers can count steps.

## Starting safely

If you have not been active for a while, start gently and build up gradually.

1. **Begin with what feels comfortable** — perhaps ten minutes at an easy pace, on flat ground.
2. **Walk regularly**, ideally most days, so it becomes a habit.
3. **Increase gradually**, adding a few minutes each week, or adding a little pace.
4. **Include rest days** if you feel tired or sore.
5. **Listen to your body.** Mild muscle tiredness is normal at first; pain is not.

Stop and seek medical help if you experience chest pain or tightness, severe breathlessness, dizziness, fainting, palpitations or pain that spreads to the arm, neck or jaw.

## Good walking technique

A few small adjustments make walking more comfortable and efficient:

- **Stand tall.** Imagine a string gently lifting the top of your head. Keep your shoulders relaxed and down.
- **Look ahead**, about a few metres in front of you, rather than at your feet — while staying aware of uneven ground.
- **Engage your core** gently, without holding your breath.
- **Swing your arms naturally**, bending them slightly if you are walking briskly.
- **Land on your heel and roll through to your toes**, pushing off with the toes.
- **Take natural strides.** To walk faster, take quicker steps rather than longer ones.
- **Breathe naturally and rhythmically.**

## Shoes and kit

### Shoes

Comfortable, supportive footwear makes walking far more enjoyable:

- **Choose walking or running trainers** with cushioning, support and a flexible sole.
- **Check the fit**: a thumb's width at the toe, a snug heel and no rubbing.
- **For rough terrain**, walking shoes or boots with grippy soles and ankle support are safer.
- **Replace worn-out shoes**, as cushioning breaks down over time.

Our guide to comfortable shoes that still look good has more advice.

### Clothing

- **Layers** are best: you warm up quickly once you start walking.
- **Breathable fabrics** keep you comfortable.
- **A waterproof jacket** makes rainy days possible.
- **Socks** that do not rub — seamless or cushioned socks reduce blisters.
- **A hat and sunscreen** in sunny weather; **a warm hat and gloves** in cold weather.

### Useful extras

- **A small backpack** for water, snacks and layers.
- **A water bottle**, especially on warm days.
- **A phone** for safety and navigation.
- **Reflective or bright clothing** if walking in low light.
- **Walking poles**, which some people find helpful for stability and on hills; ask for advice on adjusting and using them correctly.

## Making walks more effective

Once you have built a regular habit, you might like to add variety:

- **Brisk intervals**: alternate a few minutes of brisk walking with a few minutes at a comfortable pace.
- **Hills and stairs**: walking uphill increases intensity and works the legs.
- **Longer walks**: a longer walk at the weekend builds stamina.
- **Nordic walking**: walking with specially designed poles engages the upper body. Classes are available in many areas.

Combine walking with strength and balance exercises on other days for a well-rounded routine; see our guides to strength training and balance exercises.

## Staying safe

- **Choose well-lit, safe routes**, especially when walking alone.
- **Tell someone** where you are going and when you expect to return, particularly on longer or remote walks.
- **Check the weather** and be prepared to change plans.
- **Watch for uneven ground**, kerbs and slippery surfaces, especially in wet or icy weather.
- **Stay hydrated**, particularly in hot weather.
- **Walk facing oncoming traffic** on roads without pavements.
- **Take your phone** and any necessary medication.

## Keeping it interesting

The best exercise is the one you keep doing. Ideas to keep walking enjoyable:

- **Explore new routes**: parks, canals, rivers, woods, beaches, historic streets.
- **Walk with others**: friends, family, neighbours or a walking group. Many communities run free, led walks for all abilities.
- **Set a goal**: a charity walk, a long-distance path in stages, or a step challenge.
- **Listen to something**: an audiobook, a podcast or music (keeping the volume low enough to hear traffic).
- **Combine walking with errands**: walk to the shops, the library or a café.
- **Notice nature**: birds, trees, seasonal changes. Some people enjoy taking photographs on their walks.
- **Walk a dog**, your own or a neighbour's.
- **Keep a log** of your walks to see your progress.

## Walking through the seasons

- **Spring**: enjoy blossoms and longer evenings.
- **Summer**: walk early in the morning or in the evening to avoid the hottest part of the day; carry water and sunscreen.
- **Autumn**: wear layers and enjoy the colours; take care on wet leaves.
- **Winter**: wear warm layers, use footwear with good grip, and consider walking in the middle of the day when it is lighter and warmer. In icy conditions, consider an indoor alternative, such as walking in a shopping centre or using a treadmill.

## A simple twelve-week walking plan

If you would like structure, this gentle plan builds up gradually. Adjust it to suit your starting point and health, and repeat a week if it feels too much.

- **Weeks 1–2**: walk 10 minutes at a comfortable pace on five days.
- **Weeks 3–4**: walk 15 minutes on five days, including a few minutes at a brisker pace.
- **Weeks 5–6**: walk 20 minutes on five days, with the middle 10 minutes brisk.
- **Weeks 7–8**: walk 25 minutes on five days, mostly brisk; add one gentle hill.
- **Weeks 9–10**: walk 30 minutes on five days at a brisk pace.
- **Weeks 11–12**: keep 30 brisk minutes on most days and add one longer, relaxed walk at the weekend.

By the end, you will be close to the recommended 150 minutes of moderate activity a week — and walking will probably feel like a natural, enjoyable part of your day.

## When walking is difficult

If you have joint pain, balance problems or other health conditions, walking may still be possible with adjustments: shorter walks, flatter routes, walking poles, or walking in water (see our guide to swimming and water exercise). A physiotherapist can suggest a programme that suits you. If walking is not possible, other forms of activity — such as chair-based exercise, cycling on a static bike or swimming — can provide similar benefits.

## The bottom line

Walking is simple, free and remarkably beneficial. Start where you are, build up gradually towards regular brisk walking most days, wear comfortable shoes, and stay safe. Mix in some variety, walk with others when you can, and enjoy the scenery. A daily walk is one of the kindest habits you can give your body and mind — and it gets easier, and more enjoyable, every week.
