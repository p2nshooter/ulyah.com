Few things affect how we feel as much as sleep. After a good night, the day seems brighter, the mind is clearer and patience comes more easily. After a poor one, everything feels harder. Many women find that sleep changes in their fifties and beyond: falling asleep takes longer, waking in the night becomes common, early-morning waking creeps in, or hot flushes and night sweats interrupt rest.

Some changes in sleep with age are normal, but persistent poor sleep is not something you simply have to accept. Many practical habits can help, and when they do not, there are effective treatments to explore with a health professional. This guide explains common changes and offers gentle, practical strategies for more restful nights.

This article provides general information and is not medical advice. If you have ongoing sleep problems, loud snoring with pauses in breathing, excessive daytime sleepiness, restless legs, low mood or anxiety, or if poor sleep is affecting your daily life, please speak to your doctor.

## How sleep changes with age

- **Lighter sleep.** Many people spend less time in the deepest stages of sleep as they get older, so they wake more easily.
- **Earlier body clock.** The internal clock often shifts earlier, leading to earlier sleepiness in the evening and earlier waking.
- **More night waking**, sometimes to use the bathroom.
- **Menopause.** Hot flushes, night sweats, mood changes and anxiety around menopause can disrupt sleep.
- **Health conditions and medicines.** Pain, breathing problems, heartburn, bladder issues and some medicines can affect sleep.
- **Life changes.** Retirement, loss of routine, bereavement, caring responsibilities and worry can all play a part.

Despite these changes, most adults still need a similar amount of sleep — generally around seven to nine hours for adults, according to many sleep organisations, though individual needs vary.

## Build a steady rhythm

Our bodies thrive on routine. A consistent rhythm helps regulate the internal clock.

- **Get up at the same time every day**, including weekends. This is often more powerful than a fixed bedtime.
- **Go to bed when you feel sleepy**, not just because it is a particular time.
- **Get daylight early.** Natural light in the morning, especially outdoors, helps set your body clock. A morning walk is ideal (see our walking guide).
- **Be active during the day.** Regular physical activity is associated with better sleep, though very vigorous exercise close to bedtime may be stimulating for some people.
- **Keep naps short and early.** If you nap, keep it to twenty to thirty minutes and before mid-afternoon, so it does not reduce your sleepiness at night.

## Create a restful bedroom

- **Cool**: a slightly cool room often helps sleep. This is particularly important if you have hot flushes.
- **Dark**: use blackout curtains or blinds, or an eye mask. Cover or turn away glowing lights.
- **Quiet**: earplugs or a gentle background sound, such as a fan or white-noise machine, can mask disturbances.
- **Comfortable**: a supportive mattress and pillows that suit your sleeping position make a difference. Consider replacing a mattress that sags or causes aches.
- **Tidy and calm**: a clutter-free bedroom feels more restful.
- **Reserved for sleep and intimacy**: try to avoid working, watching television or scrolling on your phone in bed, so your brain associates bed with sleep.

## A calming evening routine

A regular wind-down routine signals to your body that it is time to sleep.

- **Dim the lights** in the hour or so before bed.
- **Reduce screen time.** The content of phones and tablets can be stimulating, and bright screens late at night may affect sleepiness. If you use devices, choose calm activities and lower the brightness.
- **Do something relaxing**: reading a book, listening to gentle music or an audiobook, a warm bath or shower, gentle stretching (see our stretching guide), or a breathing exercise.
- **Write down worries or a to-do list** for tomorrow, to help clear your mind.
- **Keep the routine consistent**, so it becomes a familiar signal.

## Food, drink and sleep

- **Caffeine**: coffee, tea, cola, energy drinks and chocolate contain caffeine, which can stay in the body for hours. Many people sleep better if they avoid caffeine from early afternoon.
- **Alcohol**: although it may help you fall asleep, alcohol tends to disrupt sleep later in the night and can worsen hot flushes and snoring.
- **Heavy meals**: a large, rich or spicy meal late in the evening can cause discomfort or heartburn. Aim to eat your main meal a few hours before bed.
- **Fluids**: stay well hydrated during the day but reduce drinks in the hour or two before bed if night-time bathroom trips wake you.
- **A light snack**: if hunger keeps you awake, a small snack can help.
- **Nicotine**: is a stimulant; stopping smoking benefits sleep and health in many ways.

## Coping with night waking

Waking in the night is common. What matters is how you respond:

- **Do not watch the clock.** Turn it away; checking the time increases anxiety.
- **Keep lights low** if you need to get up.
- **If you cannot get back to sleep** after what feels like twenty minutes or so, get up, go to another dimly lit room and do something quiet and relaxing — reading or listening to calm music — until you feel sleepy, then return to bed. This helps prevent the bed becoming associated with frustration.
- **Try relaxation techniques**, such as slow breathing (breathing in gently through the nose and out slowly through the mouth), progressive muscle relaxation (tensing and relaxing each muscle group in turn), or a body scan.
- **Be kind to yourself.** Lying awake worrying about lost sleep makes it harder to fall asleep. Resting quietly still has value.

## Hot flushes and night sweats

For women going through menopause, hot flushes and night sweats can be a major cause of disturbed sleep.

- **Keep the bedroom cool** and use a fan.
- **Choose breathable bedding and nightwear**, such as cotton, linen or moisture-wicking fabrics.
- **Use layers** of bedding you can easily add or remove.
- **Keep a glass of cool water** by the bed.
- **Notice triggers**, such as alcohol, caffeine, spicy food or stress.
- **Talk to your doctor** about treatment options if symptoms are troublesome. Effective treatments are available, and a doctor can discuss benefits and risks for you.

## Managing a busy mind

Worry and stress are common reasons for poor sleep.

- **Set aside "worry time"** earlier in the evening to write down concerns and possible next steps.
- **Practise relaxation** or mindfulness during the day as well as at night.
- **Talk to someone**: a friend, family member or counsellor.
- **Seek help for anxiety or low mood**; these are common, treatable and closely linked with sleep.

## Sharing a bed

A partner's snoring, different bedtimes, a restless sleeper or a pet on the bed can all disturb sleep. Some practical options:

- **Encourage a snoring partner** to see a doctor, especially if there are pauses in breathing, which can signal a treatable condition.
- **Use earplugs** or a gentle background sound.
- **Try separate duvets**, which reduce disturbance from movement and suit different temperature preferences — particularly helpful during menopause.
- **Agree on a wind-down routine** that suits both of you, such as reading rather than television in bed.
- **Consider separate rooms** on some nights. Many couples find this improves sleep and relationships; it is a practical choice, not a sign of trouble.
- **Keep pets off the bed** if they wake you.

## Light, screens and the evening

Our body clocks respond strongly to light. Bright morning light helps us feel alert during the day and sleepy at night; bright light late in the evening can do the opposite. Use warm, dimmer lamps in the evening, keep the bedroom dark, and if you wake to use the bathroom, choose a soft night light rather than switching on a bright overhead light.

## When to see a doctor

Speak to your doctor if:

- poor sleep lasts for several weeks and affects your daytime life;
- you snore loudly, gasp or stop breathing during sleep (which a partner may notice), or wake with headaches — signs that can be associated with sleep apnoea;
- you have an uncomfortable urge to move your legs in the evening or at night;
- you feel very sleepy during the day, or fall asleep unintentionally;
- pain, breathing problems, bladder problems or other symptoms disturb your sleep;
- you feel low, anxious or hopeless;
- you are considering sleeping tablets or over-the-counter sleep aids.

For persistent insomnia, cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is a widely recommended treatment and is available in many places through health services, therapists or digital programmes. Sleeping tablets may be considered in some situations but are usually intended for short-term use and can have side effects, particularly in older adults, so discuss them carefully with a doctor.

## A simple sleep plan

1. Get up at the same time every day.
2. Go outside in daylight in the morning.
3. Be active during the day.
4. Limit caffeine after early afternoon and go easy on alcohol.
5. Keep naps short and early.
6. Wind down for an hour before bed with a calming routine.
7. Keep the bedroom cool, dark and quiet.
8. If you cannot sleep, get up and do something relaxing until you feel sleepy.
9. Seek help if problems persist.

## The bottom line

Sleep often changes with age, but many women can enjoy more restful nights with a steady daily rhythm, daylight and activity, a calm evening routine, a comfortable bedroom and sensible habits around caffeine, alcohol and meals. Be patient — new habits take a few weeks to make a difference — and do not hesitate to seek help if sleep problems persist. Good sleep is not a luxury; it is one of the foundations of feeling well.
