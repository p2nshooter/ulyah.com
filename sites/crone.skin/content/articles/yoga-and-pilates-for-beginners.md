Yoga and Pilates are often pictured as activities for the young and supremely flexible: impossible poses, perfect leggings and studios full of twenty-somethings. In reality, both are adaptable practices that suit people of every age, shape and ability. Many women discover them in their fifties, sixties and beyond, and find they become a cherished part of the week — a time to move, breathe, strengthen and unwind.

This guide explains the differences between yoga and Pilates, their benefits, how to choose a class or teacher, how to adapt exercises, and a gentle sequence to try at home. It is general information, not medical advice. If you have osteoporosis, joint replacements, back problems, high blood pressure, glaucoma, or other health conditions, ask your doctor or physiotherapist for guidance, and tell your instructor before class.

## Yoga and Pilates: what is the difference?

### Yoga

Yoga originated in ancient India and combines physical postures (asanas), breathing practices and, in many styles, relaxation and meditation. There are many styles, from gentle and restorative to vigorous and athletic.

Styles often suitable for beginners and older adults include:

- **Gentle yoga**: slow-paced classes with simple postures.
- **Hatha yoga**: a broad term often used for classes with held postures and breathing, usually at a moderate pace.
- **Chair yoga**: postures adapted for seated practice or using a chair for support.
- **Restorative yoga**: very gentle, using props to support the body in restful positions.
- **Yin yoga**: slow practice with longer holds in passive stretches. Approach with care if you have joint problems or hypermobility.
- **Iyengar yoga**: emphasises precise alignment and makes extensive use of props, which can be helpful for beginners.

More vigorous styles, such as power yoga, Ashtanga or hot yoga, may be less suitable for beginners or those with health conditions.

### Pilates

Pilates was developed in the twentieth century by Joseph Pilates. It focuses on core strength, posture, controlled movement and breathing. Pilates can be practised on a mat or using specialised equipment such as the reformer.

- **Mat Pilates**: uses body weight, sometimes with small props such as balls, bands and rings.
- **Reformer Pilates**: uses a machine with springs to provide resistance and support.
- **Clinical Pilates**: taught by physiotherapists or specially trained instructors, often for rehabilitation.

### Which is right for you?

Both build strength, flexibility, balance and body awareness. Broadly:

- **Yoga** often places more emphasis on flexibility, breathing, relaxation and mindfulness.
- **Pilates** often places more emphasis on core strength, posture and precise control.

Many women enjoy both. The best choice is the one you enjoy and will keep doing.

## The benefits

Regular practice of yoga or Pilates is commonly associated with:

- **Improved flexibility** and range of movement (see our stretching guide).
- **Greater strength**, particularly in the core, back, hips and legs.
- **Better balance** and coordination (see our balance guide).
- **Improved posture** and body awareness.
- **Relaxation and stress relief**, through focused breathing and calm movement.
- **A sense of community** in group classes.

Health guidelines for older adults recommend activities that improve strength and balance on several days a week; yoga and Pilates can contribute to this, alongside aerobic activity such as walking.

## Choosing a class

### Look for beginner-friendly options

- **Beginner or gentle classes**, or classes for older adults or "seniors".
- **Small classes**, where the instructor can give individual attention.
- **Qualified instructors**: ask about their training and experience with older beginners.
- **Clinical or physiotherapist-led classes** if you have health conditions or injuries.

### Questions to ask

- Is the class suitable for complete beginners?
- Can exercises be adapted for my needs?
- Are props such as blocks, straps, chairs and cushions available?
- What is the pace and style?
- Can I try a single class before committing?

### Online classes

Online classes and videos can be convenient, but it is harder to get feedback on technique. If possible, attend a few in-person classes first to learn the basics safely, and choose online programmes designed for beginners or older adults from reputable instructors.

## Adapting exercises

Good yoga and Pilates teaching is about adapting to your body, not forcing your body into a shape.

- **Use props.** Blocks bring the floor closer; straps extend your reach; cushions and blankets add comfort; a chair adds support.
- **Bend your knees** in forward bends to protect your back.
- **Keep movements smaller** if your range is limited.
- **Use a chair** for standing balances or seated alternatives.
- **Avoid positions that cause pain.** Discomfort from effort is normal; sharp or joint pain is not.
- **Rest whenever you need to.**

### Special considerations

- **Osteoporosis or low bone density**: avoid deep forward bends, forceful twisting of the spine and sit-ups. Ask for modified poses and exercises.
- **High blood pressure or glaucoma**: avoid inversions where the head is lower than the heart, such as headstands and some deep forward folds, unless advised otherwise.
- **Hip or knee replacements**: follow your surgeon's or physiotherapist's guidance on positions to avoid.
- **Wrist problems**: use fists, forearms or a chair instead of weight-bearing on open hands.

## A gentle beginner sequence

This short sequence combines simple yoga and Pilates-inspired movements. Move slowly, breathe steadily, and skip anything that does not feel right. Use a non-slip mat and have a chair nearby.

### 1. Seated breathing (two minutes)

Sit tall on a chair or cushion. Place one hand on your belly and one on your chest. Breathe in slowly through your nose, feeling the belly rise, and breathe out slowly. This calms the mind and prepares the body.

### 2. Seated cat-cow

Sitting tall, hands on knees, breathe in as you gently arch your back and lift your chest; breathe out as you round your back and draw your belly in. Repeat five to eight times.

### 3. Seated side stretch

Raise one arm and lean gently to the opposite side. Hold for a few breaths, then switch.

### 4. Mountain pose (standing)

Stand tall with feet hip-width apart, weight evenly spread, shoulders relaxed, arms by your sides. Breathe steadily for a few breaths, noticing your posture. Hold a chair if needed.

### 5. Chair-supported tree pose

Holding a chair, shift your weight onto one foot and place the sole of the other foot against your ankle, calf (not the knee) or simply rest the toes on the floor. Hold for a few breaths, then switch.

### 6. Standing hip hinge

With hands on the back of a chair, step back until your arms are straight, and hinge forward from the hips with a long, flat back and soft knees, until your body forms an "L" shape. Hold for a few breaths, then walk forward to stand.

### 7. Pelvic tilts (lying)

Lie on your back on a mat, knees bent, feet flat. Gently flatten your lower back into the mat by tilting your pelvis, then return to neutral. Repeat eight to ten times. This gently engages deep core muscles.

### 8. Bridge

From the same position, press through your feet and lift your hips, then lower slowly. Repeat five to eight times.

### 9. Knee sways

Lying on your back with knees bent, gently let both knees sway to one side, then the other, keeping shoulders on the mat. Keep the movement small and comfortable.

### 10. Relaxation (three to five minutes)

Lie on your back with a cushion under your knees and a blanket over you, or sit comfortably. Close your eyes, breathe naturally and let your body relax.

If getting down to the floor is difficult, do steps 7–10 lying on your bed or replace them with seated exercises.

## Building a practice

- **Start with one or two sessions a week**, and build up as you feel ready.
- **Practise little and often**: ten minutes at home on non-class days helps.
- **Be patient**: flexibility and strength improve gradually.
- **Notice progress**: easier movement, better balance, more calm.
- **Combine with other activity**: walking, swimming and strength training complement yoga and Pilates.

## Breathing: the heart of both practices

Both yoga and Pilates place great importance on breathing, and learning to breathe well can be one of the most valuable things you take from a class. In many styles of yoga, slow breathing through the nose helps calm the nervous system, and longer, gentle exhalations can feel especially soothing. In Pilates, breathing is coordinated with movement, often breathing out during the effort. In both, the golden rule is the same: never hold your breath while exercising. If you lose the rhythm, simply pause, breathe naturally and begin again.

## Common worries — and gentle answers

- **"I'm not flexible enough."** Flexibility is a result of practice, not a requirement to begin.
- **"I'll be the oldest in the class."** Many classes are full of women in their fifties, sixties and seventies — and beginner and seniors' classes are designed with you in mind.
- **"I can't get down to the floor."** Chair-based yoga and standing Pilates sequences are widely available, and teachers can show you safe ways to get down and up using a chair.
- **"I don't know the terminology."** Instructors explain everything, and you will pick it up quickly.
- **"I'm worried about injury."** Tell the instructor about any conditions, choose a gentle class, use props and move within your comfortable range.

## What to wear and bring

- **Comfortable clothing** that allows movement and does not ride up.
- **Bare feet or non-slip socks** for grip.
- **A mat**, though many studios provide them.
- **A water bottle** and perhaps a small towel.
- **A layer** for relaxation at the end, when you may cool down.

## The bottom line

Yoga and Pilates are welcoming practices that suit women of every age. Choose a beginner-friendly class with a qualified instructor, use props and adaptations, respect your body's limits, and practise regularly. Over time, you are likely to feel stronger, steadier, more flexible and calmer — benefits that ripple into every part of daily life.
