Opening a crowded wardrobe can feel overwhelming. Clothes are squeezed onto hangers, shelves overflow, and somewhere among them are the things you actually love — if only you could find them. Many of us keep clothes for years "just in case": in case we lose weight, in case a formal occasion arises, in case the style comes back. Meanwhile, we wear the same few pieces again and again.

Editing your wardrobe is one of the most freeing things you can do. It reveals what you really wear, highlights what suits you now, makes getting dressed quicker and calmer, and often uncovers forgotten favourites. This guide offers a gentle, practical method that respects the emotional side of letting go.

## Why edit now?

The fifties and sixties are often a time of transition: careers change, children leave home, bodies change, and personal style evolves. A wardrobe built for a different stage of life may no longer serve you. Editing helps you:

- **see clearly** what you own;
- **dress for the life you have now**, not the one you had;
- **wear clothes that fit and flatter** your current body;
- **reduce stress** in the mornings;
- **free up space** — and perhaps raise some money;
- **shop more thoughtfully** in future.

## Before you begin

- **Choose a good time.** Set aside a few hours, or plan to work through one category at a time over several days.
- **Prepare the space.** Clear the bed or a table, have good light and a full-length mirror.
- **Gather supplies.** Bags or boxes labelled "Keep", "Alter or repair", "Donate", "Sell", "Recycle" and "Maybe".
- **Invite a friend** whose taste and honesty you trust, if you like company and a second opinion.
- **Put on underwear and shoes** you normally wear, so you see clothes properly.

## Step one: take everything out

Work through one category at a time: tops, trousers, skirts, dresses, knitwear, jackets, coats, shoes, accessories. Take every item in the category out of the wardrobe and drawers. Seeing everything together reveals duplicates and gaps.

## Step two: try things on

Trying on is essential. Clothes can look fine on a hanger but feel wrong on the body. For each item, ask:

1. **Does it fit?** Not "will it fit one day", but now.
2. **Is it comfortable?** Can you sit, move and breathe?
3. **Does it suit me?** Is the colour flattering? Is the style right for my body and personality now?
4. **Is it in good condition?** Look for stains, pilling, fading, stretched necklines and broken zips.
5. **Have I worn it in the past year?** If not, why not?
6. **Do I love it?** Does it make me feel good?
7. **Does it go with other things** I own?
8. **Would I buy it today?**

Be honest but kind to yourself. The aim is clarity, not judgement.

## Step three: sort into piles

### Keep

Clothes that fit, suit you, are in good condition and that you wear and love. These form the foundation of your wardrobe — the beginning of a capsule (see our guide to building a capsule wardrobe).

### Alter or repair

Items you love that need a small fix: a hem, a button, a zip, taking in a waist or shortening sleeves. Be realistic: only keep these if you will actually get them done. Set a deadline — perhaps a month — and if the item is still waiting, let it go. Our guide to tailoring and alterations explains what is worth doing.

### Maybe

Items you are unsure about. Put them in a box, date it, and store it out of sight. If you do not miss or retrieve anything within three to six months, donate the box without opening it.

### Donate or sell

Items in good condition that no longer suit you.

### Recycle

Items that are worn out, stained or damaged beyond repair.

## The difficult categories

Some clothes are harder to let go of. Recognising why can help.

### "When I lose weight" clothes

Many women keep clothes in smaller sizes, hoping to fit into them again. These clothes can become a daily reminder of dissatisfaction. If you do change size, you may well want new clothes in styles that suit you then. Consider keeping one or two special pieces and letting the rest go.

### "Expensive mistake" clothes

It is painful to part with something that cost a lot but never felt right. But the money is already spent; keeping the item does not bring it back. Selling it may recover some of the cost, and letting it go removes the guilt.

### "Just in case" clothes

Formal outfits for occasions that rarely happen, work suits for a job you have left, clothes for hobbies you no longer pursue. Keep one or two versatile special-occasion outfits and let the rest go. If an occasion arises, you can borrow, rent or buy then.

### Sentimental clothes

A wedding dress, a mother's coat, a favourite T-shirt from a memorable trip. These carry memories, and you do not have to part with them. Some ideas:

- **Keep a small number** of truly special pieces, stored carefully.
- **Photograph items** before letting them go; the photo preserves the memory.
- **Repurpose** fabrics into cushions, quilts or bags.
- **Pass them on** to family members who will treasure them.

## Step four: let go well

### Donating

- **Charity shops** welcome clean, good-condition clothing.
- **Local organisations**, such as women's shelters, refugee support groups and community clothing banks, often need specific items such as coats and professional clothing.
- **Check what is accepted** before donating, and wash items first.

### Selling

- **Online marketplaces and resale apps** are useful for good-quality and branded items.
- **Consignment and dress agencies** sell clothing on your behalf for a share of the price.
- **Clothes-swap events** let you exchange clothes with others.
- **Photograph items well**, in natural light, and describe them honestly.

### Recycling

- **Textile recycling banks** accept worn items in many areas.
- **Some retailers run take-back schemes** for old clothes.
- **Use rags** from old cotton items for cleaning.

## Step five: organise what remains

Once you have edited, organise your wardrobe so you can see and reach everything.

- **Use matching, slim hangers** to save space and create a calm look.
- **Group clothes by category**, then by colour within each category.
- **Fold knitwear** rather than hanging it, to prevent stretching.
- **Store out-of-season clothes** separately, in breathable bags or boxes, cleaned and protected from moths.
- **Keep everyday items** at eye level and within easy reach.
- **Store shoes** where you can see them.
- **Display accessories** so you remember to use them (see our guide to accessories).

## Step six: identify gaps

After editing, you may notice gaps: perhaps you have many tops but few trousers that fit, or no warm coat you like. Make a list of what you genuinely need, and shop for these pieces slowly and thoughtfully. Our guide to building a capsule wardrobe can help you prioritise.

## Keeping it edited

A wardrobe stays manageable with a few simple habits:

- **One in, one out.** When you buy something new, let go of something you no longer wear.
- **The hanger trick.** Turn all hangers the "wrong" way at the start of the year; when you wear an item, turn the hanger round. After a year, you can see what you never wore.
- **Seasonal reviews.** When you swap seasonal clothes, quickly edit both sets.
- **Repair promptly.** Fix small problems as they arise.
- **Shop with a list.**

## Editing with a partner, friend or family

Editing alongside someone else can make the process quicker and more fun, but it helps to set ground rules. Agree that the final decision on every item is yours, ask for honest but kind feedback on fit and colour, and avoid letting a helper talk you into keeping or discarding something against your instinct. If you are helping an elderly parent with their wardrobe, go slowly, respect their attachment to familiar clothes, and focus on comfort, safety (such as replacing slippery slippers) and ease of dressing.

## Editing shoes, bags and outerwear

The same questions apply beyond clothes. Shoes that pinch, boots that leak, bags that are too heavy to carry comfortably and coats you never reach for all take up space. Keep the pairs and pieces you wear regularly, repair good-quality items that need new heels or linings, and let go of the rest. Check outerwear before winter: a warm, waterproof coat you love is worth more than several that are almost right.

## A short editing checklist

1. Work one category at a time.
2. Try everything on with the right underwear and shoes.
3. Keep what fits, flatters and feels good now.
4. Set a deadline for alterations and repairs.
5. Box the "maybes" and revisit in a few months.
6. Donate, sell or recycle the rest promptly.
7. Organise by category and colour.
8. Note the gaps before you shop.

## The emotional reward

Many women describe a sense of lightness after editing their wardrobe. Getting dressed becomes easier, clothes feel more "me", and the wardrobe feels calm rather than chaotic. There is also satisfaction in knowing that unwanted clothes are going to people who will use them.

## The bottom line

Editing your wardrobe is about making space for the clothes and the person you are now. Take everything out, try it on, keep what fits, flatters and feels good, deal gently with sentimental and aspirational pieces, donate and recycle responsibly, and organise what remains so you can see it. The result is a wardrobe that works for you — and mornings that start with ease rather than frustration.
