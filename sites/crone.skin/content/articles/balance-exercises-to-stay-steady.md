Balance is something we take for granted until it starts to slip. Perhaps you have noticed that you hold the banister a little more firmly, feel wobbly putting on socks while standing, or have had a near-miss on an uneven pavement. Balance naturally changes over the years, but it is a skill — and like any skill, it can be practised and improved.

Good balance helps you move confidently, carry things, walk on uneven ground, dance, travel and stay independent. It also plays an important part in reducing the risk of falls. This guide explains why balance changes, offers a simple routine of exercises you can do at home, and suggests everyday habits that help keep you steady.

This is general information, not medical advice. If you have had a fall, feel dizzy, have unexplained unsteadiness, take medicines that affect balance, or have conditions such as osteoporosis, arthritis, neuropathy or inner-ear problems, speak to your doctor or a physiotherapist. Many areas offer falls-prevention programmes and strength-and-balance classes led by trained instructors.

## Why balance changes with age

Balance depends on several systems working together:

- **Muscles and strength**, especially in the legs, hips, ankles and core.
- **Joints and flexibility**, particularly in the ankles and hips.
- **Vision**, which tells the brain where you are in space.
- **The inner ear (vestibular system)**, which senses head movement and position.
- **Sensation in the feet**, which tells the brain about the ground beneath you.
- **The brain**, which coordinates all of this information and reacts.

With age, any of these systems can change: muscles can weaken, reaction times slow, vision changes, sensation in the feet may reduce, and some medicines can affect balance. Inactivity makes things worse, because balance and strength decline faster when we do not use them.

The encouraging news is that research and health guidelines consistently support regular strength and balance exercise for older adults. The World Health Organization, for example, recommends that older adults include varied activity emphasising balance and strength on three or more days a week.

## Getting ready safely

- **Use a sturdy support**, such as the back of a solid chair, a kitchen worktop or a wall rail. Make sure it will not slide.
- **Clear the area** of rugs, clutter and pets.
- **Wear supportive shoes** or exercise in bare feet on a non-slip surface, depending on what feels steadiest.
- **Have someone nearby** if you are unsure about your balance.
- **Start with both hands on the support**, then progress to one hand, then fingertips, then no hands — only when you feel completely confident.
- **Stop** if you feel dizzy, unwell or in pain.

## A daily balance routine

Do these exercises daily, or at least three times a week. Each takes only a minute or two. Stand tall, look straight ahead, and breathe normally.

### 1. Feet together stand

1. Stand with your feet together, holding the support.
2. Hold for up to 30 seconds.
3. If steady, reduce your support.

### 2. Semi-tandem stand

1. Place one foot slightly in front of the other, so the heel of the front foot is beside the big toe of the back foot.
2. Hold for up to 30 seconds.
3. Switch feet.

### 3. Tandem stand (heel-to-toe)

1. Place one foot directly in front of the other, heel touching toe, as if standing on a tightrope.
2. Hold the support and stay for up to 30 seconds.
3. Switch feet.

### 4. Single-leg stand

1. Holding the support, lift one foot slightly off the floor.
2. Hold for up to 10 seconds, building towards 30 seconds over time.
3. Switch legs.

### 5. Heel-to-toe walk

1. Next to a wall or worktop for support, walk forward placing the heel of one foot directly in front of the toes of the other.
2. Take ten steps, turn carefully and repeat.

### 6. Heel raises

1. Holding the support, rise onto your toes.
2. Lower slowly.
3. Repeat ten times.

This also strengthens the calves and ankles, which help balance.

### 7. Toe raises

1. Holding the support, lift your toes and the front of your feet off the floor, keeping your heels down.
2. Lower slowly.
3. Repeat ten times.

### 8. Side leg raises

1. Holding the support, lift one leg out to the side, keeping your body upright and toes facing forward.
2. Lower slowly.
3. Repeat ten times, then switch legs.

### 9. Sit-to-stand

1. Sit on a sturdy chair, feet flat.
2. Stand up without using your hands, if possible, then sit down slowly.
3. Repeat ten times.

This builds leg strength that is essential for balance. See our guide to strength training for more strengthening exercises.

### 10. Walking sideways

1. Holding a worktop, step sideways along it, bringing your feet together each time.
2. Take ten steps one way and ten steps back.

## Progressing safely

As your balance improves, gradually increase the challenge — but only one change at a time, and only when you feel steady:

- **Reduce your support**: two hands, one hand, fingertips, no hands (keeping the support within reach).
- **Hold positions for longer.**
- **Turn your head slowly** from side to side while holding a position.
- **Close your eyes briefly** — only with support within reach and someone nearby, as this is much harder.
- **Stand on a cushion** or folded towel to create a less stable surface — again, only with support within reach.
- **Add movement**: reach for objects, swing your arms gently or pass a ball from hand to hand while balancing.

## Activities that build balance

Many enjoyable activities also improve balance:

- **Tai chi**, which involves slow, flowing movements and weight shifts. It has been widely studied for balance and falls prevention in older adults.
- **Yoga** and **Pilates**, which build strength, flexibility and body awareness (see our guide to yoga and Pilates for beginners).
- **Dancing**, from ballroom to line dancing.
- **Walking**, especially on varied terrain (see our walking guide).
- **Bowls, golf and gardening**, which involve bending, reaching and shifting weight.

Look for classes designed for older adults or beginners, and tell the instructor about any health conditions.

## Everyday habits that help

Balance is influenced by more than exercise. These habits support steadiness:

### Look after your eyes and ears

- **Have regular eye tests** and keep glasses up to date and clean.
- **Take care with varifocal or bifocal lenses** on stairs and kerbs; some people find single-vision distance glasses safer for walking outdoors.
- **Have your hearing checked**, and see a doctor about dizziness or vertigo.

### Review medicines

Some medicines, or combinations of medicines, can cause dizziness or drowsiness. Ask your doctor or pharmacist to review your medicines if you notice these effects. Never stop prescribed medicines without advice.

### Footwear and feet

- **Wear well-fitting shoes** with non-slip soles and a firm heel, indoors and out.
- **Avoid loose, backless slippers**, which are a common cause of trips.
- **Look after your feet**: see a podiatrist about painful feet, corns or long toenails, which can affect walking.

### Get up slowly

Some people feel lightheaded when standing up quickly, especially after lying down. Sit on the edge of the bed for a moment before standing, and stand up slowly.

### Stay hydrated and nourished

Dehydration and skipping meals can cause dizziness. Drink regularly and eat balanced meals.

### Make your home safer

- **Keep floors clear** of clutter, cables and loose rugs.
- **Use good lighting**, including night lights between the bedroom and bathroom.
- **Install grab rails** in the bathroom and by steps if helpful.
- **Use non-slip mats** in the bath and shower.
- **Keep frequently used items** within easy reach, avoiding the need to climb.
- **Have rails on both sides of stairs** if possible.

Our guide to a home that ages with you covers this in more detail.

### Be careful with alcohol

Alcohol affects balance and reaction times. Be cautious, particularly in the evening and on stairs.

## If you do fall

Knowing what to do can reduce fear:

1. **Stay calm** and take a moment to check whether you are hurt.
2. **If you are not hurt and feel able**, roll onto your side, then onto your hands and knees, crawl to a sturdy chair, and use it to get up slowly.
3. **If you are hurt or cannot get up**, call for help, keep warm and move gently to stay comfortable.
4. **Consider a personal alarm** or keep a phone with you if you live alone.

Tell your doctor about any fall, even if you were not injured; it may be worth checking for underlying causes.

## Building confidence

Fear of falling can make people less active, which in turn weakens muscles and balance. Practising balance exercises regularly, joining a class and making the home safer often restore confidence. Celebrate small improvements: standing on one leg a few seconds longer, or walking more steadily on uneven ground.

## The bottom line

Balance is a skill that can be practised and improved at any age. A few minutes of simple exercises each day, combined with strength training, activities such as tai chi or dancing, good footwear, regular eye checks and a safe home, can help you stay steady, confident and independent. Start with support, progress gradually and enjoy the growing sense of stability.
