If a mineral had to be chosen to represent the mineral wealth of northern Spain, many collectors would choose Asturian fluorite. Its transparent cubes, deep purple or honey yellow, often perched on white barite or calcite, are recognized in the showcases of the world's great museums. Behind that beauty there is a very specific mining history: for decades, Asturias was one of the main fluorite producing districts in Europe, and the mines that supplied the chemical and steel industry left, in the process, some of the best specimens of the species.

In this guide you will see what fluorite is, why it has so many colors, where the word fluorescence comes from, which are the best-known Asturian mining areas, what this mineral is used for in the industry, how to choose a good specimen and how to care for it, because behind its robust appearance hides a quite delicate mineral.

## What is fluorite

Fluorite is calcium fluoride. It crystallizes in the cubic system and usually forms cubes, sometimes octahedrons and, less frequently, combinations of both forms.

| Property | Fluorite |
|---|---|
| Composition | calcium fluoride |
| crystalline system | cubic |
| Common forms | cubes; also octahedrons and combinations |
| Hardness on the Mohs scale | 4; It is the reference mineral of that grade |
| Exfoliation | perfect, in four directions, which give octahedrons when broken |
| Brightness | vitreous |
| Colors | purple, violet, yellow, green, blue, pink, colorless, often in areas |
| Fluorescence | frequent, especially bluish, although not in all specimens |

Its name comes from the Latin *fluere*, to flow, because since ancient times it was added to furnaces as a flux to help metals melt and flow better. That same use is still valid today in the steel industry.

## Why does it have so many colors?

Pure fluorite is colorless. Their colors are due to small amounts of other elements that replace calcium, such as some rare earth elements, and to defects in the crystal structure, often caused by natural radiation from nearby rocks over millions of years. As these conditions change as the crystal grows, it is very common to find:

- **Color zoning:** bands parallel to the faces of the cube, for example with a yellow core and a purple edge.
- **Ghosts:** internal outlines that draw the stages of crystal growth, like cubes within cubes.
- **Inclusions:** small sulfide crystals, such as pyrite or chalcopyrite, or other minerals trapped during growth.

This variety means that no two specimens are the same and explains a good part of the appeal of fluorite for collectors.

## Fluorite and fluorescence

The word fluorescence comes precisely from fluorite. In the mid-19th century, British physicist George Gabriel Stokes studied how certain materials absorb invisible ultraviolet light and emit visible light, and he named the phenomenon after this mineral, which often shows it with a blue or violet glow.

Not all fluorites are fluorescent, nor all with the same intensity: it depends on the impurities they contain. If you want to check it, use a specific ultraviolet lamp for minerals and always protect your eyes and skin, because shortwave ultraviolet radiation is harmful.

## The mining areas of Asturias

Asturian fluorite comes from various areas in the center and east of the region, where it forms veins and mantles in limestone and sandstone, accompanied by barite, calcite, quartz and sulfides.

| Area | Municipality | What is usually associated with it |
|---|---|---|
| Berbes | Ribadesella | purple and violet cubes, highly prized, sometimes with quartz |
| The Collada | Siero | fluorite of various colors, with barite and calcite |
| Solis | Corvera | yellow or honey-colored cubes with barite, like those from the Moscona mine |
| Lorone | Colunga | transparent yellow cubes with barite, like those from the Emilio mine |
| Villabona | Llanera | purple and colorless fluorite, with marked zones |
| Caravia | Caravia | purple fluorite in a district with a long mining tradition |

They are indicative associations: each mine has produced specimens of varied colors and appearances throughout its history, and some have already closed. As in any collection, the label with the specific lead is what gives value and meaning to the specimen.

## What is fluorite used for?

Fluorite is not mined for collectors, but for industry. Collectible specimens are a fortunate byproduct of mining activity.

| Use | What is it for? |
|---|---|
| Flux | facilitates fusion in the manufacture of steel and other metals |
| chemical industry | It is the main raw material to obtain hydrofluoric acid and, from it, numerous fluorine compounds |
| Optics | very pure fluorite transmits ultraviolet and infrared light well; Today, synthetic fluorite is mainly used in high-quality lenses. |
| Ceramics and glass | intervenes in some enamels and special glasses |

The European Union considers fluorspar a critical raw material due to its industrial importance and the risk in its supply, which gives a strategic dimension to European deposits, including those in Asturias.

## Choose a good specimen

| Appearance | What to value |
|---|---|
| Color | intense and attractive; sharp zoning is highly appreciated |
| Transparency | clean crystals, without visible internal fractures |
| Edges | alive, without bumps or exfoliation marks |
| Composition | contrast with the matrix, like purple cubes on white barite |
| Brightness | shiny faces, without corrosion or coatings |
| Origin | specific mine, not just “Asturias” |

### Warning signs

- **Perfect and loose octahedrons:** most of those sold in stores are not natural crystals, but fragments obtained by taking advantage of the exfoliation of fluorite. They are legitimate as an item, but they are not crystals.
- **Faces that are too shiny or uniform:** some specimens are polished or treated to improve their appearance.
- **Stuck crystals:** As with other minerals, it is advisable to check the bond with the matrix with a magnifying glass.
- **Generic labels:** There is a lot of fluorite from China, Mexico or Morocco circulating on the market, often very beautiful, which should not be presented as Asturian.

## A more delicate mineral than it seems

Fluorite seems resistant, but it has three weak points:

1. **Exfoliation:** It breaks easily following its exfoliation planes. A blow to an edge can detach a triangular sheet.
2. **Heat:** sudden changes in temperature can cause fractures. Never subject it to hot water or leave it next to a hot light.
3. **Light:** some colors may fade with prolonged exposure to the sun. Display it away from windows and with lighting that does not emit heat.

### How to clean it

- **Powder:** soft brush or low pressure air.
- **Adhered dirt:** water at room temperature and a soft brush, being careful on the edges. Dry immediately.
- **No ultrasound:** vibrations can open the exfoliation planes.
- **No chemicals:** Some specialists use certain acids to remove iron stains, but the risk of damaging the specimen, and of harming oneself, is high.

## faceted fluorite

Fluorite is sometimes cut as a collector's gem, with beautiful colors. But its low hardness and exfoliation make it unsuitable for rings or bracelets: it scratches and chips easily. As a pendant or earring for occasional use, protected from shocks, it can work; As a stone for daily use, no. ## Where to see Asturian fluorite

The Geomining Museum in Madrid preserves top-level Asturian specimens, and in Asturias the Museum of Mining and Industry, in El Entrego, helps to understand the mining context of the region, although it focuses above all on coal. The mineral fairs in northern Spain are also a good place to see many specimens together and compare qualities. ## Frequent errors

- **Wash the fluorite with hot water,** which can fracture it.
- **Use ultrasound** to clean it.
- **Expose it to the sun** in a lighted window or display case.
- **Buy exfoliation octahedrons** believing they are natural crystals.
- **Accept vague labels** without mine or municipality.
- **Mount it on a ring** for daily use.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why is Asturian fluorite so famous?

Because Asturias was one of the great producing districts in Europe and its mines produced transparent cubes of intense colors, often on barite or calcite, of great aesthetic quality.

### Does all fluorite glow under UV light?

No. Many fluorites are fluorescent, but the intensity and color depend on their impurities, and some do not react.

### Can fluorite be washed?

Yes, with water at room temperature and a soft brush, drying it immediately. Avoid hot water, ultrasound and chemicals.

### Are fluorite octahedra natural?

Most of those sold loose are obtained by breaking the fluorite along its cleavage planes. Natural octahedra exist, but are less common.

## In summary

Fluorite is calcium fluoride, a cubic mineral of hardness 4 with very varied, zoned colors and, often, fluorescence, a word that comes precisely from its name. Asturias was one of the great producing districts in Europe, with classic areas such as Berbes, La Collada, Solís, Loroñe, Villabona or Caravia. When purchasing, consider the color, transparency, edges and specific origin, and be wary of exfoliation octahedrons sold as crystals. To preserve it, protect it from shocks, heat and sun, and clean it only with water at room temperature and very gently.
