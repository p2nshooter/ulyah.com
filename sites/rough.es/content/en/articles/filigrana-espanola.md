Few jewelry techniques require as much patience as filigree. With silver or gold threads thinner than a hair, twisted, flattened and folded one by one, the goldsmith builds metallic lace that seems impossible: rosettes, spirals, flowers and stars full of air and light. In Spain, filigree has ancient roots and two undisputed capitals: Córdoba, heir to a long silver tradition, and Salamanca, whose charro button has become a symbol of the city and one of the most recognizable traditional jewels in the country.

In this guide you will see what filigree is and where its name comes from, how it is made step by step, what characterizes the traditions of Córdoba and Salamanca, what other popular Spanish jewelry uses it, how to distinguish a handmade piece from a cast or stamped imitation and how to care for it so that it retains its delicacy for generations.

## What is filigree

Filigree is a goldsmithing technique that consists of forming openwork drawings with fine threads of precious metal, usually twisted, which are welded together and to a framework of thicker thread. The name comes from the Latin *filum*, thread, and *granum*, grain, because in its origins it was often combined with granulation: small metal spheres welded to the surface.

The technique is very old and was practiced in the Mediterranean, the Middle East and Asia. In the Iberian Peninsula it had a notable development in al-Andalus and was later maintained in workshops in many regions, which adapted it to their own tastes and their traditional costumes.

## How to work filigree

1. **Prepare the metal:** the silver or gold is melted with the desired alloy and a bar is obtained.
2. **Stretch the thread:** the bar is rolled and passed through a spinneret, a plate with increasingly smaller holes, until a very fine thread is obtained.
3. **Twisting:** Two threads are twisted together, giving them a cord-like appearance.
4. **Flatten:** The twisted yarn is lightly rolled so that it lies flat and shows a pattern of small notches.
5. **Form the framework:** with a thicker thread the outline of the piece and its interior divisions is built.
6. **Filling:** Using tweezers, the goldsmith bends the fine thread into spirals, waves and scrolls, and fits them inside the frame.
7. **Soldering:** Solder powder and flux are applied and carefully heated to join all the wires without melting them.
8. **Clean and shape:** After stripping, the piece may bulge, join other parts and be completed with grain.
9. **Finish:** it is polished, whitened or darkened in specific areas to highlight the drawing.

Each step requires pulse and experience. A complex part can require many hours of work, and a mistake in welding can melt threads that took hours to assemble.

## Granulation, the sister technique

Granulation has accompanied filigree since Antiquity. It consists of decorating the surface with tiny metal spheres, placed one by one and welded without losing their shape. To obtain them, the goldsmith cuts small fragments of thread or sheet metal and heats them over coal or in a bed of refractory powder: when melted, the surface tension turns them into almost perfect balls, which are then classified by size.

The difficulty is in soldering them. If the heat is excessive, the spheres flatten or fuse with the base; If it is insufficient, they become loose with use. Antique goldsmiths developed very sophisticated methods to achieve this, and even today fine graining is considered a test of mastery. In the charro button, the small granules that surround the concentric circles are an essential part of its character, and their regularity is one of the indicators of a well-made piece.

## The Cordoba filigree

Córdoba is one of the great silver centers in Spain. Its tradition is linked to Andalusian goldsmithing and centuries of guild activity: the Cordoban silversmiths organized themselves into their own school and celebrated their patron saint, San Eloy, for generations. Today the city combines artisan workshops with a powerful jewelry industry.

Cordoba filigree is recognized for its delicate compositions, with a predominance of fine spirals and floral motifs, applied to brooches, earrings, pendants and decorative figures. Many pieces are made of sterling silver, sometimes with gold plating or details. In the city's workshops it is possible to see filigree makers at work and buy directly from the manufacturer.

## Salamanca and the charro button

The charro button was born as a silver button for the traditional costume of the Salamanca countryside, the charro suit. It is a filigree hemisphere with a bulging central finial and concentric circles decorated with spirals and small granules, often in a variable number of layers or rings depending on the model. Over time it went from clothing to jewelry: today it is worn as a pendant, earrings, cufflinks or brooch, and it is one of the most typical gifts from Salamanca, also at weddings and baptisms.

Beyond the button, Charro jewelry includes necklaces, earrings, crosses and silver and gold reliquaries, often with filigree and granules, which are worn with the costume at parties and pilgrimages. They are pieces with a strong identity load, which many families preserve and transmit.

## Other peninsular traditions

Filigree appears in popular jewelry from many Spanish regions, especially in earrings, crosses, medals and buttons associated with regional costumes. In neighboring Portugal, the filigree of the north of the country, with pieces as well known as the heart of Viana, is part of the same family of Iberian traditions. Comparing pieces from different regions is a great way to learn to recognize styles and eras. ## Handmade, cast or stamped

The demand for filigree has given rise to imitations that reproduce its appearance with other techniques.

| Type | How to recognize it |
|---|---|
| Hand filigree | individual threads visible, with small irregularities; soldered joints and real gaps between the wires |
| Molten | filigree appearance but with somewhat blunt details, without separate threads and with the reverse sometimes solid or with casting marks |
| Printed | relief drawing on sheet metal, without real openwork or with die-cut openwork |
| Mixed | cast or stamped frame with filigree fillings |

With a magnifying glass, the difference is usually clear: in authentic filigree you can follow each thread with your eyes. Cast or stamped parts are not fraud if sold as such, but they are not of the same workmanship or value. The casting technique is explained in the guide on [lost wax casting](/es/fundicion-a-la-cera-perdida/).

## What to check when buying

- **Ask how the piece is made** and ask that it appear on the invoice.
- **Check the welds:** clean, without stains or excess material.
- **Check the hallmark:** the grade of the metal and the manufacturer's mark, as we explain in the guide on [hallmarks and guarantee marks](/es/contrastes-y-marcas-de-garantia/).
- **Look at the reverse:** in the hand filigree, also the reverse shows the threads.
- **Rate solidity:** Closures, hooks and rings must be firm, because filigree is delicate.
- **Buy from identifiable workshops** when looking for artisan pieces.

## Filigree care

The filigree is resistant for its fineness, but it does not withstand pressure or shock.

- **Store it separately** in a box with compartments or a cloth bag, without other pieces on top that could crush it.
- **For silver,** use antioxidant bags or sulfur absorbing strips that delay darkening.
- **Clean gently:** warm water, a few drops of neutral soap and a very soft brush; Rinse well and dry with air or a lint-free cloth.
- **Avoid immersion silver cleaning liquids** on pieces with many gaps, because debris may get trapped between the threads.
- **Do not pull the threads** with hard cloths or brushes.
- **For old or damaged pieces,** go to a specialized silversmith.

## Frequent errors

- **Buy cast filigree** believing it to be handmade.
- **Keep it mixed** with other jewelry that crushes it.
- **Clean it with aggressive products** that remain between the threads.
- **Ignore the contrast** of the piece.
- **Repair old pieces** in workshops without experience in filigree.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the charro button?

A filigree silver button, shaped like a semisphere and with a central finish, originating from the traditional costume of Salamanca. Today it is used as jewelry in pendants, earrings and cufflinks.

### Is the filigree always silver?

No. They work in silver and gold. In Spain silver predominates, although there are traditional gold pieces.

### How do I know if a filigree is handmade?

With a magnifying glass: in hand filigree you can distinguish the individual threads, twisted and welded, with real gaps between them.

### Can a filigree piece be repaired?

Yes, but it requires a silversmith with experience in the technique, because the heat from soldering can damage nearby wires.

## In summary

Filigree is a goldsmithing technique that constructs openwork drawings with fine silver or gold threads, twisted, flattened, folded and soldered to a frame. In Spain, Córdoba maintains a silver tradition with Andalusian roots, and Salamanca has made the charro button a symbol, present today in pendants, earrings and cufflinks. To distinguish a handmade piece from one cast or stamped, look closely for individual threads and actual gaps, check welds and hallmarks, and buy from identifiable workshops. Store it separately and clean it gently.
