Many of the rings, pendants and earrings sold today began as a piece of wax. A jeweler carved it by hand, or a computer designed it and a printer made it in resin; Then, that piece was wrapped in refractory material, made to disappear with heat, and molten metal was poured into the gap it left. It is the lost wax technique, one of the oldest in metallurgy, which was used thousands of years ago and which today, with modern equipment, continues to be the basis of jewelry throughout the world.

In this guide you will see how the model is made, why the parts are assembled in a tree shape, what the coating is and how the wax is removed, what methods are used to pour the metal, how the parts are cleaned and finished, what defects are common and why they appear, how a cast part differs from a handmade one, and what you can check as a buyer to recognize a good casting.

## An ancient technique

Lost wax casting appears in many ancient cultures, from Mesopotamia and Egypt to pre-Hispanic goldsmithing in America, which brought it to an extraordinary degree of refinement. The principle is always the same: a model of a material that is melted or burned is wrapped in a heat-resistant mold, removed, and the metal takes its place with astonishing fidelity.

In today's jewelry it is also known as microfusion, because it is applied to small pieces with a very high level of detail. In Spain, jewelry centers like Córdoba, with a long silver tradition and a very active jewelry industry, use it on a large scale.

## The model

| Model type | How to obtain | Advantages |
|---|---|---|
| hand carved wax | The jeweler carves blocks of hard wax with files, burins and burs | creative freedom and artisanal character |
| injected wax | Wax is injected into a rubber mold obtained from a master model | allows you to reproduce many identical copies |
| 3D printed resin | It is designed on the computer and printed with burnable resins | precision, symmetry and complex shapes |

Carving waxes are manufactured in different hardnesses: the hardest ones allow for fine details and sharp edges; the softest, organic shapes. With 3D printing, digital design also allows you to adjust the size of the ring or the size of the stones before manufacturing anything.

## the tree

The models are attached with small wax channels, called sprues, to a central trunk mounted on a rubber base. The result looks like a small tree with pieces hanging from its branches. The design of the tree is an essential part of the craft: the sprues must be of the right thickness and position so that the metal reaches all corners and solidifies in the correct order.

Before proceeding, the wax tree is weighed: by multiplying that weight by a factor that depends on the density of the alloy, the caster calculates how much metal he needs.

## The coating

The tree is placed inside a metal cylinder and filled with coating, a mixture of gypsum and silica that is prepared with water and hardens like a special plaster, capable of resisting high temperatures. To eliminate air bubbles, which would become metal balls in the piece, the mixture is vacuum treated before and after pouring. When it sets, the tree is enclosed in a refractory block.

## Dewaxing and calcination

The cylinder is placed in an oven with a programmed temperature cycle that lasts several hours. First, the wax melts and comes out of the base; Then, at a higher temperature, the remains are burned and the coating acquires the necessary resistance. 3D printing resins need specific cycles, because they behave differently than wax and can leave residue or crack the mold if not heated properly. In the end there is a mold with the exact shape of the negative pieces.

## The laundry

The metal is melted in a crucible and placed in the hot mold. There are three common methods:

| Method | How it works |
|---|---|
| Centrifuge | The cylinder rotates at high speed and the centrifugal force pushes the metal inward |
| empty | a pump draws air from the mold while the metal is poured |
| Vacuum and pressure | combines aspiration with gas pressure on the metal, for very thin pieces |

The temperature of the metal and the temperature of the mold are precisely controlled: if the metal is too cold, it does not fill the details; If it is too hot, porosities and rough surfaces appear.

## Demoulding and finishing

1. **Cooled:** After a few minutes, the cylinder is immersed in water and the coating falls apart.
2. **Cleaning:** The remains of the coating are removed with pressurized water and the oxides are removed with a stripping bath.
3. **Cutting sprues:** each piece is separated from the tree.
4. **Filing and sanding:** sprue marks and small imperfections are removed.
5. **Polishing:** with brushes, wheels and pastes, until the desired shine; some pieces receive matte finishes or textures.
6. **Setting:** The stones are placed by hand in the settings provided in the design.
7. **Plating and hallmarking:** White gold is usually rhodium-plated, and precious metal pieces are marked according to regulations, as we explain in the guide on [hallmarks and guarantee marks] (/es/contrastes-y-marcas-de-garantia/).

## Frequent defects

| Defect | How it looks | Usual cause |
|---|---|---|
| Porosity | small holes on the surface or inside | trapped gases, overheated metal, or poor shrinkage |
| Incomplete pieces | missing areas or rounded edges | cold metal, insufficient sprues or cold mold |
| Burrs | thin fin-shaped metal sheets | cracks in the coating |
| metal balls | small spheres glued to the piece | air bubbles in the coating |
| rough surface | grainy texture | poorly prepared coating or inappropriate temperatures |
| Inclusions | plating spots caught in the metal | loose remains in the mold |

A piece with porosity may seem perfect until it is polished: the pores then appear as small craters that cannot be eliminated without filling them, and weaken areas such as the rings of the rings or the claws of the settings.

## Casting with stones

In some cases, certain heat-resistant stones are placed in the wax before casting and are set when the metal is poured. It saves labor in large productions, but it is only possible with stones that withstand high temperatures, and even then it carries risks of damage or color changes. Heat-sensitive gems, such as emeralds, opals or pearls, are never fused with the piece: they are set later, by hand.

## Cast, handmade or stamped

| Technique | Features |
|---|---|
| Foundry | great freedom of shapes and faithful reproduction of details; quality depends on execution |
| hand work | hammered, cut and welded sheet metal and wire; very dense metal and artisanal character |
| Stamping | die-cut pieces with presses; compact metal and mass production |

None is better in all cases. A ring cast with good technique and good finish can be excellent; a poorly welded handmade one can break. What matters is the quality of the workmanship, the thickness of the metal and the finish.

## How to recognize a good foundry

- **Check the surface with a magnifying glass:** no pores, craters or rough areas on the polished parts.
- **Look inside the rings** and the less visible areas, where the finish is sometimes neglected.
- **Check the claws and settings:** they must be firm and of sufficient thickness.
- **Assess the weight:** a piece that is very light for its size may have walls that are too thin.
- **Look for the contrast** with the metal law and the manufacturer's mark.

## Safety for fans

Anyone who wants to cast in their own workshop should know that the coating contains silica, the dust of which should not be breathed when mixing; that furnaces and molten metal require ventilation, face protection, appropriate gloves and natural fiber clothing; and that work should never be done in a hurry or in unprepared domestic spaces. It is best to start in a workshop or school with equipment and supervision.

## Frequent errors

- **Thinking that melted means poorer quality.**
- **Buy pieces with visible porosity** in polished areas.
- **Choose very fine settings** for important stones.
- **Melt heat sensitive stones** with the piece.
- **Mix coating without mask.**

## Frequently asked questions

### What is microfusion?

It is the name given in jewelry to lost wax casting applied to small, detailed pieces.

### Is a cast jewel less valuable than a handmade one?

Not necessarily. It depends on the design, the metal, the thickness and, above all, the quality of the execution and finish.

### Can jewelry be 3D printed?

Yes. The usual thing is to print the model in castable resin and cast it later. There are also direct metal printing techniques, less widespread in jewelry.

### Why do pores appear in a piece of jewelry?

Due to trapped gases, inadequate temperatures or poorly controlled contraction during metal solidification.

## In summary

Lost wax casting, or microcasting, starts with a wax or resin model that is mounted on a tree, wrapped in refractory lining, removed with heat, and replaced with molten metal using centrifuge, vacuum, or pressure. It is then cleaned, the sprues are cut, filed, polished, set and marked. Its most common defects are porosity, incomplete parts and burrs. A cast piece is no worse than a handmade one: it's the execution that counts. When purchasing, check the surface with a magnifying glass, the settings, the weight and the contrast.
