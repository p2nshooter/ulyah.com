In the shop windows of the historic center of Toledo, hundreds of pieces shine with the same unmistakable contrast: a deep black background on which arabesques, flowers, birds and gold fretwork are drawn. It is damascene, a technique that embeds gold and silver threads in a steel or iron surface, and which in Spain has two proper names: Toledo, with its long tradition of swordsmiths and craftsmen, and Éibar, the Gipuzkoan gunsmith town where the technique experienced an extraordinary renaissance in the 19th century.

In this guide you will see what damascene is and where its name comes from, why it should not be confused with Damascus steel, how it is made step by step, what role Toledo and Eibar played in its history, what objects and jewelry are made today, how to distinguish a handmade piece from an imitation and how to care for it so that the steel does not rust or the gold is scratched.

## What is damascene

Damascene is a decorative technique that involves embedding threads or sheets of a soft, precious metal, usually gold or silver, on the surface of a harder metal, such as steel or iron. The background is then darkened so that the precious metal stands out. The name comes from Damascus, a city in Syria famous in the Middle Ages for its decorated metal work, from where the technique spread throughout the Mediterranean.

On the peninsula, the inlay of precious metals in iron and steel was practiced in Andalusian times and was applied for centuries to weapons, armor and luxury objects.

## It is not Damascus steel

It is advisable not to confuse two things with similar names:

| Concept | What is it |
|---|---|
| Damascene | gold or silver decoration embedded in the surface of a piece of steel or iron |
| Damascus steel | steel with its own wavy pattern, which is obtained by the structure of the material or by forging together layers of different steels; does not contain precious metals |

Knife blades with wavy patterns are Damascus or ply steel; the black plates and brooches with gold patterns are damascened.

## How to do it, step by step

The traditional Spanish damascene is made on a mild steel base and with fine gold or silver thread, which due to its softness adheres to the background when hammered.

1. **Prepare the piece:** the steel base is cut and shaped, be it a plate, a brooch or an object.
2. **Chop the surface:** With a sharp blade and a small hammer, the craftsman scratches the entire area that will have decoration with very fine lines crossed in various directions. That microscopic texture is what will hold the gold.
3. **Trace the drawing:** the motif is marked on the pitted surface.
4. **Embed the thread:** The gold or silver thread is placed following the drawing and struck with a punch so that it penetrates the scratching and remains firmly anchored. Large areas are covered with sheets or closely spaced threads.
5. **Pavonar:** the piece undergoes a chemical and thermal treatment that darkens the steel to an intense black, while the gold retains its color.
6. **Chiseling:** with burins and fine chisels, details such as leaf veins, feathers or textures are engraved on the gold.
7. **Burnish and finish:** the shine of the gold is enhanced and the whole is protected.

A small piece can require many hours of work, and complex designs can require days. The regularity of the cut, the precision of the thread and the fineness of the chiseling are the marks of a good damascene cutter.

## Toledo: swords, arabesques and memories

Toledo was for centuries a great center for sword manufacturing, and the decoration of hilts, fittings and blades was part of that craft. The Toledo damascene is associated with motifs of Mudejar and Renaissance inspiration: laces, arabesques, stylized flowers and scenes with birds, on a black background.

Today artisan workshops that maintain the complete technique coexist in the city with souvenir shops where imitations abound. In some workshops you can see the artisans at work, which is the best way to understand the technique and learn to recognize a well-made piece.

## Éibar: gunsmiths and a renaissance

Éibar, in Gipuzkoa, is a town with an enormous tradition in the manufacture of weapons. In the 19th century, the Zuloaga family promoted the recovery of damascening: Eusebio Zuloaga, gunsmith, and especially his son Plácido Zuloaga, who took the technique to an exceptional artistic level with monumental pieces and luxury objects that were presented in international exhibitions. Plácido was also the father of the painter Ignacio Zuloaga.

The Eibar tradition is distinguished by its enormous variety of designs and its technical quality, and during the 20th century the town maintained numerous workshops. The Museum of the Arms Industry of Éibar allows us to know the context of that history, in which weapons, mechanical precision and decoration went hand in hand.

## Styles and motifs

Learning to recognize the motifs helps to appreciate the pieces and distinguish careful work from repetitive work.

| Family of motifs | Traits |
|---|---|
| Lacework | geometric intertwining of Andalusian and Mudejar tradition, with stars and polygons |
| Ataurique | stylized plant decoration, with curling stems and leaves |
| Renaissance | scrolls, grotesques, vases and fantastic figures |
| Wildlife | birds, deer and other animals among branches |
| Scenes | landscapes, monuments or literary characters, very common in souvenir pieces |
| Geometric | fretwork, borders and bands that frame the drawing |

In addition to gold, many pieces combine silver threads to create two-tone contrasts, and some artisans use gold of different colors in the same composition. In quality pieces, the black background is deep and uniform, without grayish areas, and the gold lines maintain the same thickness in curves and straight lines. The borders and edges, where it is most difficult to work, usually reveal the hand of a good damascene cutter.

## Handmade or imitation

| Trait | Handcrafted piece | Usual imitation |
|---|---|---|
| gold relief | slightly embossed and textured to the touch | flat, like a print |
| Stroke | small irregularities typical of manual work | perfectly uniform and repeated lines |
| chiseled details | ribs and textures etched in gold | absent or simulated with ink |
| Base | steel, which attracts a magnet | often brass or non-magnetic alloys |
| Price | according to working hours | suspiciously low |

Not all cheap parts are scams: there are products made with industrial techniques that are honestly sold as such. The problem is paying a craft price for a decal. Always ask how the piece was made, what metal it uses and who made it, and ask for it to appear on the invoice.

## How to care for a damask piece

- **Keep dry:** The steel base can rust with moisture and sweat.
- **Clean it with a soft, dry cloth,** without polishing products or metal cleaners.
- **Avoid abrasives:** fine gold is very soft and scratches easily.
- **Store it separately** in a cloth bag or case, away from the humidity of bathrooms and kitchens.
- **If rust stains appear,** consult the workshop before attempting to remove them.
- **Follow the artisan's instructions** on protecting the steel, because some recommend a very thin layer of wax.

## Frequent errors

- **Confuse damascene with Damascus steel.**
- **Buy printed imitations** believing they are handmade.
- **Clean with metal cleaner** that damage the bluing.
- **Store parts in humid places.**
- **Do not ask about the base and metal** of the inlay.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does damascene work have real gold?

In handcrafted pieces, yes: fine gold or silver thread is used. Imitations can use paints, inks or foils without precious metal.

### How do I know if a piece is authentic?

Run your finger over the drawing: in artisanal damascening the gold has relief and texture. Also look at the chiseled details with a magnifying glass and check that the base is made of steel.

### Why is the background black?

Because the steel is blued, a treatment that darkens and protects it, and makes the gold stand out.

### Can you wear damascene jewelry every day?

Yes, with care: avoid humidity, intense sweat and bumps, and store it dry after use.

## In summary

Damascene embeds gold or silver threads on chopped steel with fine cross lines, which are then blued to a deep black and chiseled. It has nothing to do with Damascus steel. In Spain, Toledo links it to its sword-making tradition and Mudejar and Renaissance motifs, and Éibar experienced a renaissance of the technique in the 19th century at the hands of the Zuloaga family. To recognize a handcrafted piece, look for gold relief, chiseled details and a steel base, and be wary of prices that are too low. Keep it dry and without abrasive products.
