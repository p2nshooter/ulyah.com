For centuries, the word ruby ​​was used to name almost any red stone: spinels, garnets and even colored glass appear as rubies in royal inventories and cathedral treasures. Today the term has a precise meaning, and behind it is one of the most coveted and, at the same time, most treated and imitated gems on the market. A quality ruby ​​can be worth a fortune; A red stone sold as a ruby ​​may be worth almost nothing, and at first glance the difference is not always evident.

In this guide you will see what exactly a ruby ​​is and how it differs from a pink sapphire, what its color depends on and what the famous expression "pigeon's blood" means, what its inclusions mean, where rubies come from, what treatments are common and which ones radically change the value, how synthetics and imitations are recognized and what to ask for before paying for one.

## What is ruby

Ruby is the red variety of corundum, an aluminum oxide. The color comes from small amounts of chromium that replace aluminum in the glass structure; Iron, when present, can darken the tone and dull the red glow.

| Property | ruby |
|---|---|
| mineral species | corundum |
| Composition | aluminum oxide with chromium |
| Hardness on the Mohs scale | 9, only below diamond |
| Tenacity | good, without exfoliation, although it may have separation planes |
| Brightness | vitreous to subadamantine |
| Fluorescence | often red under ultraviolet light, more intense the less iron it has |
| Pleochroism | shows two shades of red depending on the viewing direction |

Its hardness and good resistance make it a very suitable gem for daily use, even in rings.

## Ruby or pink sapphire

Corundum appears in almost all colors. When it is red it is called ruby, and when it has any other color it is called sapphire: blue, yellow, pink, violet, green. The border between a light ruby ​​and a pink sapphire is not set by a universal standard: it depends on hue and saturation, and laboratories and markets in different countries apply somewhat different criteria. A deep pink stone may be called a ruby ​​in one report and a pink sapphire in another. Therefore, in important pieces, the name that appears on the laboratory report is the one that counts. Sapphires have their own guide: that of [sapphire](/es/zafiro/).

## Color, decisive factor

In ruby, color weighs more than any other quality factor. It is valued:

| Appearance | What are you looking for? |
|---|---|
| Tone | pure red or slightly purple, neither too light nor so dark that it appears black |
| Saturation | intense and lively, without brown nuances |
| Distribution | uniform, without colorless areas or very marked bands |
| Brightness | that the carving returns the light and the red fluorescence enlivens the color |

"Pigeon's blood" is a commercial expression that designates rubies of a very intense and pure red, with a slight bluish hue. It is not a scientific category, and its use depends on the criteria of each laboratory or merchant. Some laboratories use it in their reports with their own criteria; others don't. A seller who uses it without the support of a report is advertising, not a description.

## Inclusions: the imprint of the ruby

Almost all natural rubies have inclusions, and far from always being a defect, they are its fingerprint:

- **Silk:** very fine rutile needles that can give a silky appearance and soften the color.
- **Crystals included:** small minerals trapped during growth.
- **Fingerprints:** Healed fissures that look like a veil or a fingerprint.
- **Color zoning:** Straight or angular bands that follow the shape of the crystal.

A ruby ​​completely clean to the naked eye is rare, and one completely clean under magnification should suggest a synthetic until a laboratory proves otherwise. When the silk is abundant and well oriented, the cabochon cut ruby ​​may show asterism: a six-pointed star that slides over the surface when moved.

## Where are rubies found?

| Region | General features |
|---|---|
| Myanmar | historical origin of the most famous rubies, such as those from the Mogok Valley |
| Mozambique | one of the great current origins of the market |
| Thailand and Cambodia | rubies from basaltic rocks, often darker due to their iron content; Thailand is also a great center of carving and trade. |
| Sri Lanka | rubies and pink sapphires of lighter shades |
| Madagascar, Tanzania and Kenya | African deposits of very varied qualities |
| Afghanistan, Tajikistan and Vietnam | rubies of marbles, of vivid color |

Geographic origin influences the price, especially in important stones, but it is a technical opinion that can only be issued by specialized laboratories based on inclusions and chemical composition. No seller can determine it at a glance.

## Ruby treatments

Most rubies on the market have received some treatment. The important thing is to know which one, because not all of them have the same effect on value or durability.

| Treatment | What does it consist of? | What does it imply? |
|---|---|---|
| Warm-up | The stone is subjected to high temperatures to improve color and reduce silkiness | very common and stable; must be declared |
| Heating with fluxes | During heating, a flux fills and heals cracks | leaves residue in the cracks; the report usually indicates its amount |
| Filled with lead glass | Fractures are filled with a lead-rich glass that hides cracks and improves transparency | radically changes the value; The stone is fragile to heat and some chemicals; must always be declared |
| Diffusion | Elements are introduced on the surface or throughout the stone to modify the color | rare in ruby and of much lower value |
| Dye and oils | They are applied to low quality material with cracks | short lasting effect |

Heavily glass-filled rubies are sometimes sold under names such as "composite ruby." A piece of jewelry with one of these stones cannot be repaired with a blowtorch or cleaned with aggressive products without risk of damaging it. Always demand that any treatment appear in writing on the invoice.

## synthetic rubies

The ruby was one of the first gems successfully manufactured in a laboratory. In the early 20th century, French chemist Auguste Verneuil perfected a still-used flame fusion method that produced synthetic rubies at low cost. Then came the flux and hydrothermal methods, which produce stones that are more similar to natural ones.

| Method | Indications that a gemologist looks for |
|---|---|
| Flame fusion | curved growth lines and gas bubbles |
| Flux | remains of flux that look like veils or drops |
| Hydrothermal | characteristic growth structures |

A synthetic ruby has the same composition and hardness as a natural one, and it is not a scam if it is sold as such. What is not acceptable is presenting it as natural. Some modern synthetics are difficult to identify even for professionals, so, in valuable stones, a laboratory report is essential.

## Imitations and confusions

| material | How it differs |
|---|---|
| red spinel | natural gem of great value, of simple refraction; was confused with ruby for centuries |
| Garnet | darker red or orange, without pleochroism and with different optical properties |
| Red tourmaline or rubellite | lighter, with strong pleochroism and other inclusions |
| glass | bubbles, mold marks and lower hardness |
| Doubles | a crown of natural corundum glued on a synthetic or glass base |

Some of these stones, such as spinel, are magnificent gems in their own right; The problem only arises when they are sold under another name. We see it in the guides on [spinel](/es/espinela/) and [garnets](/es/granates/).

## What to ask before buying

1. **A written description** that says "natural ruby" or "synthetic ruby" and states the treatments.
2. **A recognized laboratory report** for stones of a certain value, with identification and treatments.
3. **The possibility of verifying the report,** for example on the laboratory website.
4. **Return conditions** in writing, in case a second opinion contradicts the seller.
5. **Calm down:** be wary of “today only” offers and prices well below the market.

To understand the value of an already purchased piece, consult the guide on [professional appraisal](/es/tasacion-profesional/).

## Ruby care

- **Ruby without fillers:** cleans with warm water, neutral soap and a soft brush. It withstands daily use well, although a strong blow can chip it.
- **Glass Filled Ruby:** Only mild soap and water, no ultrasound, steam or chemicals, and notifying the jeweler before any repairs.
- **Check the settings** periodically to avoid losing the stone.
- **Keep it separately** because it may scratch other gems and other pieces.

## Frequent errors

- **Relying on expressions like “pigeon blood”** without any report to support them.
- **Buying a glass filled ruby** believing it is only heated.
- **Thinking that a ruby ​​without inclusions** is necessarily better.
- **Accept the seller's opinion of origin.**
- **Ultrasonic cleaning** a stone with fillers.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a heated ruby worth less?

In general, an unheated natural ruby ​​of comparable quality pays more, but heating is a common, stable and accepted treatment if stated.

### How do I know if my ruby ​​is filled with glass?

Sometimes bubbles or a distinct shine can be seen in the fissures under magnification, but reliable identification is the responsibility of a gemologist or laboratory.

### Is a synthetic ruby ​​a bad buy?

No, if it is purchased as synthetic and at a synthetic price. It is the same substance as natural ruby, but its value is much lower.

### What is the difference between a ruby ​​and a red spinel?

They are different minerals. Spinel has simple refraction and does not show pleochroism, among other differences that a gemologist easily detects.

## In summary

Ruby is the red variety of corundum, colored by chromium, with hardness 9 and good resistance for daily use. Its value depends mainly on color, and expressions like "pigeon blood" only mean something with a report to back them up. Almost all rubies have some treatment: heating is common and accepted, but filling with lead glass completely changes the value and requires special care. Synthetics have existed for more than a century and many imitations. Before purchasing, require a written description, a recognized laboratory report for valuable stones and return conditions.
