A faceted gem is, at its core, a small optical instrument. Each facet is cut at a calculated angle and orientation so that light entering through the top bounces back inside and back out to the viewer's eye. If the angles are correct, the stone shines; If they are not, the light escapes through the bottom and the gem looks dull, with a transparent window in the center or dark areas that no amount of polishing can fix.

In this guide you will see how a modern faceting machine works, what a faceting diagram is and how to read it, why the angles depend on each material, what the meeting points are and why they obsess over faceters, what the complete process from pavilion to crown is, what discs and polishes are used, what the most common mistakes are and how to get started if you want to learn.

## The parts of a faceted gem

| Part | What is it |
|---|---|
| Table | the large, flat facet of the top |
| Crown | the upper part, between the table and the rondís |
| Rondis | the perimeter edge that separates the crown and pavilion; It is the area that holds the setting |
| Pavilion | the lower part, responsible above all for reflecting light |
| collet | the point or small facet at the lower end of the pavilion |

The crown disperses the light and gives it life; the pavilion returns it upwards. That is why the angles of the pavilion are the ones that most influence the brightness.

## The faceting machine

A modern machine does not carve alone: it is a precision tool that allows the faceter to repeat angles and positions exactly.

| Component | Function |
|---|---|
| rotating disk | flat sheet where the stone is cut and polished |
| Mast | vertical support that supports the arm and allows height adjustment |
| Arm or head | Hold the dop with the stone and tilt it to the desired angle |
| angle protractor | indicates the inclination of each facet with respect to the disk |
| indexing wheel | gear with a fixed number of teeth, often 96, marking the turning position of each facet |
| Stop | stops cutting at desired depth |
| dripping water | cools and drags waste during cutting |
| transfer block | allows you to turn the stone from one dop to another while maintaining alignment |

Before modern machines, and still today in some traditional workshops, carving was done with a peg system on a post with holes, which requires enormous skill and a very trained eye.

## What is a faceted diagram

A diagram is the recipe for a carving. It shows the drawing of the stone seen from above, from below and in profile, and a table with each group of facets.

| Diagram column | What does it indicate? |
|---|---|
| Facet | facet group name or number |
| Angle | inclination at which the arm should be positioned |
| Indices | indexing wheel positions where each facet of the group is cut |
| Instructions | cut to the center, to a meeting point, to the rondís, etc. |

The diagrams also indicate the refractive index for which they have been calculated and often the cutting order. There are libraries of diagrams shared by amateurs and professionals, and ray-traced design programs, such as GemCad, that allow you to simulate how light will behave before hitting the stone.

## Why angles depend on the material

When light hits a facet of the pavilion from inside the stone, it is reflected upward if the angle of incidence exceeds a value called the critical angle; If it does not exceed it, the light passes through the facet and escapes. That critical angle depends on the refractive index of each gem.

| refractive index | Examples | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Low | quartz, beryl | needs more inclined pavilions to avoid windows |
| Medium | tourmaline, topaz, spinel | intermediate margin |
| High | corundum, garnet, zircon | admits somewhat less inclined pavilions and returns more light |

That's why the same design can work on one gem and fail on another, and many diagrams include variants for different materials. The angles of the crown also influence the dispersion, the play of colored flashes.

## Meeting points: the key to precision

In a well-made carving, the facets meet exactly at single points, without small parasitic facets or mismatched lines. Achieving this depends not only on cutting at the correct angle, but also on cutting each facet to the exact point. Modern diagrams are usually designed to work by meeting points: each facet is cut until it touches a point already established by the previous ones, so that precision is built step by step.

Clean meeting points are not just an aesthetic issue: they indicate that the angles and positions are consistent and, therefore, that the stone will return light as intended.

## The pavilion to crown process

1. **Choose the rough:** clean in the area that will be used, without cracks that cross the future gem.
2. **Orient:** decide where the table will go taking into account color and pleochroism. In tourmaline, for example, a poor orientation darkens the stone; We explain it in the guide on [tourmaline](/es/turmalina/).
3. **Preshaping:** roughing the rough on a wheel to an approximate shape.
4. **Assemble on the dop:** glue the stone centered, with the future table facing the dop.
5. **Cut the pavilion:** with a thick disc to give the shape, following the diagram and establishing the meeting points.
6. **Pre-polish and polish the pavilion:** with increasingly finer discs and polishes.
7. **Transfer:** Using the transfer block, stick the stone to another dop across the pavilion, maintaining alignment.
8. **Cut and polish the crown:** following the same order, with special care in the rondís.
9. **Cutting and polishing the table:** often with a special adapter.
10. **Disassemble and clean:** remove the adhesive and check the stone with a magnifying glass.

## Discs and polishes

| Phase | Common discs and abrasives |
|---|---|
| Court | coarse to medium grit diamond discs |
| Pre-polished | fine grain diamond discs |
| Polished | discs made of tin, ceramic, composites or soft metals, with very fine diamond paste or oxides |

Each material has combinations that work best. Quartz, for example, is usually polished well with cerium oxide, while corundum requires diamond. Finding the right combination is part of the craft, and faceting associations share many of those recipes.

## Common mistakes when faceting

| Error | Effect |
|---|---|
| Little inclined pavilion | window: light escapes and the center is transparent |
| Pavilion too inclined | dark zones of extinction |
| Misaligned meeting points | parasitic facets and neglected appearance |
| Bad transfer | crown rotated with respect to the pavilion |
| irregular rondis | makes setting difficult and weakens the stone |
| Excessive polishing | rounded edges and facets that lose sharpness |
| Residual stripes | tarnished shine |

## Precision carving and native carving

In many producing countries, gems are cut to retain as much weight as possible, because they are sold by the carat. The result is deep pavilions, windows or irregular shapes: this is what is called native carving. Precision carving, on the other hand, prioritizes optical performance. Recutting a native stone can greatly improve its appearance, at the cost of losing weight, and the decision depends on the value of the gem and its destination.

## How to get started

- **Start with quartz:** It's cheap, has adequate hardness, and polishes well.
- **Choose simple designs,** such as the standard round brilliant, before moving on to complex cuts.
- **Learn with someone:** a club, a workshop or a course correct mistakes from the beginning.
- **Take care of the machine:** Accuracy depends on the blade being flat and the neck properly adjusted.
- **Always work with water and glasses,** as in any lapidary task. General precautions are explained in the guide on [how to cut a cabochon](/es/como-se-talla-un-cabujon/).

## Frequent errors

- **Use a diagram without checking** what refractive index it is calculated for.
- **Skip the meeting points** by relying only on the transporter.
- **Transfer in a hurry.**
- **Polish with the wrong polish** for the material.
- **Start with expensive stones.**

## Frequently asked questions

### How many facets does a round brilliant have?

The standard round brilliant has 57 facets, or 58 if you count the collet as a facet.

### Why does my gem have a window in the center?

Because the angles of the pavilion are too low for its refractive index and the light escapes through the bottom instead of being reflected upwards.

### Can any stone be faceted?

Technically, many; but opaque or highly included stones do not gain anything from faceting, and some soft or fragile stones are very difficult to facet with good results.

### What is a meeting point?

The exact point where several facets coincide. Cutting to these points ensures that the carving is accurate and symmetrical.

## In summary

Faceting is cutting and polishing flat faces with angles and positions calculated so that light reflects within the gem and returns to the eye. The faceting machine allows these angles to be repeated precisely thanks to the protractor, indexing wheel and transfer block, and the diagrams indicate the angle and indices of each facet. The angles depend on the refractive index, and the meeting points are the basis of accurate carving. The process goes from pavilion to crown, with increasingly finer discs and polishes. Start with quartz and simple designs, and learn in company.
