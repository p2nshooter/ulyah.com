No other gemstone offers a palette like that of tourmaline. There are red, pink, green, blue, yellow, brown, black and colorless; There are some with two or three colors in the same glass, like a slice of watermelon, and there are some with an electric greenish blue that seems illuminated from within. This variety has meant that for centuries it was confused with other stones, from ruby ​​to emerald, and it is still one of the favorite gems of designers and collectors today.

In this guide you will see what tourmaline is, where its name comes from and why it was confused with the emerald in colonial Brazil, what are its best-known varieties, what is special about Paraíba tourmaline, how pleochroism influences the cut, why this gem generates electricity, what treatments and imitations exist and how to choose and care for a good piece.

## What is tourmaline

Tourmaline is not a mineral, but a group of complex borosilicates with the same structure and variable compositions. The species that gives the most colored tourmalines is elbaite; plover, black tourmaline, is the most common in nature.

| Property | Tourmaline |
|---|---|
| Composition | complex aluminum borosilicates with sodium, lithium, iron, magnesium and other elements |
| crystalline system | trigonal |
| usual way | elongated prisms, striated along the length, with a rounded triangular section |
| Hardness on the Mohs scale | between 7 and 7.5 |
| Exfoliation | little marked; can be fragile |
| Pleochroism | marked: color changes depending on direction |
| Electrical properties | pyroelectric and piezoelectric |

## A Sri Lankan name and a Brazilian confusion

The word tourmaline comes from Sinhalese, the language of Sri Lanka, where a similar term was used to name stones of various colors that arrived mixed in merchants' lots. European merchants adopted it without knowing exactly what it meant.

In Brazil, at the end of the 17th century, expeditionaries searching for emeralds in the interior found green and transparent stones that they took for them. They were tourmalines. The confusion lasted for decades, until mineralogy allowed them to be differentiated. Today Brazil is one of the largest producers of tourmaline in the world, along with several African countries, Afghanistan, Pakistan and the United States.

## Paraíba Tourmaline

At the end of the 1980s, a tourmaline of an intense greenish blue, almost neon, appeared in the Brazilian state of Paraíba, unlike anything known. Its color is due to copper, an unusual element in tourmaline. Its scarcity quickly made it one of the most sought-after colored gems.

Years later, similar-looking copper-bearing tourmalines were found in Nigeria and Mozambique. In commerce, the name Paraíba is often applied to all of them, regardless of origin, following criteria that some laboratories have adopted. That is why it is important to distinguish two things: that a tourmaline is "Paraíba type" due to its color and copper content, and that it comes from Brazil, which is a separate opinion of origin. With stones of this type, a report from a recognized laboratory is essential.

## Bicolors and watermelon

During its growth, the composition of the fluids changes and the tourmaline registers this in the form of color zones. The two-tone ones show two tones along the length of the glass, for example pink at one end and green at the other. Watermelon tourmalines have a pink core and a green edge, and are cut into transverse slices that show that concentric pattern. They are highly appreciated pieces for their unique character.

## Pleochroism and size

In many tourmalines, the color is darker when viewed along the axis of the prism than perpendicular. If the lapidary orients the stone incorrectly, the table may be in the dark direction and the gem may appear almost black. This is why green and blue tourmalines are usually cut with the table parallel to the axis of the crystal, often into elongated shapes such as rectangles or emeralds. When buying, turn the stone: a good cut shows the most beautiful color from the front. We explain it in the guide on [machine faceting and diagrams](/es/facetado-maquina-y-diagramas/).

## An electric gem

Tourmaline is pyroelectric: when heated or cooled, its ends acquire opposite electrical charges. It is also piezoelectric: it generates charge when subjected to pressure. In the 18th century, Dutch merchants noticed that tourmalines heated in the sun or by a fire attracted ash and dust, and they used them to clean their pipes. Today this property has a practical consequence: a tourmaline exposed under shop window spotlights can attract dust more easily.

## Where is it formed

Tourmaline needs boron, a scarce element that is concentrated in the last fluids left when a granitic magma crystallizes. That is why gem-quality tourmalines appear mainly in pegmatites, giant crystal rocks that form from these residual fluids. In some pegmatites, cavities open, called pockets, where the crystals grew freely and preserve perfect faces and terminations; The large collector's specimens from Brazil, Afghanistan, Pakistan, Madagascar and the western United States come from them.

Tourmaline also forms in metamorphic rocks, such as some marbles and schists, where it is usually brown or black. And it appears included in other minerals: tourmaline quartz, pierced by fine black needles, is a very popular material for cabochons, beads and decorative objects, with a different drawing on each piece.

## Tourmaline in Spain

In the pegmatites and granites of Galicia, Extremadura or the Central System, among other areas, black tourmaline or plover is relatively common, sometimes in long prisms of interest to collectors. Gem-quality colored tourmalines, on the other hand, are not part of Spanish production. If you are interested in the country's minerals, consult the guide on [minerals of Spain](/es/minerales-de-espana/).

## Treatments

| Treatment | What does it consist of? | What does it imply? |
|---|---|---|
| Warm-up | lightens very dark green or blue tourmalines and adjusts the color of some tourmalines with copper | habitual and stable; must be declared |
| Irradiation | intensifies pinks and reds | common in rubellites; generally stable |
| Crack filling | resins that disguise fractures | common in material with cracks; reduces durability and value |

Synthetic tourmaline has been obtained in the laboratory for research purposes, but has no relevant commercial presence. Imitations are, above all, glass and other synthetic gems of similar colors.

## How to choose a tourmaline

1. **Look at the color from the front and from the side:** check that the size does not leave the dark direction on the table.
2. **Compare with daylight and warm light:** some rubellites turn brownish tones in artificial light.
3. **Check the inclusions:** in rubellites and watermelons they are normal; in greens and blues cleaner stones are expected.
4. **Ask about fillers,** especially in stones with visible cracks.
5. **Requires a report** for copper tourmalines, quality indicolites and valuable stones.

## Tourmaline care

- **Cleaning:** warm water, neutral soap and a soft brush.
- **No ultrasound or steam:** they can aggravate cracks and damage fillings.
- **Be careful with heat:** sudden changes in temperature can fracture it; Inform the jeweler before any torch repair.
- **Shocks:** Its hardness is good, but it can chip; in rings, better protective settings.
- **Storage:** separately, away from harder stones.

## Frequent errors

- **Choose a dark tourmaline** without rotating it to see its best color.
- **Pay Paraíba price** without a report confirming the copper.
- **Confuse "Paraíba type"** with Brazilian origin.
- **Clean with ultrasound** stones with fissures or fillings.
- **Expose tourmalines to sudden changes in temperature.**

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does tourmaline have so many colors?

Because its composition is very variable and admits many different elements, such as iron, manganese, copper or chromium, which produce different colors that change during the growth of the crystal.

### What is a watermelon tourmaline?

A tourmaline with a pink core and a green edge, which is cut into slices to show that pattern.

### Does all Paraíba tourmaline come from Paraíba?

No. The name also applies to tourmalines with copper of similar color from Nigeria or Mozambique. The Brazilian origin is a separate opinion that must be stated in the report.

### Is tourmaline suitable for an everyday ring?

Yes, with precautions: it has good hardness, but it can chip when hit and is sensitive to heat, so a protective setting is advisable.

## In summary

Tourmaline is a group of borosilicates with an unmatched range of colors: rubellite, indicolite, greenlite, plover, dravite, bicolor, watermelon and the copper tourmalines known as Paraíba. Its name comes from Sri Lanka, and in colonial Brazil it was confused with the emerald. Its marked pleochroism conditions its size, and its electrical properties make it attract dust when heated. It is heated, irradiated and sometimes filled, but it has almost no commercial synthetics. To choose it, look at its color from the front and with various lights, ask about the fillings and request information on the valuable stones.
