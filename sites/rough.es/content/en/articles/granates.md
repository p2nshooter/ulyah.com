For many people, garnet is a dark red stone, somewhat old-fashioned, typical of grandmother's brooches. It is a very unfair image. Garnets are a family of minerals with a stunning range of colors, from wine red to tangerine orange, emerald green to raspberry pink, and include some of the brightest and rarest gemstones in existence. They are almost never treated, they are resistant and have a history in jewelry that goes back thousands of years, with a very prominent chapter in the Iberian Peninsula.

In this guide you will see which species make up the garnet group, which varieties have their own names, what properties they share, why the Hoyazo de Níjar garnets, in Almería, are a unique geological case, what role they played in Visigoth jewelry, what imitations exist and how to choose and care for a good garnet.

## A group, not a stone

Garnet is not the name of a mineral, but of a group of silicates with the same structure and different compositions. The most important species in jewelry are these:

| Species | Basic composition | Common colors |
|---|---|---|
| Almandine | iron and aluminum silicate | dark red, violet red |
| Compliment | magnesium aluminum silicate | intense red, sometimes almost without brown nuances |
| Spessartine | manganese aluminum silicate | orange, from yellowish to reddish |
| Grossular | calcium aluminum silicate | green, orange, yellow, colorless |
| Andradite | calcium iron silicate | green, yellow, brown, black |
| Uvarovite | calcium chromium silicate | intense green, in tiny crystals |

In nature, most garnets are mixtures of several species, so commercial variety names are often more useful than species names.

## Varieties with their own name

| Variety | What is it | Traits |
|---|---|---|
| Rhodolite | mixture of pirope and almandine | raspberry pink to violet red, luminous |
| Tsavorite | green grossular | deep green from vanadium or chromium; usually found in small sizes |
| Hessonite | orange grossular | cinnamon and honey tones, with a wavy interior appearance |
| Demantoid | green andradite | light dispersion superior to that of diamond; some have horsetail-shaped fibrous inclusions |
| mandarin garnet | vivid orange spessartine | one of the most intense oranges in gemology |
| color change garnet | piropo and spessartine mixtures | change from teal or gray to purple or red depending on the light |
| star garnet | almandine with oriented inclusions | four or six pointed star in cabochon |

## Common properties

| Property | Garnets in general |
|---|---|
| Hardness on the Mohs scale | between 6.5 and 7.5 depending on the species |
| Exfoliation | absent |
| Refraction | simple: without pleochroism |
| Brightness | vitreous to subadamantine; very intense in the demantoid |
| Treatments | very rare |
| Shape of the crystals | dodecahedra and trapezohedrons, very characteristic |

## The garnets of Nijar

In the southeast of the peninsula, within the Cabo de Gata-Níjar Natural Park, is the Hoyazo de Níjar, a small volcanic structure from the Miocene, partly surrounded by fossil coral reefs. The volcanic rocks of Hoyazo dragged fragments of metamorphic rocks rich in almandine garnet from the depths, and erosion gradually released the garnets, which accumulated in the sands and boulevards of the area.

In the past, these sands were exploited to obtain garnet for abrasives, such as sandpaper. Níjar garnets are, in general, small and dark, with mineralogical rather than gemological interest, but they tell an extraordinary geological story: they are messengers of rocks that were dozens of kilometers deep.

Cabo de Gata-Níjar is also a natural park and UNESCO world geopark, so collecting minerals is prohibited. To learn about the garnets in the area, the best options are visitor centers and museums. ## Garnets in the history of jewelry

Red garnets have been used since ancient times in rings, pendants and engraved pieces. In the Iberian Peninsula, one of the most brilliant chapters is that of goldsmithing from the Visigothic period: the eagle-shaped fibulae, which are preserved in museums such as the National Archaeological Museum of Madrid, combine metal cells filled with red stones, often garnets carved into thin sheets, with colored glass. It is the technique known as tipping, which made the red of the stones shine on metal sheets.

Centuries later, in the 19th century, the small Bohemian compliments, set in tight clusters, were the protagonists of a fashion that spread throughout Europe. In ancient inventories, furthermore, cabochon-cut red garnets often appear under the generic name of carbuncles, and very dark almandines had their backs hollowed out to allow more light to pass through. If you are attracted to antique jewelry, you will find more in the guide on [vintage jewelry and styles](/es/joyeria-de-epoca-estilos/).

## Much more than a gem

Garnets have a very active life outside of jewelry. Their hardness and the way they break into angular fragments make them excellent as an abrasive: ground garnet is used in sandpaper, in surface blasting and in waterjet cutting machines, which cut steel, stone or glass with a stream of pressurized water loaded with garnet particles.

For geologists, furthermore, garnet is an archive. Its composition changes depending on the pressure and temperature at which it grows, and many crystals preserve areas that record how those conditions evolved. By analyzing them, specialists reconstruct the history of rocks that were buried at great depth during the formation of the mountain ranges, exactly what the Níjar garnets tell.

And in technology, a synthetic crystal with a garnet structure, YAG, is the basis of some of the most used lasers in industry and medicine. That these same crystals were used for a time to imitate diamonds is further proof of how versatile this family is.

## Almost always untreated

One of the great advantages of garnets is that they are almost never treated: the color you see is what the stone had when it came out of the earth. This makes them a good option for those who prefer natural gems without interventions. As always, in stones of a certain value, a laboratory report will confirm the identity and the absence of treatments.

## Imitations and doublets

| material | How to recognize |
|---|---|
| red glass | bubbles, rounded edges and lower hardness |
| Garnet and glass doublet | a thin layer of garnet cast on colored glass; With the stone face down on a white paper you can see a red ring |
| synthetic garnet | Synthetic garnets with a garnet structure, such as YAG or GGG, were used as diamond imitations and are not natural garnets. |
| Other red gems | ruby, spinel, tourmaline or zircon have different properties that the gemologist identifies |

Interestingly, garnet and glass doublets were used to imitate many other gems, not just garnets: the garnet layer added hardness and shine to the surface, and the glass, the desired color.

## How to choose a garnet

1. **Look at the color in different lights:** Very dark almandines can appear almost black in low light.
2. **Rate the brilliance:** A shallow cut can lighten dark garnets.
3. **Check for cleanliness:** Most red garnets are fairly clean; in the demantoid, horsetail inclusions may increase its interest.
4. **Ask for the exact variety:** rhodolite, tsavorite or mandarin do not have the same value as a common almandine.
5. **Request a report for rare stones,** such as demantoids, large tsavorites or color change garnets.

## Caring for garnets

- **Cleaning:** warm water, neutral soap and a soft brush.
- **Shocks:** its hardness is good but lower than that of corundum; Protects garnets in everyday wear rings.
- **Heat:** avoid sudden changes in temperature.
- **Ultrasounds:** with caution in stones with inclusions; in the demantoid, it is better to avoid them.
- **Storage:** separately, away from harder stones.

## Frequent errors

- **Believe that all garnets are red.**
- **Confusing high-value varieties** with common almandines, or the other way around.
- **Buy doublets** believing they are whole stones.
- **Collect garnets in Cabo de Gata,** a protected area.
- **Choose almandines that are too dark** without seeing them in real light.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is garnet a precious or semi-precious stone?

The traditional distinction between precious and semiprecious is not scientific. Some garnets, such as demantoid or tsavorite, are rare and highly valued gems.

### Are there blue garnets?

There are color change garnets that can look blue-green or gray in certain lights, but a deep blue garnet in all lights would be extraordinary.

### Can you pick garnet in Níjar?

No. El Hoyazo is within the Cabo de Gata-Níjar Natural Park, where collecting minerals is prohibited.

### Are garnets treated?

Seldom. It is one of its advantages over other colored gems.

## In summary

Garnets form a group of minerals with species such as almandine, pyrope, spessartine, grossularia or andradite, and varieties as different as rhodolite, tsavorite, hessonite, demantoid or mandarin garnet. They have good hardness, simple refraction and are almost never treated. In Spain, the Hoyazo de Níjar offers almandine garnets dragged by an ancient volcano, today protected in a natural park and geopark. They shone in Visigothic goldsmithing and in 19th century jewelry. To choose one, look at the color in different lights, ask for the exact variety, and be wary of doublets.
