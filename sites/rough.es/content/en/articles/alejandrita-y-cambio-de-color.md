There are gems that look like two different stones depending on where you look at them. In daylight, next to a window, a fine alexandrite looks green or bluish-green; at night, under a warm lamp or candlelight, it turns red or purple. During the 19th century it was said that it was "an emerald by day and a ruby ​​by night", and this dual personality has made it one of the most fascinating and, also, most imitated gems that exist.

In this guide you will see what alexandrite is, where its name comes from, why it changes color, how this phenomenon differs from other similar ones, how the quality of the change is assessed, what other gems present it, how to recognize synthetics and imitations, very common on the market, and how to check the effect with your own eyes before buying.

## What is alexandrite

Alexandrite is a variety of chrysoberyl, an oxide of beryllium and aluminum, colored by small amounts of chromium.

| Property | Alexandrite |
|---|---|
| mineral species | chrysoberyl |
| Composition | beryllium aluminum oxide with chromium |
| Hardness on the Mohs scale | 8,5 |
| Tenacity | very good |
| Pleochroism | intense, with three different colors depending on the direction |
| Characteristic phenomenon | color change depending on light source |

Due to its hardness and resistance, it is a gem that is very suitable for daily use, even in rings.

## A gem named after a tsar

Alexandrite was discovered in the Ural Mountains of Russia in the 1830s in the emerald mines of the Yekaterinburg region. According to the most widespread tradition, it was presented to the heir to the throne, the future Tsar Alexander II, coinciding with his coming of age, and hence its name. Its two colors, green and red, also coincided with the military colors of imperial Russia, which contributed to its fame at court.

The deposits of the Urals were soon exhausted in their best qualities, and for a long time alexandrite was a rarity. Today it is also extracted in Sri Lanka, Brazil, India and several countries in East Africa.

## Why does it change color

The explanation lies in how it absorbs light. The chromium within chrysoberyl strongly absorbs yellow light and allows two zones of the spectrum to pass through: one in the blue-green and one in the red. The stone is, so to speak, in balance between two colors, and it is the light that illuminates it that tips the balance.

| light source | Dominant composition | Color seen in alexandrite |
|---|---|---|
| daylight | rich in blue and green tones | green or teal |
| Incandescent or candle light | rich in red and yellow tones | red, purple or reddish pink |
| LED lights | It depends on your color temperature | variable; some reduce the effect |

It is not the stone that changes, but the mixture of colors that reaches our eyes. That's why an alexandrite can look different in a jewelry store, at home, and on the street.

## Not to be confused with other phenomena

| phenomenon | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| color change | color changes depending on the light source | alexandrite, some sapphires and garnets |
| Pleochroism | the color changes depending on the direction you look from | tourmaline, iolite, alexandrite itself |
| Iridescence or play of color | flashes of colors that change when you move the stone | opal, labradorite, mother of pearl |
| Photochromism | The color changes temporarily when the stone is exposed to light and returns in the dark | some varieties of sodalite |

The pleochroism of tourmaline, for example, is explained in the guide on [tourmaline](/es/turmalina/).

## How to evaluate color change

| Appearance | What are you looking for? |
|---|---|
| Intensity of change | a marked, almost complete change from one color to another |
| Purity of colors | clean greens and reds, without brown or gray tones |
| Transparency | clean stones; many alexandrites have inclusions |
| Brightness | a size that takes advantage of light and does not leave dark areas |
| Size | fine alexandrites of good size are very rare |

An alexandrite with a weak change, from a brownish green to a reddish brown, is worth much less than one with a strong change between pure colors. Some laboratories describe the degree of change in their reports.

## Other color change gems

| Gem | Typical change |
|---|---|
| color change sapphire | blue in daylight to violet or purple in warm light |
| color change garnet | teal, gray or beige to purple or red, depending on the composition |
| color change spinel | bluish gray to violet |
| Fluorite | some specimens, from blue to violet |
| Diasporus | yellowish green to pinkish; It is marketed under various trade names |

None of them are alexandrite, although they are sometimes described as "alexandrite effect." The expression is correct to describe the phenomenon, but not to name the stone. We also see it in the guides on [sapphire](/es/zafiro/) and [garnets](/es/granates/).

## Synthetic alexandrite and imitations

A large portion of inexpensive jewelry “alexandrites” are not natural alexandrites.

| material | What is | Tracks |
|---|---|---|
| synthetic alexandrite | lab-made chrysoberyl with chromium | same substance; Identified by inclusions and growth characteristics |
| Synthetic corundum with vanadium | color change synthetic sapphire | goes from a blue-gray to violet; very common in antique souvenir rings and jewelry |
| synthetic spinel | color change imitation | simple refraction, without the pleochroism of alexandrite |
| glass | cheap imitation | bubbles, rounded edges and weak change |

Synthetic corundum with vanadium deserves a special mention: for much of the 20th century it was sold so much as "alexandrite" that many families kept rings with these stones believing them to be authentic. It is not a fraud attributable to their owners, but it is advisable to know before appraising or selling them.

## Chrysoberyl Cat's Eye

Chrysoberyl has another famous variety: cat's eye, also called cymophane. When it contains very fine parallel inclusions and is cut as a cabochon, it shows a luminous line that moves when the stone is moved, like the pupil of a cat. In the trade, the term "cat's eye" without further precision is traditionally reserved for chrysoberyl; Other gems with the same effect should be named, for example, "cat's eye quartz." There are even cat's eye alexandrites, which combine the two phenomena.

## Carving and jewelry

Carving an alexandrite is a bit of a puzzle. In addition to changing color with light, the stone shows three different colors depending on the direction from which it is viewed, and the lapidary must orient the rough so that the table offers the best possible change, often at the sacrifice of weight. That is why many alexandrites are small and are cut in a cushion or oval, shapes that allow better use of the material.

In jewelry, alexandrite works very well in rings, thanks to its hardness and tenacity, and is usually accompanied by diamonds or white metals that do not interfere with the perception of the two colors. Yellow gold, on the other hand, reflects warm tones that can dull green. ## How to check the effect before buying

1. **Look at the stone in natural light** next to a window, without direct sun.
2. **Look at it later in warm light:** an incandescent lamp, a candle or a warm light flashlight.
3. **Be wary of store lighting:** spotlights can exaggerate or attenuate the effect.
4. **Compare with another reference stone** if possible.
5. **Requires a laboratory report** confirming that it is natural chrysoberyl and not a synthetic stone or another species.
6. **Ask for the written description** with the exact name: natural alexandrite, synthetic alexandrite or color change sapphire.

## Care

- **Cleaning:** warm water, neutral soap and a soft brush.
- **Ultrasounds:** generally safe on stones without cracks or fillings; Consult the jeweler if you have questions.
- **Shocks:** very resistant, but a sharp blow on an edge can chip it.
- **Storage:** separately, because it scratches most gems.

## Frequent errors

- **Believe that any stone that changes color** is alexandrite.
- **Buy a cheap "alexandrite"** without report.
- **Judge the effect only with the light of the tent.**
- **Confuse pleochroism with color change.**
- **Appraise as alexandrite** an antique ring with synthetic corundum.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does all alexandrite change from green to red?

Not always with the same intensity. The best ones show a marked change between green and red; others go from greenish tones to purple or reddish brown, with less value.

### Why does my “alexandrite” look blue-gray and purple?

It is most likely a synthetic color change corundum, widely used as an imitation during the 20th century. A gemologist can confirm this.

### Do LED lights affect color change?

Yes. The effect depends on the composition of the light, and some LED lights reduce or modify the change. That is why it is advisable to check it in natural light and with warm light.

### Is alexandrite about?

Rarely. In some stones, cracks are filled with oils or resins, something that must be declared.

## In summary

Alexandrite is a chrysoberyl with chromium, with a hardness of 8.5, which looks green in daylight and red or purple in warm light, because chromium allows two areas of the spectrum to pass through and the light that illuminates it decides the color. It was discovered in the Urals and was named after the future Tsar Alexander II. The color change is valued by its intensity and the purity of the two colors. Other gems, such as certain sapphires, garnets and spinels, exhibit the same phenomenon, and imitations abound, especially synthetic corundum with vanadium. Check the effect with natural light and warm light, and demand a laboratory report.
