Very few minerals have a place in Spain in their name, and one of them is studied in all geology faculties in the world: aragonite. It owes its name to Molina de Aragón, a town that, despite what it suggests, is not in Aragón but in the province of Guadalajara. More than two centuries ago, hexagonal-looking crystals emerged from its reddish clay fields that caught the attention of European mineralogists and that today remain an essential piece in any collection of Spanish minerals.

In this guide you will see what aragonite is and how it differs from calcite, why Molina crystals look like hexagonal prisms without being so, in which rocks they form, what other places and living beings make aragonite, how to choose a good specimen and how to clean and preserve it without damaging it.

## A mineral with a Spanish name

Aragonite was described at the end of the 18th century by the German mineralogist Abraham Gottlob Werner, one of the founding figures of mineralogy, based on specimens from Molina de Aragón. The town, head of the historic Señorío de Molina, thus became the type locality of the mineral: the reference place of whose specimens the first description was made.

For a collector, having an aragonite from the type locality has a special appeal: it is a piece of scientific history, as well as a showy mineral. And, unlike other classics, it is not a rare or unattainable specimen.

## What is aragonite

Aragonite is calcium carbonate, exactly the same composition as calcite. The difference is in how the atoms are arranged: they are two different minerals with the same formula, which in mineralogy is called polymorphs.

| Property | Aragonite | Calcite |
|---|---|---|
| Composition | calcium carbonate | calcium carbonate |
| crystalline system | rhombic | trigonal |
| Hardness on the Mohs scale | between 3.5 and 4 | 3 |
| Density | somewhat higher, about 2.9 | around 2.7 |
| Exfoliation | little marked | perfect in three directions, in rhombohedrons |
| Reaction with dilute acid | effervescence | effervescence |
| Surface stability | metastable; over time it tends to transform into calcite | stable |

That last property is important: under the conditions of the Earth's surface, aragonite is less stable than calcite and, over geological times, can transform into it. That is why aragonite is more common in young materials or in particular environments, such as the plasters and clays where the Molina crystals grew.

## The Molina twins

What makes Molina aragonite famous is not a single crystal, but a twin: several crystals that grow together in a regular orientation. In this case, three individuals of the rhombic system are joined rotated relative to each other, and the whole takes on the appearance of an almost perfect hexagonal prism. That is why we talk about pseudohexagonal twins: they look hexagonal, but they are not.

If you look carefully at the base of a good specimen, you can sometimes distinguish sutures between individuals or small indentations on the sides, signs that it is not a single crystal.

| Feature | The usual in Molina |
|---|---|
| Shape | short hexagonal-looking prisms, sometimes grouped |
| Color | reddish, violet, brown or orange, due to inclusions of clay and iron oxides |
| Transparency | opaque or not very translucent |
| Size | from less than a centimeter to several centimeters |
| Presentation | almost always loose, freed from clay by erosion |

The color is not from pure aragonite, which is colorless or white, but from clay and iron particles that were trapped while the crystal grew. That warm coloring, so characteristic, is what allows many specimens from the area to be recognized at first glance.

## Where they are formed: Keuper clays

Molina twins appear in variegated clays and gypsum from the Upper Triassic, a set of rocks known to geologists as the Keuper facies. They were formed more than two hundred million years ago in very saline lagoon and coastal plain environments, where the evaporation of water left deposits of gypsum and salt.

Within these sediments, calcium carbonate crystallized as aragonite, favored by the particular chemistry of the waters. Much later, the erosion of the clays left the crystals loose on the surface of slopes and fields, where farmers and shepherds found them for generations. Similar twins appear in other Keuper outcrops on the peninsula, but Molina remains the classic locality.

Other characteristic minerals also appear in these same clays, such as the doubly terminated reddish quartz known as Compostela hyacinths, which we talk about in the guide on [minerals from Spain] (/es/minerales-de-espana/).

## Aragonite that turns into calcite

As aragonite tends to transform into calcite, some ancient specimens are partially converted: they retain the shape of the twin, but inside a part of the material is already calcite. In mineralogy we speak of paramorphosis. It is not visible to the naked eye, and for the collector it is not a problem, but it explains why some specimens show softer areas or unexpected exfoliation when broken.

## The aragonite beyond Molina

Aragonite not only forms mineral crystals. It is one of the favorite materials of living beings:

- **Mollusc shells:** many are made entirely or partially of aragonite.
- **Mother of pearl and pearls:** The iridescent shine of mother of pearl is due to very thin stacked sheets of aragonite.
- **Corals:** The skeleton of many current corals is made of aragonite.
- **Caves:** Some cave formations, such as certain spiers and eccentrics, are made of aragonite.

Radial aggregates of brown aragonite in the shape of a star, from North Africa, and blocks of banded aragonite carved as decorative objects are also abundant in fairs and stores. They are legitimate materials, but should not be sold as Molina aragonite.

## How to distinguish aragonite and calcite

| Test | What does it indicate? |
|---|---|
| Shape | pseudohexagonal twins are typical of aragonite |
| Exfoliation | calcite breaks into rhombohedrons; aragonite does not |
| Weight in hand | aragonite is somewhat denser |
| Hardness | aragonite slightly scratches calcite |
| Instrumental analysis | X-ray diffraction or Raman spectroscopy undoubtedly distinguish them |

Both minerals effervescent with dilute hydrochloric acid, so this test is useless to separate them. In addition, the acid damages the specimen: do not do it on a collector's item.

## Choose a good specimen

| Aspect | What to value |
|---|---|
| Shape | well-defined twin, with straight sides and sharp ends |
| Integrity | no bumps on the edges or fractures |
| Color | uniform and warm |
| Grouping | multiple or group twins are more attractive |
| Size | In equality of quality, the large ones are rarer |
| Origin | label with a specific location, not just “Spain” |

They are affordable specimens compared to other classics, so it is better to wait to find a well-formed one instead of settling for the first one.

## Cleaning and conservation

- **Adhered clay:** Remove with warm water and a soft brush. Aragonite does not dissolve in water, but it is soft, so do not rub hard.
- **Never use acids or vinegar:** they dissolve the carbonate and destroy the surface of the glass.
- **Avoid ultrasounds** on specimens with cracks, which can open.
- **Dry each piece well** before storing.
- **Keep them separate** from harder minerals, which could scratch them.

In general, Molina aragonite is a stable and easy-to-preserve mineral, without the humidity requirements that others require, such as pyrite, which we talk about in the guide on [Navajún pyrite](/es/pirita-de-navajun/).

## Visit the Señorío de Molina

Molina de Aragón preserves a large castle, a notable historic center and an environment of moors and gorges next to the Alto Tajo Natural Park. It is a good getaway for those who want to see the landscape from which the crystals came, but remember that the fields and slopes have owners, and that in the natural park it is prohibited to collect minerals, rocks and fossils. Before collecting anything, consult the guide on [responsible mineral collection](/es/recoleccion-responsable-de-minerales/).

## Frequent errors

- **Think that Molina de Aragón is in Aragón;** it is in Guadalajara.
- **Confuse twins with hexagonal crystals** of other minerals.
- **Clean with vinegar or acids,** which dissolve carbonate.
- **Buy aggregates from other sources** labeled as Molina.
- **Pick up in the natural park** or on farms without permission.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does aragonite look hexagonal if it is rhombic?

Because Molina's specimens are twins of three crystals united with a regular orientation, which together imitate a hexagonal prism.

### Is aragonite a gem?

It is not generally used in jewelry: it is soft and, in the case of Molina, opaque. Its interest is mineralogical, historical and aesthetic. Of course, the nacre of pearls is made up of aragonite.

### Why do they have that reddish color?

Due to the inclusions of clay and iron oxides trapped during crystal growth in the Keuper clays.

### Are Molina aragonites expensive?

They are usually affordable specimens, although large, perfect or group twins are rarer and are valued more.

## In summary

Aragonite is a calcium carbonate with the same formula as calcite but a different structure, and it owes its name to Molina de Aragón, in Guadalajara, its type locality. There it forms twins of three crystals that imitate hexagonal prisms, colored reddish or violet by the clay and iron of the Keuper layers in which they grew. To choose a specimen, look for clear shapes, healthy edges and a specific location; To preserve it, clean it only with water and a soft, acid-free brush, and collect it only where permitted.
