Few countries in Europe bring together a mineral variety like that of Spain in such a small space. Names that are used throughout the world today were born here, such as aragonite, which owes its name to Molina de Aragón; Here is the largest mercury deposit that has ever been exploited, in Almadén; and from here come some of the specimens that collectors from any continent recognize at first glance, such as the pyrite cubes from Navajún or the transparent blende from the Picos de Europa.

This guide is a map to get you started. It is not intended to be a complete catalogue, because the list of Spanish localities of mineralogical interest is in the thousands, but rather a selection of the classics that any fan should know: why they are famous, where they are located and where you can see good specimens without having to go out into the countryside. At the end you will find advice to start a collection of Spanish minerals with good judgment and some warnings that should be kept in mind.

## Why Spain is a country of minerals

The mineral wealth of the peninsula has a geological explanation. In the west and center the Iberian Massif emerges, a fragment of very old crust, folded and metamorphosed during the Variscan orogeny, more than three hundred million years ago. In these old rocks, quartz veins, tin and tungsten deposits from Galicia and Extremadura, gold exploited by the Romans and massive sulfides from the southwest abound.

To the east and south, the alpine mountain ranges, such as the Pyrenees and the Baetics, are much younger and have produced very different deposits: lead, zinc, iron and minerals linked to volcanic activity, such as those of Cabo de Gata, in Almería. Between them stretch sedimentary basins with marl, gypsum and salts, where crystals as characteristic as twinned aragonite or reddish quartz from the Triassic are formed.

Added to this geology is a mining history of several millennia. Phoenicians, Romans and Arabs exploited metals on the peninsula, and modern mining opened thousands of works in the 19th and 20th centuries. Each mine, active or abandoned, has left specimens in museums and collections, and many localities are only known today for the pieces that came from them.

## Minerals with history

### Almadén and mercury

The name Almadén comes from Arabic and means, precisely, "the mine." For centuries, its deposits of cinnabar, the deep red sulfide of mercury, supplied much of the world, and Almadén's mercury was essential for the processing of silver in colonial America. Exploitation ceased at the beginning of this century, and in 2012 UNESCO declared the Mercury Heritage complex, which Almadén shares with the Slovenian town of Idrija, a World Heritage Site. The Mining Park allows you to tour galleries and learn how they worked.

### Río Tinto and the Pyrite Belt

In the north of the province of Huelva extends the Iberian Pyritic Belt, one of the largest concentrations of massive sulfides on the planet, which continues towards Portugal. Metal has been extracted in the Río Tinto basin for millennia, and the river that gives its name to the area owes its reddish color to very acidic waters loaded with iron. For the amateur, the area is an exceptional geological landscape rather than a collection site: much of the land is still mining property or protected space.

### Las Médulas and Roman gold

In Bierzo, in León, Roman engineers dismantled entire mountains with water to extract gold from the sediments, with a technique known as *ruina montium*. The resulting landscape, of reddish peaks among chestnut trees, has been World Heritage since 1997. Specimens are not sought there, but few places better explain the relationship between geology, mining and history.

### Gavà and Neolithic mining

In the Baix Llobregat, near Barcelona, there are mines from about six thousand years ago, excavated to extract variscite, a green stone with which beads and pendants were made. It is one of the most important prehistoric mining complexes in Europe and can be visited in the town's archaeological park.

## Crystals that are recognized at first glance

Some Spanish minerals have become emblems of world mineralogy due to the perfection of their crystals:

- **Navajún Pyrite:** metal cubes with sharp edges, sometimes twinned or grouped, that appear to be hand-carved and are formed naturally within the marls.
- **Molina Aragonite:** twins of three individuals that imitate hexagonal prisms, of violet, reddish or brown color, and which gave their name to the species.
- **Asturian fluorite:** transparent cubes of purple, yellow, blue or colorless, often accompanied by barite, calcite or quartz.
- **Blenda de Áliva:** transparent crystals and exfoliation fragments, honey or reddish in color, with a light dispersion greater than that of diamond. They are used to cut collectible gems, which are too soft for everyday jewelry.
- **Eugui Dolomite:** transparent and shiny rhombohedral crystals, highly appreciated by collectors, on a white magnesite matrix.
- **Compostela hyacinths:** small quartz crystals with two points, colored red by hematite inclusions, which appear loose among the clays and gypsum of the Triassic.

## The Pulpí geode

In 1999, a group of amateurs exploring an old lead mine in the Sierra del Aguilón, in Pulpí (Almería), found a cavity about eight meters long lined with transparent gypsum crystals, some almost two meters long. It is one of the largest known geodes in the world and, after years of study and conditioning works, it opened to the public in 2019 with guided tours in small groups to protect the crystals.

The geode sums up the ethos of modern collecting well: an extraordinary find that, rather than being dismantled to sell its crystals, is kept in place for everyone to see. If you are going to visit it, book in advance, because places are limited.

## Where to see good specimens

It is not necessary to go out into the countryside to discover Spanish minerals. Some essential places:

| Place | What it offers |
|---|---|
| Geomining Museum, in Madrid | one of the most complete collections of Spanish minerals, organized by region, in a historic building |
| National Museum of Natural Sciences, in Madrid | collections of minerals, rocks and meteorites, with temporary exhibitions |
| Regional and university natural science museums | regional collections, often with specimens from closed mines |
| Mining parks, such as those of Almadén or Riotinto | galleries, machinery and historical context of mining |
| Pulpí Geode and Cardona Salt Mountain | sites visitable with guide |
| Mineral and fossil fairs | copies for sale and contact with other collectors |

Before going, check schedules and conditions on the official website of each center, because they change frequently and some visits require a reservation.

## How to start a collection of Spanish minerals

A thematic collection dedicated to Spain is one of the best ways to start: the specimens are relatively accessible, the documentation abounds and each piece tells something about the territory.

1. **Start with the classics.** A cube of pyrite from Navajún, a twin of aragonite from Molina, an Asturian fluorite and some hyacinths from Compostela form an excellent base.
2. **Prioritize quality over quantity.** A small specimen with intact crystals is worth more, in every way, than a large one full of dents.
3. **Locality required.** A mineral without provenance loses much of its interest. A serious merchant indicates the mine, the municipality and the province.
4. **Ask about repairs.** Some examples have glued crystals or restored tips; the seller must say so.
5. **Label and catalog from day one.** Note number, species, location, date and origin of each piece, and preserve old labels.
6. **Visit museums before buying.** Seeing reference specimens educates the eye and helps recognize good pieces.

## Before going out into the field

Searching for minerals on your own is part of the hobby, but in Spain it has clear limits. Mineral resources are subject to mining legislation, many areas belong to concessions or private owners, and in protected natural areas it is usually prohibited to collect rocks, minerals and fossils. Added to this are the regulations of each autonomous community on geological and paleontological heritage.

Safety deserves the same respect: abandoned galleries and wells can collapse, accumulate gases or hide vertical drops. Never enter an abandoned mine. We explain it in detail in the guide on [responsible mineral collection](/es/recoleccion-responsable-de-minerales/).

## Frequent errors

- **Buy copies without a locality,** or with a vague locality such as "Spain".
- **Confusing similar minerals** such as pyrite with brandsite or chalcopyrite, or fluorite with colored quartz.
- **Wash or rub delicate specimens,** such as halite, which dissolves in water, or pyrite, which can be altered by humidity.
- **Enter abandoned mines** to look for specimens.
- **Collect in protected spaces** or on other people's land without permission.
- **Pay for exoticism,** without comparing with reference specimens in museums and fairs.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most famous Spanish mineral in the world?

It is difficult to choose one, but Navajún pyrite, Molina de Aragón aragonite, Asturian fluorite and Picos de Europa blende are among the most recognized by collectors around the world.

### Why is aragonite called that if Molina is in Guadalajara?

Because the mineral was described from specimens from Molina de Aragón, a town that, despite its name, belongs to the province of Guadalajara, in Castilla-La Mancha.

### Can gems be cut with Spanish minerals?

Yes. Áliva blende is one of the most appreciated collection materials for faceting, and fluorites, garnets and other minerals have also been carved in Spain. Many of these gems are collector's items, rather than everyday jewelry, due to their low hardness.

### Where can I see the best collection of Spanish minerals?

The Geomining Museum of Madrid is a must-see, and should be completed with the regional museums and mining parks in each area.

## In summary

Spain brings together a very varied geology, with ancient rocks from the Iberian Massif, young Alpine mountain ranges and sedimentary basins with gypsum and salts, and a mining history of millennia. From there come world classics such as Navajún pyrite, Molina de Aragón aragonite, Asturian fluorite, Almadén cinnabar, Áliva blende, Eugui dolomite or the Pulpí geode. To get to know them, start with the museums and mining parks; To collect them, prioritize quality and documented provenance; and before going out into the field, find out the rules and never enter abandoned mines.
