A red stone can be a ruby, spinel, garnet, tourmaline, synthetic ruby ​​or a piece of glass. At first glance, even a trained eye can doubt. That is why gemology relies on instruments: relatively simple devices that measure how light behaves when passing through a stone, how much it weighs in relation to its volume or what is hidden inside. With them, a gemologist can identify most gems in a few minutes, without damaging them.

This guide presents the basic instruments of classical gemology: what each one is for, how it is used in general terms and what an amateur can verify with practice. It also explains its limits, because today many treatments and synthetics are only detected with laboratory equipment, and knowing those limits is as important as knowing how to use a magnifying glass. If you consider learning, you will eventually find reasonable starter equipment and safety tips.

## What an instrument can and cannot say

Classical instruments measure physical and optical properties: refractive index, behavior under polarized light, pleochroism, absorption spectrum, density, fluorescence and inclusions. By combining several of these tests, the species of a gem is identified with considerable certainty. What they usually can't say is:

- **If a gem is treated,** in many modern treatments.
- **If it is natural or synthetic,** when the synthetic is of good quality.
- **Where it comes from,** something that only specialized laboratories express as an opinion.

No isolated test is enough: identification is made by adding coherent results.

## The 10x magnifying glass

It is the most important instrument and the first one to master. A good gemological loupe is of the triplet type, with three lenses that correct shape and color distortions.

**How ​​to use:** Bring the magnifying glass close to the eye, almost touching the eyebrow; Hold the stone with tweezers and bring it closer to focus; Rest your elbows on the table so you don't shake. Change the lighting: reflected light to see the surface and light that passes through the stone, from the side or from below, to see the interior.

| What to observe | What can it indicate? |
|---|---|
| Facet edges | Rounded or worn edges are typical of soft materials, such as glass |
| Round or elongated bubbles | glass or certain synthetics |
| Curved growth lines | synthetics manufactured by flame fusion |
| Crystalline inclusions and traces | common in natural gems |
| Duplication of background edges | marked double refraction, as in zircon or peridot |
| Joints and layers | doublets and triplets |
| Remains of filler or different shine in cracks | possible filler treatments |

## Tweezers, light and cloth

They seem like details, but they make a difference. Gemological tweezers with grooves hold the stone without it jumping. A balanced white light, similar to daylight, allows color to be judged; and a warm light is used to check color changing gems. A lint-free cloth keeps the stone clean, because a fingerprint can be mistaken for an inclusion.

## refractometer

It measures the refractive index, that is, how much the light bends when entering the stone. Each species has a characteristic value, and those with double refraction show two values ​​whose difference is called birefringence.

- **How ​​it works:** A drop of a special liquid is placed on the crystal of the device, a flat, polished facet of the gem is placed on top, and the value is read on a scale through the eyepiece.
- **What allows:** to distinguish species such as corundum, spinel, garnet, tourmaline, beryl or quartz with great reliability.
- **Its limits:** it needs a flat and polished facet, it does not measure above a certain value, so it is not suitable for diamond and other very refractive stones, and the contact liquid is toxic and must be handled with care.

## Polariscope

It has two polarizing filters, one below and one above the stone. When rotated between them, the gem behaves differently depending on its structure:

| Behavior | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Stays dark when turned | simple refraction: for example spinel, garnet or glass |
| Lights up and darkens four times in one full turn | double refraction: for example corundum, tourmaline or quartz |
| Remains clear in all positions | polycrystalline material, such as chalcedony or jade |
| Shows irregular dark spots or bands | internal stresses, common in glass and some synthetics |

It is a cheap and very useful instrument to separate, for example, a ruby from a spinel or a red glass.

## Dichroscope

It allows you to see separately the colors that a pleochroic gem shows in different directions. When looking through it, two windows appear next to each other: if they have different colors, the stone is pleochroic. The ruby ​​shows two different reds; spinel and garnet, none. It is especially useful in gems such as tourmaline, iolite or alexandrite, which we talked about in the guide on [alexandrite and color change](/es/alejandrita-y-cambio-de-color/).

## handheld spectroscope

It breaks down the light that passes through or reflects off the stone and shows dark lines or bands where the gem absorbs certain colors. Some elements leave characteristic traces: the chromium of rubies, emeralds or red spinels produces fine lines in the red; the iron of many blue sapphires and almandine garnets leaves bands in the blue and green. It requires bright light and a lot of practice, but it is very informative.

## ultraviolet light

Long-wave and short-wave ultraviolet lamps reveal the fluorescence of some gems: the intense red of many rubies, the blue of many diamonds or the reactions of some fillings and treatments. It is complementary evidence, never conclusive on its own. Ultraviolet radiation, especially short-wave radiation, damages the eyes and skin: always wear protective glasses and do not look directly into the lamp.

## Balance and density

A carat scale measures the weight of loose gems; One carat is equivalent to 0.2 grams. With a small accessory, the same scale allows you to calculate the relative density: the stone is weighed in air and then submerged in water, and the weight in air is divided by the difference between the two. Each species has an approximate value, and the test is useful for unmounted stones. There are also dense liquids for separating gems by flotation, but they are toxic and are not for home use.

## Color filters

The best known is the Chelsea filter, which allows red and a strip of yellowish green to pass through. Some gems and dyes appear a characteristic color through it. It was very useful in its time, but today there are so many synthetics and imitations that its results should be taken only as a clue.

## diamond testers

Thermal testers measure heat conductivity and separate the diamond from most imitations, but not from synthetic moissanite, which also conducts heat very well. That is why combined devices are used that also measure electrical conductivity. None of them distinguishes a natural diamond from one grown in a laboratory: for that there are specific screening equipment and, ultimately, the laboratory.

## A first team

| Instrument | What does it contribute? | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| 10x triplet magnifying glass | observation of surface and inclusions | essential |
| Gemological tweezers and cloth | safe and clean handling | essential |
| White flashlight and warm light | lighting and checking color changes | highly recommended |
| Polariscope | single or double refraction and tensions | highly recommended |
| Dichroscope | pleochroism | recommendable |
| refractometer | species identification | when you have training |
| handheld spectroscope | coloring elements | when you have practice |
| carat scale | weight and density | recommended for loose stones |

## What only a laboratory can do

| Question | Common laboratory techniques |
|---|---|
| Is this stone heated? | advanced microscopy and spectroscopy |
| Is there diffusion of beryllium? | high sensitivity chemical analysis |
| Is it a natural or cultured diamond? | spectroscopy and specific luminescence equipment |
| What is its geographical origin? | combination of inclusions and trace element chemistry |
| What filling does an emerald have? | infrared and Raman spectroscopy |

Therefore, when faced with valuable stones, the appropriate response is not to buy more instruments, but to request a report from a recognized laboratory. We explain it in the guide on [professional appraisal](/es/tasacion-profesional/).

## Security

- **Refractometer fluid:** toxic; work with ventilation and wash your hands.
- **Ultraviolet light:** Protective glasses and minimal skin exposure.
- **Thick liquids:** are not for domestic use.
- **Loose stones:** work on a tray or cloth so as not to lose them.

## Where to learn

Gemology is best learned with a regulated course that combines theory and practices with real stones. In Spain, the Spanish Gemological Institute has been offering training for decades, and there are other national and international centers with recognized diplomas. Complement the training by visiting museums, fairs and laboratories, and always practicing with stones of known identity before facing unknown ones.

## Frequent errors

- **Base an identification on a single test.**
- **Use the magnifying glass without tweezers or good light.**
- **Rely on a thermal tester** to separate diamond and moissanite.
- **Handle toxic liquids** without precautions.
- **Believe that basic instruments detect** all treatments and synthetics.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which instrument should I buy first?

A 10x triplet magnifying glass and some good tweezers. With practice, they allow you to detect many imitations, doublets and obvious synthetics.

### Can I identify a gem with just a magnifying glass?

Sometimes, but not for sure. Reliable identification combines several tests: refractometer, polariscope, dichroscope, spectroscope and inclusions.

### Does a diamond tester detect lab-grown diamonds?

No. Thermal and electrical testers only separate diamond from imitations such as zirconia or moissanite. Cultured diamonds require specific equipment.

### Do I need a laboratory if I have instruments?

For valuable stones, yes. Many treatments, modern synthetics, and opinions of origin can only be determined with laboratory techniques.

## In summary

Classical gemology relies on simple instruments: the 10x magnifying glass to see surface and inclusions; the refractometer to measure the refractive index; the polariscope to know if the refraction is simple or double; the dichroscope for pleochroism; the spectroscope for coloring elements; ultraviolet light for fluorescence, and the balance for weight and density. Combined, they identify most species. But many treatments, modern synthetics and origins are only determined in the laboratory. Start with a magnifying glass and tweezers, train at a recognized center and always work with caution.
