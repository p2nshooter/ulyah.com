Whoever sees a Navajún pyrite cube for the first time usually asks the same question: who carved it? The flat faces, shiny like a brass mirror, and the straight edges look like the work of a machine. And yet, no one has touched them: those cubes grow like this within the gray loams of a small town in La Rioja, and from there they have traveled to museums and collections around the world, where they are probably the most recognizable Spanish mineral.

In this guide you will see what pyrite is, why in Navajún it forms such perfect cubes, what shapes and groupings are most appreciated, how to distinguish it from gold and other similar sulfides, what to check before buying a specimen and, above all, how to preserve it so that it does not deteriorate over the years, one of the most neglected points in collections.

## What is pyrite

Pyrite is an iron sulfide, one of the most common minerals in the Earth's crust. It appears in rocks of almost all types and ages, but only in a few places does it form collector-quality crystals.

| Property | Pyrite |
|---|---|
| Composition | iron disulfide |
| crystalline system | cubic |
| Common forms | cube, pyritohedron (dodecahedron with pentagonal faces) and octahedron |
| Color | pale brass yellow, with metallic luster |
| Stripe | greenish black |
| Hardness on the Mohs scale | between 6 and 6.5 |
| Density | high, around 5 |
| Fracture | irregular; the mineral is brittle |

Its name comes from the Greek *pyr*, fire, because when hit with steel or another hard stone it releases sparks. Its golden color earned it the nickname "fool's gold", although, as we will see, distinguishing it from gold is easy.

## Navajún, a tiny town with world fame

Navajún is a small town in La Rioja Baja, in the Cervera del Río Alhama region, very close to the border with Soria. Its few neighbors coexist with a landscape of hillsides of marl and clay where, for decades, pyrite cubes have been extracted that have given the town a fame disproportionate to its size. In the same area there are other towns with pyrite, such as Ambasaguas, but the name of Navajún has become almost a brand for collectors.

The crystals appear within marls, sedimentary rocks made up of clay and carbonate, which break down relatively easily. This allows the cubes to be extracted whole or left partially included in the rock, which gives rise to the matrix specimens so characteristic of the locality.

## Why are such perfect cubes formed?

The shape of a crystal is the external expression of its atomic structure. Pyrite has a cubic structure, and under the right conditions it grows developing the faces of a cube. For these faces to come out flat and the edges to be straight, several circumstances are necessary:

- **Slow and stable growth,** with a moderate and continuous contribution of iron and sulfur.
- **A medium that does not get in the way,** such as a fine-grained rock that allows the crystal to grow without deforming.
- **Absence of subsequent shocks,** which would have broken or deformed the glass.

In Navajún these conditions were met, and the result is cubes that rigorously comply with the geometry of the cubic system. It is a perfect illustration of one of the first laws of crystallography, formulated in the 17th century by Nicolás Steno: the angles between the equivalent faces of the crystals of the same mineral are constant, no matter where the crystal grows.

## Ways to know

- **Loose cubes:** the best known format. They are valued for the brightness of the faces, the sharpness of the edges and the absence of bumps in the corners.
- **Cubes in matrix:** one or more cubes included in the loam. They are the most aesthetic specimens when the contrast between the gray of the rock and the gold of the glass is well composed.
- **Twins and interpenetrated cubes:** two or more cubes that grow through each other, sometimes in the shape of a cross.
- **Groups:** cubes of different sizes joined together, sometimes in a ladder.
- **Striated faces:** fine parallel lines on the faces of the cube, which alternate their direction from one face to the other. They are a trace of crystal growth and not a defect.

## Pyrite, gold, Marcasite and Chalcopyrite

| Mineral | How to recognize it |
|---|---|
| Pyrite | pale brass color, greenish black stripe, hard and brittle; does not scratch with a razor |
| Gold | intense yellow, yellow stripe, very soft and malleable; It deforms without breaking and is much heavier |
| Marcasite | same composition as pyrite but another crystalline system; paler color, crystals in ridges or spears, and much more prone to deterioration |
| Chalcopyrite | more intense yellow, sometimes with iridescence; softer than pyrite |

The streak test involves rubbing the mineral on unglazed porcelain: pyrite leaves a dark powder, while gold leaves a golden streak. Don't do it on a good specimen, because it leaves a mark; a worthless fragment is enough.

## What to check when purchasing a copy

| Appearance | What to watch |
|---|---|
| Edges and corners | bumps, chips or rounded corners due to handling |
| Faces | uniform shine; be wary of faces that are too smooth throughout the specimen, which may have been polished |
| Union with the matrix | cubes stuck on someone else's loam; look for adhesive residue or filled gaps |
| Matrix | that it is natural loam and not a reconstructed paste |
| Surface | whitish dust, cracks or stains, which may indicate deterioration |
| Label | specific location and declaration of repairs |

It is common for the cubes to be extracted loose and for some matrix specimens to be later assembled by gluing crystals to the rock. It is not illegal or immoral if it is declared, but a mounted specimen is worth much less than a natural one. Always ask, look at the base of the cube with a magnifying glass and, if the seller does not know or does not want to answer, look for another.

Cubes from other origins also circulate, for example from Peru or China, presented as Rioja. Their appearance may be very similar; That is why the documented provenance and reputation of the seller are so important.

## pyrite disease

It is the great enemy of any collection with sulfides. In humid environments, pyrite can react with oxygen and water in the air to form iron sulfates and sulfuric acid. The specimen is covered with a whitish or yellowish powder, cracks appear and, in advanced cases, the glass crumbles. The released acid can also damage labels, boxes and neighboring minerals.

Well-crystallized pyrite, such as that from Navajún, is usually much more stable than fine-grained pyrite or Marcasite, but it is not immune. To prevent:

1. **Maintain a low and stable relative humidity.** As a guide, many collections aim to keep it no higher than 50%.
2. **Avoid sudden changes** in temperature and exposure to bathrooms or kitchens.
3. **Use sealed boxes with desiccant sachets,** such as silica gel, and replace them when they become saturated.
4. **Check specimens every few months** and immediately remove any that show dust or cracks.
5. **Do not store pyrite next to delicate minerals,** in case it deteriorates.

If a specimen already shows signs of deterioration, consult a conservator or a mineralogical association before applying any treatment.

## How to clean pyrite

- **Powder:** a soft brush or an air bulb.
- **Footprints:** a dry, lint-free cloth.
- **Water, only in loose buckets and on rare occasions,** drying them thoroughly immediately. Never wet a specimen in matrix: the loam softens and can fall apart.
- **No acids or household cleaners.** Some specialists use chemical products to remove stains, but it is easy to damage the specimen and they are dangerous to handle.
- **Avoid grease and oils,** which trap dust and do not protect against deterioration.

## Pyrite in jewelry

What is traditionally called "marcasite" in jewelry is usually, in reality, pyrite: small faceted pieces set in silver, very popular in jewelry at the beginning of the 20th century. Navajún cubes are also sometimes mounted as pendants or cufflinks, but it is worth knowing that pyrite is fragile, that sweat and humidity accelerate its deterioration and that a blow can break an edge. As an occasional piece, stored dry after each use, it can work; As a daily jewel, it is not the best choice.

The region of Cervera del Río Alhama offers mountain landscapes, small towns and a very close relationship with its geological heritage. ## Frequent errors

- **Buy without asking if the cube is glued** to the matrix.
- **Wash specimens on matrix,** which dissolve with water.
- **Store pyrite in humid places** such as basements or bathrooms.
- **Ignore the whitish dust,** the first sign of deterioration.
- **Confusing pyrite with gold** and paying for it.
- **Rely on vague labels** without a specific location.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are the Navajún cubes carved?

No. They grow naturally with that geometry due to the cubic structure of the mineral and the slow and stable growth conditions in which they were formed.

### Does pyrite have any value like gold?

It does not contain usable gold in collector specimens. Its value is mineralogical and aesthetic: it depends on the perfection of the crystals, the composition, size and origin.

### Why is my pyrite covered in white powder?

Probably due to the so-called pyrite disease, an alteration caused by humidity. Separate it from the rest of the collection, store it in a dry environment and consult a specialist.

### Can I go to Navajún to look for pyrite?

Only with authorization from the holders of the mining rights and the owners of the land. Without permission, picking or digging is not permitted.

## In summary

Pyrite from Navajún, in La Rioja, forms natural cubes with shiny faces and straight edges thanks to the cubic structure of the mineral and slow growth within the marls. It is distinguished from gold by its dark stripe, hardness and fragility, and from Marcasite by its color and crystals. When purchasing, check edges, faces and the union with the matrix, and demand the locality. To preserve it, keep it in a dry, stable environment, dry clean it, and watch for signs of deterioration.
