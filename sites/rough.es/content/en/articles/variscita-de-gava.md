About six thousand years ago, in some hills of Baix Llobregat, a few kilometers from where the metropolitan area of ​​Barcelona extends today, Neolithic communities dug wells and galleries with stone tools to reach green veins. That mineral, variscite, was later converted into beads and pendants that circulated hundreds of kilometers. The Gavà mines are today one of the most important prehistoric mining complexes in Europe and a place where the history of gems and the history of humanity come together.

In this guide you will see what variscite is and how it differs from turquoise, how they were discovered and what the Gavà mines were like, how its miners worked, what archaeologists call the calaíta, what you can visit and, if you are attracted to this stone for a piece of jewelry, how to buy and care for it.

## What is variscite

Variscite is a hydrated aluminum phosphate. It usually occurs in compact masses, nodules or vein fillings, and very rarely in visible crystals.

| Property | Variscite |
|---|---|
| Composition | hydrated aluminum phosphate |
| Usual appearance | compact masses, nodules and veins |
| Color | apple green, yellowish green, or bluish green, sometimes with dark streaks |
| Brightness | waxy to vitreous once polished |
| Hardness on the Mohs scale | approximately between 3.5 and 4.5 |
| Porosity | variable; some varieties absorb liquids and fats |

Its name comes from Variscia, the old Latin name for Vogtland, a region of Germany where it was first described. Today, sites are known on several continents, including some very famous ones in the western United States and Australia.

## Variscite and turquoise

At first glance, variscite and turquoise can be confused, and for centuries they have been called by interchangeable names.

| Appearance | Variscite | Turquoise |
|---|---|---|
| Composition | aluminum phosphate | copper aluminum phosphate |
| Typical color | green, yellowish to bluish | light blue to greenish blue |
| Color origin | small amounts of chromium and iron | copper |
| Frequent treatments | stabilization with resins, waxing, dye | stabilization, dyeing, reconstitution |

To safely separate them, a laboratory is needed: composition analyzes or spectroscopy allow them to be distinguished without damaging the piece.

## The Gavà mines

The mines were discovered in the 1970s, during some works in the Can Tintorer area, in Gavà. Subsequent archaeological excavations revealed a complex of dozens of shafts and galleries dug into ancient slate rocks to follow veins of variscite. Some works reach several meters deep and branch out into galleries at different levels, a remarkable work for a society without metal tools.

The dating places the main activity in the Neolithic, approximately six millennia ago. Tools, ceramic remains and burials appeared in the mines, and among the best-known finds is the so-called Venus of Gavà, a ceramic female figure that has become a symbol of the site.

## How Neolithic miners worked

From what has been found at the site, archaeologists have reconstructed a surprisingly organized mining process:

1. **Location of the veins:** probably from outcrops visible on the surface.
2. **Opening of wells:** vertical or inclined, with just dimensions so that a person could work.
3. **Galleries:** that followed the route of the vein inside the rock.
4. **Tools:** stone picks and clubs and antler tools, with which the rock was torn out.
5. **Selection:** outside the mine, the usable variscite fragments were separated.
6. **Size:** The fragments were cut, drilled and polished to obtain beads and pendants.

The effort that all this required says a lot about the value that those communities gave to the green stone: it was not a simple ornament, but a prestigious asset that was exchanged and buried with the deceased.

## The calaíta and the green beads of prehistory

For a long time, archaeologists called calaíta the green beads that appeared in prehistoric graves in the peninsula and other regions of Western Europe. The term comes from a green stone cited by classical authors, but does not designate a specific mineral. Modern analyzes have shown that many of these beads are variscite, and that others are made of different minerals, such as turquoise, green muscovite or serpentine.

Thanks to these analyses, we now know that the Gavà variscite circulated through large areas of the northeast of the peninsula and beyond. Ancient variscite exploitations are also known in other areas of the peninsula, such as in the province of Zamora, which shows that appreciation for this green stone was a widespread phenomenon.

## Why a green stone

Variscite was not the only green stone that fascinated European Neolithic societies. At the same time, polished jadeite axes from the Western Alps traveled thousands of kilometers to the Iberian Peninsula, Britain or northern Europe, often with no signs of having been used for work. Everything indicates that they were prestigious objects, valued for their rarity, their difficulty in obtaining and their color.

Why green was so attractive is something that can only be hypothesized: perhaps because of its relationship with vegetation, water or the renewal of life, or simply because it was a rare color in the decorations of the time. What the sites do show is that green beads frequently appear in the graves of people to whom a special trousseau was dedicated. Seen this way, the Gavà mine was not just a quarry for ornaments: it was the source of a material loaded with social meaning, and those who controlled it occupied a prominent place in the exchange networks of their time.

## Visit the archaeological park

The site can be visited in the archaeological park of the Gavà mines, which preserves part of the work and explains how they were worked, and the town museum completes the visit with the most important finds. Check schedules and conditions on the municipal website before going.

Remember that this is an archaeological site protected by historical heritage legislation: it is prohibited to extract any material, and the same applies to any other archaeological site in the country. ## Variscite in jewelry today

The variscite found on the jewelry market today comes from deposits in different countries, and is usually worked into cabochons or polished beads. The most appreciated pieces have an intense and uniform green, or a well-balanced pattern of dark veins.

### Before buying

| What to ask or review | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Is it stabilized? | many variscites are impregnated with resins to harden them and fix the color |
| Is it dyed? | some cheap materials are dyed to imitate the most prized greens |
| Is it variscite or an imitation? | Dyed howlite or magnesite, glass and plastics are sometimes sold as green stones |
| What is its origin? | a serious seller knows it and indicates it |
| How is it mounted? | a soft stone needs a setting to protect it from impacts |

Stabilization is a common and accepted practice if declared, but it changes the value of the piece compared to an untreated natural variscite. We explain it when talking about treatments in the guide on [basic gemological instruments](/es/instrumentos-gemologicos-basicos/).

## Care of variscite

Variscite is relatively soft and, in many cases, porous. To preserve its color and shine:

- **Avoid contact with creams, perfumes, oils and sweat,** which can alter its color over time.
- **Do not expose it to heat** or sudden changes in temperature.
- **Clean only with a soft, dry cloth,** or lightly dampened if necessary.
- **No ultrasound, steam or solvents,** which can damage the stone and the stabilization resins.
- **Store it separately** so it doesn't get scratched by harder stones.
- **Take it off** to scrub, play sports or work with your hands.

## Frequent errors

- **Confuse variscite with turquoise** or dyed imitations.
- **Buy without asking** for stabilization or tint.
- **Clean it often with soap and water** or with chemicals.
- **Wear it in rings** exposed to bumps and scratches.
- **Think that material can be collected** in archaeological sites.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Gavà variscite sold as a gem?

No. The mines are a protected archaeological site and are not exploited. The variscite on the current market comes from other deposits in the world.

### Why is the Gavà site so important?

Because it is one of the most notable Neolithic mining complexes in Europe, with shafts and galleries excavated with stone tools to extract an ornamental stone, revealing an organization and appreciation for gems surprising for the time.

### What is calaita?

A term archaeologists used for prehistoric green beads. It is not a specific mineral: many turned out to be variscite, but others are turquoise or other green minerals.

### Does variscite lose color?

It can be altered by heat, chemicals, oils and sweat, especially if it is porous. With simple care it retains its color for a long time.

## In summary

Variscite is a green, soft and sometimes porous aluminum phosphate, which is often confused with turquoise. In Gavà, in the Baix Llobregat, Neolithic communities excavated wells and galleries about six thousand years ago with stone tools to extract it and make beads that circulated throughout large areas, an exceptional site that can be visited today and is protected. If you purchase variscite for jewelry, ask about stabilization, dye, and origin, choose a protective setting, and care for it away from heat, cosmetics, and chemicals.
