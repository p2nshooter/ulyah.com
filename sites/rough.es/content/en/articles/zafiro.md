When someone says sapphire, almost everyone thinks of a blue stone. And it is logical: the blue of the sapphire is one of the most recognizable colors in jewelry, present in crowns, famous engagement rings and pieces of high jewelry from all eras. But sapphire is much more than that. There are yellow, pink, orange, violet, green and colorless sapphires, sapphires that change color with light and sapphires with a six-pointed star. They are all the same mineral species, corundum, the same one that makes up ruby.

In this guide you will see what sapphire is and what produces its blue, how that color is valued, what fantasy sapphires and the coveted padparadscha are, where sapphires come from, what treatments are common and which ones should be monitored, how to distinguish synthetics and imitations, why the "sapphire crystal" of watches is not a natural gem and what to check before buying.

## What is sapphire

Sapphire is corundum, an aluminum oxide, in any color other than red, which is reserved for ruby.

| Property | Sapphire |
|---|---|
| mineral species | corundum |
| Composition | aluminum oxide with traces of other elements |
| Hardness on the Mohs scale | 9 |
| Tenacity | good, no exfoliation |
| Brightness | vitreous to subadamantine |
| Pleochroism | frequent: color changes slightly depending on direction |
| Density | high, so a sapphire weighs more than a diamond of the same size |

Like the ruby, it is a gem very suitable for daily wear. The difference in density has a practical consequence: a one-carat sapphire appears smaller than a one-carat diamond, because it takes up less volume.

## Blue: where it comes from and how it is valued

The blue of sapphire is due to the interaction between small amounts of iron and titanium within the crystal. Small variations in these elements explain the enormous range of blues, from almost transparent light blues to blues so dark that they look like ink.

| Appearance | What are you looking for? |
|---|---|
| Tone | medium to medium dark; neither pale nor almost black |
| Saturation | bright blue, without marked gray or greenish nuances |
| Distribution | uniform; sapphire tends to show bands of color that a good cut hides |
| Transparency | clean; some sapphires have a slightly velvety appearance due to very fine inclusions |

Commercial terms such as “cornflower blue” or “royal blue” describe highly prized types of blue, but do not conform to a single standard. As with ruby, they only mean something when a laboratory uses them with explicit criteria.

## fantasy sapphires

In the trade, those that are not blue are called fantasy sapphires.

| Color | Observations |
|---|---|
| Yellow | from lemon to gold; often by iron or by treatments |
| Pink | by chromium in smaller quantities than in ruby |
| Orange | scarce when natural; monitor the treatments |
| Violet and purple | combination of pink and blue mechanisms |
| Green | usually extinguished, by iron |
| Colorless | called white sapphire; sometimes used as an alternative to diamond, with less fire |
| Bicolor | two or more colors in the same stone, in defined areas |

## Padparadscha: the most discussed sapphire

Padparadscha is a sapphire that is intermediate in color between pink and orange, like a sunset or a lotus flower, which is what its name means in Sinhalese. It is one of the most sought-after sapphires and also one of the most controversial: there is no universal definition of what shades are, and each laboratory applies its own criteria.

Two warnings. First, at the beginning of this century, large quantities of orange and orange-pink sapphires obtained by diffusion of beryllium, a treatment that imitated these colors, appeared on the market. Second, the color of some sapphires in this range may be due to unstable defects that fade with light. For a padparadscha, a report from a recognized laboratory is not optional.

## Color change and stars

- **Color Change Sapphires:** They show one color in daylight, often blue, and another in incandescent light, usually violet or purple. The phenomenon is explained in the guide on [alexandrite and color change](/es/alejandrita-y-cambio-de-color/).
- **Star Sapphires:** When well-oriented rutile needles are abundant, a cabochon may display a six-point star. Natural stars tend to be somewhat irregular; A perfect star, very marked and on a very uniform background may indicate a synthetic.

## Origins of sapphire

| Region | General features |
|---|---|
| Sri Lanka | great tradition; Luminous blues and abundant fantasy sapphires |
| Kashmir | almost exhausted historical site, famous for velvety blues; very scarce on the market |
| Myanmar | intense and saturated blues |
| Madagascar | one of the great current origins, with very varied qualities |
| Australia, Thailand and Cambodia | sapphires from basaltic rocks, often darker |
| Montana, in the United States | blue-green and fancy sapphires, often small |
| East Africa | sapphires of many colors, including those of unusual shades |

As with the ruby, determining the origin is a technical opinion that only specialized laboratories can issue, and origins such as Kashmir or Myanmar drive up the price. Do not accept a prestigious provenance without a report to back it up.

## Sapphire treatments

| Treatment | What does it consist of? | What does it imply? |
|---|---|---|
| Warm-up | high temperatures to intensify or lighten color and reduce inclusions | very common and stable; must be declared |
| Heating of milky material | certain whitish stones turn blue when heated | traditional Sri Lankan practice; stable result |
| Beryllium diffusion | Beryllium penetrates the stone at a very high temperature and changes the color | produces yellows, oranges and orange-pinks; greatly reduces the value; requires laboratory |
| Titanium surface diffusion | creates a very thin blue layer on the surface | re-polishing may remove the color; low value |
| Irradiation | intensifies certain yellows and oranges | color may not be stable |
| glass fillings | hide fractures in low quality material | lower durability; must be declared |

## Synthetics and imitations

| material | Indications |
|---|---|
| synthetic sapphire | same substance as the natural one; often curved growth lines, bubbles or traces of flux, depending on the method |
| Blue synthetic spinel | It was widely used as an imitation; simple refraction |
| Iolite | purplish blue with very marked pleochroism |
| Tanzanite | purplish blue, softer |
| glass | bubbles, rounded edges and lower hardness |
| Doubles | natural sapphire top bonded to a synthetic base |

## The sapphire in the watch

Many watches boast “sapphire crystal.” It is synthetic, transparent and colorless corundum, manufactured industrially for its enormous scratch resistance. It is an excellent material to protect the sphere, but it has nothing to do with a natural gem. We explain it in the guide on [mechanical watches](/es/relojes-mecanicos/).

## How to choose a sapphire

1. **Look at the color in various lights:** daylight, warm light and shop light; some blues become very dark in artificial light.
2. **Look for uniform brilliance:** Many stones are cut with deep pavilions to conserve weight, and others feature clear “windows” in the center.
3. **Check the color bands** by turning the stone.
4. **Request the treatment declaration** in writing.
5. **Requires a recognized report** for valuable stones, for prestigious origins and for any padparadscha.
6. **Compare size, not just weight:** Two sapphires of the same weight can look very different depending on the size.

The guarantees you have as a buyer are explained in the guide on [buying jewelry in Spain](/es/comprar-joyas-en-espana/).

## Sapphire care

- **Cleaning:** warm water, neutral soap and a soft brush; dry with a cloth.
- **Ultrasounds:** only on stones without fractures or fillings, and better with the approval of the jeweler.
- **Shocks:** Although it is very hard, a sharp blow on an edge can chip it.
- **Storage:** separately, because it scratches almost any other gem.

## Frequent errors

- **To think that only blue sapphires exist.**
- **Pay a premium for an origin** without a report to support it.
- **Buy a padparadscha** without a lab report.
- **Confusing the sapphire crystal** of a watch with a natural gem.
- **Choose only by weight** without looking at size and brightness.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are sapphire and ruby the same stone?

They are the same mineral species, corundum. Red is called ruby ​​and all other colors are called sapphire.

### Is it normal for a sapphire to be heated?

Yes. Most sapphires on the market are. It is a stable and accepted treatment, as long as it is declared.

### What is a white sapphire?

A colorless sapphire. It is used as an alternative to diamond, although it has less brilliance and dispersion.

### Why does a one carat sapphire appear smaller than a diamond?

Because corundum is denser than diamond: the same weight takes up less volume.

## In summary

Sapphire is corundum of any color except red. Its blue is born from iron and titanium, and is valued for a medium tone, vivid saturation and uniform distribution. There are yellow, pink, orange, violet, green, colorless, color change and star sapphires, and the padparadscha is the most sought after and discussed. Heating is common and accepted, but diffusion of beryllium or titanium and fillers changes the value greatly. Before buying, look at the stone in different lights, ask for a declaration of treatments and demand a recognized report for stones of value or prestigious origins.
