Black, light and warm to the touch, jet is one of the most unique gems that exist: it is not a mineral, but fossil wood transformed over millions of years. In Spain it has a very particular history. For centuries, the artisans of Santiago de Compostela carved it for pilgrims in the shape of shells, figures of the apostle and amulets, and one of the cathedral's facades still bears the name of the azabacheros. Much of this material came from the Asturian coast, where the extraction and work of jet have remained a family tradition.

In this guide you will see what jet is and how it is formed, why jet from Asturias is so appreciated, what relationship it has with the Camino de Santiago and with the fig, the amulet against the evil eye, how it is worked, how to distinguish it from its many imitations and how to care for a piece so that it retains its shine for generations.

## What is jet

Jet is a variety of lignite, a charcoal of vegetable origin, compact and homogeneous, which can be polished brilliantly. That is why it is considered an organic gem, just like amber, coral or pearls.

| Property | Jet |
|---|---|
| Origin | fossil wood, transformed by burial and pressure |
| Color | intense black, sometimes with brown hues |
| Brightness | raw velvety and almost mirror-like when polished |
| Hardness on the Mohs scale | low, around 2.5 to 4 |
| Density | very low, somewhat higher than that of water |
| Touch | warm, unlike glass or stone |
| Stripe | brown |
| Other properties | It charges with static electricity when rubbed, like amber |

## From wood to gem

Jet was formed from the trunks of trees, especially ancient conifers, that were washed down rivers and buried in fine sediments, often in low-oxygen marine or coastal environments. Over time, pressure and temperature transformed the wood into a black, compact material. Unlike common charcoal, jet retains a very homogeneous structure, without layers that crumble, which allows it to be carved and polished.

In Asturias, jet appears in Jurassic rocks of the central and eastern coastal strip, the same coast known for its ichnites, fossilized dinosaur footprints. It appears in veins or pockets of irregular size, sometimes with the shape of the original trunks still recognizable.

## The Asturian jet

The area most associated with jet in Asturias is the parish of Oles, in the council of Villaviciosa, although it has been extracted in other parts of the coast. For generations, families in the area looked for it in small galleries and sold it raw or carved, and today some artisans keep that tradition alive.

Asturian jet is valued for its deep black color, its homogeneity and its ability to take an intense polish. It is a scarce material, which is extracted in small quantities and requires a lot of manual work. That is why it is advisable to be wary of large, abundant and very cheap pieces presented as Asturian jet.

## Jet and the Camino de Santiago

Since the Middle Ages, pilgrims arriving in Compostela bought souvenirs that demonstrated their trip and served as protection for their return. Among the most appreciated were the jet pieces: scallop shells, figures of pilgrim Saint James, crosses, rosaries and amulets. The artisans who carved them were organized into a guild of azabacheros, and the north façade of the Santiago cathedral is still known as the Azabachería façade, due to the workshops that were grouped around it.

Many of those pieces traveled throughout Europe and are today preserved in museums and collections. Jet is still worked in Compostela, and the Jacobean pieces continue to be one of the most characteristic souvenirs of the Camino.

## The fig and other amulets

The fig is an amulet in the shape of a closed fist, with the thumb protruding between the index and middle fingers. In the popular tradition of the peninsula, and in many American countries, newborns were placed on it to protect them from the evil eye, and jet was the preferred material to make it.

Today the fig is appreciated above all as a piece of tradition and craftsmanship, a baptism gift with centuries of history. As with any amulet, its value is cultural and sentimental, not proven protection. If you give it as a gift for a baby, also think about safety: small parts and cords should not be left within the child's reach without supervision.

## Jet in the history of jewelry

The use of jet as an ornament is very old: beads and pendants have been found in prehistoric sites in several European countries, and the Romans appreciated it for rings, pendants and small figures. Its big fashion moment came in the 19th century, when mourning jewelry became fashionable in Victorian England and jet from Whitby, on the Yorkshire coast, became the preferred material for restrained brooches, necklaces and earrings. That demand also multiplied the imitations of black glass and ebonite that still appear in antique jewelry.

In Spain, on the other hand, jet was linked above all to devotion and popular tradition: Jacobean pieces, rosaries, crosses and children's amulets. This double life, between international fashion and local crafts, explains the enormous variety of antique pieces found today on the second-hand market, which we talk about in the guide on [period jewelry and styles](/es/joyeria-de-epoca-estilos/).

## How to work jet

The work of jet is slow and almost completely manual:

1. **Selection of the rough:** areas with cracks, impurities or brown layers are discarded.
2. **Cutting and roughing:** with fine saws and files the basic shape is obtained.
3. **Carving:** with knives, files and burins the details are defined, such as the ribs of a scallop or the fingers of a fig.
4. **Sanding:** with increasingly finer abrasives, tool marks are eliminated.
5. **Polishing:** with soft polishes and cloths the characteristic mirror shine is achieved.

Jet dust, like that of any coal, should not be breathed: workshops work with ventilation, aspiration and a mask. If you are interested in stone working, check out the guide on [how to cut a cabochon](/es/como-se-talla-un-cabujon/).

## Jet and imitations

Jet has been imitated for centuries, and some imitations are very convincing.

| Material | How it differs |
|---|---|
| black glass | cooler to the touch, much heavier; may show bubbles or mold marks |
| Ebonite or vulcanite | hardened rubber; It can take on a brownish hue over time and, when heated, gives off a sulfur smell. |
| Plastics and resins | often mold marks and different feel; some give off a chemical smell when heated |
| Dyed onyx or chalcedony | much harder and colder to the touch; They do not scratch easily |
| Obsidian | volcanic glass, colder and heavier, with conchoidal fracture |
| Jet from other countries | authentic material, but not Asturian; its origin must be indicated |

There are home tests, such as bringing a hot needle close to it to notice the smell, but they damage the piece and it is not advisable to do them on a piece of jewelry. If you have questions about a valuable piece, take it to a gemologist, who can identify it without ruining it with non-destructive methods.

## How to buy a piece of jet

- **Ask about the origin** of the material and ask that it appear on the invoice.
- **Buy from identifiable artisans or businesses,** who are responsible for what they sell.
- **Check the polish:** it should be uniform, without streaks or matte areas.
- **Look at the setting:** traditional pieces are usually in silver; Check the marks on the metal and the strength of the fasteners.
- **Be wary of prices that are too low** for large or very elaborate pieces.

## Jet care

Due to its low hardness, jet is easily scratched and can crack with blows or sudden changes in temperature.

- **Store separately** in a cloth bag or lined case, away from stones and metals that could scratch it.
- **Avoid heat:** do not leave it in the sun, next to radiators or in the car in summer.
- **Put the piece on last:** after perfumes, creams and hairsprays, which can dull its shine.
- **Clean with a soft, slightly damp cloth** and dry immediately.
- **No ultrasound, steam or chemicals.**
- **If it loses shine,** a craftsman can re-polish it.

## Frequent errors

- **Confuse jet with black glass** or tinted onyx.
- **Store it with other jewelry** that will scratch it.
- **Clean it with chemicals** or ultrasound.
- **Do heat tests** on a valuable piece.
- **Buy large and cheap pieces** like Asturian jet without asking for guarantees.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is jet a stone?

Not quite. It is an organic gem: fossil wood transformed into a compact variety of lignite that can be carved and polished.

### How do I know if my fig is real jet?

An authentic piece is very light and warm to the touch, and its stripe is brown. But imitations can be deceiving, so for valuable pieces it is best to consult a gemologist.

### Why is jet associated with Santiago?

Because for centuries Compostela artisans carved jet for pilgrims, and the north façade of the cathedral is called Azabachería because of the workshops that surrounded it.

### Can jet be worn daily?

Yes, carefully. It is soft and scratches easily, so it is best to remove it for sports or manual tasks and store it separately.

## In summary

Jet is fossil wood transformed into a compact, black and light lignite, which can be mirror polished. That of Asturias, coming mainly from the Jurassic coast of Villaviciosa, supplied for centuries the azabacheros of Santiago, who carved shells, figures and figs for pilgrims. To recognize it, look at its lightness, its warm touch and its brown stripe, and if you have valuable pieces, go to a gemologist. To care for it, store it aside, protect it from heat and chemicals, and clean it only with a soft cloth.
