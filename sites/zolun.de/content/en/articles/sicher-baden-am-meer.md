A summer day by the sea, the children run into the water, the sun glitters on the waves. Most bathing days on the North and Baltic Seas go exactly the way you want them to. But the sea is not a swimming pool: there are currents that you can hardly see from the outside, wind that can blow an air mattress out in minutes, and water that is significantly colder than the air. Anyone who can read the signs on the beach will swim more relaxed and react correctly in an emergency. This guide explains the flags, the typical dangers of both coasts, how to deal with jellyfish and what to do if someone needs help. It doesn't replace a swimming course or a first aid course, but it does help you recognize the most important situations.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - If possible, swim on patrolled beaches and between the red and yellow flags. Lifeguards are on duty there.
> - Yellow means caution, red means swimming is prohibited. An orange windsock warns of offshore winds.
> - On the Baltic Sea, currents arise primarily on groynes, piers and sandbanks, on the North Sea through the tides and in tidal creeks.
> - If you get caught in a current, don't swim against it, but swim out parallel to the bank and draw attention to yourself.
> - Children in the water need supervision at arm's length who does nothing else. In an emergency, dial 112.

## Guarded swimming areas: why they make the difference

Lifeguards are on duty on many beaches on the North and Baltic Seas during the season, for example from the DLRG, the DRK water rescue service or a local rescue service. You can recognize the guarded sections by the rescue tower and the flags. The guard only applies at certain times and usually only in the high season. When and where is stated on the boards at the beach entrance or at the spa administration.

Lifeguards know their beach. They know where channels formed that day and what the current is as the tide changes. Feel free to ask them before you go into the water, especially if you are traveling with children. In an emergency, outside of guarded times and areas, there will be no one nearby who can help immediately.

## What the flags and signals mean

Today, a uniform system largely applies to German beaches. Individual locations may use additional signs, so it's worth taking a look at the board at the beach entrance.

| signal | Meaning | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Red and yellow flag | Guarded swimming area, lifeguards on duty | Swim between two red and yellow flags |
| Yellow flag | Swimming is dangerous, unsuitable for children, the elderly and inexperienced swimmers | Stay in shallow water or avoid it |
| Red flag | Bathing ban | Don't go into the water |
| Black and white checkered flag | Area for water sports, such as surfing | Don't swim there |
| Orange windsock | Offshore wind | Do not use air mattresses, swimming animals or boards |

The flags change throughout the day. So look not just in the morning, but every time before you go into the water.

## Recognize currents and react correctly

### Ripple currents and groynes on the Baltic Sea

The Baltic Sea often seems harmless, but when there is onshore wind and waves, water is pushed onto the beach. It has to go back again and does so in narrow, strong channels, the so-called rip currents. The areas on groynes, i.e. the rows of wood or stones that lead from the beach into the sea, and on piers are particularly dangerous. There the water flows along the structures towards the sea. Keep a good distance from groynes and do not climb on them, they are slippery and often surrounded by rocks.

A second trap is sandbanks. When the water is calm, children run out onto a sandbank and stand there safely. But behind and in front of them there are deeper gullies in which they can suddenly lose their footing, often right on the way back.

How you can recognize a rip current:

- a strip of calmer, darker water with hardly any waves breaking
- Foam, seaweed or blown sand drifting seaward
- a gap in the surf between otherwise even waves

If you get into it:

1. **Keep calm.** The current is pulling you out, not down.
2. **Don't swim against it.** Even good swimmers quickly lose their strength.
3. **Swim parallel to the shore** until you no longer feel the pull, then diagonally back to the beach.
4. **If that doesn't work: let yourself drift, raise an arm and call.** The pull usually decreases behind the surf zone.

### Tidal currents and tidal creeks on the North Sea

In the North Sea the tide comes into play. When the water runs out, the current moves seaward; when the water comes in, the tidal channels, i.e. the channels in the mudflats, fill up quickly and become deep and rapid. On some islands, swimming is only permitted at certain times around high water. The bathing times are posted on the beach and change every day. Never cross a tidal creek when the water is running up and do not allow yourself to be trapped by the returning water on sandbanks. We explain how to read a tide calendar in [Understanding ebb and flow](/reisen/ebbe-und-flut/).

## Offshore wind, air mattresses and swimming animals

Offshore wind blows from land to sea. The tricky thing is that the water looks particularly calm close to the shore because the land shields the waves. An air mattress, a swimming ring or an inflatable animal floats out faster than you can swim after it. Stand-up paddlers are also often underestimated and carried far out.

Here's how you deal with it:

- Only use air mattresses and floating animals when there is onshore or light wind and only where you can stand.
- Never swim after a floating toy. Let it go and tell the lifeguards so they don't come looking for a human.
- Water wings and swimming rings are not life-saving devices. A child can slip out of a ring or fall upside down with it.
- Anyone paddling wears a life jacket, secures the board with a leash and stays close to the shore.

## Cold water and your own strength

Even in midsummer, the sea is significantly colder than your body. Anyone who jumps in while sweating runs the risk of cold shock: the body reacts with an involuntary inhalation and rapid breathing, and the heart suddenly beats harder. If your head is under water at this moment, it becomes dangerous. So go in slowly, wet your arms, neck and chest first and never jump headfirst into unknown water, because you don't know the depth or the stones underneath.

The body cools down much faster in water than in air. Shaking, blue lips, and clumsy fingers are signs to go out immediately, dry yourself off, and dress warmly.

Assess your strengths honestly. The open sea is not a basin: there is no edge to hold on to, and waves take energy. Swim parallel to the shore rather than out, never alone and never after drinking. If you have heart disease, epilepsy or another illness that can play a role in swimming, discuss with your doctor before your vacation what makes sense. At the first thunder you leave the water immediately.

## Jellyfish: harmless or stinging?

Jellyfish belong to the North and Baltic Seas. Most of the ones you see are harmless, but some are painful.

- **Eye jellyfish:** transparent, plate-shaped, with four clearly visible rings in the middle. Particularly common on the Baltic Sea. Harmless for most people, at most a slight tingling sensation on sensitive skin.
- **Fire jellyfish, also yellow hair jellyfish:** yellowish to reddish-brown, with many long, thin tentacles that float far below the umbrella. Their nettles burn badly and leave red welts.
- **Other stinging species:** In the North Sea, for example, the bluish stinging jellyfish and the compass jellyfish with brown, wedge-shaped stripes are found.

Jellyfish that have been washed up and torn tentacles can still sting. Children should therefore not touch them.

Did a jellyfish get you:

1. **Get out of the water** and do not rub or scratch the area.
2. **Rinse with sea water**, not fresh water, as this can trigger any remaining stinging cells.
3. **Remove tentacle residue carefully**, for example with the edge of a card or tweezers, not with your bare fingers.
4. **Cool afterwards.** Lifeguards will help you, and the pharmacy will give you advice on remedies.
5. **Get medical attention** if large areas are affected, the face or eyes are hit, a small child is affected, or shortness of breath, dizziness, or circulatory problems occur. If you have a strong reaction, dial 112 immediately.

## Children on the water in view

Drowning is quiet. A child who is in distress usually does not call or wave, but instead goes quietly. A few moments of distraction are enough. These rules have proven themselves:

- **Arm length:** Non-swimmers and beginners are always within reach of an adult in the water.
- **A fixed supervision:** One person is always responsible for the water, without a cell phone and without a book. You say the change out loud: “You have the children now.” So not everyone looks a little and no one really looks.
- **Seahorse is not safe to swim:** The badge is a nice first step. According to the DLRG, a child is only considered a safe swimmer when they are a free swimmer, and even then the sea is more demanding than a pool.
- **High Visibility Swimwear:** Bold, bright colors are easier to see in the water than blue or green.
- **Agree on a meeting point:** Agree on a fixed point, such as the lifeguard tower or the number of the beach entrance, and explain to children that they can report to the lifeguards. A bracelet with your phone number also helps.
- **Keep sand holes shallow:** Deep holes can collapse. Dig up to your knee at most and fill it up again at the end.

## In an emergency: help properly without endangering yourself

If you see someone in distress, alert the lifeguards immediately. If no one is on duty, dial 112. Keep an eye on the person and point your arm at them so that the helpers can find them. Throw something that floats, such as a lifebuoy from the pier, a ball, or a board. Only swim out if you are trained to do so, as untrained helpers can endanger their lives.

On the phone, state your location, the section of beach and, if available, the number of the beach entrance or lifeguard tower, and then answer the control center's questions. If you miss a child, report it to the lifeguards immediately. They know how to search the beach and water at the same time. A refreshed first aid course before your vacation will give you additional security.

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

1. **Swim on the empty, unguarded beach.** A few minutes' walk to the guarded section is well spent.
2. **Take the air mattress with you in offshore winds.** Pay attention to the windsock and then leave it on land.
3. **Swim after the ball.** No toy is worth the risk.
4. **Swim close to groynes or piers.** This is where the currents are strongest.
5. **Supervision without consultation.** Determines who is responsible and when.
6. **Jump warmed up into the cold water.** Going in slowly protects against the cold shock.

## Frequently asked questions

### If the flag is red, can I at least go into shallow water?

No. Red means swimming is prohibited because conditions can be dangerous even in shallow water, for example due to surf and back currents. Follow the lifeguards' instructions.

### Do the same rules apply at lakes?

Many basic rules also apply there, but the dangers are different. More about this in [Swimming in lakes and rivers](/reisen/baden-in-seen-und-fluessen/).
