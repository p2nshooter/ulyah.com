Not every city trip has to go to Berlin, Hamburg or Munich. Some of the most beautiful places in Germany are small towns whose old town you can wander through in an afternoon and yet want to stay for days: half-timbered houses leaning over cobblestones, city walls with towers, market squares with fountains and churches that seem much larger than the city would suggest. This guide presents a selection, sorted by region, and explains how you can experience the places without crowds.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Many small old towns are UNESCO World Heritage Sites, such as Bamberg, Quedlinburg, Goslar, Stralsund and Wismar.
> - During the day, many bus groups come to well-known places like Rothenburg. It is much quieter in the morning and evening. An overnight stay is almost always worth it.
> - Parking spaces are usually outside the city walls. Many places are easily accessible by train.
> - Comfortable shoes are mandatory: cobblestones, stairs and wall paths put a strain on your feet.
> - Small towns can be easily combined with nature, for example with the Harz, the Elbe or the Baltic Sea coast.

## Why small old towns?

Small towns have advantages not found in big cities. The distances are short, the atmosphere is quieter, and you get to talk to people who live here. Many of these places have preserved their old town for centuries because they were less destroyed during the war or because there was no money later for demolition and new construction. Today they are living museums in which everyday life still takes place: bakers, weekly markets, schoolchildren on the way home.

At the same time, small old towns are sensitive. When ten coaches arrive at midday, the market square is full. If you come early or stay overnight, you will experience the place from its most beautiful side.

## Franconia and Bavaria

**Rothenburg ob der Tauber** is the prime example of a medieval city. The city wall has largely been preserved, and you can walk along large parts of it on the covered battlements. The Plönlein, a small fork in the road with a half-timbered house and two gate towers, is one of the most famous photo motifs in Germany. In the evening, a night watchman leads visitors through the streets and tells stories from the city's history. Try a snowball, a pastry made from strips of dough. Rothenburg is on the Romantic Road and is very crowded during the day.

**Dinkelsbühl**, also on the Romantic Road, is smaller and quieter than Rothenburg, with well-preserved city fortifications, towers and colorful gabled houses. If you already know Rothenburg or avoid crowds, this is the right place for you.

**Bamberg** is larger, but its old town as a whole is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The Old Town Hall stands on a small island in the middle of the Regnitz, the fishermen's houses on the shore are called Little Venice, and the cathedral and residence are enthroned on the Domberg. Bamberg is also a beer town with many breweries. The smoked beer is well known, the malt of which is dried over a beech wood fire.

## In the Harz

**Quedlinburg** on the northern edge of the Harz is a half-timbered wonder. Around 2,000 half-timbered houses from several centuries line the streets, and the collegiate church, castle and old town are UNESCO World Heritage Sites. The town is quiet, with cafes on the market square and beautiful paths up the Schlossberg.

**Wernigerode** is known for its colorful town hall with pointed towers and the castle that stands on a hill above the city. The Brockenbahn, a steam train that runs up to the highest mountain in the Harz Mountains, starts from here.

**Goslar** combines a magnificent old town with mining history. The old town, the Imperial Palace and the former Rammelsberg ore mine are together World Heritage Sites. You can go underground in the mine on guided tours.

The three cities are close to each other and can easily be combined to form a tour through the Harz, with hikes and nature trips in between.

## Saxony

**Meißen** on the Elbe is the cradle of European porcelain. In the porcelain factory you can watch craftsmen at work. The Albrechtsburg Castle and the Gothic cathedral stand on a rock above the old town. From Dresden you can reach Meißen by S-Bahn or, in summer, by steamboat on the Elbe.

**Görlitz** in the far east is one of the best-preserved cities in Germany, with Gothic, Renaissance, Baroque and Wilhelminian style buildings. Because of its backdrop, films have often been shot here. You can walk to Zgorzelec in Poland via the Old Town Bridge.

## Northern Germany

**Stralsund** and **Wismar** on the Baltic Sea coast are both UNESCO World Heritage Sites as examples of Hanseatic brick Gothic. In Stralsund, the Oceaneum, a large maritime museum at the harbor, is worth visiting. Wismar impresses with its old harbor, a wide market square and the mighty brick churches. You can combine both cities well with a beach holiday. More about the coast in the guide [The Baltic Sea coast from west to east] (/reisen/ostseekueste-regionen/).

**Celle** in Lower Saxony has an old town with more than 400 half-timbered houses and a castle with one of the oldest theaters in Germany. The city lies on the edge of the Lüneburg Heath, which blooms purple in late summer.

## west and south

**Monschau** in the Eifel, near the Belgian border, is located in a narrow valley of the Rur. Slate roofs and half-timbered buildings crowd the river, and a castle stands above everything. The place is also known for its mustard mill and, in Advent, for an atmospheric Christmas market.

**Tübingen** am Neckar is a lively university town with steep streets, a market square with a town hall and the Hölderlin Tower on the bank. In summer, punts glide over the Neckar, similar to gondolas, only with long poles. Because of the students, the city is younger and more lively than many other old towns.

## This way you can experience small old towns without the crowds

- **Come early or stay overnight.** Popular places are almost empty before ten in the morning and after five in the evening. The light is then at its most beautiful anyway.
- **Travel during the week.** Lots of day trippers come on weekends when the weather is good.
- **Keep an eye on bus parking areas.** If there are a lot of coaches there, start your tour at the other end of town.
- **Book a tour.** City guides will show you farms and stories that you can't find on your own. Night watchman tours are particularly atmospheric.
- **Explore side streets.** You are often alone a few meters away from the main street.

## Arrival and parking

Many small old towns have a train station or are on a regional train line. From there it is usually a short walk to the center. With the Germany ticket you can visit several cities one after the other. More about this in the guide [Go on vacation with the Germany ticket](/reisen/deutschlandticket-im-urlaub/).

If you come by car, you usually park in signposted parking spaces in front of the city wall. You are often only allowed to go into the old town itself for loading and unloading if your hotel is there. Ask beforehand where you can park.

## Overnight in the old town

A room in an old half-timbered house or an inn on the market square is part of the experience. But note:

- **Stairs instead of elevator.** In historic houses there are often steep stairs and no elevator.
- **Bells ringing.** Nearby churches may ring early in the morning.
- **Small rooms.** Historic homes have low ceilings and sloping floors, which is part of the charm.

## Small old towns with children

Children often find old cities more exciting than adults expect if they are told correctly. A few ideas:

- **On the Wall.** A tour of the city wall with embrasures and defensive towers feels like an adventure for children.
- **Night watchman and tower guard.** Evening tours with lanterns and stories are often the highlight of the trip for school children.
- **On the go with tasks.** Many tourist information centers offer city rallies or children's tours in which coats of arms, dates or figures on houses are looked for.
- **Nature in between.** After a morning in the old town, plan an afternoon in the park, by the river or in the forest.

Cobblestones are difficult with a stroller. A stretcher is a better choice in many old towns. Further tips in the guide [Holiday in Germany with children] (/reisen/urlaub-mit-kindern/).

## Travel time

Small old towns are attractive all year round. **Spring and autumn** are ideal: pleasant temperatures, fewer visitors and colorful foliage on the city walls in autumn. **In summer** the well-known places are full, smaller towns remain pleasant. **During Advent**, many old towns with Christmas markets are transformed into picture-book backdrops, and accommodations are quickly booked out on the weekend. Tips on this can be found in the guide [Visit Christmas markets in Germany] (/reisen/weihnachtsmaerkte/).

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

1. **Only come at midday.** That's when it's the most crowded and the light is the harshest.
2. **Too many cities in one day.** Two old towns per day is enough, one is often better.
3. **Drive into the old town.** Alleys are narrow and access roads are often closed.
4. **Wear uncomfortable shoes.** Cobblestones are tiring.
5. **Only see the main attraction.** The most beautiful corners are often in the side streets.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which small old town is the most beautiful in Germany?

There is no right answer to this. Rothenburg and Quedlinburg are often mentioned, Bamberg and Görlitz inspire with their diversity, and Monschau or Meißen with their location on the water. Choose by region and interests.

### How much time does it take to visit a small old town?

Two to three hours are enough for a tour. If you want to visit museums, eat well and experience the atmosphere in the evening, stay at least one night.

### Can several cities be combined into one tour?

Yes. The Romantic Road with Rothenburg and Dinkelsbühl, a Harz tour with Quedlinburg, Wernigerode and Goslar or the Baltic Sea coast with Wismar and Stralsund are popular.
