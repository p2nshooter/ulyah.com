A dark head appears between the waves, two large eyes look curiously at the beach, then it disappears again. Anyone who sees a seal on the North Sea rarely forgets this moment. And anyone standing on the Bodden coast in October, when thousands of cranes fly trumpeting to their roosts at sunset, will experience one of the most impressive natural spectacles in Europe. The German coast is a wildlife watching paradise if you know when and where to look. This guide shows you the best opportunities and explains how to observe animals without disturbing them.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Seals and gray seals live in the Wadden Sea. The best way to see them is on seal trips on excursion boats or on the Heligoland dune.
> - Seals have their pups in early summer, gray seals in winter.
> - If you find a single seal pup on the beach, do not touch it and keep your distance. Inform the seal station, the police or the national park administration.
> - The Wadden Sea is one of the most important resting points for migratory birds in the world. The flocks are largest in spring and autumn.
> - In autumn, tens of thousands of cranes rest on the Western Pomeranian Bodden coast.

## Harbor seals and gray seals

Two species of seal live on the German coast. The **seal** is smaller, has a round head and a short snout that almost looks like a dog's face. It is the most common seal in the Wadden Sea. The **grey seal** is significantly larger, the bulls become very heavy, and their head is more elongated, with a straight, almost horse-like nose. It is the largest free-living predator in Germany.

When the tide is low, seals rest on sandbanks in the mud flats and digest their food. They are very sensitive to disturbances. If a boat or person comes too close to them, they flee into the water. This costs energy that they actually need for hunting and raising their young.

| | seal | Gray seal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| size | small, rounded head | significantly larger, elongated head |
| Birth of the boys | around June to July | around November to January |
| Cubs | can swim shortly after birth | stay on land for the first few weeks, with white fur |
| Good observation sites | Sandbanks in the Wadden Sea, by ship | Helgoland dune, sandbanks off Amrum and isolated in the Baltic Sea |

### Seal trips

In many ports on the North Sea coast and on the islands, excursion ships leave for the seal beds during the season. The ships approach the animals to a distance permitted by the national park's conservation rules, and the crew explains what you see. Some cruises also offer a fishing viewing experience, where a net is briefly cast and crabs, fish and starfish are displayed before they return to the sea.

There are seal trips, for example, from ports in East Frisia, from Büsum, from North Frisia and from many islands. Departure times depend on the tide because the seals lie on the banks at low tide. Binoculars are worth it.

### Gray seals on Heligoland

On the Helgoland Dune, the small bathing island off the main island of Heligoland, gray seals and harbor seals often lie directly on the beach. This is an impressive sight, especially in winter when the gray seals have their pups. There are fixed distance rules in place, which are pointed out by signs and rangers. Be sure to stop them because gray seals can bite and mothers defend their young. You can find out more about the island in the guide [Helgoland](/reisen/helgoland/).

### Seal stations

There are seal stations in Norddeich and Friedrichskoog that nurture orphaned or sick young animals and later release them back into the wild. Both have exhibits and pools where you can see seals up close. A visit is particularly worthwhile with children and is good for days with bad weather.

## A howler on the beach: what to do?

A howler is a young seal that has been separated from its mother and calls out to her, hence the name. However, not every seal pup is alone on the beach. The mother often goes out looking for food and returns when there are no people around.

This is how you behave correctly:

1. **Do not touch.** Seals can bite and transmit disease. In addition, the mother may no longer accept a young that smells like a human.
2. **Keep your distance.** At least a few hundred meters so that the mother can come back. Be sure to keep dogs on a leash.
3. **Do not feed or carry into water.**
4. **Report.** Inform the nearest seal station, the police, the national park administration or a seal hunter. The numbers are often written on the beach entrances.
5. **Keep other people away** when onlookers gather.

## Migratory birds in the Wadden Sea

The Wadden Sea is a huge gas station for migratory birds. Millions of birds stop here on their way between breeding grounds in the Arctic and winter quarters in Western Europe and Africa. In the mudflats you can find worms, mussels and snails in large quantities.

Particularly impressive are:

- **Brent Geese**, small dark geese that rest in large numbers on the salt marshes and Halligen in spring before flying on to Siberia. On the Halligen, this event is celebrated every spring with special days of action.
- **Knutts and Dunlins** circling over the mudflats in huge flocks, changing their shape like a cloud.
- **Oystercatchers**, black and white birds with bright red beaks, whose loud calls are part of the sound of the coast.
- **Avocet** with its beak curved upwards.

The best times are spring, around April and May, and late summer to autumn, around August to October. Every year in October Migratory Bird Days take place in the Wadden Sea, with guided tours, lectures and observation stations.

### Bird Watching Tips

- **Take binoculars with you.** Without binoculars you will only see dots of many species.
- **Observe when the water is rising.** When the tide comes in, the birds crowd into the last dry areas near the dike. Then you can see them particularly well.
- **Use guided tours.** National park rangers and nature conservation groups offer bird tours.
- **Keep your distance.** Do not scare away resting birds. Every escape costs them energy to continue their flight.

## Cranes on the Bodden coast

In autumn, the Western Pomeranian lagoon landscape becomes one of the most important resting places for cranes in Europe. Between the end of August and the beginning of November, tens of thousands of birds from Scandinavia and the Baltics gather here. During the day they feed in harvested fields, and in the evening they fly to their sleeping places in the shallow water of the Bodden.

The arrival of the cranes at sunset is the highlight. Dozens of groups march across the sky in a wedge formation, trumpeting. Good places are the region around Zingst and the Darß peninsula as well as the area between Rügen and the mainland. At the crane information center in Groß Mohrdorf you can learn a lot about the birds and get tips on observation sites. There are also viewing platforms and guided crane tours. You can find out more about the region in the guide [The Baltic Sea coast at a glance] (/reisen/ostseekueste-regionen/).

**Important:** Cranes are very shy. Keep a good distance, stay on the designated paths and platforms and avoid loud noises. Cars should not stop at the edges of fields if the birds are feeding there.

## Porpoises and little discoveries on the beach

Hardly anyone knows that there are whales off the German coast. The harbor porpoise is a small toothed whale, barely larger than a human, and is the only species of whale that regularly lives in German waters. It is quite common in the North Sea, especially west of Sylt. There is a protected area there where many porpoises raise their young. It is significantly rarer in the Baltic Sea. With a bit of luck, when the sea is calm, you will see from a ferry or the beach a small triangular dorsal fin that briefly appears and disappears again.

Small animals are often the most exciting for children. When the tide is low you will find sand piles of lugworms, cockles, periwinkles and small crabs in the mudflats. On the Baltic Sea you can discover shrimps, sticklebacks and mussels on stones in shallow bays. A magnifying glass, a bucket of sea water and an identification book turn every walk on the beach into a small expedition. Then put the animals back exactly where you found them. Many national park houses also offer guided tours for children, during which rangers show what lives in the mudflats and along the mudflats.

## Protection rules in the national park

The Wadden Sea and the Western Pomerania Lagoon landscape are national parks. Rules to protect animals apply there:

| rule | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Stay on paths and do not enter protection zones | Breeding and resting places are not disturbed |
| Keep dogs on a leash | Birds and seals flee from dogs |
| Don't fly drones | Birds react strongly to drones |
| Keep your distance from seals and flocks of birds | every escape costs energy |
| Take nothing with you except memories | Shells and plants are part of the habitat |

Information centers and national park houses on the coast explain the rules and offer exciting exhibitions.

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

1. **Get too close to seals.** A good photo isn't worth scaring away animals.
2. **Want to save a Howler.** The only thing that helps is: keep your distance and report it.
3. **Go without binoculars.** You'll hardly see birds in particular otherwise.
4. **Come at the wrong time.** You can see seals when the tide is low, birds best when the tide is up, and cranes in autumn.
5. **Let the dog run free.** This is forbidden and harmful in protected areas.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you see seals from the beach?

Sometimes yes, especially on the islands and quiet beaches. A seal trip is more reliable because the ships head for the sand banks where the animals lie.

### When is the best time for cranes?

Most cranes rest on the Bodden coast from around mid-September to mid-October. The exact number varies from year to year and depends on the weather.

### Are seal trips suitable for children?

Yes, they are a popular family outing. Pack warm clothing as it is often cool and windy on the water.
