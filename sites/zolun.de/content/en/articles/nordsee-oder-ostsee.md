Anyone planning a holiday on the German coast for the first time quickly ends up with a fundamental question: North Sea or Baltic Sea? The neighbor swears by one, the colleague swears by the other. The honest answer is: both coasts are wonderful, but they feel fundamentally different. On the North Sea the sea comes and goes, on the Baltic Sea it almost always stays where it is. One coast is flat, wide and characterized by mudflats, the other alternates between cliffs, bays, fjords and forests. This comparison will help you decide not based on reputation, but rather based on your vacation days: What do you do in the morning, what in the afternoon, and what does your child, your dog or both of you need to make it feel like relaxation?

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - The North Sea has tides: the water drains and rises twice a day. In many places on the mainland, the tide determines when you can swim.
> - The Baltic Sea has no noticeable tides in everyday life. Their water level changes primarily due to wind.
> - The North Sea means mudflats, dikes, salt marshes and wide skies. The Baltic Sea means cliffs, bodden, forest all the way to the beach, pier and resort architecture.
> - The Baltic Sea is often less complicated for families with small children, while the North Sea is easier for nature fans who want to explore the mud flats and open spaces.
> - You can reach Baltic Sea islands such as Rügen, Usedom and Fehmarn via bridges, while most North Sea islands can only be reached by ship, train or plane.

## The biggest difference: tides or almost none

The North Sea is open to the Atlantic. The moon and sun pull on the water, and this creates the rhythm of ebb and flow: the water drains out for around six hours and comes back up again around six hours later. Because this cycle does not exactly match our day, high and low water shifts from day to day, on average by almost an hour.

On many mainland locations in the North Sea, when the tide is low, there is a wide, shiny mud flat in front of the dike, and the sea is far out. Swimming there is only possible around flood times. Things are different on the islands: the beaches on the side facing the open sea have water within reach even at low tide, but the waves and currents are stronger. Anyone who goes on holiday on the North Sea should check the tide calendar every day. The Federal Maritime and Hydrographic Agency publishes the official forecasts, and many municipalities also post the times. We explain how you can read such a calendar and experience the mudflats safely in [Understanding ebb and flow](/reisen/ebbe-und-flut/).

The Baltic Sea is an almost closed sea that is only connected to the North Sea via narrow straits. Tides physically exist, but you don't notice them during your everyday vacation. The water level here fluctuates primarily with the wind: if a strong wind from the sea pushes water onto the coast, the level rises; if it blows from land to sea for a long time, it falls. Flooding can occur during storms in autumn and winter. In summer, the sea is there morning and evening, and you can spontaneously go into the water.

**If this helps:** Anyone who likes to live according to plan and looks forward to a daily rhythm often finds the tides fascinating. Anyone who wants to remain spontaneous with small children sometimes finds the constant thinking a burden.

## Landscape: mud flats and dike or cliffs and bay

### The North Sea: flat, wide and lively

The German North Sea coast is dominated by the Wadden Sea, which is protected in three national parks and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The mudflats seem empty at first glance, but they are full of life: lugworms leave behind their curly piles of sand, crabs and mussels live in the mud, and migratory birds rest in their thousands. Behind the coastline lie green dikes with grazing sheep, salt marshes that bloom purple in late summer, and flat marshland with canals and ditches. The islands have high dunes and long sandy beaches. The sky is huge and the light is constantly changing.

### The Baltic Sea: varied and often forested

The Baltic Sea coast is more fragmented. In Schleswig-Holstein, fjords cut deep into the country, such as the Flensburg and Kiel Fjords, plus the long, narrow Schlei. Further east there are steep banks made of clay and chalk, most famously the chalk cliffs on Rügen. Behind peninsulas like Fischland-Darß-Zingst and behind Rügen lie the bays, shallow lagoons with reeds and fishing villages. In many places, forest extends right down to the beach, so after swimming you walk back through pine or beech trees. Sandy beaches alternate with stony sections.

A note about cliffs: The edges keep breaking off, even without warning. Keep your distance from the edge at the top and the foot of the wall at the bottom, and pay attention to closed paths.

## Water, wind and weather

The North Sea is saltier and there is usually a fresh wind blowing there. Many find this so-called stimulating climate to be invigorating. The waves are noticeable on open beaches, the currents in tidal creeks are treacherous because the water quickly comes back when the tide is rising.

The Baltic Sea is a brackish sea with significantly less salt. After bathing, the skin feels less salty and the waves are gentler on many days. However, the Baltic Sea is not always calm: when there is an onshore wind, strong surf and back currents arise, especially on groynes and piers.

There is no general answer as to which coast has the warmer water in summer. It depends on the weather over the past few weeks, the wind and the location. On the Baltic Sea, offshore wind can even bring cooler water from the depths to the surface, making it suddenly cool on a sunny day. Expect refreshing water on both coasts and pack a warm layer for each day. Wind also means that you often only notice sunburn in the evening.

The following applies on both coasts: It is best to swim on patrolled sections of the beach and follow the lifeguard flags. What the flags mean and how you can recognize currents can be found in [Swim safely in the North and Baltic Seas](/reisen/sicher-baden-am-meer/).

## Atmosphere of the places

On the North Sea, small sluice harbors dominate the picture, where colorful shrimp boats moor, as well as brick churches on terps, thatched roof houses and dikes on which you can walk for miles. In East Frisia, tea time with Kluntje and cream is part of everyday life. The seaside resorts on the mainland are more practical than splendid, while the islands with promenades, spa houses and dunes seem like a world of their own.

On the Baltic Sea you will encounter resort architecture: white villas with decorated balconies and verandas that were built during the time of the large seaside resorts in the 19th and early 20th centuries. Long piers lead out onto the water, and promenades invite you to stroll. Heiligendamm is considered the oldest seaside resort in Germany. In addition, old Hanseatic cities such as Lübeck, Wismar, Rostock and Stralsund are close to the coast. This is a real advantage when the weather doesn't cooperate.

## Arrival and islands

The North Sea is often closer from North Rhine-Westphalia and the west of Lower Saxony, and the Baltic Sea is usually closer from Berlin, Brandenburg and Saxony. From Hamburg you can reach both coasts in a manageable amount of time. From southern Germany, every coast is a long journey that can be made more relaxing with an overnight stay or a train ride.

When it comes to the islands, the difference lies in how you get there. The large Baltic Sea islands of Rügen, Usedom and Fehmarn are connected to the mainland via bridges, the small island of Poel via a dam. You drive over by car, and you can also take the regional train to Rügen and Usedom. You can only reach Hiddensee by boat and there are no private cars there.

On the North Sea, the crossing is part of the holiday. You can reach the East Frisian Islands, Föhr, Amrum, most of Halligen and Helgoland by ship, and some islands can also be reached by small island planes. Some ferries base their departure times on the tide. Many North Sea islands are car-free; you leave the car on the mainland. Sylt is a special case: the island can be reached by train via the Hindenburg Dam, and cars are taken on a car train.

## With children, with a dog or as a couple

**With small children** the Baltic Sea is often the easier choice. The water is always there, many sandy beaches are shallow, and the waves are small on calm days. The North Sea has something that no playground can offer: At low tide, the mudflats are a huge, hands-on natural history museum. On the islands children have wide sandy beaches and dunes, on the mainland you will find guarded swimming areas and play areas on the dikes in many places. Plan beach time there after the tide.

**With a dog** you will find designated dog beaches on both coasts, often with clear boundaries and separate entrances. In national parks and protected areas, additional rules apply, such as keeping dogs on a leash during the breeding season. Each community determines what is permitted. Check your vacation destination's rules before booking.

**As a couple** the choice depends on what relaxes you. The North Sea offers vast, guided mudflat hikes, bike tours on the dike and sunsets over the open sea on the west-facing beaches. The Baltic Sea offers promenades, piers, Hanseatic towns, forest paths and a coast that can be easily explored by bike.

## Typical mistakes when choosing and how to avoid them

1. **Do not check the bathing beach.** On the North Sea, the beach in front of the dike is sometimes a grassy beach with a swimming area, not a sandy beach. Before booking, look at photos and maps of the specific beach section.
2. **Ignore the tides.** If you book a week on the mainland North Sea with a small child who wants to splash around all day, you should know that the water is sometimes far away. A place with a swimming area independent of the tide or an island may then be a better choice.
3. **Think of the Baltic Sea as a warm sea.** It can be pleasant in midsummer, but remains a northern sea. Neoprene shirts or bathing shoes for rocky beaches make bathing easier for children.
4. **Underestimate the wind.** A beach chair or wind shell is more than comfort on both coasts. Without wind protection, a long day at the beach quickly becomes uncomfortable.
5. **Book the island without a plan in high season.** Ferries with car parking, luggage transport and accommodation are fully booked early during the summer holidays. Clarify your arrival before you book the holiday apartment.

## Which coast suits whom?

| If that's important to you | tendency | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bathe at any time of day | Baltic Sea | Hardly noticeable tides, the water is always there |
| Nature experience Watt | North Sea | Wadden Sea with guided hikes and birdlife |
| Toddlers in the water | more like the Baltic Sea | Often gently sloping sandy beaches and small waves |
| Children who like to explore | both | Watts on the North Sea, stone beaches and shells on the Baltic Sea |
| Dog | both | There are dog beaches on both coasts, rules per municipality |
| Island feeling with a crossing | North Sea | Most islands only by boat, many are car-free |
| By car to the island | Baltic Sea | Rügen, Usedom and Fehmarn can be reached via bridges |
| Cities and culture nearby | Baltic Sea | Hanseatic cities like Lübeck, Wismar and Stralsund close to the coast |
| Fresh wind and space | North Sea | Open coast, big skies, stronger surf |
| Forest and shadows on the beach | Baltic Sea | In many places the forest extends right up to the dunes |

If you still can't decide based on this table, both coasts are probably suitable. Go one way this year and the other the next.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I swim in the North Sea at low tide?

Usually not on the mainland because the water is far out and the mudflats are not a swimming area. On the islands, water is within reach on the beach facing the open sea, even at low tide. Only swim in patrolled areas and ask the lifeguards how the current behaves when the tide changes.

### Is the Baltic Sea better suited for a first coastal vacation?

For many, yes, because you can plan without a tide calendar and the islands are accessible by car. If nature, mudflats and space appeal to you, the North Sea is just as good a place to start. More important than the coast is the right place with the right beach.
