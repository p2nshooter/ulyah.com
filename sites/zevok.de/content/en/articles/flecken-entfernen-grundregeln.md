A glass of red wine tips over at the birthday dinner, the tomato sauce drips onto the light-colored shirt in the canteen, the child comes out of daycare with cocoa on his sweater. In moments like this, the same thing almost always happens: you grab a napkin and rub it vigorously, hold the fabric under hot water or pour the first thing you come across from the bathroom cupboard onto it. It is precisely this first minute that often decides whether a stain will come out later or stay forever.

The good news: You don't need a shelf full of special products. If you know a few basic rules and can determine the type of stain, you can fix most mishaps yourself. Here you will find out what you should do immediately, how to distinguish between types of stains, why some stains only tolerate cold water and when it is better to clean textiles.

## Act correctly immediately: the first few minutes

The fresher a stain is, the easier it is to remove. A stain that has dried, set with heat or rubbed deep into the fabric is much more stubborn. That's why it's important to act as quickly as possible, even if you only have water and a cloth at your disposal.

1. **Remove coarse parts.** Remove solid residues such as sauce, porridge or soil with a spoon or the back of a dull knife. Do not push it over the fabric, but lift it upwards.
2. **Blot instead of rubbing.** Press a clean, light-colored cloth or paper towel onto the stain, remove it, and repeat with a fresh spot. Rubbing pushes the liquid deeper into the fibers, enlarging the stain and roughening the surface, which can result in light or matted areas.
3. **Work from the outside in.** Start at the edge and work your way toward the center. This means the spot becomes smaller instead of larger, and an edge is less likely to form.
4. **Use an absorbent surface.** Place a folded white cloth or several layers of kitchen paper under the area. The stain should migrate into the base, not into the second layer of fabric or onto the table top. For pants and shirts, the underlay belongs between the front and back.
5. **Rinse from the reverse side.** Hold the fabric, stain side down, under the stream of cold water. This way you can flush the stain out of the fibers as quickly as possible instead of pushing it through the entire fabric.

When you're on the go, step two with a napkin and some cold water is often enough. The only important thing is that you don't do anything that fixes the stain until you get home: no hot water, no hand dryer, no hairdryer.

## Recognize stain types

Stains behave very differently depending on their components. If you know what you're dealing with, you'll know which treatment will help and which will hurt. This overview classifies the most common stains in the household:

| Stain type | Examples | First measure | Cold or warm? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water soluble | Lemonade, syrup, sugar water, salt rims from road salt | Rinse with water, then wash normally | cold to lukewarm |
| Fatty | Cooking oil, butter, frying fat, salad dressing, skin cream, chain oil from bicycles | Dab off excess fat, incorporate dishwashing liquid or gall soap | as warm as the care label allows |
| Protein-rich | Blood, egg, milk, sweat, milk porridge | Rinse immediately with cold water, no heat | definitely cold |
| Tanning and dye | Red wine, coffee, tea, blueberries, cherries, beetroot | Rinse immediately with cold water, do not allow to dry | first cold, warmer when washing, if permitted |
| pigment | Earth, mud, soot, pencil, dust | Allow to dry, brush or vacuum, then wash | first dry, then according to label |
| Combination stains | Cocoa, chocolate, lipstick, makeup, tomato sauce, grass, pen | Treat the components one after the other: first protein, then fat, then color | first cold, then warmer |

When it comes to combination stains, it's worth taking a look at the ingredients. Cocoa contains milk, i.e. protein, fat and brown dye. If you rinse it with hot water first, the milk will coagulate and retain the color in the tissue. If you first treat it with cold water and then use a little detergent to remove the grease, you often end up with just a light shadow that normal washing removes. It's similar with tomato sauce: first dissolve the fat, then treat the red dye.

If you don't know what caused the stain, first treat it like you would a protein stain: cold, without heat, with careful blotting. This means you won't damage anything with any type of stain and you can always switch to warmer water or another agent afterwards.

## Why protein stains are treated cold

Protein changes with heat. You know this from breakfast eggs: the liquid, transparent egg white turns solid and white when cooked, and this process cannot be reversed. The same thing happens with blood, milk or sweat in the tissue. Hot water causes the protein to coagulate, causing it to stick to the fibers and often turn yellowish or brownish. What would have come out almost on its own with cold water is now stuck.

For protein-containing stains this means: cold water for rinsing, no hot water when pre-treating and no dryer or iron as long as something is still visible. Only when the stain has disappeared can the item be washed at the temperature permitted by the label. Many detergents contain enzymes that break down protein. They work at low to medium temperatures and help with soaking. An exception are wool and silk: These fibers themselves consist of protein, which is why they belong in a wool or delicate detergent without protein-splitting enzymes.

By the way, the heat rule doesn't just apply to protein. Almost any stain becomes more stubborn when placed in the dryer or under the iron. After washing, check when wet and again in daylight to see whether the stain is really gone, and if in doubt, let the item air dry.

## First the care label, then the material test

Before applying any product, check the care label. It tells you what material the item is made of, how warm you can wash it and whether bleach is allowed. For example, a crossed-out triangle means: no bleach, and therefore no stain remover with oxygen bleach. If it says that the item cannot be washed, dry cleaning is usually the safer option. We explain what the individual symbols mean in the guide [Understanding care symbols] (/ratgeber/pflegesymbole/).

The test then follows in a hidden location. This is how you do it:

1. Find a place that won't be seen later: the inside seam allowance, the hem, the inside of a bag or, in the case of upholstery, the back of a pillow.
2. Put a drop of the product on it, exactly as you would like to use it on the stain, diluted or undiluted.
3. Wait a few minutes and then dab it with a white cloth. If the cloth stains, the color bleeds out.
4. Allow the area to dry completely and examine it in daylight. Only now do you recognize edges, bright spots or a changed surface.

If nothing shows up, you can treat the stain. If the color or structure changes, leave it out. You should be particularly careful with silk, wool, viscose, velvet, leather and fabrics with coatings or prints.

## The little stain toolbox

For the vast majority of everyday stains, just a few things are enough. A separate special cleaner for each type of stain is not necessary.

| Means | Above all, it helps | What you pay attention to |
|---|---|---|
| Dishwashing liquid | Fat, oil, sauces, make-up | just a few drops, rinse thoroughly, otherwise a border will remain |
| Gall soap | Fat, protein and lots of everyday stains | Work in, leave to act briefly, rinse; Test on colored and sensitive fabrics first |
| Oxygen bleach (stain salt) | Tanning agents and dyes, graying | only for white and colorfast fabrics, not for wool and silk; Pay attention to the bleach symbol |
| Alcohol (methylated spirits or isopropanol) | Ballpoint pen, grass, some dyes | highly flammable, only with the window open and away from flames; Test color fastness |
| White cloths, kitchen paper, soft brush | Dab, underlay, brush out | only white cloths so that no color transfers to the stain |

The guide [How to use gall soap correctly](/ratgeber/gallseife/) shows you how to dose gall soap correctly and where it reaches its limits. We have deliberately not included bleaches containing chlorine in our toolbox: they attack many fibers, bleach colors and must never be mixed with acids or cleaners containing ammonia.

Three rules apply to all remedies. Firstly: only use one product at a time and rinse thoroughly with water before using the next one. Mixtures have no advantage and can be dangerous. Secondly: little resources, but several rounds. A large amount will spread the stain and leave residue that will later attract new dirt. Third: All products remain in their original packaging and out of the reach of children and pets. You can find out more about this on our page [Safety when cleaning](/sicherheit-beim-putzen/).

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

- **Hot water on unknown stains:** It fixes protein and often also dyes. Always start cold.
- **Strong vigorously:** This spreads the stain and damages the fiber surface. Blot and rinse instead.
- **Only work on the stain from the front:** This will press it through the entire fabric. Rinse from the back.
- **Too much medium:** Residue forms edges and quickly turns gray again. It's better to use a little and rinse several times.
- **Put it in the dryer:** Heat makes residual stains permanent. First check, then dry.
- **Water marks on sensitive fabrics:** If only the stain area gets wet, loosened dirt will dry on the edge. Lightly moisten the area and pat from the outside in so that the moisture drains out gently.
- **Adopt tips from the Internet without checking:** Salt on red wine, hairspray on a ballpoint pen or mixtures of several home remedies often do not deliver what they promise. We check which classics are suitable in [Home Remedies Myths in Check](/ratgeber/hausmittel-mythen/).

## When textile cleaning is the better choice

Not every stain belongs in your own hands. Professional cleaning makes sense if:

- the care label prohibits washing and only requires professional care,
- the item is made of silk, velvet, leather, suede or fur,
- it is a jacket, coat or dress with inlays, lining or elaborate workmanship,
- the stain is large, old or of unknown origin,
- the piece is valuable or has high personal value, such as a wedding dress or an heirloom.

Bring the item there as soon as possible and say what caused the stain and whether you have tried anything yet. The less experimentation done beforehand, the better the chances are, because an unsuitable home remedy can change a stain to such an extent that even experts can no longer remove it. However, no one can promise guaranteed success with old stains.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a stain still come out after washing?

Often yes, as long as the item has not been tumble dried or ironed. When wet, treat the area again using the appropriate method and wash again. If the stain is fixed by heat, the chances decrease significantly.

### How long can a product last?

Follow the instructions on the packaging. With dishwashing liquid or gall soap, a few minutes are usually enough. Do not let the agent dry on the fabric, as new edges will easily appear.

### What to do about an old, unknown stain?

First soak the item in cold water with a little detergent and then work your way from mild to stronger: dishwashing liquid, gall soap and, for suitable fabrics, oxygen bleach, each time with a thorough rinse in between. For valuable items, textile cleaning is the better choice.

## Briefly summarized

Act quickly, pat instead of rubbing, work from the outside in and rinse from the back. Determine the type of stain: egg whites always cold, grease with detergent and heat, brush out pigments first when dry, combination stains step by step. Read the care label beforehand, test each product on a hidden area and only use one product at a time. Valuable, sensitive items and old stains of unknown origin belong in the textile cleaning department.
