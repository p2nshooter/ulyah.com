Tiles themselves are quickly wiped off. What makes a bathroom or kitchen look old and unkempt are usually the joints. Over the years, light gray has turned into a mottled dark gray, black dots appear in the corners of the shower, and the joints on the kitchen floor have turned into dark lines between clean tiles. The reason: Joints are rough and often porous. Dirt, soap residue, grease and moisture get stuck where a wipe can hardly reach.

With the right tools and a little patience, most joints become significantly lighter. In this guide you will find out what types of joints there are and what they can withstand, how to clean cement joints thoroughly, what applies to silicone and mold and how to care for joints so that they stay clean for a long time.

## What joints do you have?

Not every joint is the same. Before you choose a remedy, you should know what you are dealing with.

| Type of joint | Where she sits | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| cement joint | between the tiles on the wall and floor | mineral, porous, absorbs dirt, sensitive to acid |
| Silicone joint | in corners, on the tub, shower tray and transitions | elastic, smooth, can be covered by mold |
| Epoxy resin joint | often in kitchens, showers and commercial spaces | very dense and resistant, hardly absorbs any dirt |

**Cement joints** are the most common. Because they absorb dirt and moisture, they turn gray over time. Acid, such as vinegar, citric acid or acidic bathroom cleaners, will slowly dissolve the cement if used frequently.

**Silicone joints** are also called maintenance joints because they don't last forever. They compensate for movements between components and have to be replaced after a few years.

## The right tool

- **Joint brush:** A narrow brush with firm plastic bristles fits exactly into the joint. It is the most important tool.
- **Old toothbrush:** Well suited for small areas and corners. An electric toothbrush with an old brush head saves energy.
- **Sponge and microfiber cloth:** For wiping and rinsing.
- **Bucket with clear water:** For rinsing so that loosened dirt does not run back into the joint.
- **Gloves:** With any cleaner, especially soda or products containing chlorine.

A **wire brush** does not belong in the joints. It scratches the tile glaze and tears out grout.

## Cleaning cement joints: step by step

### 1. Roughly pre-clean

First, wipe down the tile surface with warm water and some all-purpose cleaner. This will help you remove loose dirt and see where the grout lines are really discolored.

### 2. Apply baking soda paste

For normal gray haze, a **paste made from baking soda and water** is a good home remedy. Mix about three parts baking soda with one part water to form a spreadable mixture. Apply it directly to the joints with a brush or a finger in a glove and leave it on for about 15 minutes. Baking soda is slightly alkaline and dissolves soap residue and light greasy dirt; the fine crystals also have a mildly abrasive effect.

### 3. Scrub

Brush the joints lengthwise with the joint brush, section by section. Do not press too hard, otherwise you will roughen the surface of the joint and it will become dirty more quickly.

### 4. Rinse thoroughly

Wipe off the paste with a damp sponge and rinse several times with clean water. Otherwise, baking soda residues will dry up as a white haze.

### 5. Let dry and check

When wet, joints look darker than they actually are. Only assess the result when everything is dry and repeat the treatment on individual areas if necessary.

## When baking soda isn't enough

**Greasy joints in the kitchen:** The dirt behind the stove and sink is often made up of grease. A **soda solution** dissolves grease more strongly than baking soda. Wear gloves when doing this because soda is significantly more alkaline and irritates the skin. The guide [baking soda, soda and baking powder] (/ratgeber/natron-soda-backpulver/) explains the differences.

**Steam cleaner:** Hot steam removes dirt from the joint, without chemicals. It is suitable for glazed tiles and intact cement joints. If you have crumbling joints, sensitive natural stone or older silicone, you should be careful and follow the instructions on the device.

**Limescale edges on joints in the shower:** An acidic cleaner helps here, but only for a short time. Moisten the joints with water beforehand so that they absorb less acid, and then rinse thoroughly. More about this in the guide [Removing limescale in the shower] (/ratgeber/kalk-in-der-dusche/).

**Commercially available joint cleaner:** There are special cleaners for cement joints. Read the label carefully as some contain acid and others contain chlorine. Products containing chlorine bleach strongly, can lighten colored joints and should never be mixed with acidic cleaners. The rules for this can be found on our page [Safety when cleaning](/sicherheit-beim-putzen/).

## Maintain silicone joints

Silicone is smooth and easier to keep clean than cement. Regular wiping with a soft cloth and mild cleaner is usually sufficient.

- **Wipe dry after showering:** Damp silicone joints are an ideal place for mold.
- **No abrasive cleaners:** Scratches in the silicone provide support for dirt and spores.
- **Rinse off soap residue:** Shower gel and shampoo form a film on which mold thrives.

**Black spots in silicone** are almost always mold. Superficial infestations can sometimes be lightened with a suitable mold remover. But if the mold has grown into the silicone, it will always come back. Then the only solution is to cut out the joint and re-joint it. The guide [Preventing and removing mold in the bathroom] (/ratgeber/schimmel-im-bad/) explains how you can treat mold safely and when a specialist company is needed.

## Mold in cement joints

Cement joints can also show mold, usually as black dots in shower corners or at the bottom of the wall. You can treat small, superficial stains with a mold remover. Follow the instructions carefully, ventilate well, wear gloves and do not mix the product with any other cleaner.

If the mold comes back after a short time or affects larger areas, the cause lies deeper: too much moisture in the room, a cold outside wall or water that gets behind the tiles. Then cleaning is not enough.

## Keep joints clean longer

- **Peel off after showering:** Water that runs off tiles and grout cannot form edges.
- **Ventilate well:** The faster the bathroom dries, the less chance there is of mold and dirt.
- **Use a little water when mopping:** Dirty mopping water ends up in the joints on the floor. Wring out the mop well and change the water in a timely manner. More about this in the guide [Mopping the floor](/ratgeber/boden-wischen/).
- **Impregnate joints:** After thorough cleaning and complete drying, you can apply joint waterproofing. It makes the surface less absorbent. Depending on the product, it must be renewed regularly.
- **Wipe away splashes immediately:** Grease, coffee and toothpaste are less likely to penetrate fresh stains.

## When joints can no longer be saved

Sometimes even the best cleaning doesn't help. Joints that are **crumbling, cracking or washing out** no longer reliably protect the wall from water. In this case, they should be renewed: the old material is removed with a joint scraper and new joint compound is inserted. If you are skilled at working with your hands, you can do this yourself for small areas. For showers and large areas, it is worth using a specialist company to ensure that the seal behind them remains undamaged.

**Joint paint** or a joint pen can visually freshen up gray but intact joints. This is a cosmetic solution and does not replace cleaning, but it often looks surprisingly good on wall tiles.

## How often which task?

| task | How often |
|---|---|
| Remove tiles and grout in the shower | after every shower |
| Wipe the joints when cleaning the bathroom | weekly |
| Clean shower joints thoroughly with baking soda paste | every one to two months |
| Thoroughly clean floor and kitchen joints | approximately quarterly |
| Check silicone joints for mold and cracks | with every basic cleaning |
| Renew waterproofing | according to manufacturer's instructions |

If you wipe the joints regularly, a single round of baking soda paste is often enough for thorough cleaning. On the other hand, if you wait for years, you will need several passes and will reach your limits if the dirt is deeply embedded.

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

- **Scrubbing with a wire brush:** Tiles will be scratched and joints will be damaged.
- **Let acid act on cement joints:** The joint is attacked and then becomes dirty more quickly.
- **Mix cleaners:** Acid and chlorine produce a dangerous gas. Always use only one agent.
- **Do not rinse off baking soda residue:** It will dry as a white veil.
- **Just paint or paint over mold in the silicone:** It will continue to grow underneath.
- **Too much water when mopping:** Dirty mopping water ends up directly in the joints.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does toothpaste help against gray joints?

Toothpaste contains fine cleaning agents and can loosen slight discoloration. However, it is more expensive and no more effective than a simple baking soda paste. You can find out more about popular home remedies and what they really do in the guide [Home Remedies Myths Checked](/ratgeber/hausmittel-mythen/).

### How often should I thoroughly clean grout lines?

In the shower every one to two months, on the floor and on kitchen walls about every three months. When cleaning the bathroom once a week, it is sufficient to wipe the joints. The guide [Cleaning the bathroom](/ratgeber/bad-putzen/) explains how to clean the entire bathroom in a sensible order.

### Why do my grout lines quickly turn gray again after cleaning?

Most of the time the surface has become rough, for example due to scrubbing too hard or frequent acid, or too much dirty water remains on the floor when mopping. Impregnation after cleaning and less water when wiping will help.

You can find further instructions in the [Bath & Toilet](/themen/bad-und-wc/) section.

## Briefly summarized

Cement joints turn gray because they are porous and absorb dirt, soap and grease. It's best to clean it with a baking soda paste and a joint brush, rinse thoroughly and only assess the result when it's dry. Soda helps against grease, and an acidic cleaner helps against limescale if you moisten the joints beforehand. Just wipe silicone joints gently and keep them dry. If mold has grown into the silicone, the joint must be replaced. Never mix acid and chlorine, wipe with little water and waterproof clean joints to keep them bright longer.
