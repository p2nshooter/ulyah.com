Hardly any other topic is as full of tips as the household. They come from grandparents, from magazines, from videos and forums, and many have been passed down for decades. Some actually work, some help just a little, and some do more harm than good. The problem: If you try a tip that doesn't work, you're not just wasting time. A stain can set in, damage a surface, and some mixtures can even be dangerous.

In this guide we examine twelve of the best-known home remedy tips. For each one you'll find out what's behind it, whether it works and what you can do instead.

## This is how we check a tip

Most home remedy tips can be classified with a few simple questions:

- **Is the agent suitable for the dirt?** Lime only dissolves in acid, fat dissolves best with surfactants or alkaline agents, protein with cold water and enzymes.
- **Do the ingredients cancel each other out?** Acid and base neutralize each other. A mixture is then weaker than each ingredient alone.
- **What is left behind?** Sugar, dyes or resins can cause new stains.
- **What does the material tolerate?** A tip that works on ceramic can ruin marble or wool.
- **Is it safe?** Some combinations release dangerous gases.

## 1. Baking soda and vinegar clean the drain

**What's wrong with it:** Little. Baking soda is a base, vinegar is an acid. If you mix the two, it bubbles vigorously because carbon dioxide is produced. At the same time, the two substances neutralize each other. What's left is essentially water with a little salt. The bubbling can loosen loose deposits a little, but it will not loosen a blockage caused by hair and soap.

**Better:** Remove hair with a drain spiral, use the suction cup or unscrew and clean the siphon. The instructions are in the guide [Cleaning the sink and bathtub] (/ratgeber/waschbecken-badewanne-reinigen/).

## 2. Salt on red wine

**What's wrong:** Salt absorbs some of the liquid, similar to paper towels. But it doesn't dissolve the dye. If it stays there for a long time, the stain underneath dries up and anyone who rubs it in rubs the fibers. **White wine** dilutes but adds sugar and acid, and **mineral water** essentially acts like tap water.

**Better:** Pat immediately with a clean cloth, dilute with cold water and pat again. For light-colored, colorfast fabrics, use oxygen bleach afterwards. Everything about this in the guide [Removing red wine stains] (/ratgeber/rotweinflecken-entfernen/).

## 3. Cola against urine stone, rust and blood

**What's wrong:** Cola contains phosphoric acid, but in far too small an amount to dissolve solid urine scale or rust. In addition, sugar and dyes are left behind, which can stick, attract insects and cause new stains. Cola has no advantage over cold water on blood stains.

**Better:** To combat urine scale, use an acidic toilet cleaner or citric acid with a long exposure time, see [Cleaning the toilet and removing urine scale] (/ratgeber/toilette-reinigen-urinstein/). To combat blood, use cold water, gall soap and detergent containing enzymes, see [Removing blood stains](/ratgeber/blutflecken-entfernen/).

## 4. Vinegar disinfects

**Here's the thing:** Vinegar can reduce the number of some germs, but is not a reliable disinfectant. It is not very suitable for mold on plaster containing lime because the lime neutralizes the acid.

**Better:** In the household, thorough cleaning and drying is usually sufficient. The guide [Kitchen Hygiene](/ratgeber/kuechenhygiene/) explains how to work hygienically in the kitchen. Special rules apply when it comes to mold, which can be found in the guide [Preventing and removing mold in the bathroom] (/ratgeber/schimmel-im-bad/).

## 5. Hairspray vs. ballpoint pen

**Here's the thing:** Hairspray used to contain a lot of alcohol, which dissolves ink. However, modern hairsprays often contain resins and care ingredients that leave sticky residue behind.

**Better:** Pure alcohol such as denatured alcohol or isopropanol, after a color test and with dabbing instead of rubbing. The instructions are in the guide [Remove ballpoint pens, wax and chewing gum] (/ratgeber/kugelschreiber-wachs-kaugummi/).

## 6. Toothpaste for grout, silver and scratches

**What's wrong:** Toothpaste contains fine cleaning agents and can loosen slight discoloration. However, it is more expensive and no more effective than baking soda paste. On the contrary, it can cause fine scratches on glass, displays, ceramic hobs or painted surfaces. Deep scratches will not disappear with toothpaste.

**Better:** Use a baking soda paste for joints, see [Cleaning tile joints](/ratgeber/fliesenfugen-reinigen/). For silver, the method with baking soda and aluminum foil, described in the guide [Baking soda, soda and baking powder] (/ratgeber/natron-soda-backpulver/).

## 7. Baking powder is the same as baking soda

**What's it:** Baking powder contains baking soda, but also an acidulant and starch. When moistened, baking soda and acid react with each other. There is therefore less effect left for cleaning and it costs more.

**Better:** Use baking soda directly. And never confuse baking soda with caustic soda.

## 8. Newspaper for streak-free windows

**What's wrong with it:** This used to be a popular trick, but is no longer necessary today. Printer's ink can rub off on light-colored frames and seals.

**Better:** A good microfiber cloth or squeegee. The guide [Cleaning windows] (/ratgeber/fenster-putzen/) explains the technique.

## 9. Coffee grounds clean the drain

**What's wrong with it:** Coffee grounds are supposed to clear pipes and bind odors. In fact, it settles in pipes and siphons and, together with grease, can even promote deposits.

**Better:** Put coffee grounds in the organic waste. Do not pour fat down the drain, let it cool down and dispose of it with kitchen paper in the trash.

## 10. A lot helps a lot

**Here's the thing:** More detergent, more cleaner and more foam doesn't mean more clean. Overdosed detergent remains in the fibers and machine, makes towels hard and can cause skin irritation. Too much cleaner on floors leaves a film that attracts dirt.

**Better:** Dose according to package instructions and water hardness. How to do this can be found in the guide [Dosing detergent] (/ratgeber/waschmittel-dosieren/).

## 11. Chlorine cleans everything

**What's wrong with it:** Cleaners containing chlorine bleach and disinfect. They hardly help against limescale, urine scale and grease. Because stains disappear, everything appears clean, even though deposits remain. But they are especially dangerous when they come into contact with acidic cleaners because this creates chlorine gas.

**Better:** For lime acid, for fat surfactants or soda, and chlorine products only in exceptional cases according to the instructions. The rules for this can be found on our page [Safety when cleaning](/sicherheit-beim-putzen/).

## 12. Lemon juice is as good as citric acid

**What's wrong:** Lemon juice contains significantly less acid than a solution made from powder, but contains sugar and pulp. It helps a little with very light limescale, but leaves behind sticky residue.

**Better:** Citric acid powder in warm water, see [Citric acid in the home](/ratgeber/zitronensaeure/).

## Home remedies that really help

There are good home remedies. Used correctly, they replace many specialist products:

- **Dishwashing liquid** in warm water for almost all greasy dirt in everyday life.
- **Baking soda** as a paste for joints, ovens and burnt pots.
- **Soda** for stubborn grease, with gloves.
- **Vinegar and citric acid** against limescale, not on natural stone.
- **Gall soap** for grease and collar stains, described in the guide [How to use gall soap correctly] (/ratgeber/gallseife/).
- **Curd soap and soft soap** for laundry and floors, more in the guide [Curd soap and soft soap] (/ratgeber/kernseife-schmierseife/).

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

- **Mixing several home remedies at the same time:** They often cancel each other out or react dangerously with each other.
- **Adopt tips without material testing:** What works on ceramic can ruin natural stone or silk.
- **Wait a long time to see whether a remedy works:** Stains set in during this time.
- **Ignore residue:** Sugar, resins and soap residues attract new dirt.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why are false tips so persistent?

Because they often partially work. Salt visibly absorbs liquid, baking soda and vinegar fizz impressively, cola makes a light coating appear a little lighter. The effect is convincing, even if a simpler method would have achieved the same or more.

### Are home remedies generally better than store-bought cleaners?

Not automatically. Home remedies are often cheap and fragrance-free, but they are also chemicals and can be harmful. A purchased cleaner can have a more targeted effect and can be dosed more economically. It is crucial to choose the right agent for the contamination and material.

### What to do if a tip has already caused damage?

Stop immediately and rinse the area thoroughly with clean water. For textiles, wash the item afterwards according to the care label without putting it in the dryer. Dull spots on natural stone, scratches on glass or faded colors usually cannot be repaired yourself. For valuable items, ask a textile cleaner or a specialist company and tell them exactly which product was used.

### How do I recognize a good tip?

He explains why it works, names the material on which it works and points out limitations and risks. Tips that promise a cure for everything are usually too good to be true.

You can find more home remedies and their limitations in the [Home Remedies](/themen/hausmittel/) and in the guide [Vinegar in the Household](/ratgeber/essig-im-haushalt/).

## Briefly summarized

Many home remedy tips don't stand up to simple testing: baking soda and vinegar neutralize each other, salt and cola hardly help, hairspray and newspaper are outdated, coffee grounds can put a strain on pipes, and chlorine is neither a panacea nor harmless. Home remedies are effective if they suit the dirt: acid against lime, baking soda and soda against grease and crusts, cold water and enzymes against protein, alcohol against ink. Test new tips in an inconspicuous area, do not mix products and pay attention to the material.
