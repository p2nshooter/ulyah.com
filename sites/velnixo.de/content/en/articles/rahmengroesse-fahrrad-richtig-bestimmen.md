The frame size is the most important number when buying a bike, and at the same time the one about which there is the most uncertainty. A bike with a frame that is too large is difficult to control, you can hardly get your feet on the ground when stopping and you have to stretch far forward. A frame that is too small initially appears maneuverable, but in the long term it leads to a slouched posture, knee pain and wasted strength.

In this guide we will show you step by step how to measure your stride length, how to calculate a frame height, why the formula is only a starting point and how you can tell during a test ride whether a bike really fits.

## What the frame size actually indicates

The classic frame size describes the length of the seat tube, i.e. the tube in which the seat post is inserted. Measurements are usually taken from the middle of the bottom bracket to the top of the seat tube. For trekking, city and racing bikes it is often given in centimeters, for mountain bikes it is traditionally in inches. However, many manufacturers now use letter sizes such as S, M, L or XL.

The problem: Manufacturers do not measure consistently. Some measure to the top of the seat tube, others to the middle of the top tube. On modern frames with a steeply sloping top tube, the seat tube length also says little about how long the bike feels. That's why frame size is a good first guide, but not the last word.

## Step 1: Measure the stride length correctly

Stride length is the measurement from your crotch to the ground. It is more meaningful for frame size than height because people of the same height can have legs of very different lengths.

This is how you measure it reliably:

1. Take off your shoes and stand barefoot with your back against a wall. The feet are about 15 to 20 centimeters apart, about as wide as on the pedals.
2. Take a hard-backed book or a spirit level and push it horizontally up between your legs until it rests lightly and noticeably against your crotch, like a saddle.
3. Mark the top edge of the book on the wall with a pencil.
4. Measure the distance from the mark to the ground. This is your stride length.

The easiest way to do this is with two people. It's best to measure twice and take the average. Even a difference of two centimeters can result in a different size in the calculation.

## Step 2: The formula for your bike type

A suitable frame height can be calculated from the stride length using a factor. The factors differ depending on the type of bike because the seating position is different.

| Type of bike | Formula | Unity |
|---|---|---|
| City and trekking bike | Stride length × 0.66 | centimeters |
| Road bike | Stride length × 0.665 | centimeters |
| Gravel bike | Inseam × 0.665 (choose slightly smaller if between two sizes) | centimeters |
| Mountain bike | Stride length × 0.226 | inches |
| Mountain bike (in cm) | Stride length × 0.574 | centimeters |

**An example:** With a stride length of 84 centimeters, this results in 84 × 0.66 = 55.4 centimeters for a trekking bike. A frame between 54 and 56 centimeters would be a good starting point. For a mountain bike this results in 84 × 0.226 = 19 inches, which corresponds to size M or L for many manufacturers.

Important: These factors are empirical values, not natural laws. They apply to typical geometries. A sporty trekking bike can be different than a comfortable model with a low entry.

## Step 3: Read the manufacturer's size chart correctly

Almost every manufacturer publishes a size chart, usually with height and sometimes with inseam. These tables are tailored to your own geometry and are therefore more precise than the general formula. Pay attention to three things as you read:

- **Does the table apply to height or inseam?** If both are specified, inseam takes precedence.
- **Do the areas overlap?** If you are on the border between two sizes, the test drive and your preferred position will decide.
- **Is the size in centimeters, inches or letters?** A 52 from one manufacturer is not automatically the same as a 52 from another.

## Between two sizes: which one should you choose?

A lot of people are exactly between two sizes. Then this rule of thumb helps:

- **Smaller size** if you want to ride nimbly and sportily, have shorter arms or torso, or climb up and down frequently.
- **Size larger** if you ride long, smooth routes, have long arms or torso, and like a stretched, smooth running position.

For growing children and young people, it is tempting to buy a bike that is too big “so that it will fit longer”. We advise against this. A bike that is too big is difficult for children to control and is therefore dangerous. More about this in the guide to children's bikes.

## More than seat tube length: stack and reach

If you want to delve a little deeper, you should know two other dimensions that are in the geometry tables of many manufacturers:

- **Stack** is the vertical distance from the center of the bottom bracket to the top edge of the head tube. A high stack means a more upright posture.
- **Reach** is the horizontal distance between the same points. A long reach means you have to stretch further forward.

Two bikes with the same frame size can have very different stack and reach values. If you know a bike fits you well, you can compare its values ​​with a new model. This is much more meaningful than the frame size alone.

## Step 4: The test drive – this is how you check the fit

No formula replaces the test drive. Ask the dealer to roughly adjust the saddle and handlebars to suit you beforehand. Then you check the following:

### Standing

- **Standover Height:** Stand over the top tube with both feet flat on the ground. On a diamond frame there should be a few centimeters of space between the crotch and the top tube. This doesn't matter for low-entry users.
- **Saddle height:** Sit on the saddle and place your heel on the pedal in the lowest position. The leg should now be almost completely stretched. When you kick normally with the ball of your foot, the knee remains slightly bent. This is the correct height.
- **Handlebar position:** With your hands on the handlebars, your arms should be slightly bent and your shoulders relaxed. If you have to push your arms straight, the bike is too long. If your knees almost touch your elbows, it's too short.

### While driving

- Can you easily look over your shoulder without losing track?
- Does the bike feel stable at slow speeds, such as when pulling away from a traffic light?
- After ten minutes, do you have pressure on your hands, neck or crotch?
- When you stop, can you safely put one foot on the ground as you slide forward from the saddle?

If multiple answers feel wrong, try the next size. Small problems can often be solved with a different stem, different handlebars or a different saddle position. There are no fundamental problems with the frame length.

## Fine tune after purchase

The perfectly fitting bike must also be adjusted to you:

- **Saddle Height:** Using the heel method as described above. If you have knee pain in the front of your knee, the saddle is often too low, and if you have pain in the back of your knee, it is often too high.
- **Saddle tilt:** Usually horizontal. A slight forward lean can reduce pressure in your crotch, too much will cause you to slide forward.
- **Saddle Offset:** When the crank is level, the front knee should be approximately above the pedal axle. This is a guideline, not a dogma.
- **Handlebar height:** A higher handlebar takes the strain off your back and hands, a lower one is more aerodynamic. Many everyday cyclists ride more relaxed with slightly higher handlebars.

Make sure that the seat post never exceeds the “Min. Insert” or “Max.” marking. is pulled out. This marking shows how far the support must be at least in the frame. If it is exceeded, the support or frame can break.

## Special cases

### E-bikes

In principle the same applies to Pedelecs. Because of the higher weight, it is particularly important to have a secure footing when stopping. Many people feel more comfortable on a Pedelec with a trapeze or low-entry frame because they don't have to tilt the heavy bike as much when getting on and off.

### Very tall and very short people

Anyone who is significantly over 1.95 meters or under 1.55 meters tall will often only find a few suitable models in the normal range. Some manufacturers offer XXL or XS sizes, and there are also specialists for large and small wheels. For short people, a bike with 27.5-inch instead of 28-inch wheels may be a better fit.

### Back problems and limited mobility

If you have back problems, a more upright position with a high stack is usually more comfortable. A low step-through makes it easier to get on, and a suspension seat post can soften shocks. If you have persistent symptoms, it's worth getting a professional bike fitting, and if you have any medical questions, it's worth talking to your doctor.

## The most common mistakes

- **“I'm 1.80 meters, so I need size L.”** The height is only a rough guide. Step length and upper body are crucial.
- **“When sitting, both feet must be flat on the floor.”** No. If the saddle is at the right height, you can usually only reach the ground with your toes when sitting. To stop, slide forward from the saddle.
- **“A bike with a frame that is too large can simply be adjusted lower.”** The saddle height, yes, but not the length of the frame.
- **“One size fits all types.”** A 56 mm trekking bike and a 56 mm road bike are completely different bikes.

## Checklist: Get the right size in five minutes

1. Measure stride length barefoot on the wall.
2. Use the formula for your bike type to calculate a frame height.
3. Compare with the manufacturer's size chart.
4. If there are two possible sizes, test drive both.
5. Have the saddle and handlebar position adjusted when purchasing and readjust after the first few rides.

If you follow these steps, in most cases you will find a bike that you feel comfortable on from day one. And a bike that you feel comfortable on is a bike that you enjoy riding.
