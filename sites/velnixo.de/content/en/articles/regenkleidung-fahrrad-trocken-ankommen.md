Anyone who uses a bicycle in everyday life will sooner or later get wet. In Germany it rains on many days of the year, and if you take a bus or car every time it rains, you hardly ride a bike in the fall. But with the right rain gear, rain is only a small obstacle. Good gear keeps you dry without sweating underneath, and it's quick to put on and take off.

This guide explains which rain gear is useful for what purpose, what the information on the labels means and how to keep your shoes, hands and head dry.

## The challenge: water from the outside, sweat from the inside

Rain gear for cycling has to do two things at the same time. It's supposed to keep out rain and splashes of water, and it's supposed to let the water vapor that you produce when you pedal out. A simple plastic jacket might keep you tight, but after ten minutes you'll be wet inside. High-quality rainwear therefore uses membranes or coatings that keep water out and let steam through, as well as ventilation openings.

### What the numbers mean

- **Water column:** Indicates how much water pressure a material can withstand before water penetrates. From around 10,000 millimeters onwards, clothing is considered rainproof for normal conditions; for constant rain and pressure from backpack straps or saddles, 15,000 to 20,000 millimeters are better.
- **Breathability:** Often stated as RET value. The lower the value, the more water vapor the material allows through. Values ​​below 6 are considered very breathable, values ​​up to 12 are considered good.
- **Taped seams:** Seams are the weak point of any rain gear. Welded or taped seams prevent water from seeping through the stitch holes.

## Rain jacket

The rain jacket is the most important piece. A good bicycle rain jacket has:

- **Extended back:** Otherwise, when sitting, the jacket will ride up and your lower back will get wet.
- **Long sleeves with adjustable cuffs:** To prevent water from running into the sleeves when you stretch your arms towards the handlebars.
- **Ventilation:** Zippers under the arms or back ventilation allow heat to escape.
- **Hood that fits under the helmet** or can be removed. A hood over the helmet restricts visibility when looking over the shoulder.
- **Reflectors and bright colors:** It's harder to see when it rains. A jacket in a signal color with reflective stripes makes you visible.
- **Small pack size:** Important for commuters and touring riders so that the jacket can always be with you.

## Rain pants

The legs get particularly wet on the bike because they are directly in the rain and get sprayed from below.

- **Width and Cut:** Pants should fit over everyday clothing, but be tight enough at the ankle to avoid getting caught in the chain. Velcro or snap fasteners on the leg help.
- **Zipper on the leg:** This allows you to put the pants on over shoes without taking them off.
- **Reinforced seating area:** The material on the saddle is particularly stressed.
- **Suspenders or high waist:** prevent pants from slipping when pedaling.

If you drive short distances, you can often get by with a rain jacket and mudguards. Rain pants are essential for longer journeys and commuters.

## Rain cape and poncho

A rain cape is a wide cape that covers the upper body, arms and thighs. It is hooked onto the thumb or the handlebars, forming a small roof over the legs.

| | Rain jacket + trousers | Rain cape |
|---|---|---|
| Protection | all around, even in wind | Upper body and thighs, lower legs remain partly open |
| Ventilation | depending on the material | very good, air circulates underneath |
| Dress up | a little more complex | in seconds over everyday clothing |
| Wind | well suited | flutters in strong winds, unfavorable in gusts |
| Use | Commuting, touring, sporty driving | City, short distances, upright sitting position |

A cape is ideal for city bikes with an upright seating position and short distances over everyday clothing. In strong winds and on fast bikes, a combination of jacket and pants is the better choice.

## Keep feet dry

Wet feet are particularly unpleasant because shoes take a long time to dry.

- **Overshoes:** They are pulled over regular shoes and keep water out. They are ideal for commuters who need dry shoes in the office.
- **Waterproof shoes:** Shoes with a membrane stay tight until water runs over the edge from above. Rain pants that extend over your shoes prevent this.
- **Gaiters:** cover the transition between pants and shoes.
- **Mudguards:** A long front mudguard with splash flaps keeps most of the spray water away from your feet.

## hands and head

- **Gloves:** Waterproof gloves are important in cool rain because cold, wet fingers can no longer brake safely. In warm summer rain, normal cycling gloves are often sufficient.
- **Handlebar cuffs:** are attached to the handlebars and protect your hands from rain and cold, especially in winter.
- **Head:** A helmet cover or thin, water-repellent cap under the helmet keeps rain away from your head and face. A small peak on the cap protects the eyes.
- **Goggles:** Clear cycling glasses keep drips out of your eyes. Glasses wearers particularly benefit from a cap with a peak.

## The bike itself

Rain gear is only half the battle. The bike also helps you stay dry:

- **Front and rear mudguards**, as long as possible and with splash flaps.
- **Waterproof pockets** for laptop, clothes and documents.
- **Chain guard,** so that the pants don't get smeared.
- **Saddle cover** when the bike is parked in the rain. A simple shower cap will do the trick too.

## The right size

Rain gear is worn over other clothing, so size is important. If possible, try on the jacket and pants with the clothes you plan to wear underneath, i.e. a sweater or fleece in winter. In the store, sit in a driving position or stretch your arms forward as if you were gripping the handlebars: the sleeves should then cover your wrists, your back should not be exposed, and your pants should not be tight at the knees. A rain jacket that is too tight pushes air out of the warming layer underneath; one that is too wide flutters in the wind and flaps when you ride fast.

## Care: so that the rain gear stays tight

- **Wash regularly:** Dirt, sweat and skin oil clog the membrane. Wash the clothing according to the manufacturer's instructions, usually with a special detergent for functional clothing and without fabric softener.
- **Impregnation:** If water no longer rolls off but instead soaks the outer material, the clothing needs a new impregnation. Many waterproofing products are reactivated with heat, such as in the dryer or with an iron on a low setting if the manufacturer allows this.
- **Store in a dry place:** After the ride, hang it up and let it dry, not crumpled up in the bag.
- **Repair minor damage:** There are special patches for holes that make the clothing tight again.

## Tips for rainy days

- **Weather app in the morning:** A look at the rain radar often shows whether a shower will be over in ten minutes.
- **Rain gear always with you:** A small, light jacket fits in every bag.
- **Change of clothes at your destination:** For commuters, a dry pair of socks in the office is worth their weight in gold.
- **Drive slower:** When wet, braking distances are longer and markings are slippery. More about this in the guide to cycling in rain, wind and darkness.

## Membrane, coating or wax?

Rainwear achieves its tightness in different ways:

- **Membrane:** A wafer-thin layer between the outer fabric and the lining keeps water out and allows water vapor to pass through. Membrane jackets are usually the most breathable, but also the most expensive.
- **Coating:** A plastic layer on the inside of the fabric makes it waterproof. Cheaper and robust, but less breathable. Completely sufficient for short distances.
- **Waxed Cotton:** Classic, durable and comfortable for everyday use, but heavier and less dense in long rain. Needs to be waxed regularly.
- **Environmental aspect:** Many manufacturers now avoid using difficult-to-degrade fluorine compounds in their impregnation. Such clothing is initially less likely to shed beads, but above all it needs regular care.

## Rainwear for children

Children often sit in bike trailers under a rain cover and stay dry. If you drive yourself, you need a rain jacket and rain pants like adults. When it comes to children's rainwear, pay attention to:

- **Robustness:** Children slide, climb and sit in puddles.
- **Easy dressing:** Pants with straps and jackets with large zippers that children can operate themselves.
- **Visibility:** Bright colors and reflective stripes because children are easily overlooked in traffic.
- **Nothing to snag:** Ribbons and long cords can get caught in spokes or chain.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a normal outdoor jacket enough?**
For short distances, yes. However, hiking jackets are often too short at the front and not extended at the back, and the hood doesn't fit under the helmet. If you often ride in the rain, you would be better off with a model designed for cyclists.

**How long does a good rain jacket last?**
With regular care, many years. The membrane itself is durable, but the waterproofing, cuffs and zippers are more likely to wear out. Many manufacturers offer repairs, which are often cheaper than a new jacket.

**Why am I wet even though I have a rain jacket?**
Mostly it's sweat. During high exertion, no membrane can remove all water vapor. Open the ventilation, drive a little slower and wear functional clothing underneath instead of cotton.

## Conclusion

With the right rain gear, cycling in the rain is no reason to change. A breathable rain jacket with an extended back, ventilation and reflective elements is the most important piece, complemented by rain trousers for longer journeys or a cape for short city trips. Overshoes, waterproof gloves and a cap under the helmet protect feet, hands and head. Mudguards and waterproof bags on the bike take care of the rest. Wash and waterproof the clothing regularly so that it will last for many years.
