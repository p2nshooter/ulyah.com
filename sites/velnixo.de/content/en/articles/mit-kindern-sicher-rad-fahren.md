Cycling gives children freedom, exercise and self-confidence. At the same time, road traffic is a particular challenge for them: children are smaller, are less visible and can only correctly assess speeds and distances over time. This is not a question of intelligence, but of development. Many of the skills needed in traffic do not fully develop until the age of around ten to twelve.

This guide accompanies you from the first balance bike to the independent journey to school. He explains which rules apply to children, how parents can properly accompany them and how children can become safer step by step.

## What children can and cannot do in traffic

So that you can realistically assess your child's abilities, it helps to take a look at their development:

| Age (approximately) | What is developing | What that means |
|---|---|---|
| 2 to 4 years | Balance, steering on the balance bike | only drive in protected areas |
| 4 to 6 years | Pedaling, braking, first bike | Practice on play streets, parks and empty squares |
| 6 to 8 years | safe driving but limited attention | Walkway, always accompanied |
| 8 to 10 years | better assessment of dangers, hand signals | Sidewalks allowed, first short stretches on cycle paths with accompaniment |
| from around 10 years old | Better estimate speeds and distances | Cycling training, step by step independently |

Every child develops differently. Some are already very careful at eight years old, others need longer. What matters is what your child shows in practice, not just their age.

## From balance bike to first bicycle

The **balance bike** is the best way to start for most children. You learn balance and steering without having to concentrate on pedaling. Many children later switch straight to a bike without training wheels because they have already mastered balancing.

When it comes to your **first bike**, the most important thing is the right size. When sitting, the child should be able to safely reach the ground with their toes, and the bike should not be too heavy. A lightweight children's bike is easier to control and more fun. Easily accessible brake levers that can be operated by small hands are also important. More about this in the guide to buying a children's bike.

**Training wheels** are often not necessary and can even make learning more difficult because the child does not get used to cornering at an angle. Those who come from the balance bike rarely need them.

## Practice in protected places

Before children take part in traffic, they should be able to ride a bike safely. Traffic-calmed areas, large empty parking lots on weekends, schoolyards and parks with wide paths are suitable. Exercises that help:

- **Starting and stopping:** Upon a signal, brake quickly and in a controlled manner with both feet on the ground.
- **Ride slowly:** If you can ride slowly but still remain stable, you will be able to ride a bike.
- **Keep in lane:** Drive along a line without swaying.
- **Look around:** Look over your shoulder without losing direction. This exercise is more difficult than it looks and is particularly important for traffic.
- **Riding with one hand:** briefly take one hand off the handlebars in preparation for hand signals.
- **Slalom and turns:** drive around cones or chalk markings.

## What rules apply to children

The StVO contains special rules for children on bikes:

- **Until their eighth birthday** children must ride on the sidewalk. If there is a cycle path that is structurally separated from the road, you can use that too.
- **Until their tenth birthday** they are allowed to ride on the sidewalk, but they don't have to.
- **A supervisor aged 16 and over** may accompany the child on the sidewalk.
- **On the sidewalk** children must be considerate of pedestrians, and at every intersection and junction they should get off and push the bike across the street.

These rules are intended to protect children from moving traffic. The greatest risk on the sidewalk is at entrances, exits and intersections, because drivers don't expect fast children on their bikes there.

## Accompany properly

How parents accompany their child depends on where they are driving:

- **On the sidewalk:** For younger children, it is best for the adult to drive closely behind the child, slightly offset from the road. This way he can keep an eye on the child and direct him with clear instructions.
- **On a cycle path or road:** Here the adult also rides behind the child, slightly offset to the left. It shields the child from traffic and can warn early. If you are traveling with two adults, one can ride in the front and one in the back.
- **Clear commands:** Short, practiced words like “Stop,” “Get to the right,” or “Slow” work better than long explanations.
- **Be a role model:** Children imitate what adults do. If you drive when the light is red or don't wear a helmet, you can hardly expect this from your child.

## Cycling training at school

In most federal states, children in the third or fourth grade undergo cycling training, which often ends with a practical test. It is carried out jointly by the school, police and traffic police. The children learn traffic rules, hand signals, turning left and how to behave at intersections, first in a safe area and then in real traffic.

Parents can support the training by practicing regularly with the child, for example on the way to school. After the test, the child is not automatically safe, but he or she has a good foundation for driving independently.

## The way to school by bike

Many children want to cycle to school after cycling training. This is how you prepare for the way to school:

1. **Choose route:** The safest route is not always the shortest. Prefer quiet streets, cycle paths and traffic light crossings, even if it's a small detour.
2. **Leave together:** Ride the route together several times, first on weekends, then during school hours. Talk about dangerous areas such as driveways, bus stops and confusing intersections.
3. **Let the child lead the way:** Let the child lead the route while you follow, intervening only when necessary. This way you can see whether it can safely handle the route.
4. **Alone at a distance:** If everything goes well, the child can drive alone while you initially follow at a distance.
5. **Check the wheel:** Brakes, lights, bell and tires must be in order. Good light is particularly important in autumn and winter.

Many schools give recommendations as to which grade children should start cycling, often after cycling training. Check with the school.

## Visibility and equipment

- **Helmet:** A suitable helmet is particularly important for children. Find out more in the bicycle helmet guide.
- **Light-colored clothing and reflectors:** Children are small and easily overlooked. Light-colored jackets, reflective stripes and a high-visibility vest help, especially in the dark season.
- **Bicycle pennant:** A pennant on a long pole makes small children on bikes more visible to drivers, for example at driveways.
- **Good light:** Children's bikes of a certain size must be equipped with lights for road use. Battery lamps are a simple retrofit.
- **Bell:** An easily accessible bell that the child actually uses.

## Shared bike tours with children

Cycling tours with the family are a wonderful experience if they suit the children:

- **Short stages:** Initially plan just a few kilometers and lots of breaks. Children often ride in short spurts and then need a break.
- **Variety:** Playgrounds, swimming areas, ice cream or an animal park make the tour attractive for children.
- **Car-free paths:** Cycle paths along rivers, on old railway lines and through parks are ideal.
- **Provisions and water:** Children get hungry and thirsty quickly.
- **Plan the way back:** Children underestimate that the way back is just as long. A rail connection as an emergency plan is helpful.

Child seats, trailers, cargo bikes or tandem couplings are suitable for smaller children who are not yet driving themselves. More about this in the guide to child seats and trailers.

## The quick safety check on the children's bike

Children's bikes are often subjected to a lot of wear and tear: they fall over, are thrown into the schoolyard and are left in the rain. A quick check once a month helps to identify problems early. It's best to do it together, then the child will learn straight away what's important.

1. **Brakes:** Pull both brake levers. The bike must no longer turn when pushed and the lever must not go all the way to the handlebars.
2. **Tires:** Check air pressure with your thumb, look at tread and sidewalls for cracks.
3. **Handlebars and saddle:** Both must be secure. As the child grows, adjust the saddle.
4. **Chain:** It should not be dry, rusty or sagging.
5. **Light and bell:** Do they both work?
6. **Screws and pedals:** Nothing should wobble or rattle.

If something is wrong, it will be repaired before the next trip. Many bike shops check out children's bikes at a reasonable price when school starts.

## Pedelecs for children and young people?

There is no legal minimum age in Germany for Pedelecs with support up to 25 km/h. There are children's and youth pedelecs, for example for family trips in hilly areas. However, experts advise caution: With a motor, children travel faster than they can assess the dangers, and the wheels are heavier. A Pedelec can only make sense when a child can safely ride a normal bike in traffic. For S-Pedelecs up to 45 km/h, a minimum age and a class AM driving license are mandatory.

## Frequently asked questions

**When is my child allowed to cycle alone?**
There is no fixed age. Many experts recommend that children only be allowed to ride alone in traffic after cycling training in the third or fourth grade, and then first on well-known, quiet routes.

**As a parent, can I drive on the sidewalk with my child?**
Yes, a supervisor aged 16 or over may accompany a child under eight years of age on the sidewalk, with respect for pedestrians.

**Does my child have to wear a helmet?**
There is no obligation to wear a helmet in Germany, not even for children. However, a well-fitting helmet is strongly recommended.

## Conclusion

Children learn cycling best step by step: first with a balance bike, then on protected areas, later on the sidewalk and only after cycling training alone in traffic. They can ride on the sidewalk up to the age of eight, up to the age of ten, and parents aged 16 and over are allowed to accompany them there. Follow your child behind him, be a good role model, practice going to school together and pay attention to visibility, a helmet and a roadworthy bike. This will help children become safe, self-confident cyclists.
