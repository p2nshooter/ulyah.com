Well-adjusted brakes are the basis for safe cycling. Over time, brake pads wear out, cables lengthen, and brake calipers slip a little. The result: The lever can be pulled further, the brake engages later, grinds or squeaks. Many of these problems can be solved yourself in just a few minutes using simple tools.

In this guide we will show you how to check your brakes, adjust V-brakes and mechanical disc brakes, center a hydraulic disc brake and eliminate typical noises. And we'll tell you exactly when you should leave it alone and go to a specialist workshop.

## Safety first

Brakes are safety-relevant. Please note:

- Only work if you are sure and with the right tools.
- Tighten screws to the torque specified by the manufacturer. Many brake screws are small and sensitive.
- After each work, test the brakes first while stationary, then during a slow test drive in a quiet place.
- If there are hydraulic brakes that need to be bled, if the lines are leaky, or if you are unsure, the bike should be taken to the workshop.

## tool

- Allen keys in common sizes, usually 4, 5 and 6 millimeters,
- possibly Torx wrench, especially T25 for brake discs,
- a small torque wrench,
- a clean rag and brake cleaner or isopropyl alcohol,
- fine sandpaper for glazed coverings,
- a plastic tire lever or a special tool for pushing back the pistons on disc brakes.

## Step 1: Check brakes

Before you adjust something, you should know what's wrong.

1. **Lever travel:** Pull both brake levers firmly. You should have a clear pressure point and not pull all the way to the handlebars. There should be at least a finger's width of space between the lever and the handle.
2. **Braking effect:** Push the wheel and pull the front brake. The front wheel must lock, the wheel lifts slightly at the rear. Same thing at the rear: the rear wheel should slip.
3. **Pads:** With rim brakes, the grooves in the pads should still be clearly visible. With disc brakes, you look into the brake caliper from above or from the side. The covering should be at least half a millimeter thick and, together with the support plate, about three millimeters thick. Exact values ​​can be found in the manufacturer's instructions.
4. **Sounds:** Turn the raised wheel. Is something grinding? Does the brake squeak when braking?
5. **Cables and Lines:** Are brake cables frayed, rusty or kinked? Are hydraulic lines leaking, oily or damaged?

## Adjust V-Brake

V-brakes are installed on many trekking, city and older mountain bikes. They are easy to adjust yourself.

### Align the coverings

When braking, the brake pads must rest fully on the side of the rim, not on the tire and not below the rim.

1. Loosen the pad's retaining screw slightly so that it can be moved.
2. Pull the brake lever so that the pad touches the rim.
3. Align the pad so that it is parallel to the side of the rim, in the middle of the braking surface, about one to two millimeters away from the tire.
4. Many mechanics place the pad at the front slightly closer to the rim than at the back, the so-called toe-in. This reduces squeaking. A narrow strip of cardboard between the rear end of the pad and the rim helps with alignment.
5. Tighten the screw again with the lever pulled.

### Adjust tension

As the pads wear, the lever travel becomes longer.

1. There is an **adjusting screw** with a lock nut on the brake lever. Loosen the lock nut and unscrew the adjusting screw. This tightens the cable and moves the pads closer to the rim.
2. If the adjustment path is not enough, turn the adjusting screw all the way back in, loosen the cable clamping screw on the brake arm, tighten the cable a little and clamp it again. Then fine-tune with the adjusting screw.
3. The distance between the pad and the rim should be around one to two millimeters on each side.

### Center the brake

If one pad is closer to the rim than the other or if one side is rubbing, the **spring preload screw** on the brake arms will help. This is usually a small Phillips or Allen screw on the side of the brake arm. If you turn it in, the spring tension on this side increases and the brake arm moves away from the rim. Adjust both sides so that the pads are the same distance from the rim.

### Refresh glazed coverings

Shiny, hard pads brake poorly and squeak. Lightly sand the surface with fine sandpaper until it is matt again. Remove any crushed stones and metal splinters with needle-nose pliers. If the pads are worn or cracked, replace them.

### Clean the rim

Dirt and brake abrasion on the rim impair the braking effect. Wipe the sides of the rim with a clean rag and some isopropyl alcohol. Check the wear indicator on the rim.

## Adjust mechanical disc brake

Mechanical disc brakes have a cable like V-brakes. Often only one pad is moved by the train, the other is fixed or is adjusted using its own screw.

1. **Centering the caliper:** Loosen the two screws securing the caliper to the frame or fork. Pull the brake lever, hold it firmly and tighten the screws alternately. This is how the saddle aligns with the disc.
2. **Setting the pad distance:** Many models have an adjustment wheel for the inner, fixed pad. Adjust it so that it is close to the disc without dragging.
3. **Tension:** As with the V-Brake, adjust using the adjusting screw on the lever or on the saddle.
4. **Testing:** Turn the wheel, no grinding, powerful braking effect with a clear pressure point.

## Hydraulic disc brakes: what you can do yourself

Hydraulic brakes adjust automatically when the pads wear. What you can do yourself is primarily centering and changing the pads. You should only bleed if you have the right tools, the right brake fluid and experience.

### Center the brake caliper to prevent grinding

1. Loosen the two brake caliper mounting screws slightly so that it can move sideways.
2. Pull the brake lever firmly and keep it pulled. A rubber band may help to hold the lever in place.
3. Tighten the screws alternately to the specified torque.
4. Release the lever and turn the wheel. If it is still grinding, look into the gap between the pad and the disc from above or against a light background. If necessary, move the saddle slightly by hand and tighten it again.

Light, even grinding immediately after installation is normal for some brakes and disappears after a few kilometers. Rhythmic grinding in one place indicates a slightly bent disc.

### Change pads

1. Remove the impeller.
2. Loosen the cotter pin or screw holding the pads and pull out the pads with the spring.
3. Push the pistons back carefully, preferably with a plastic tool, not a screwdriver, so as not to damage the pistons.
4. Insert and secure new pads with the spring.
5. Install the impeller, pull the lever several times until the pistons rest against the pads again.
6. Braking in new surfaces: brake sharply several times from medium speed on a quiet path without blocking.

Be careful not to touch the pads and disc with your fingers on the braking surfaces. Skin oil can reduce the braking effect.

### Slightly bent disc

A slightly bent brake disc can be carefully straightened using a special straightening tool. If the disc is severely deformed, cracked or has fallen below the minimum thickness, which is usually stamped on the disc, it must be replaced.

## Typical noises and their causes

| noise | possible cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Squeaking on V-Brake | Pads without toe-in, glazed pads, dirty rims | Adjust toe-in, sand pads, clean rims |
| Squeaking on disc brakes | contaminated surfaces, wet, not braked | Brake the pads, replace the pads if they have oil, clean the disc |
| Grinding constantly | Saddle not in the middle, pads too close | Center saddle, adjust pad spacing |
| Loops in one place | Bent disc, lateral runout in the impeller | Align the disc, center the impeller |
| Metallic scratching | Pads worn, carrier plate on disc or rim | Replace pads immediately, check disc or rim |

## If oil gets on the brakes

If chain oil, grease or a care spray gets on the brake disc or pads, the braking effect often drops dramatically. Thoroughly clean the disc with brake cleaner or isopropyl alcohol. Contaminated organic deposits usually cannot be reliably saved and should be replaced. Then be sure to brake and test.

## When it's the workshop's turn

- The pressure point of a hydraulic brake is spongy or wanders, even after several lever pulls.
- Brake fluid is leaking, lines or connections are oily.
- The brake needs to be bled or the fluid changed and you don't have the right tools.
- The rim has reached its wear limit or is showing cracks.
- After a fall, the brake lever, caliper or disc are damaged.
- You're not sure about a step.

## After the adjustment: the test drive

1. Pull the lever firmly several times: Firm pressure point, no pulling through to the handlebars.
2. Rotate impellers: No grinding.
3. Check screws: Everything is tightened with the correct torque.
4. Slow test drive in a quiet place: first brake gently, then brake harder.
5. Only then into traffic.

## Brakes on Pedelecs and cargo bikes

Pedelecs and cargo bikes are heavier and often travel faster than normal bikes. Your brakes therefore have to convert more energy into heat and the pads wear faster. Check the pads here more often, about once a month, and use the pads approved by the manufacturer, often sintered metal for heavy wheels. Many manufacturers prescribe larger brake discs for Pedelecs. Therefore, only exchange discs for the approved size. When it comes to cargo bikes with children, the following applies: It's better to go to the workshop more than once too little.

## A small maintenance routine for brakes

- **Weekly:** Pull the lever, check the pressure point, listen for noises.
- **Monthly:** View pads, clean rims or discs.
- **Semi-annually:** Check cables and casings, align pads, center saddles.
- **Annually:** Inspection; for hydraulic brakes, have the condition of the brake fluid checked.

## Conclusion

Adjusting the brakes is not rocket science. With a little patience you can align the pads, adjust the tension, center the saddles and change the pads yourself. What is important is clean work, the right torque and a test drive before the first use in traffic. However, if you have hydraulic problems, leaks or are unsure: go to the workshop. Good brakes are not a question of technology alone, but of regular care.
