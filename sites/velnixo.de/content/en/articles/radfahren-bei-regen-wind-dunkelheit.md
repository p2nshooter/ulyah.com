Anyone who uses a bicycle in everyday life doesn't just ride when the sun is shining. In Germany it rains on many days of the year, in autumn and winter it is dark in the mornings and evenings, and there is often a strong wind on open stretches. But with the right equipment and a few adapted habits, that's no reason to leave your bike behind. Many commuters ride all year round, and bad weather often feels less bad from the saddle than from the window.

This guide explains how driving changes in wet, windy and dark conditions, how to brake and steer safely, how to stay visible and when it is wiser to switch to the train or bus.

## Wet roads: braking, curves and risk of slipping

When it rains, the grip of the tires changes. The first few minutes after it begins to rain are particularly slippery, when water combines with dust, oil and rubber abrasion to form a greasy film. After a long period of rain, the road becomes a little more grippy again, but remains wet.

### Braking in wet conditions

- **Plan braking distance:** The braking distance becomes significantly longer when wet, especially with rim brakes because the rim has to be rubbed dry first. Keep more distance and brake earlier.
- **Pre-braking:** With rim brakes, it helps to brake slightly before an intersection so that the rims dry and the brake takes effect immediately if necessary.
- **Dose evenly:** Brake with both brakes evenly and without jerky movements. A wheel locks up more quickly on wet roads.
- **Disc brakes** are more reliable in wet conditions, but even with them, tire grip is the limiting factor.

### Curves and steering movements

When it is wet, drive corners more slowly and with less lean angle. Steer calmly and brake before the curve and not in it. You should be particularly careful about:

- **Road markings** such as zebra crossings, arrows and stop lines,
- **Manhole covers, manhole covers and tram tracks,**
- **cobblestones** that can become slippery like soap when wet,
- **wet leaves,** which is one of the biggest slippery hazards in autumn,
- **Wooden bridges and metal plates,** for example at construction sites.

### The correct tire pressure

When it rains, slightly lower tire pressure can improve grip because the contact area becomes larger. But stay within the range specified by the manufacturer on the tire sidewall. If the pressure is too low, the risk of a breakdown increases. Tires with good tread and a soft rubber compound grip better in wet conditions than worn or very hard tires.

## Stay dry: the right equipment

Getting wet is unpleasant on short journeys, but on long journeys it can get cold. With this equipment you will stay largely dry:

| Equipment | Benefit |
|---|---|
| Front and rear mudguards | prevent splashing water from below, which often wets more than the rain itself |
| Rain jacket with ventilation | keeps your upper body dry without you sweating underneath |
| Rain pants or rain cape | protects the legs, a cape is quickly put on |
| Overshoes or waterproof shoes | prevent wet feet |
| Waterproof gloves | important in cool rain |
| Waterproof panniers | protect laptop, clothing and documents |
| Helmet cover or cap under the helmet | keeps rain away from your face |

Find out more in the rainwear guide. A good tip for commuters: A change of clothes at work makes wet days much more relaxing.

## Visibility and visibility in the rain

Rain not only worsens your vision, but also that of others. Drivers see worse through wet windows and reflective roads, and windshield wipers obscure part of their field of vision.

- **Turn on the light:** You can turn on the front and rear lights even during the day in heavy rain.
- **Bright and reflective clothing:** A rain jacket in a signal color with reflective stripes stands out significantly more than a dark one.
- **Goggles:** Clear cycling glasses protect the eyes from raindrops. If you wear glasses, a cap with a peak under your helmet helps.
- **Distance and eye contact:** Slow down at intersections and make eye contact with drivers.

## Driving in the dark

In autumn and winter, many commuters drive in the morning and evening in the dark. In addition to good light, these rules help:

- **Good lighting:** A bright, well-adjusted headlight shows potholes, branches and broken glass in a timely manner. A powerful taillight is crucial for rear visibility.
- **Slow down on unfamiliar paths:** Unlit cycle paths through parks and across fields contain obstacles that you see late in the dark, such as bollards, curbs and broken roots.
- **Avoid glare:** If a car approaches you with high beams on, look to the right side of the road and slow down until your eyes recover.
- **Pedestrians and dark-clad cyclists:** Expect others to be out and about without lights, especially on bike paths.

In the bicycle lighting guide you will find everything about approved lamps, brightness and the correct setting.

## Fog

Fog sometimes reduces visibility to a few meters. Then the following applies:

- Turn on the lights, even during the day.
- Drive significantly slower so that you can stop within sight.
- Wear reflective clothing.
- Be particularly careful on country roads without cycle paths because cars will see you late. If possible, choose an alternative with a cycle path.

## Wind and storm

Strong winds are stressful for cyclists and can be dangerous.

- **Headwind:** Instead of fighting against the wind, shift into an easier gear and pedal at a steady cadence. A slightly lower position reduces the attack surface.
- **Crosswinds and gusts:** Hold the handlebars with both hands and do not ride hands-free. Gusts often come suddenly, for example between houses, on bridges, in fields or when a truck drives past. Drive a little further away from the edge of the road and others so that a gust doesn't endanger you.
- **Luggage:** High bags, baskets and especially a child trailer catch a lot of wind. In strong crosswinds the bike becomes restless.
- **Wheels with high rims** are more sensitive to crosswinds, which is particularly noticeable on racing bikes.

### When it's better not to drive in a storm

If there are storm warnings with hurricane gusts or thunderstorms, the bike is not the right means of transport. Falling branches, falling trees and strong gusts are serious hazards. Pay attention to the warnings from the German Weather Service and take a train or bus in such cases.

## Thunderstorm

In a thunderstorm you are not safe on your bike, even if the tires are made of rubber. Seek shelter in a building or an enclosed vehicle. If this is not possible, get off, move away from individual trees, masts and bodies of water and wait out the storm in a squatting position with your feet together. Don't keep driving hoping to get home quickly.

## Care for the bike after riding in the rain

Moisture and dirt take a toll on the bike. A few minutes of care significantly extends the lifespan:

1. **Wipe and oil the chain:** After riding in the rain, wipe the chain with a cloth and re-oil it after it has dried. A wet lubricant sticks better in the rain.
2. **Check the brakes:** With rim brakes, the pads and rims wear out more quickly in wet and dirty conditions. Check the pads regularly.
3. **Let the bike dry:** Place the bike in a dry, well-ventilated place.
4. **Electrics for Pedelecs:** Pedelecs are protected against rain, but are not made for high-pressure cleaning or submersion. Keep the battery contacts dry.

## Checklist for bad weather days

- Front and rear lights work and are charged
- Mudguards installed
- Packed a rain jacket and rain pants or cape
- Waterproof pockets for laptop and documents
- Tire pressure checked, tread OK
- Allow more time for the journey
- Weather warning checked

## Pedelec and cargo bike in bad weather

Pedelecs and cargo bikes are heavier than normal bikes. This has two consequences in wet conditions: the braking distance becomes longer, and when starting off with a high level of assistance, the rear wheel can spin briefly on slippery surfaces. When it rains, choose a lower support level for starting off and cornering, and brake even earlier than usual. Cargo bikes with children or heavy loads also react more strongly to crosswinds, especially if they have a roof.

You don't have to remove the battery of a Pedelec when it rains; the systems are protected against splash water. However, the range decreases at very low temperatures. Charge the battery at room temperature and only insert it shortly before driving, then it will last longer.

## Commuting all year round: routines that help

Anyone who cycles to work all year round develops routines over time that make bad weather almost irrelevant:

- **Prepare in the evening:** Check the weather report, get rain gear and lights ready, charge battery lamps.
- **Plan a time buffer:** Drive slower in rain, wind and darkness. Five to ten minutes more take the pressure off.
- **Drying place for clothes:** Set up a hook or corner at the workplace for wet jackets and pants.
- **Weather Protected Route:** Sometimes a different route offers more protection from wind, such as through built-up areas rather than open fields.
- **Know Plan B:** Know which bus or train connection is an alternative in the event of a storm or ice, and whether the bike can be taken on the train.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is cycling more dangerous in the rain?**
The risk of accidents increases because liability decreases and everyone sees worse. If you drive slower, brake earlier, avoid markings and leaves and are clearly visible, this will largely compensate for this.

**Do I need different tires when it rains?**
Good all-weather tires with tread and a sticky rubber compound are sufficient for everyday use. Special rain tires are not necessary, but you should replace worn tires before autumn.

**Can I drive on the sidewalk when it rains?**
No, the same rules apply even in bad weather. If a cycle path cannot be used because of puddles or leaves, you can move onto the road.

## Conclusion

Rain, wind and darkness are no reason to give up your bike. When wet, drive slower and with more distance, brake early and evenly and avoid slippery surfaces such as markings, rails and wet leaves. In rain, fog and darkness, turn on the lights and wear light-colored, reflective clothing. Stay dry with mudguards, good rain gear and waterproof bags. And if there is a storm warning or thunderstorm, it is wise to leave your bike and take the train or bus.
