A used bike can be a real stroke of luck. A solid trekking bike that is ten years old and well maintained often rides better than a cheap new bike from the hardware store. Buying used also saves resources and your wallet. At the same time, the used market is full of pitfalls: worn parts, hidden damage, unrealistic prices and, unfortunately, stolen wheels.

With this checklist you can systematically check a used bike, identify expensive repairs in advance and protect yourself from buying stolen goods.

## Before the appointment: Read the advert carefully

The ad itself reveals a lot. Pay attention to the following points:

- **Photos:** Good displays show the entire bike from both sides and details such as gears and brakes. Only a blurry photo from a long distance is a warning sign.
- **Description:** Are the brand, model, frame size and approximate year of manufacture mentioned? Are repairs or defects mentioned? An honest seller also describes scratches.
- **Price:** A branded bike that cost 1,200 euros new for 150 euros? This is either a wreck or an indication of theft.
- **Origin:** Questions about proof of purchase and frame number should be answered in a friendly manner.

Arrange the appointment during daylight hours and, if possible, at the seller's home address. Those who only want to meet in parking lots or train stations sometimes have good reasons, but sometimes not.

## Tools and aids for the appointment

Take a few things with you that will make the exam easier:

- a small flashlight for looking into corners and at weld seams,
- a clean cloth,
- your smartphone for photos of the frame number and for the frame number search,
- a chain wear gauge, if available,
- a printed purchase contract.

## The frame: the heart of the bike

A damaged frame is the most expensive defect and is often a total loss. Therefore, check it particularly carefully.

- **Cracks and dents:** Light up the welds on the head tube, bottom bracket and dropouts. Fine cracks often look like scratches in the paint. Dents in the top or down tube indicate a fall.
- **Delay:** Stand in front of and behind the bike and check whether both wheels are in line. A warped frame or bent fork after an accident often shows up like this.
- **Rust:** Surface rust on steel frames is usually harmless, but deep rust on weld seams or in the bottom bracket area is not. With aluminum, corrosion appears as a whitish deposit.
- **Carbon:** Damage is difficult to detect with carbon frames. Without specialist knowledge or a test in the workshop, we would advise against used carbon frames.

## Steering and headset

Pull the front brake and push the wheel back and forth. If there is a clicking or jerking noise in the handlebar area, the headset is loose or worn. Then lift the front wheel and slowly turn the handlebars left and right. It should move evenly and without stopping in the middle position. A “rest stop” straight ahead is a sign of a run-in tax rate.

## Brakes

Brakes are safety-relevant and must function properly.

- **Rim brakes:** Check the brake pads. Do they still have clear grooves? Are they hard and cracked? Also look at the sides of the rim: Many rims have a wear indicator, a groove or a point that disappears when the rim is worn. A concavely ground rim is a safety risk.
- **Disc brakes:** Does the lever feel tight or can it be pulled all the way to the handlebars? A spongy pressure point on hydraulic brakes indicates air in the system. Check the brake discs for deep grooves and discoloration and ask about the condition of the pads.
- **Coaster brake:** When pedaling backwards, the rear wheel must be able to lock immediately and firmly.

New pads are cheap. A new rim or a new hydraulic brake, on the other hand, can quickly cost a three-digit amount including installation.

## Drive: chain, sprockets, chainrings

The drive wears out and is often the reason why a used bike initially seems cheap and then becomes expensive.

- **Chain:** Is it rusty, stiff or dry? You can measure the elongation with a wear gauge. If the chain is very elongated, the sprockets and chainrings are often worn out. Then all three parts have to be replaced.
- **Teeth:** Pointed, shark-fin-like teeth on sprockets and chainrings show heavy wear.
- **Shift:** Shift through all gears while stationary with the rear wheel raised. Does the chain jump, does it drag or does it shift with a delay? This can often be adjusted, but sometimes cables, casings or the rear derailleur are worn out.
- **Hub gear:** Gears should click into place cleanly. A gear that is slipping can indicate incorrect adjustment or wear in the hub.

## Bottom brackets and cranks

Grab both cranks and try to move them sideways against the frame. Play indicates a worn or loose bottom bracket. Turn the cranks backwards: they should move easily and without grinding. Cracking noises when pedaling on a test ride can come from the bottom bracket, the pedals or even the frame.

## Wheels and tires

- **Rotation:** Lift each wheel and rotate it. Does the rim wobble sideways or bounce up and down? Slight lateral impacts can be centered, but severe vertical impacts or dents in the rim often cannot.
- **Spokes:** Pinch adjacent spokes together. They should all feel similarly firm. Loose or broken spokes are a warning sign.
- **Hubs:** Grab the tire and shake sideways. Play in the hub means bearing wear.
- **Tires:** Cracks in the sidewalls, worn tread or porous rubber mean new tires are due. Check the manufacturing date if it is written on the flank.

## Spring elements

With suspension forks and shocks, you press hard on the handlebars or saddle. The suspension should compress evenly and come back without any clicking. Oil on the standpipes, deep scratches or significant play are expensive defects. Ask about the last maintenance. Many manufacturers recommend regular service, which is not cheap depending on the model.

## Used e-bikes: special caution

Pedelecs also come with a battery, motor and electronics, which are difficult to test and can cost a lot. A new battery often costs several hundred euros. Ask about the age of the battery, charging cycles and whether the dealer can read the battery. We have written our own detailed guide to buying used e-bikes.

## The test drive

Always ride the bike yourself. Check:

1. Do they both brake evenly and strongly?
2. Does it shift smoothly into all gears under load?
3. When rolling hands-free, where this is permitted and safe, does the bike run straight?
4. Are there noises such as cracking, grinding or rattling?
5. Does the frame size fit?

## Stolen bike warning sign

Unfortunately, a lot of stolen bicycles change hands in Germany every year. Anyone who knowingly or negligently buys stolen goods may be guilty of receiving stolen goods. In addition, the rightful owner can demand his bike back, even if you have paid for it. Watch out for these warning signs:

- **Unrealistically low price** for a high quality bike.
- **No frame number** or a filed, painted over frame number.
- **No proof of purchase** and the seller cannot say anything about the origin.
- **Broken or missing lock**, scratch marks on frame where there was a lock.
- **Seller doesn't know the bike:** He doesn't know how the gears work or what size the frame is.
- **Meeting point in public space**, time pressure and cash payment without receipt.

The frame number is usually stamped under the bottom bracket. Photograph them and, if possible, match them with stolen bike databases. Some police departments and bike coding services offer queries. An honest seller has no problem with this.

## The purchase contract

Always conclude a written purchase contract. It should contain:

- Name, address and signature of buyer and seller,
- Brand, model, color and frame number,
- purchase price and date,
- known defects,
- the seller's declaration that he is the owner and the bike is free of third-party rights.

Show the seller's ID card and write down the ID number if they agree. Many bicycle associations offer templates for sales contracts.

## How much can a used bike cost?

There is no general rule, but a few points of reference can help:

- A well-preserved branded bike loses significantly in value in the first few years, after which the loss in value levels off.
- Deduct any wear parts from the price: chain, sprockets, tires, brake pads and cables quickly add up to 100 to 200 euros.
- Compare multiple listings of similar models in your area.
- For very cheap bikes without a well-known brand, repairing them is sometimes not worth it.

## Alternatives to private purchase

In addition to classified ads, there are other ways to find a used bike:

- **Bicycle dealers** often sell used bikes that have been checked and come with a warranty.
- **Bike fairs** organized by clubs and associations usually take place in spring, often with coding and checking of origin.
- **Lost property offices** regularly auction off unclaimed lost bikes.
- **Self-help workshops** sometimes offer refurbished bikes and help with testing.

## After purchase: the first review

Even if the test ride was good, it is worth checking it thoroughly after purchasing it before using the bike for everyday use. It takes two to three hours on a free afternoon or costs a manageable amount in the workshop.

1. **Check screws:** Check stem, handlebars, seat post, seat clamp, luggage rack and mudguards for tightness. Where a torque is printed, tighten with the torque wrench.
2. **Clean and oil the chain:** A dirty chain wears out quickly and causes the gears to become stuck. How to do this can be found in the chain care guide.
3. **Adjust tire pressure:** You can find the recommended range on the side of the tire. Many used bikes are sold with far too little air.
4. **Adjust the brakes:** Align the pads, re-tension the cables, and if you have disc brakes, clean the discs with brake cleaner.
5. **Test light:** Headlights, taillights and reflectors must comply with regulations. Our guide to bicycle lighting explains exactly what is required.

If you are unsure, take the bike to a workshop and have it inspected. This is a good investment, especially for older bikes, because it also reveals parts that are easy to overlook as a layperson.

## Code and register your bike

A newly purchased bike is the ideal opportunity to protect it against theft. Take a photo of it from both sides, note the frame number, brand, model and color and keep the purchase contract in a safe place. Many police departments and bicycle associations offer coding in which a personal identifier is engraved into the frame. A coded bike is less attractive to thieves and is easier to identify after theft. Of course, a good lock is also part of it; you can find out more about this in the guide to theft protection.

## Conclusion

A used bike is a smart choice if you check thoroughly. Take your time, go through the checklist point by point and don't let yourself be put under pressure. If something is wrong, a defect is too expensive or the origin is questionable, the most important rule of buying used applies: you can always say no. The next good offer is sure to come.
