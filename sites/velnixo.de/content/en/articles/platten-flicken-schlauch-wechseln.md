A flat tire is the most common breakdown when cycling. Sooner or later, it hits everyone: a shard of glass on the bike path, a sharp stone, a thorn in the dirt road or simply a tire pressure that is too low and the tube gets caught on an edge. The good news: With a little practice, a flat tire can be fixed in fifteen to twenty minutes, even on the side of the road.

In this guide we will show you what tools you need, how to remove the wheel, find the hole, patch or change the tube and how to prevent the tire from going flat again straight away.

## Tools and material

For repairs on the go, just a few things that fit in a small saddle bag are enough:

- **two to three tire levers** made of plastic,
- **Repair kit** with patches, vulcanizing solution and sandpaper, or self-adhesive patches,
- **a replacement hose** of the correct size and with the correct valve,
- **a pump** that fits your valve,
- possibly a **multitool** with an Allen key and open-end wrench if your wheels are screwed together.

At home, a floor pump with a pressure gauge and a bowl of water for finding small holes are helpful.

## What valves are there?

Before you buy a hose or pump, you need to know your valve:

- **Dunlop valve (lightning valve, bicycle valve):** Classic on everyday bikes, with a union nut.
- **Sclaverand Valve (Presta, French Valve):** Narrow, with a small knurled nut on the tip that must be unscrewed before pumping. Typical on racing, gravel and many trekking bikes.
- **Schrader valve (car valve):** Like on cars, often on mountain bikes and some pedelecs. It can also be inflated at the gas station.

The size of the tube is written on the tire sidewall, for example “42-622” or “28 x 1.60”. The first number is the tire width in millimeters, the second is the rim diameter. The hose must fit this size.

## Step 1: Remove the impeller

### front wheel

1. With rim brakes, open the brake so that the tire can fit through. With V-brakes, the draw tube is hung out of the holder.
2. Open the quick release or loosen the axle nuts. For quick-release axles, unscrew the axle.
3. Take the wheel out of the fork. With disc brakes, do not pull the brake lever while the wheel is removed, otherwise the pads will move together.

### Rear wheel with derailleur gears

1. Shift into the lowest gear at the rear. This makes removal and installation easier.
2. Open the brake on rim brakes.
3. Loosen quick release, quick release axle or nuts.
4. Pull the rear derailleur slightly backwards and guide the wheel downwards out of the frame.

### Rear wheel with hub gear

In the case of hub gears, the shift cable must also be loosened; in the case of coaster brakes, the brake strut on the frame must be released. How exactly this works depends on the model. It's worth practicing at home or having a dealer show you how to do it. You can also mend a tube on the go without completely removing the rear wheel: you pry off the tire on one side, pull out the tube, patch the hole and put it back in.

## Step 2: Remove the tire from the rim

1. **Release residual air:** For Dunlop, loosen the union nut, for Presta, unscrew and press the knurled nut, for Schrader, press in the valve core.
2. **Press the tire into the center of the rim:** Press the tire bead all around from the edge of the rim into the center of the rim. The rim base is deeper there and the tire becomes looser.
3. **Attach tire lever:** Slide a tire lever under the tire bead opposite the valve and lever the tire over the edge of the rim. Hook the jack onto a spoke.
4. **Attach the second jack:** Place the second jack approximately ten centimeters next to it and further pry off the tire. The tire can then usually be removed all around using the jack or your fingers.
5. **Remove the tube:** Finally push the valve through the valve hole on the rim.

It is usually enough to just remove one side of the tire from the rim.

## Step 3: Find the hole

1. **Inflate the tube:** Pump some air into it so that the tube is slightly stretched beyond its normal size.
2. **Hear and feel:** With larger holes you can hear the hissing sound or feel the air flow on your cheek or lips.
3. **Water bath:** For small holes, pull the hose section by section through a bowl of water. Where bubbles rise is the hole. A puddle or some water from the drinking bottle helps on the way.
4. **Mark:** Mark the spot with a ballpoint pen, chalk or a fingernail.

Pay attention to the type of hole:

- **A small single hole** usually comes from a foreign body such as glass or a thorn.
- **Two small slits next to each other**, so-called snakebites, are caused by a puncture on one edge when the tire pressure was too low.
- **A hole on the inside** towards the rim indicates a slipped or defective rim tape or a sharp spoke edge.
- **A crack in the valve** often occurs when driving with too little pressure and the tube has migrated into the tire.

## Step 4: Find the cause - the most important step

Before you patch, you need to figure out what caused the flat. Otherwise the tire will be flat again after a few meters.

1. **Place the tube on the tire:** Place the tube next to the tire as it sat in it, with the valve at the valve hole. This will help you find the spot on the tire that corresponds to the hole.
2. **Feel the inside of the tire:** Carefully run your fingers along the inside of the tire. Be careful, broken glass and wires can be sharp. Feel slowly and with light pressure.
3. **Check the outside of the tire:** Look for stuck splinters, thorns or stones.
4. **Check rim tape:** It must completely cover all spoke holes and must not be slipped or damaged.

Carefully push out foreign bodies from the inside out using a tire lever or tweezers.

## Step 5a: Patch the hose

1. **Roughen the area:** Use the sandpaper from the repair kit to slightly roughen the area around the hole, slightly larger than the patch.
2. **Apply vulcanizing solution:** Brush a thin layer onto the roughened area.
3. **Let dry:** Depending on the product, a few minutes until the solution is matt and no longer sticky. This is important, otherwise the patch won't hold.
4. **Press the patch on:** Peel off the protective film from the patch without touching the adhesive surface and press the patch firmly onto the hole. Stroke from the center outwards and press firmly for some time.
5. **Wait briefly:** Then inflate the hose slightly and check whether it is tight.

**Self-adhesive patches** are faster, but usually don't last as long as classic vulcanized patches. They are a good emergency solution for on the go.

## Step 5b: Change hose

It is often quicker to insert a replacement hose when you are on the move and then repair the defective hose in peace at home.

1. Inflate the new hose slightly so that it becomes round. This means it doesn't twist or get jammed during assembly.
2. Insert the valve through the valve hole in the rim.
3. Place the tube all around the tire without twisting it.

## Step 6: Reinstall tires

1. **Start at the valve:** Press the tire bead opposite the valve over the edge of the rim with your hands and then work evenly in both directions.
2. **Press the bead into the center of the rim:** To ensure that the tire has enough play, repeatedly press the already installed bead into the deep center of the rim.
3. **The last piece:** It's getting tight at the valve. Use both thumbs to roll the tire over the edge. If that doesn't work, carefully use a tire lever, but be careful not to pinch the tube.
4. **Push the valve up:** Briefly press the valve into the tire so that the hose on the valve is not caught under the tire bead.
5. **Check:** Check all around whether the tube is sticking out anywhere between the tire and the rim.

## Step 7: Inflate and install impeller

1. **Inflate slowly:** First build up only a little pressure and check whether the tire is seated evenly. The fine line on the side of the tire should run evenly all around just above the rim.
2. **Inflate to operating pressure:** Observe the recommended pressure range from the tire sidewall.
3. **Install the impeller:** In the reverse order of removal. The wheel must be in the middle, the quick release must be closed tightly or the axle must be tightened correctly.
4. **Close the brake:** For rim brakes, be sure to reconnect the brake. With disc brakes, pull the lever a few times until the pads fit properly again.
5. **Test drive:** Test the brakes, check whether the wheel runs smoothly and nothing rubs.

## Prevent breakdowns

- **Proper tire pressure:** Too little pressure is one of the most common causes of flat tires. Check the pressure regularly with a pressure gauge.
- **Anti-puncture tires:** Tires with a protective layer under the tread will keep out a lot of splinters and thorns. They are a little heavier, but highly recommended for everyday use and touring.
- **Check tires regularly:** Small splinters that have not yet penetrated can be removed before they cause damage.
- **Avoid broken pieces:** If possible, drive around glass on the bike path, do not drive over curbs head-on with little pressure.
- **Tubeless and sealant:** Some bikes, especially mountain bikes and gravel bikes, run tubeless with sealant. Small holes then seal themselves. The system has advantages and disadvantages and requires suitable tires and rims.

## If the tire is damaged

If a foreign body has made a major cut in the tire, the tube will push out and burst again. On the way, a piece of solid plastic, a folded banknote or a special tire patch from the inside can serve as an emergency solution to slowly get home. The tire must then be replaced. Cracks in the sidewall or visible tissue are also a reason for a new tire.

## Conclusion

A flat tire is annoying, but not a drama. With tire levers, a repair kit, a spare tube and a pump, you are prepared for the most common breakdown. The most important thing is to find the cause and eliminate it, otherwise you'll end up on the side of the road again after a short time. Practice the process in peace at home, then it will be much more relaxed on the road, and with the right tire pressure and good tires, the next flat tire will be less likely.
