Data sheets, test reports and size tables are helpful, but they cannot replace one thing: the feeling on the bike. Two bikes with almost identical equipment can ride completely differently. One appears calm and familiar, the other nervous or sluggish. That's why the test drive is the most important step before buying, and at the same time the one that is often done most hastily: once across the parking lot, back, "feels good".

With a little preparation, you can learn a lot more about a bike in ten to fifteen minutes. In this guide we will show you how to plan a test drive, what to check and what questions you should ask the dealer.

## Before the visit: go to the store well prepared

The more clearly you know what you are looking for, the better the dealer can advise you and the more meaningful the test drive will be.

- **Note purpose:** Commuting, shopping, touring, sports? How long are your typical routes and how hilly is it?
- **Set budget:** including lock, helmet and accessories. This way you avoid falling in love with a bike that is significantly larger than your frame.
- **Measure inseam length:** This allows the dealer to pre-select the right frame size more quickly.
- **Wear appropriate clothing:** It's best to wear the clothing you'll be riding in later. If you want to cycle to the office, don't test in sportswear.
- **Bring your own pedals or shoes:** If you ride clipless pedals, bring them with you or ask if the dealer has suitable ones.
- **If necessary, take luggage with you:** If you carry a heavy backpack or pannier every day, you should also test the bike that way.

Schedule time. A Saturday morning in spring is the busiest time of the week in many stores. During the week, the staff often has more peace and quiet to provide detailed advice.

## Compare multiple bikes

If possible, test ride at least two, preferably three bikes: different models in the same category or the same model in two frame sizes. Differences that are not noticeable on the first bike suddenly become clear in a direct comparison. After each trip, jot down your impressions briefly, otherwise they will quickly become blurred.

## Before setting off: have the bike adjusted

A test ride on an incorrectly adjusted bike says little. Ask the dealer to adjust the following beforehand:

1. **Saddle height:** With the heel on the pedal in the lowest position, the leg should be almost straight.
2. **Saddle position:** Horizontal, not too far forward or back.
3. **Handlebar height:** A little higher for everyday use, lower for sporty driving.
4. **Brake levers:** They should be accessible with a slightly bent wrist, the distance from the handle appropriate to your hand size.
5. **Tire pressure:** Too little air makes every wheel sluggish, too much makes every wheel restless. Ask about the pressure or check it briefly.

For Pedelecs, have the operation explained to you: switching on and off, assistance levels, range display and, if available, the pushing aid.

## The test track: more than a parking lot

Ask if you can drive a slightly longer route. Many dealers allow a walk around the block or even a longer test drive for a deposit. A route with:

- a straight section to pick up speed,
- a curve or a roundabout,
- a small incline to test the gears and, on Pedelecs, the motor,
- uneven surfaces such as cobblestones or a curb,
- a quiet place where you can safely try hard braking.

Ride the same route with each bike you test. This makes the impressions comparable.

## What you check while driving

### Seating position and comfort

- Do you feel confident on the bike or do you have to work hard to keep your balance?
- Can you look straight ahead in a relaxed manner without overextending your neck?
- Are your arms slightly bent or are you straightening them?
- After a few minutes, do you feel pressure on your hands, numbness in your fingers, or discomfort in the seating area?
- Can you look over your shoulder comfortably without swaying?

### Driving behavior

- **Starting:** Is starting off easy, even from a standstill on an incline?
- **Slow riding:** Does the bike remain stable at walking pace? This is important in the city and when stopping.
- **Corners:** Does the wheel turn naturally, or does it feel sluggish or nervous?
- **Bumps:** How do cobblestones and edges feel? Does it rattle or do the tires and frame dampen well?
- **Roll Freehand:** If it is safe to do so, a short roll with loose hands will show whether the wheel is going straight.

### Brakes

Brake firmly in a safe place. Check:

- Do the brakes apply immediately and evenly?
- Can the braking force be adjusted well, or does the wheel lock abruptly?
- How much hand strength do you need?
- Is something squeaking or grinding?

### Circuit

- Does the circuit shift cleanly and quickly, even under light load?
- Do you find the gears intuitively, without thinking?
- Is the lightest gear enough for the inclines in your everyday life?
- For hub gears: Does shifting work smoothly when stationary?

### Additionally for Pedelecs

- Does the motor start smoothly or does it push jerkily?
- How natural does the support feel? Does it slow down gently at 25 km/h?
- How loud is the engine?
- How well can the bike be pushed and lifted without a motor? Feel free to try it out, as if you had to carry it into a basement.
- Where is the battery located and how easy is it to remove?

## The sounds of the wheel

Listen carefully. A new bike should run almost silently. Cracking when pedaling hard, clattering over cobblestones or grinding when braking are all indications of suboptimal assembly on a new bike. This can usually be easily remedied, but should be done before handover.

## After the ride: look at the bike while it is stationary

- **Processing:** Are weld seams clean, cables laid neatly, mudguards firmly installed?
- **Equipment:** Is everything you need included? Light, stand, luggage rack, bell, reflectors?
- **Suitability for everyday use:** Does the bike fit in your basement, on your bike rack, in your elevator? How hard is it?
- **Luggage:** Is the luggage rack suitable for your bags and how much load can it carry?

## Questions for the dealer

Good advice is reflected in how openly the dealer responds to questions. You should ask these questions:

1. **Which inspections are included in the purchase price?** Many dealers offer a free initial inspection after a few hundred kilometers because cables and spokes settle.
2. **What does the warranty look like and is there a manufacturer’s warranty on the frame, motor or battery?**
3. **How long are spare parts available?** This is particularly important for Pedelecs.
4. **Can I adjust the bike later?** For example a different stem, a different saddle or different grips if problems arise after a few weeks.
5. **Are there exchange or adjustment options if the size doesn't fit?**
6. **Which lock do you recommend for my bike, and are there any insurance requirements?**
7. **How long does a repair take for you and can I get a replacement wheel during this time?**

## Typical errors during the test drive

- **Testing too short:** A lap around the parking lot is not enough to assess the seating position and comfort.
- **Only ride one bike:** Without comparison there is no scale.
- **Do not have it adjusted:** An incorrectly adjusted saddle completely distorts the impression.
- **Let the design guide you:** You won't enjoy riding a beautiful bike that makes you feel unsafe.
- **Let yourself be pressured:** “Only today at this price” is not a good reason to buy. A reputable dealer gives you time.

## Test drive at online retailers and direct mail order companies

Many bikes are sold online today. Test drives are then more difficult, but not impossible. Some consignors have showrooms or partner dealers, others offer a return policy after a certain test period. Read the conditions carefully: Who pays for the return shipping, what condition does the bike have to be in, how many kilometers can you ride? Also keep in mind that you often have to assemble and adjust a bike you buy online yourself and that not every workshop is happy to service bikes purchased from someone else.

## The handover: What should happen when you pick it up

Once you have decided, the handover is the second important moment. A good dealer takes the time to do this. Make sure that the following points are taken care of:

- **Final adjustment:** Saddle, handlebars, brake levers and possibly the suspension are adjusted to suit you, ideally while you are sitting on the bike.
- **Explanation of the technology:** How does the circuit work, how do you turn on the light, how do you remove the impeller? For Pedelecs: charging, removing the battery, understanding the displays.
- **Documents:** Invoice with frame number, operating instructions, warranty conditions, for Pedelecs the documents for the motor and battery as well as the key for the battery lock with key number.
- **Date for the initial inspection:** After the first few hundred kilometers, cables and spokes seize up. A follow-up check ensures that everything remains precise.

Also ask the dealer to enter the frame number on the invoice and have the bike coded straight away if necessary. This helps enormously if it is stolen.

## After the first few weeks: readjust

Even the best adjusted bike sometimes feels different after a few weeks as you get used to the new bike and ride longer distances. Typical subtleties:

- **Numb hands:** Grips with palm rests or a slightly different handlebar angle often help.
- **Pressure in the seat area:** Change the saddle inclination minimally or try a different saddle. Many dealers have test saddles.
- **Knee pain:** The saddle is often a little too low or too high. Even a few millimeters make a difference.
- **Tight neck:** A slightly higher or shorter stem relieves the strain.

Make a note of the complaints and address them during the initial inspection. Small adjustments cost little and make a good bike your bike.

## Conclusion

A good test drive requires some preparation and ten to fifteen minutes of attention. Have the bike adjusted, drive a varied test route, compare several models and pay attention to the seating position, brakes, gears and noises. Ask the dealer important questions about service and warranty. And above all: trust your feelings. The bike that you feel safe and comfortable on after ten minutes is, in the vast majority of cases, the bike that you will most enjoy riding in the next few years.
