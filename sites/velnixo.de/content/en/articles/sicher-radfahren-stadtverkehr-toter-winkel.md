The city is both everyday life and a challenge for cyclists. Short distances, cycle paths and cycle streets often make cycling the fastest means of transport. At the same time, you share space with cars, vans, buses, trucks, trams, pedestrians and e-scooters. Most accidents involving cyclists occur in urban areas, and many of them occur at intersections and junctions.

The good news: Many typical accidents can be avoided if you know the danger spots and drive with foresight. This guide shows the most important situations and how to behave in them.

## The blind spot: the biggest danger when turning

Trucks, buses and large vans have areas that the driver cannot see or can only see with difficulty despite the mirrors. The area to the right of the vehicle and directly in front of the driver's cab is particularly dangerous. If a truck turns right while a cyclist is driving straight ahead, the most serious accidents in city traffic occur.

Newer trucks have turn assistants and additional mirrors, but not every vehicle is equipped with them and no system is error-free. The responsibility lies with the driver of the truck, but there is only one thing that can ensure your safety: do not go into the blind spot.

### This is how you behave correctly

- **Do not stand to the right of a truck.** At the traffic lights, it is better to stop behind the truck, a few meters away. When you stand there, the driver sees you in the mirror.
- **Don't pass on the right.** If a truck is slowly approaching an intersection, don't overtake it on the right, even on the bike path. He could turn.
- **Make eye contact.** If you see the driver's face in the mirror, he probably sees you too. If you don't see him, he doesn't see you.
- **Observe the turn signals.** A truck that is flashing right will turn even if it is still stationary. Wait for him to turn.
- **Distance ahead.** There is also a blind area directly in front of a truck. Do not stand directly in front of the driver's cab at traffic lights.

Many cities have advanced stopping lines for cyclists at intersections, so-called staging areas. They put you in front of the cars and trucks and into the drivers' field of vision. Use it when you can reach it safely, i.e. when the color is red and not while the column is already moving.

## Turning accidents involving cars

Turning accidents are also common in cars. A car turns right and misses the cyclist on the bike path, or a car turns left and misjudges the speed of an oncoming Pedelec. It is helpful:

- **Expect errors.** Don't assume a driver will see you just because you have the right of way. Drive at intersections with your fingers on the brake levers, ready to brake.
- **Position visibly.** Do not ride in the slipstream of large vehicles, and be particularly careful at junctions on cycle paths that run behind parked cars.
- **Adjust your pace.** With a Pedelec you are often faster than others expect. Driving a little slower at intersections gives you and others more time.

## Dooring: the opening car door

A suddenly opened car door poses a serious danger to cyclists. Anyone who drives into the door or avoids it and ends up in the flow of traffic can have a serious fall. The driver must pay attention to traffic before opening the door, but for your safety:

- **Keep your distance:** Drive past parked cars at a distance of about one meter, even if this means moving further into the lane. This is allowed and makes sense.
- **Look for clues:** A person in the driver's seat, interior lights, a car that has just parked, or a taxi that is stopped are warning signs.
- **Drive Steady:** Don't weave between parked cars. A straight line is easier for everyone to assess.

If you drive a car yourself, you can use the so-called Dutch handle: Open the driver's door with your right hand. As a result, the upper body automatically turns and the view goes backwards towards the bike path.

## The correct position on the road

Many cyclists ride on the sidelines out of insecurity. This is often more dangerous than you think: cars then overtake too closely, manhole covers and potholes force you to make sudden evasive movements, and you are less visible at junctions.

- **Distance from the edge:** Drive about 80 centimeters from the curb and about one meter from parked cars.
- **Drive in the middle of narrow streets:** If a street is so narrow that a car cannot overtake with the prescribed distance of 1.5 meters, you can drive in such a way that a close overtaking is not even attempted. This is not provocation, but self-protection.
- **Get in line in time before obstacles:** If you have to get past an obstacle, look over your shoulder early, give a hand signal and get in line quickly.

## Roundabouts

In a roundabout, traffic in the circle has the right of way if the roundabout sign and the give way sign are in place. There are two variants for cyclists:

- **Bike path around the roundabout:** Here you cycle around the outside and often have priority at the exits if it is marked that way. Still, expect cars to miss you.
- **Riding in the roundabout:** If there is no cycle path, you ride in the roundabout. Then drive clearly in the middle of the lane, not on the right edge. This means no one can overtake you in the roundabout and turn right in front of you. Indicate the departure with a hand signal.

## Tram tracks, storm drains and wet markings

Tram tracks are treacherous for cyclists. If a tire gets into the groove, a fall is almost inevitable.

- **Cross the rails at as steep an angle as possible,** ideally almost at right angles. The flatter the angle, the greater the risk of the front wheel slipping into the groove.
- **Do not drive directly next to tracks** if you can avoid it. You have to do it, preferably with sufficient distance.
- **Be particularly careful when wet.** Metal from rails and manhole covers as well as road markings become very slippery when it rains. Do not steer or brake sharply when cornering or braking on such surfaces.

## Pedestrians, e-scooters and other cyclists

Pedestrians have priority on shared sidewalks and cycle paths and in approved pedestrian zones. Ringing the bell is allowed, but only to draw attention to you, not to force space. A kind word often works better.

- **Children and the elderly** sometimes move unpredictably. Drive slower and keep your distance.
- **E-scooters** are allowed to use cycle paths. They accelerate quickly, often drive without hand signals and are smaller than you think.
- **Overtaking other cyclists:** Announce yourself with the bell, overtake on the left with enough distance and look over your shoulder beforehand to make sure someone else isn't overtaking you.

## Driving with foresight: the most important skill

Experienced cyclists recognize dangers before they arise. These habits will help:

1. **Look far ahead,** not just at the front wheel. Watch for cars coming out of driveways, doors, and pedestrians between parked cars.
2. **Look over your shoulder before every lane change,** even on cycle paths. A rearview mirror on the handlebars can also help.
3. **Drive ready to brake:** At intersections and in confusing areas, keep your fingers on the brake levers.
4. **Drive clearly and predictably:** Give hand signals, drive in straight lines, do not move between the sidewalk and the road.
5. **Avoid your own mistakes:** No driving against the direction of travel, no red lights, no cell phone in your hand.

## Visibility day and night

Being seen is crucial in city traffic. During the day, light-colored clothing and daytime running lights help. At dusk and at night, good front and rear lights are mandatory; reflective elements on clothing, bags and tires also make you visible. More about this in the guide to bicycle lighting.

## Planning routes: the safe route is not always the shortest

It's worth choosing your route carefully, especially for your daily commute to work. A parallel side street, a bicycle lane or a path through a park is often only a little longer, but much more relaxed than a main road. Many navigation apps for cyclists offer routes that favor low-traffic streets. It's best to start a new route on the weekend when there is less traffic.

## Typical accident situations at a glance

| situation | Danger | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Truck turns right | Cyclists in the blind spot | Wait behind the truck, never pass on the right |
| Car turns right, bike path behind parked cars | Cyclist is seen late | Drive ready to brake, reduce speed at junctions |
| Oncoming car turns left | Speed is underestimated | Make eye contact, do not insist on right of way |
| Parked cars | opening door | keep a distance of one meter and pay attention to people in the car |
| Exits and properties | Car drives out without looking | Detect movements and reversing lights early |
| Tram tracks | Front wheel slips into the groove | Cross steeply, be particularly careful when wet |
| Cycle path on the left without permission | Cars don't count on you | only drive in the approved direction |

## If it does happen: what to do after an accident

Even if you drive carefully, you can get into an accident. Then the following applies:

1. **Secure and help:** Get yourself and others out of the danger zone and provide first aid. If anyone is injured, call 112.
2. **Call the police:** If someone is injured, it is unclear who was to blame or if the other person involved does not want to provide any information, call the police on 110.
3. **Exchange data:** Write down the name, address, license plate number and insurance of the other party in the accident, as well as the names and telephone numbers of witnesses.
4. **Document:** Take photos of the accident scene, the vehicles, the bike and any injuries.
5. **Go to the doctor:** Even for seemingly minor injuries, as some consequences only become apparent later. A medical report is also important for claims.
6. **Have the bike checked:** After a fall, the frame, fork or a carbon part can be damaged without being immediately noticeable. Have the bike checked in the workshop before your next ride.

In Germany, accidents on the way to work or home are usually covered by statutory accident insurance. Report such a commuting accident to your employer.

## Conclusion

In city traffic, intersections and junctions are the most dangerous places. Keep your distance from trucks and buses and never stand to the right of a vehicle that might be turning. Drive past parked cars at a distance of one meter, consciously take your space on narrow streets and cross tram tracks as perpendicularly as possible. Drive with foresight, be prepared to brake and be predictable, expect other people's mistakes and choose quiet routes where possible. This makes the journey through the city safer and more relaxed.
