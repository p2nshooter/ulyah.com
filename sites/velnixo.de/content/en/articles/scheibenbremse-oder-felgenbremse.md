Brakes are the most important safety component on a bicycle. Nevertheless, when making a purchase decision, people often talk more about gears, color and weight than about the brakes. The various systems differ significantly: in terms of braking power in wet conditions, controllability, maintenance requirements and the costs of wearing parts.

In this guide we introduce the common types of brakes, explain their strengths and weaknesses and give clear recommendations for different types of bikes and purposes. You will also find out what the law requires and how you can tell when a horsefly needs attention.

## What the law requires

According to road traffic registration regulations, a bicycle must have two independent brakes. The regulation does not specify what type of construction this is. Two rim brakes are permitted, as are two disc brakes or a combination of handbrake and coaster brake. It is important that both brakes work and act independently of each other. A bike without brakes, as you sometimes see on rigid track bikes, is not permitted on the road.

## The rim brake: proven and simple

With the rim brake, two brake pads press against the side of the rim. The most well-known designs are:

- **V-Brake:** Widely used on trekking, city and older mountain bikes. Two long brake arms are pulled together using a train. V-brakes are powerful, light and affordable.
- **Side pull brake:** Typical for racing bikes. A compact brake housing sits above the tire.
- **Hydraulic rim brake:** Instead of a cable, oil transmits the power. These brakes are very powerful, easy to control and require little maintenance and have long been popular on high-quality trekking bikes.

### Advantages

- **Easy to purchase and maintain.** Pads cost little and are easy to replace.
- **Lightweight.** Rim brakes weigh less than disc brakes.
- **Simple technology.** Cable, lever, pads: many things can be adjusted yourself using simple tools.

### Disadvantages

- **Weaker when wet.** A wet rim must first be wiped dry before the brakes fully engage. That costs valuable meters.
- **Rim wear.** Every time you brake, the rim is also worn. At some point it becomes so thin that it needs to be replaced. Many rims have a wear indicator for this.
- **Dirt and abrasion.** Mud, sand and brake abrasion settle on the rim and accelerate wear.
- **Sensitive to lateral impacts.** An out-of-round wheel will drag on the pads or brake unevenly.

## The disc brake: powerful in any weather

With the disc brake, a steel brake disc is located on the hub. A brake caliper on the frame or fork presses the pads against the disc. There are two variants:

- **Mechanical disc brake:** A cable operates the brake caliper. It is cheaper and easy to maintain, but less powerful and less easy to control than the hydraulic variant.
- **Hydraulic disc brake:** A brake fluid transmits the power. This ensures high braking performance with little manual force, a clear pressure point and automatic adjustment in the event of pad wear.

### Advantages

- **Consistent braking performance in all weathers.** Wet, mud and snow affect the disc much less than the rim.
- **No rim wear.** The rim is protected and therefore lasts longer.
- **Can be finely adjusted.** Hydraulic brakes in particular can be operated in a very controlled manner with one finger.
- **Independent of lateral runout.** A slightly out-of-round wheel has little influence on the braking effect.

### Disadvantages

- **Higher price.** Good hydraulic disc brakes cost more.
- **Higher weight.** Discs, saddles and the reinforced hubs make the bike slightly heavier.
- **Maintenance requires knowledge.** Bleeding a hydraulic brake requires tools, the correct brake fluid and practice. Many cyclists leave this to the workshop.
- **Sensitive to oil.** If chain oil or grease gets on the disc or pads, they hardly brake anymore. Then the pads often have to be replaced and the disc cleaned.
- **Grinding noises.** A slightly warped brake caliper or a bent disc can grind. This is mostly harmless, but annoying and can be adjusted with a little patience.

## The coaster brake: comfortable, but limited

The backpedal brake is located in the rear wheel hub and is activated by pedaling backwards. It is common on children's bikes, Dutch bikes and some city bikes.

**Advantages:** Low maintenance, independent of weather, intuitive for people who grew up with it, and leaves a free hand for the hand signal.

**Disadvantages:** It only affects the rear wheel, which is relieved when braking and can lock. On long descents, the hub can become hot and lose effectiveness. If the chain comes off, the brake is ineffective. In addition, the pedal position is restricted when starting off because you cannot pedal backwards freely.

A coaster brake should therefore always be combined with a powerful front wheel brake.

## Roller brake and drum brake

On some city bikes you will find roller or drum brakes in the hub. They are encapsulated, weatherproof and very low-maintenance. Their braking performance is lower than that of a disc brake, but is often sufficient for quiet city driving. Roller brakes occasionally require a special grease to be added to the hub.

## Our recommendation by bike type

- **Pedelec and cargo bike:** Hydraulic disc brakes are practically mandatory here. Higher weight and higher speed require powerful, stable brakes.
- **Trekking bike for touring and commuting:** Hydraulic disc brakes offer the most safety in any weather. Good V-brakes or hydraulic rim brakes are a solid alternative for dry areas and smaller budgets.
- **City bike for short distances:** V-brake, roller brake or backpedal with powerful front wheel brake are usually sufficient. Anyone who drives a lot in the rain will benefit from a disc brake.
- **Mountain Bike:** Hydraulic disc brakes are standard and essential for steep descents.
- **Road bike and gravel bike:** Modern bikes almost always have disc brakes. Rim brakes are lighter and perfectly adequate in dry conditions, but are less common.
- **Children's bike:** Two handbrakes with child-friendly, easily accessible levers. For small children, a coaster brake is often also installed.

## Brake properly

The best brake is of little use if you use it incorrectly. These basic rules will help:

1. **Use both brakes.** The front brake can transfer most of the braking force because the weight shifts forward when braking. If you only brake at the rear, you waste braking distance.
2. **Pull in a measured manner.** Not jerkily, but quickly and evenly. This means the front wheel remains stable and the rear wheel does not lock.
3. **Shift your weight backwards.** If you come to an emergency stop, stretch your arms slightly and push your body backwards.
4. **Brake beforehand when cornering.** Braking hard in the middle of a corner can cause the wheel to slip.
5. **Brake earlier when wet.** Especially with rim brakes: brake lightly once so that the rim is dry, then brake hard.
6. **Brake alternately on long descents.** Don't let it drag constantly, but brake hard at intervals. This prevents the discs and pads from overheating.

It's best to practice emergency braking in an empty parking lot. This way you will learn how your bike reacts before you need it in an emergency.

## How you can tell that the brake needs attention

- The brake lever can be pulled almost to the handlebars.
- The brake squeaks loudly or constantly grinds.
- The braking effect has noticeably decreased.
- For rim brakes: The pads no longer have grooves, or the rim wear indicator is no longer visible.
- For disc brakes: The lining is thinner than about half a millimeter, the disc has deep grooves or has become visibly thinner.
- The pressure point of a hydraulic brake moves or feels spongy.

We show you how to check brakes and make simple adjustments yourself in our guide to adjusting brakes. If you have hydraulic brakes that need to be bled and if you have any doubts, it's better to go to a specialist workshop.

## Retrofitting: Is that possible?

Anyone who has a bike with rim brakes sometimes wonders whether they can convert to disc brakes. In most cases this does not make sense or is not possible. The frame and fork need special mounts for the brake calipers, and the hubs must have a disc mount. A conversion often means new wheels, a new fork and new brakes, which quickly becomes more expensive than a new wheel. Better pads, new cables and careful adjustment, on the other hand, often bring a lot with a good rim brake.

## Brake pads: which type for which purpose?

There are two basic pad compounds for disc brakes:

- **Organic pads** (also called resin) brake quietly, grip quickly and are inexpensive. However, they wear out more quickly, especially in wet and muddy conditions, and are less able to tolerate extreme heat on long descents.
- **Sintered metal coverings** are harder, last longer and remain stable even in hot and wet conditions. On the other hand, they are louder, take a little longer to fully grip, and cause a little more wear on the disc. Not every disc is approved for sintered coatings; this information is usually stated on the disc.

Sintered surfaces are often the better choice for Pedelecs, cargo bikes and mountain regions, while organic ones are the better choice for light bikes in flat areas. When it comes to rim brakes, there are special pads for wet conditions and for carbon rims. For everyday aluminum rims, standard pads from a well-known manufacturer are usually sufficient.

New pads need to be **braked in**: On a quiet path, drive off at medium speed about twenty times and brake hard, but without blocking, until you almost come to a standstill. Only then will the coverings achieve their full effect.

## Brake fluid: DOT or mineral oil

Hydraulic brakes work with either **mineral oil** or **DOT brake fluid**, which is also used in cars. The two must never be mixed up, otherwise the seals will be destroyed and the brake will fail. Which fluid your brake needs is usually stated on the brake lever or the expansion tank. DOT fluid absorbs water over time and should be changed regularly. Mineral oil is less aggressive, but also ages. Let the workshop do the bleeding if you don't have the right tools or experience with it.

## Conclusion

For most people who use their bike in everyday life in all weathers, a hydraulic disc brake is now the safest and most comfortable choice, especially for Pedelecs and cargo bikes. But good rim brakes are by no means bad: they are cheap, light and completely sufficient for many applications if you look after them. In the end, it's not just the design that matters, but the condition: a well-adjusted rim brake brakes better than a neglected disc brake. Check your brakes regularly, practice braking correctly and, if in doubt, get help.
