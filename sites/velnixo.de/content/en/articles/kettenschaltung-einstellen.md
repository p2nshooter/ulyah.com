A well-adjusted derailleur gear works almost silently: one click and the chain jumps cleanly onto the next sprocket. A misaligned gearshift, on the other hand, rattles, jumps between gears, shifts with delay or, in the worst case, throws the chain into the spokes. The most common cause is banal: the shift cable has elongated or settled a little, especially with new bikes in the first few weeks.

In this guide we explain how a rear derailleur works, what screws there are and how you can adjust the gears yourself step by step. With a little patience, all you need is a small screwdriver and a few minutes.

## This is how the rear derailleur works

The rear derailleur is located on the rear wheel under the cassette. It has two small rollers over which the chain runs and is moved by a shift cable. When you shift one click further on the shifter, the cable tightens or loosens and the rear derailleur moves the chain inwards or outwards by exactly one sprocket distance.

For a clean function, three things have to be right:

1. **The tension:** This determines whether each click corresponds to exactly one pinion.
2. **The stops:** They limit the outward and inward travel of the rear derailleur so that the chain does not fall off the smallest sprocket or get into the spokes.
3. **The distance to the cassette:** This determines how close the upper roller runs to the sprockets.

In addition, the **derailleur hanger** must be straight. This is the small metal part that attaches the rear derailleur to the frame. It is intentionally soft so that in the event of a fall, the derailleur hanger bends and not the frame. A bent derailleur hanger is a very common cause of shifting problems that cannot be solved with any adjustment.

## The screws on the rear derailleur

- **Cable adjustment screw (adjustment screw):** is located where the cable runs into the rear derailleur, often on the shifter. It changes the tension in fine steps.
- **H screw (High):** limits the path to the smallest sprocket, i.e. to the fastest gear.
- **L-Screw (Low):** limits the path to the largest sprocket, i.e. to the lightest gear.
- **B screw:** changes the distance between the upper pulley and the cassette.

The screws H and L are usually labeled. If not, a look at the instructions or a test will help: The screw that changes the position in the lowest gear when turned is the H screw.

## Preparation

1. **Set up the bike safely:** It's best to do it in a repair stand so that you can turn the rear wheel and shift gears at the same time. If necessary, head first on the saddle and handlebars.
2. **Clean and oil the drive:** A dry or dirty chain shifts poorly. First clean, then adjust.
3. **Check chain and sprockets:** A very elongated chain or worn sprockets cannot be adjusted properly.
4. **Check the derailleur hanger:** Look at the bike from behind. The two derailleur rollers should be vertical under the cassette, parallel to the sprockets. If the rear derailleur is at an angle, the derailleur hanger is probably bent. Then first have the derailleur hanger adjusted or replaced in the workshop.

## Step 1: Check the stops

You only rarely need to adjust the stops, for example after installing a new rear derailleur or if the chain jumps past the smallest or largest sprocket. If the circuit has worked well so far and no longer shifts smoothly, the cable tension is usually the problem. Then you can go directly to step 3.

### H screw (smallest pinion)

1. Shift to the smallest sprocket at the rear and the largest chainring at the front.
2. Look from behind: The upper pulley should be exactly under the smallest sprocket.
3. If it is further out, screw in the H screw. If it is further in and the chain rattles on the second smallest sprocket, unscrew it.

### L screw (largest pinion)

1. Shift to the largest sprocket at the rear and the smallest chainring at the front. Use your hand to gently push the rear derailleur towards the spokes to see if it can go too far.
2. The upper pulley should be right under the largest sprocket and not go any further towards the spokes.
3. If the rear derailleur can be pushed past the largest sprocket, screw in the L screw. If the chain does not reach the largest sprocket, carefully unscrew it.

**Important:** An incorrectly adjusted L stop can cause the chain to fall between the sprocket and spokes. This can damage spokes, rear derailleur and frame and lead to a fall. Therefore, check this setting very carefully.

## Step 2: The B screw

The B screw adjusts the distance between the upper pulley and the cassette. If the distance is too small, the roller will rub against the large sprockets or it will rattle. If it is too large, the circuit switches sluggishly.

1. Shift to the largest sprocket.
2. Turn the crank backwards and observe the distance between the upper pulley and the sprocket.
3. Many manufacturers specify a distance of a few millimeters, and there is often even a gauge for this. Without gauge: as close as possible without the roller touching the sprocket or chattering.

## Step 3: Adjust the tension

This is the step that fixes most shifting problems.

1. **Smallest rear gear:** Shift to the smallest sprocket.
2. **To shift up a gear:** Click the shift lever toward the larger sprocket once while turning the crank.
3. **Watch:**
   - If the chain **does not jump onto the second sprocket or only hesitantly**, the tension is too loose. Turn the tension adjusting screw **out**, i.e. counterclockwise, half a turn.
   - If the chain jumps **too far** onto the third sprocket or rattles towards larger sprockets, the pull is too tight. Turn the set screw **in**, clockwise.
4. **Repeat:** Shift back and test again until the change from first to second gear is clean and quick.
5. **Test all gears:** Shift up and down through all gears one after the other. Each click should move the chain to the next sprocket cleanly, without chatter.

Note: The adjusting screw is turned in the direction in which the chain should move. If you want the chain to be more towards the large sprockets, unscrew the screw towards you when you are standing behind the wheel.

### Fine adjustment under load

What looks perfect in the assembly stand can still rattle slightly under load. Take a short test drive and shift through all gears under normal load. You can often make small corrections of a quarter turn to the adjusting screw on the gearshift lever while driving.

## The front derailleur

If your bike has two or three chainrings at the front, there is also a front derailleur. It is more sensitive to adjust than the rear derailleur.

1. **Height and Angle:** The front derailleur's outer baffle should be parallel to the chainrings and approximately one to three millimeters above the teeth of the largest chainring.
2. **Stops:** The front derailleur also has an L and an H screw. They prevent the chain from falling inwards onto the bottom bracket or outwards over the large chainring.
3. **Tension:** Via an adjusting screw on the shift lever. The chain should move cleanly to each chainring and not drag on the front derailleur in any sensible gear combination.
4. **Trim:** Many shifters have a trim function: a half click that moves the derailleur minimally to avoid dragging when the chain runs at an angle.

If you get stuck with the front derailleur, the workshop is a good address. An incorrectly installed front derailleur can throw off the chain.

## Common problems and their solutions

| problem | possible cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Chain doesn't jump onto larger sprocket | Pull too loose | Unscrew the adjusting screw |
| Chain rattles, jumps too far | Pull too tight | Turn the adjusting screw in |
| Gearshift only works in some gears | bent derailleur hanger, dirty cable | Check derailleur hanger, replace cable and housing |
| Chain falls off the smallest sprocket | H stop too wide open | Turn in the H screw |
| Chain falls into spokes | L stop too wide open | Turn in the L screw and check the spokes |
| Shifting difficult | Cable or cover dirty or kinked | Replace cable and casing |
| Jumps under load despite setting | elongated chain, worn sprockets | Measure wear, replace parts |

## When to replace the cable and casing

Shift cables and cable housings wear out. Rust, dirt and kinks increase friction, making the gearshift sluggish or inaccurate despite correct adjustment. Typical signs include a stiff shifter, a gearshift that hesitates when downshifting, and settings that are lost after a short time. A new train with covers costs little and often noticeably improves the circuitry. The replacement can be done yourself with a little practice, but requires a good draw cutter to ensure the ends are clean.

## When the workshop helps

- The derailleur hanger is bent and needs to be straightened or replaced.
- The rear derailleur was damaged in a fall.
- The chain has fallen into the spokes and spokes may be damaged.
- Electronic circuits need to be adjusted or updated.
- You've tried all the steps and it still doesn't work.

## Adjustment for new wheels

Anyone who buys a new bike almost always experiences this: after a few weeks, the gears no longer shift as precisely as they did on the first day. This is not a defect. New shift cables lengthen minimally and the cable housings sit in their end caps. That's why many dealers offer a free initial inspection. You can also adjust the tension yourself by half a turn on the adjusting screw, as described above. After that, the setting usually remains stable for a long time.

## Maintain the circuit so that it stays adjusted

- Clean and oil the chain regularly.
- Occasionally remove dirt from the shift rollers.
- Do not place the bike on the rear derailleur side and pay attention to the rear derailleur when parking it on bike stands.
- Protect the rear derailleur when transporting it in the car or train.
- Always check the derailleur hanger after a fall.

## Conclusion

Most shifting problems can be solved in just a few minutes using the cable adjustment screw. If you also understand the stops and the B screw and pay attention to a straight derailleur hanger, you can almost always adjust your derailleur gear perfectly yourself. It is important to work in small steps, test all gears after each change and check the setting under load at the end. And if the drive is worn out, no adjustment in the world will help: then new parts are the best solution.
