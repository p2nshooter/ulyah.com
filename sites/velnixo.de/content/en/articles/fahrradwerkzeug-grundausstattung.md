If you care for your bike yourself, you don't need a fully equipped professional studio. For most everyday tasks, a handful of good tools are sufficient, and together they cost less than a single major inspection. With each additional skill, the workshop grows as needed.

In this guide we present useful basic equipment, explain what the individual tools are used for, and show the levels at which you can expand the workshop. You'll also get tips on what you should pay attention to when buying and which tools should be in your saddle bag when you're on the go.

## Basic principle: rather few, but good tools

Cheap tools are often expensive. Soft Allen keys round off screw heads, wobbly tire levers break, and a pump without a pressure gauge leaves you guessing. Good tools last for decades, protect the components and make work more pleasant. It is therefore better to buy individual, high-quality tools one at a time rather than a large, cheap suitcase with many parts that you will never need.

## Level 1: the absolute minimum

With these tools you can carry out the most common tasks: pumping up air, repairing plates, adjusting the saddle and handlebars, and maintaining the chain.

### Floor pump with pressure gauge

The most important purchase of all. A floor pump pumps much faster than a mini pump, and the pressure gauge shows the pressure accurately. Look for a pump head that fits your valve or a head that can handle Dunlop, Presta and Schrader valves. A stable base and a long hose make work easier.

### Allen key (Allen)

Almost all screws on modern bicycles have a hexagon socket. A set in sizes 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 8 millimeters covers almost everything. Keys with a ball head make it easier to work at oblique angles, but you should use the straight side for tightening. High-quality keys are dimensionally accurate and do not round off screws.

### Torx key

Many brake discs, chainring bolts and components on newer bikes use Torx screws, especially T25. A small set of T10 to T30 makes sense.

### Tire lever

Two to three sturdy plastic tire levers. Metal jacks can damage rims and tubes.

### Repair kit and spare hose

Classic repair kit with vulcanizing solution and patches as well as a replacement tube in the right size.

### Chain oil, rag and brush

A chain oil that suits your conditions, old cotton rags and a brush for the drivetrain.

## Level 2: for regular care and small repairs

### Torque wrench

A torque wrench is the best insurance against screws that are too tight or too loose. The stem, handlebars and seat post in particular often have low torques of 4 to 8 Newton meters, which are specified. Aluminum and carbon parts can tear if they are too tight, and if they are too loose they can twist while driving. For bicycles, a key with a range of around 2 to 15 or 3 to 25 Newton meters is usually sufficient.

### Chain wear gauge

Costs little and saves a lot of money because you change the chain in good time.

### Chain tool and chain lock pliers

For shortening, opening and closing the chain. The chain lock pliers make opening modern chain locks much easier.

### Side cutters or pull cutters

For clean cutting of brake and gear cables and casings. A normal side cutter often crushes cables and casings, but a special cable cutter cuts them cleanly.

### Open-end wrench 15 millimeters

Many pedals and older axle nuts require a size 15 wrench. There are special, narrow and long pedal wrenches for pedals.

### Phillips and slotted screwdrivers

For the adjustment screws on the rear derailleur, front derailleur and some brakes.

### Spoke wrench

For small corrections to lateral strokes. Make sure you choose the right size for your spoke nipples. You should start centering carefully, because an incorrectly centered wheel is worse than a slight lateral runout.

## Level 3: for ambitious DIY enthusiasts

### Assembly stand

A work stand keeps the bike at working height and makes almost all jobs more convenient. The bike is clamped to the seat post, never to the frame tube of a carbon or thin-walled aluminum bike. For heavy Pedelecs you need a particularly stable stand or a stand that holds the bike at the dropouts.

### Cassette puller and chain whip

For removing the cassette from the rear wheel, for example when changing the sprockets. The puller must fit the cassette system.

### Bottom bracket tool

For removing and installing the bottom bracket. There are many different standards, so check carefully which system your bike has before buying.

### Crank puller

For older cranks with square or splined mounts. Modern cranks can often be loosened with an Allen key.

### Truing stand

For serious wheel work. For most hobby mechanics, a truing stand is only worthwhile if they frequently build or center wheels.

### Bleeding set for hydraulic brakes

Only suitable for the brand of your brake and with the correct brake fluid. Without experience, bleeding is a task for the workshop.

## This belongs in the saddlebag

You need a compact selection when you're on the go:

- **Multitool** with Allen 3 to 6 mm, Torx T25, Phillips and ideally a chain riveter,
- **two tire levers**,
- **Replacement hose** and self-adhesive patches,
- **Mini pump** or CO2 cartridge with adapter,
- **Chain lock** to match your chain,
- **a few cable ties** and a piece of tape for emergency repairs,
- **Disposable gloves** and a small cloth.

All of this fits into a small bag under the saddle and weighs only a few hundred grams.

## Set up workshop space

A good workplace makes work easier:

- **Good light:** A bright lamp or headlamp so you can see small screws and crevices clearly.
- **Order:** A perforated wall, tool box or magnetic strip so that every tool has its place.
- **Underlay:** An old mat or cardboard under the wheel protects the floor from oil.
- **Small parts tray:** So that screws, washers and springs are not lost when disassembling. Place the pieces in the order in which you removed them.
- **Smartphone:** Take a photo of what something looks like before disassembling. This helps enormously when assembling.

## Consumables

In addition to tools, you will need some consumables:

- Chain oil, possibly two types for dry and wet conditions,
- Assembly grease for threads, bearings and seat posts made of aluminum or steel,
- Assembly paste for carbon parts that improves clamping force,
- brake cleaner or isopropyl alcohol,
- bicycle cleaners and sponges,
- Replacement tubes, brake pads and chain locks for your bike.

## What you don't have to buy

- **The large tool box for 200 euros** if you just patch tires and adjust the saddle.
- **Special tool for systems that your bike does not have.** Before making any purchase, check which standards your bike uses.
- **Expensive cleaning equipment** when a cloth and brush are sufficient.

For rare work involving expensive special tools, it is worth visiting a self-help workshop. There you can use tools and often get help from experienced mechanics.

## Use tools correctly

Good tools only help if you use them correctly. These basic rules protect screws, components and fingers:

- **Insert the key completely:** An Allen key that is only halfway into the screw head completes it. Always insert it completely and place it straight.
- **First loosen, then turn:** Loosen stuck screws with a short, strong jerk instead of slowly increasing force. When it comes to pedals, remember: The left pedal has a left-hand thread and is loosened clockwise.
- **Tighten with feeling:** Small screws on the stem, handlebars, brakes and gears have low torques. Without a torque wrench, there is a high risk of over-tightening. Tighten the multiple clamps crosswise and in several steps as with the stem.
- **Grease threads:** Most threads and clamping surfaces made of steel and aluminum receive a little assembly grease to prevent them from rusting. Exception: brake disc screws and other screws for which the manufacturer specifies screw locking. Carbon parts receive carbon assembly paste instead of grease.
- **Don't force anything:** If a part cannot be loosened or assembled, the wrong tool is often involved, a thread is tilted or a left-hand thread has been overlooked. It's better to read it briefly than to destroy an expensive component.

## Tools and Pedelecs

Most work on a Pedelec can be done using the same tools as on a normal bicycle. Additionally you should know:

- Before carrying out any work, remove the battery or switch off the system.
- Motor, battery, display and cable connections are not opened. There are neither useful hobby tools nor safe instructions for laypeople.
- A Pedelec weighs a lot. A sturdy workstand that is weight rated or a stand that holds the bike to the ground is important.
- Cranks on Pedelecs are often located on special mounts on the motor. There are some manufacturer-specific tools for triggering and assembly.

## Self-help workshops: sharing tools instead of buying them

There are bicycle self-help workshops in many cities, often run by clubs, youth centers or universities. There you will find well-equipped workstations with assembly stands and special tools that are not worthwhile for a single household, such as truing stands, bottom bracket tools or presses for headsets. Experienced volunteers often help the first time. For a small donation or contribution, you can repair your bike yourself and learn a lot at the same time.

## Maintain tools

Tools also need a little care: wipe off oil and dirt after work, clean pump heads occasionally, replace the seal in the pump head if there is a leak, and turn the torque wrench back to the lowest value after use if the instructions say so. Keep tools dry so they don't rust. This means the workshop will remain reliable for many years.

## Typical torques on a bicycle

The exact values can be found on the components or in the manufacturer's instructions and always take precedence. The following ranges just give you a sense of how low many values are:

| Component | usual size |
|---|---|
| Stem clamp on the handlebars | about 4-6 Nm |
| Stem clamp on the fork shaft | about 5-8 Nm |
| Seat post clamp | about 4-8 Nm |
| Brake caliper attachment | about 6-8 Nm |
| Brake disc screws | about 2-6 Nm |
| Pedals | about 30-40 Nm |

## Conclusion

A good home bicycle repair shop starts with a few high-quality tools: a floor pump with a pressure gauge, a set of Allen and Torx wrenches, tire levers, a repair kit and chain oil. With a torque wrench, chain tool and wear gauge you can significantly expand your options. You only buy special tools when you really need them, and there are self-help workshops and specialist workshops for everything else.
