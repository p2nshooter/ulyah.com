Bikepacking is the light, sporty sister of the classic bike tour. Instead of large panniers on the luggage rack, the luggage hangs in narrow bags directly on the frame, saddle and handlebars. The bike remains maneuverable, even on gravel, forest paths and narrow paths. Many bikepackers ride off the main bike paths, spend the night in a tent, under a tarp or in simple accommodation and travel with as little luggage as possible.

This guide explains what you need to get started, which bags are useful, how you can sleep light but still safely, which rules apply in Germany for sleeping outside and how you can plan your first weekend tour.

## What differentiates bikepacking from a bike trip

| | Classic bike tour | Bikepacking |
|---|---|---|
| Luggage | Panniers on the luggage rack, often 15 to 25 kg | Bags on the frame, usually 6 to 10 kg |
| underground | especially asphalt and paved cycle paths | also gravel, field paths, forest paths |
| wheel | Trekking bike or touring bike with luggage rack | Gravel bike, mountain bike, also trekking bike |
| Overnight stay | Hotels, guesthouses, campsites | Campsites, simple accommodation, bivouac where permitted |
| Pace and stages | quiet, with plenty of time for places | often more sporty, longer days in the saddle |

The boundaries are fluid. Many start with bikepacking bags on their existing bike and stay in guesthouses. This is a good way to try out traveling light without having to buy camping gear.

## The right bike

You don’t need a special bike for bikepacking. It is important that the bike fits the planned routes and functions reliably.

- **Gravel bike:** fast on asphalt, good on gravel, with wide tires and often lots of eyelets for bottles and bags. Very popular for bikepacking.
- **Mountain bike (hardtail):** robust and comfortable on difficult trails, slightly slower on the road. Ideal for tours through low mountain ranges and forests.
- **Trekking bike:** works well on paved paths and light gravel. The luggage rack can also be used.
- **Road bike:** only suitable for asphalt tours with very light luggage.

More important than the type of wheel are **wide, puncture-proof tires**, a **transmission with easy gears** and **reliable brakes**. With luggage and on loose surfaces, easy gears are crucial.

## The bags

### Saddlebag

A large saddle bag hangs from the seat post and seat rails and holds approximately 8 to 16 liters. Light, voluminous things go here, such as sleeping bags, down jackets or clothing. It should be packed tightly so that it doesn't swing back and forth. Bikes with a lowerable seat post require a special holder or a smaller bag.

### Frame bag

The frame bag fills the frame triangle completely or half and carries the heavy parts: tools, provisions, stove, power bank. Because the weight sits low and centrally here, it has the least influence on driving behavior. A half frame pocket leaves space for drinking bottles.

### Handlebar roller

A waterproof roll on the handlebars holds the tent, sleeping mat or tarp. It shouldn't be too heavy so that the bike remains easy to steer, and it shouldn't interfere with the brake cables or the light.

### Small bags

- **Top tube pocket:** for snacks, cell phone, sunscreen, ready to hand while riding.
- **Fork bags or cages:** on the eyelets of the fork for water bottles or small pack sacks.
- **Food bag on the handlebars:** for a water bottle or snacks.

## Sleep: light and warm enough

On multi-day tours, a good night's sleep is more important than every gram you save. The sleeping equipment consists of three parts:

| part | What to pay attention to |
|---|---|
| Shelter | Light tent (one to two people, preferably under 1.5 kg), alternatively tarp with bivouac bag for experienced people |
| sleeping bag | Comfort temperature matching the expected nighttime temperature; Down is lighter and smaller, synthetic fibers tolerate moisture better |
| sleeping pad | Inflatable mat with sufficient insulation value, more important in spring and autumn than many people think |

The nights in Germany are often cool even in summer; in spring and autumn it can be close to zero degrees at night. If in doubt, take a slightly warmer sleeping bag with you instead of freezing.

## Spending the night outside: what applies in Germany

Wild camping is generally **not allowed** in Germany, but the rules differ depending on the federal state and area. In many forests and in all nature reserves and national parks, camping outside of designated areas is prohibited. Some federal states allow a single overnight stay without a tent, for example in a bivouac bag, under certain conditions. Don't rely on hearsay, but find out the rules in your region before the tour.

Good legal alternatives are:

- **Campsites:** almost everywhere, often with areas for cyclists and hikers.
- **Trekking sites and bivouac sites:** In several regions there are simple, officially designated places for a night in a tent, usually for a small fee and with advance notice.
- **Private properties:** Camping is possible with the permission of the owner, for example on a farm. Ask politely.
- **Simple accommodation:** Youth hostels, nature lovers' houses, guesthouses.

Where you stay: Leave no trace. No open fires, take rubbish with you, keep your distance from bodies of water, be quiet and do not disturb animals.

## Find routes

Bikepacking thrives on paths away from traffic. This is how you find suitable routes:

- **Existing routes:** There are numerous described bikepacking routes in Germany, many of them with GPX files, information on route conditions and supplies.
- **Own planning:** Route planners with gravel or mountain bike settings prefer field and forest paths. Check the condition of the route before you decide, because not every route on the map is easy to drive with luggage.
- **Plan supplies:** Shops away from the towns are rare. Note where you get water and food, especially on weekends and holidays.
- **Exit points:** Train stations along the route are your plan B.

## Packing list for a first weekend

| area | What should with |
|---|---|
| Sleep | Tent or tarp, sleeping bag, sleeping mat |
| Clothing | Cycling shorts, jersey, warm layer, rain jacket, leg warmers, hat, night clothes |
| Food and drink | two to three drinking bottles, provisions for a day, possibly a light stove and pot |
| tool | Spare tube, repair kit, tire lever, pump, multi-tool with chain riveter, chain lock, cable ties, duct tape, tubeless repair kit if necessary |
| Light and electricity | Front and rear light, headlamp, power bank, cable |
| Hygiene and health | Sunscreen, seat cream, small first aid kit, toothbrush, trash bag |
| Orientation | Mobile phone or bicycle navigation system with offline maps, route as GPX |

## The first tour: start small

To get started, we recommend a tour with **one overnight stay**, no further than 50 to 80 kilometers per day, with a campsite or simple accommodation as the destination. This way you can test your equipment, packing and your own pace without risking much. After the first night you know exactly what was missing and what was unnecessary.

## Common mistakes

- **Handlebar castor too heavy:** The bike steers sluggishly and sways. Heavy items belong in the frame bag.
- **Swinging saddle bag:** When packed loosely, it swings and chafes. Grip tightly and tighten the straps.
- **Too little water:** Consumption is high, especially on gravel paths in summer. Better one more bottle.
- **Unrealistic stages:** Gravel and inclines cost time. Expect significantly fewer kilometers than on asphalt.
- **Test new equipment on the go:** Put up the tent, light the stove and attach the bags. Practice at home beforehand.

## Driving technique with luggage on gravel

A loaded bike rides differently on loose surfaces than on the road. A few basic rules help:

- **Reduce air pressure:** On gravel and forest paths, a slightly lower tire pressure provides more grip and comfort. Stay within the allowable range and check the pressure when switching back to asphalt.
- **Stay relaxed:** Arms and knees slightly bent, do not hold the handlebars tightly. The bike can move slightly underneath you.
- **Look ahead:** Look far ahead, not at the front wheel. This way you can choose the best line through grooves, stones and puddles early on.
- **Brake carefully downhill:** Use both brakes evenly, slow down before corners and do not brake hard in the curve. The luggage moves along.
- **Pushing steep places:** If you get off and push on a steep, loose ramp, you save energy and protect the material. This is not defeat, but experience.

## Weather, safety and emergency

Away from the major bike paths, you are often far away from the next town. That's why it's worth taking a little extra care:

- **Check the weather report daily:** Thunderstorms are particularly dangerous in the forest and on open heights. Plan stages so that you can reach a location or accommodation in time if there is a severe weather warning.
- **Leave route:** Let someone know your route and where you plan to stay overnight, especially if you're driving alone.
- **Save battery:** The cell phone is a card, telephone and emergency call at the same time. Flight mode in dead spots, power bank in your luggage.
- **Emergency call:** Throughout Europe you can reach the emergency services and fire brigade on 112. If possible, use an app that sends your location.
- **Small first aid kit:** Plasters, disinfection, blister plasters, a rescue blanket and personal medication weigh little and help a lot.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a gravel bike for bikepacking?**
No. Many people start with their trekking bike or mountain bike. A gravel bike is practical, but more important are wide tires, easy gears and a bike that is well maintained.

**How much does the basic equipment cost?**
A set of saddle, frame and handlebar bags starts at around 150 to 300 euros, high-quality bags cost more. For the first tour you can also rent a tent and sleeping bag, for example from a friend or a rental company.

**Can I spend the night in the forest?**
Mostly not. In Germany, camping outside designated areas is generally prohibited; the details are regulated by the federal states. Use campsites, trekking sites or ask property owners for permission.

## Conclusion

Bikepacking means traveling light with bags directly on your bike, often away from major bike paths. A gravel bike, mountain bike or well-equipped trekking bike with wide tires and easy gears is enough to get you started. Heavy items belong in the frame bag, light items in the saddle bag and handlebar roll. Plan your overnight stay legally, because wild camping is usually not allowed in Germany; Campsites, trekking sites and private meadows with permission are good alternatives. Start with a short one-night tour and you'll quickly realize how much freedom there is in just a few kilograms of luggage.
