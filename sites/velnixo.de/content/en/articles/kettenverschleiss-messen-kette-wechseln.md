Bicycle chains “lengthen” over time. That sounds strange, because of course the individual metal parts don't get any longer. What this means is that the joints between the bolts and sleeves wear out. This creates a tiny amount of play in each link, and this adds up to a measurable lengthening over the entire chain. An elongated chain no longer grips the teeth of the sprockets and chainrings cleanly and wears them unevenly.

If you change the chain in a timely manner, you can drive several chains with one set of sprockets. If you change too late, you have to replace the chain, cassette and often chainrings together. This guide will show you how to measure wear, when the right time is and how to install a new chain yourself.

## Why the right time is so important

Sprockets and chainrings adapt over time to the chain they are ridden with. As long as the chain is only slightly elongated, the teeth hardly wear out. However, if the chain is stretched significantly, the rollers grind the teeth into sharp, slanted shapes. If you then install a new chain, it no longer fits the worn teeth: it jumps under load, especially on the frequently used sprockets.

The costs speak for themselves: a new chain costs around 15 to 50 euros for everyday bikes. Depending on the number of gears and quality, a new cassette costs between 30 and over 100 euros, and chainrings are also included. So it's worth changing in good time.

## Wear limits: When is the chain due?

The elongation is given in percent. The following rules of thumb apply:

| Chain type | Wear limit |
|---|---|
| Chains for 11- and 12-speed gears | about 0.5% |
| Chains for 9- and 10-speed gears | 0.5 to 0.75% |
| Chains for up to 8-speed gears | about 0.75% |
| Chains for hub gears and single speed | up to 1% |

Some manufacturers provide their own values. If in doubt, follow the manufacturer's instructions or change earlier. In very narrow modern chains with many gears, the sprockets are more sensitive to wear.

## This is how you measure chain wear

### With a chain wear gauge

The easiest and most reliable method. A chain gauge only costs a few euros and usually has two sides, for example for 0.5 and 0.75 percent.

1. Place the gauge on the chain so that the hook sits on one side between two rollers.
2. Try to lower the other side of the teaching into the chain.
3. If the gauge falls completely between the rollers on the 0.5 percent side, this limit has been reached. If it also falls on the 0.75 percent side, the chain is significantly worn out.
4. Measure in several places on the chain because it can wear unevenly.

Make sure to put the gauge on without force. Anyone who presses distorts the result. Simple gauges sometimes also measure the wear of the rollers and therefore show wear a little earlier. This is not a disadvantage for everyday life.

### With a ruler or tape measure

Bicycle chains have a link spacing of half an inch, i.e. 12.7 millimeters. Twelve full links, i.e. 24 rivets, are exactly 12 inches or 304.8 millimeters long in a new chain.

1. Keep the chain under slight tension, preferably on the bike, while you press lightly on the pedal.
2. Place the zero point of a ruler exactly in the middle of a rivet bolt.
3. Continue counting 24 rivets and read.
4. At around 306.3 millimeters the chain is elongated by 0.5 percent, at around 307 millimeters by 0.75 percent.

This method requires precise work, but also works without special tools.

### The rough thumb test

Pull the chain forward on the largest chainring at the point where it rests at the front. If it can be lifted so far that you can see large parts of a tooth, it is probably worn out. This test is imprecise and does not replace a measurement, but it can be an initial indication.

## How often should you measure?

For everyday bikes, it is sufficient to measure every 500 to 1,000 kilometers or every major cleaning. With Pedelecs that transmit more power and when riding in the rain and in winter, it is better to do it more often. Write down the mileage when changing so you can get a feel for how long your chains will last you.

## The right new chain

Chains differ primarily in width. It must match the number of sprockets:

- **Simple (single speed, hub gear):** Chains with 1/8 inch or 3/32 inch inner width, depending on the sprocket.
- **5- to 8-speed:** 3/32-inch chains with an outside width of approximately 7 millimeters.
- **9x, 10x, 11x, 12x:** increasingly narrower chains. Here the chain must match the number of gears exactly, and many manufacturers recommend combining the chain and cassette of the same system.

It is best to buy the chain with the appropriate name for your gears. New chains are usually too long and need to be shortened. Chains with 114 to 126 links are common.

## Chain changing tool

- **Chain riveter:** for opening and shortening the chain and, depending on the system, for closing it.
- **Chain lock pliers:** makes it easier to open and close chain locks.
- **Chain lock** or **headpin**, suitable for the chain. Many chains come with a lock.
- **Gloves and rags.**

## The correct chain length

If the chain is too short, it can damage or even break the rear derailleur in the highest gear. If it is too long, it will sag in the lowest gear, hit the frame or jump off.

The easiest method with the same drive: **Lay the old and new chain next to each other** and shorten the new one to the same length. Important: The old chain is slightly longer due to wear, so count the links instead of just comparing the length.

If you need to redetermine the length:

1. Place the chain on the largest chainring and sprocket without passing it through the rear derailleur.
2. Pull them together tightly.
3. Depending on the manufacturer's specifications, usually add two links, i.e. an outer and an inner link.
4. Separate rules apply to full-suspension mountain bikes and special gears. Then read the instructions from the circuit manufacturer.

## Open chain and remove old chain

- **With chain lock:** Use the chain lock pliers to press the two links next to the lock together until the lock comes loose.
- **Without chain lock:** Press out a rivet bolt with the chain riveter. On modern chains with many gears, a pin that has been pushed out should not be reused.

## Install new chain

1. **Thread the chain:** Thread the new chain around the smallest chainring and sprocket, then through the rear derailleur, so that it runs properly in front of the upper guide plate and between the rollers. Make sure that it does not run past a bar on the rear derailleur.
2. **Note running direction:** Some chains have a running direction or an outside with a label. This should face outwards.
3. **Shorten the chain:** Cut off the excess links with the chain tool.
4. **Close chain:**
   - **With chain lock:** Insert the two lock halves, push them together, turn the chain so that the lock is at the top between the chainring and the sprocket, and then press the pedal firmly or use the pliers until the lock clicks into place.
   - **With rivet pin:** Press in the special pin with the chain riveter until it protrudes evenly on both sides, then break off the guide part.
5. **Resolve Stiff Link:** After riveting, the link is often a little stiff. At this point, carefully bend the chain sideways until it runs freely.

## After assembly

1. Turn the crank backwards and pay attention to stiff links.
2. Shift through all gears. The chain should run cleanly and be neither too tight in the highest gear nor too loose in the lowest gear.
3. A new chain is usually greased at the factory. This factory grease is often a good lubricant. Just wipe off any excess grease on the outside.
4. Test drive: shift under load and check whether the chain jumps on a sprocket. If it jumps, the sprockets are probably worn out too.

## Chain jumps after changing? Then the cassette is due

If the new chain jumps under load on the frequently used sprockets, these sprockets are so worn that they no longer fit the new chain. Then the only thing that helps is a new cassette, and sometimes new chainrings. That's the price of changing the chain too late. Next time it's worth measuring more often.

## Strategy for frequent drivers: rotate the chains

Some frequent drivers use two or three chains alternately and swap them every few hundred kilometers. This means that wear is distributed more evenly and the cassette and chainrings last longer. This works well with chain locks and thorough cleaning of the chain that is not currently installed. For most everyday cyclists, regular measurements and timely changes are enough.

## Special features for hub gears and Pedelecs

With hub gears, the chain wears more slowly because the chain runs straight. In addition to the lengthening, the **chain tension** is important here: After changing, the rear wheel or the chain tensioner must be adjusted so that the chain sags slightly but cannot come off.

On Pedelecs with a mid-engine, high forces act on the chain. Many manufacturers offer particularly robust e-bike chains for this purpose. Change them in good time, because a worn drive can suddenly slip when the motor is fully supported.

## Conclusion

Measuring chain wear takes a minute and costs almost nothing with a simple gauge. If you change your chain in a timely manner when the elongation is around 0.5 to 0.75 percent, you protect the cassette and chainrings and save a lot of money over the years. You can easily install a new chain yourself using a chain riveter and chain lock. Make sure you have the right width, the right length and a properly closed lock, then the drive will run like new again.
