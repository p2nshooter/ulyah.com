Anyone who buys a bike for the first time or is looking for a new one after many years is faced with a confusing choice. There are bikes hanging in the shop that at first glance hardly look any different, but cost between 400 and 4,000 euros. Terms like gravel, urban, fitness bike, cross bike or SUV bike appear on the Internet. The good news: There are only a handful of basic ideas behind all of these names. Anyone who knows them can choose the right bike with a calm mind.

In this guide we will go through the most important types of bikes one by one. For each type you will find out what it is built for, who it is suitable for and what its limits are. At the end there is a simple decision-making aid with five questions that will lead you to the right category in just a few minutes.

## First the most important question: What do you need the bike for?

Before you delve into frame materials and groupset, it's worth taking an honest look at your everyday life. A bike is a tool, and the best tool is the one that fits the job. It's best to make a quick note:

- **Which routes do you drive most often?** Five kilometers to work, the round trip on the weekend or long tours with luggage?
- **On what surface?** Smooth asphalt, cobblestones, gravel paths or forest paths?
- **What do you need to transport?** A laptop, groceries, a child, camping gear?
- **Where is the bike?** In the basement, in the hallway, on the street or in the office?
- **How fit are you and how hilly is your area?**

These answers are more important than any test grade. A road bike is a wonderful machine, but it's a poor choice for the cobblestone walk to the bakery. A heavy Dutch bike is comfortable, but quickly becomes torture on a day tour through the low mountain ranges.

## City bike and Dutch bike: relaxed through the city

The city bike is the classic everyday bike. It has a low entry or a trapezoidal tube, an upright seating position, mudguards, a luggage rack, a chain guard and usually a permanently installed lighting system with a hub dynamo. A hub gear with three, seven or eight gears is often installed, which requires little maintenance and can also be shifted while stationary.

The Dutch bike is the particularly comfortable version: even more upright, with curved handlebars, often heavy and robust. You sit on it like a chair and keep a good eye on the traffic.

**Suitable for:** short to medium everyday trips in the city, shopping, trips in normal clothes, people who value comfort and low maintenance.

**Limits:** You notice the weight on long journeys and on inclines. The upright posture catches a lot of wind. The usually narrow, smooth tires are not intended for gravel and forest paths.

## Trekking bike: the all-rounder for everyday life and touring

The trekking bike is the secret bestseller in Germany, and for good reason. It combines the everyday features of a city bike with a slightly sportier seating position, more gears and tires that also work well on firm gravel paths. This almost always includes mudguards, luggage racks, lighting systems and stands. A derailleur gear with 18 to 30 gears is often installed, sometimes a high-quality hub gear.

**Suitable for:** Medium distance commuting, weekend rides on bike paths, multi-day bike trips with panniers, people looking for a single bike for almost everything.

**Limits:** It reaches its limits in real terrain. Because it is fully equipped, it usually weighs between 15 and 18 kilograms. If you only drive short distances in the city, you often don't need the many gears.

One type is the **cross bike** or fitness bike: a trekking bike without mudguards and luggage racks, lighter and sportier. Many manufacturers offer accessories for retrofitting so that you can put together the equipment as needed.

## Mountain bike: for forests, roots and mountains

Mountain bikes are built for unpaved trails and terrain. They have wide, coarse tires, powerful disc brakes, a suspension fork and, on full-suspension models (fullys), an additional rear shock. The gear ratio is designed for steep climbs.

There are many subtypes within the category: cross-country bikes for fast, long laps, trail and all-mountain bikes for varied trails, enduro and downhill bikes for steep descents. For most beginners, a **hardtail**, i.e. a bike with a suspension fork but no rear suspension, is a good and cheaper start.

**Suitable for:** Forest paths, field paths, mountains, sporty off-road excursions.

**Limits:** Mudguards, lights and luggage racks are usually missing in everyday life. Coarse tires roll loudly and sluggishly on asphalt. A suspension fork needs regular maintenance. If you only ride a mountain bike in the city, you are carrying a lot of technology with you that you don't use.

## Road bike: fast and light on asphalt

The road bike is optimized for speed and efficiency on slippery roads. It is light, has narrow tires, a racing handlebar with multiple grip positions and a stretched seating position. Modern racing bikes often have disc brakes and slightly wider tires than before, which has significantly improved comfort.

**Suitable for:** sporty rides on roads, longer distances at high speeds, training, people who enjoy speed.

**Limitations:** Little comfort on poor surfaces, hardly any space for luggage, the bent-over position is not for everyone. There is no equipment for everyday use, and you don't want to hook up an expensive, light racing bike in front of the supermarket.

## Gravel bike: fast on asphalt and gravel

The gravel bike is a comparatively young category and combines elements of racing bikes and mountain bikes. It has a racing handlebar that is slightly flared at the bottom, wider tires with a light tread, disc brakes and a more relaxed geometry than a racing bike. Many models have attachment points for mudguards, luggage racks and bags.

**Suitable for:** People who want to ride quickly but don't want to be limited to asphalt, for touring on gravel roads, for bikepacking and for sporty commuting.

**Limitations:** A mountain bike is superior in demanding terrain. The racing handlebar position is sportier than on a trekking bike. And if you never ride gravel, a racing bike or fitness bike is just as good.

## Folding bike: the bike for trains, buses and small apartments

Folding bikes can be folded down to a manageable size in just a few simple steps. They have small wheels, usually between 16 and 20 inches, and fit in the trunk, under the desk or on the train.

**Suitable for:** Commuters who combine train and bike, people with limited space, campers and boat owners.

**Limitations:** Small wheels roll less confidently over potholes and curbs. Good folding bikes are not cheap because of the complex mechanics. They are only partially designed for long tours, even if some people cover amazing distances with them.

## Cargo bike: the car for short journeys

Cargo bikes transport children, shopping or tools. They are available as a Long John with a loading area at the front, as a Long Tail with an extended luggage rack at the rear and as three-wheeled models with a box at the front. Many cargo bikes today have an electric motor because the loads quickly become heavy.

**Suitable for:** Families, craftsmen, people who want to replace car trips in the city.

**Limitations:** High price, requires a lot of space when parking, driving behavior takes some getting used to. We explain how to find the right model in our own guide to cargo bikes.

## E-bike and Pedelec: tailwind at the push of a button

Almost every type mentioned is now available with electrical support. In everyday life we ​​usually talk about an e-bike, but in most cases what we mean is a **Pedelec**: The motor only provides support when you pedal and only up to 25 km/h. Legally, such a Pedelec is a bicycle. You don't need a driver's license, no insurance number and there is no requirement to wear a helmet, even if a helmet makes sense.

There are also **S-Pedelecs** that support speeds of up to 45 km/h. They are considered mopeds and are subject to completely different rules. We explain the differences in detail in the guide to e-bikes and pedelecs.

**Suitable for:** longer commutes, hilly areas, people who want to avoid arriving sweaty, returnees, cargo cyclists.

**Limits:** Weight, price, battery care and risk of theft. A Pedelec can quickly weigh 22 to 28 kilograms and is harder to carry into the basement.

## The types of bicycles in direct comparison

| Type | Typical use | underground | Equipment | Weight (without motor) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| City bike / Dutch bike | City, shopping | Asphalt, pavement | full (light, mudguards, carrier) | 15-20kg |
| Trekking bike | Commuting, touring | Asphalt, solid gravel | full | 14-18kg |
| Fitness/cross bike | everyday sporting life | Asphalt, light gravel | reduced | 10-13kg |
| Mountain bike | terrain, mountains | Forest, trails | mostly none | 11-16kg |
| Road bike | Sport, street | smooth asphalt | none | 7-10kg |
| Gravel bike | Sport, touring, gravel | Asphalt and gravel | often retrofittable | 8-12kg |
| Folding bike | Train, little space | Asphalt | depending on the model | 10-14kg |
| Cargo bike | Transportation, family | Asphalt | loading area | 25-45kg |

The weights are rough guidelines. They depend heavily on the material, features and price range.

## Frame shape: diamond, trapezoid or low entry?

Regardless of the type, there are different frame shapes. The classic **diamond frame** with a horizontal or slightly sloping top tube is the stiffest and lightest. The **trapezoidal frame** has a lower top tube and makes climbing easier. With the **low-entry**, the top tube is practically completely missing, so you can climb through it comfortably.

People used to talk about men's and women's bikes, but this classification is outdated. A low-entry bike makes sense for anyone who often gets on and off the bike, transports children in a child seat or has limited mobility. A diamond frame is worthwhile if you ride sporty or transport heavy loads because it twists less under load.

## Five questions that will lead you to the right bike

1. **Do you mostly ride in the city and for less than ten kilometers at a time?** Then a city bike or a trekking bike with a hub gear is usually the best choice.
2. **Do you regularly commute more than ten kilometers or are you planning tours with luggage?** Then there is hardly any way around a trekking bike, preferably as a pedelec for hills and long distances.
3. **Do you want to do sports above all?** On asphalt it will be a racing bike, on mixed surfaces it will be a gravel bike, in the forest it will be a mountain bike.
4. **Do you often combine the bike with a train or car, or do you have little space?** Then you should look at folding bikes.
5. **Are you transporting children or heavy shopping?** Then it's worth taking a look at cargo bikes or a sturdy trekking bike with a trailer hitch.

## Typical mistakes when buying a bike

- **Buy based on appearance rather than use.** The stylish single speed looks great, but is a challenge on the first long climb.
- **Too much technology for too little use.** A suspension fork that you never use makes the bike heavier and more maintenance-intensive.
- **Underestimate the frame size.** An incorrectly sized bike is uncomfortable in the long run and can put strain on your knees and back. How to find the right size can be found in the frame size guide.
- **Buy without a test ride.** Even if all dimensions are correct, every bike feels different. Ten minutes of test driving say more than any table.
- **Forget accessories.** A good lock, light if not installed, and possibly bags can easily cost between 100 and 250 euros. Plan for them from the start.

## New or used?

A used bike can be a very good and sustainable choice, especially if you are looking for a solid trekking or city bike. However, you should know what to look out for: Wear parts, hidden damage and, unfortunately, stolen wheels are common on the used market. Our guide to buying used contains a detailed checklist.

If you buy from a specialist retailer, however, you will receive advice, an adjustment to your body measurements, a warranty and often a free initial inspection. Especially when it comes to Pedelecs, specialist retailers are often the safer choice because of the battery, motor and software.

## Conclusion: The best bike is the one you enjoy riding

There is no perfect bike for everyone, but there is a very good one for almost everyone. If you know your routes, your terrain and your transport needs, you can quickly narrow down your selection to one or two types of bike. Then frame size, test drive and budget decide.

And one last thought: A bike that is comfortable, rides reliably and can be locked well will be used more often. That's exactly what it's about. Take your time when choosing, test drive several bikes and don't be impressed by equipment lists that have nothing to do with your everyday life.
