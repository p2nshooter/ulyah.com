With Pedelecs, it is not only the size of the battery that determines the driving experience, but also the position of the motor. A motor in the bottom bracket feels different than one in the front hub or rear wheel. Each design has its strengths and weaknesses, and depending on the use, budget and driving style, one or the other is a better fit.

In this guide we explain the three common types of drive, how they work, how they drive, how much maintenance they require and who they are suitable for. It's also about torque, sensors and the question of how much engine power you really need in everyday life.

## Basics: power, torque and sensors

All Pedelec motors have a legally limited **rated continuous power of 250 watts** and support up to 25 km/h. They can deliver significantly more power for a short time, for example when starting off or on hills. Therefore, the wattage says little about the actual power.

The **torque** in Newton meters (Nm) is more meaningful. It describes how much power the motor puts on the crank or impeller. Typical values:

- **40 to 50 Nm:** gentle support, ideal for city and flat country, often light and quiet.
- **50 to 70 Nm:** powerful all-rounders for trekking, commuting and hilly areas.
- **70 to 90 Nm and more:** very powerful, for e-mountain bikes, cargo bikes and steep climbs.

The **sensor** that measures how you step is also important:

- A **torque sensor** measures your pedaling power and provides proportional support. This feels natural.
- A **cadence or motion sensor** only detects whether you are pedaling and provides fixed support depending on the level. This is cheaper, but sometimes feels a bit pushing.

## The mid-engine: the standard for most Pedelecs

The mid-engine is located in the bottom bracket area and drives the crank. Its power is transmitted to the rear wheel via a chain or belt and the gears.

### Advantages

- **Balanced weight distribution:** The weight sits low and in the middle. The bike rides stable and similar to a normal bike.
- **Natural driving experience:** Mid-engines almost always have a torque sensor. The support adapts finely to your pedaling power.
- **Uses the gears:** Because the engine works via gears, it can run in a favorable speed range. This is efficient, especially on the mountain.
- **Wheels remain normal:** The front and rear wheels are ordinary bicycle wheels. Changing tires and removing them is easy.
- **Large range:** Almost all well-known manufacturers rely on mid-engines, so the selection of bikes and workshops is correspondingly large.

### Disadvantages

- **Higher wear on the drive:** Chain, sprocket and chainring also have to transmit the engine power and wear out more quickly.
- **Shifting under load:** With derailleur gears, you should briefly reduce the pedaling force when shifting, otherwise the drive will suffer. Many systems have switch detection for this.
- **Price:** Mid-motor systems are usually more expensive than hub motors.
- **Engine noise:** Some mid-engines are clearly audible under load.

### Suitable for whom?

For almost everyone: trekking, commuting, touring, mountain bike, cargo bike. If you are looking for a Pedelec for versatile use, you can rarely go wrong with a mid-engine.

## The front engine: simple and cheap

With a front engine, the motor is located in the hub of the front wheel. In a sense, it pulls the bike forward.

### Advantages

- **Cheap:** Front motors are technically simple and are often installed in inexpensive Pedelecs.
- **Can be combined with hub gears and coaster brakes:** Because the drive is located at the front, the rear wheel remains free for any gear changes.
- **Low wear on the drive:** Chain and sprockets are not subjected to any additional load.
- **All-wheel feeling:** The front wheel pulls, the rear wheel is powered by your legs. For some people this feels safe on solid ground.

### Disadvantages

- **Traction on slippery surfaces:** The unloaded front wheel can spin on gravel, wet leaves, snow or steep inclines.
- **Steering Feel:** The additional weight in the front wheel hub changes the steering behavior, some find it cumbersome.
- **Often simple sensors:** Cheap front motors often work with motion sensors, the support feels less natural.
- **Load on the fork:** The fork must be designed for the engine forces.
- **Removing the front wheel:** If there is a breakdown at the front, the motor cable must be detached.

### Suitable for whom?

For short city routes and flat terrain, for people with a small budget and for everyone who absolutely wants a coaster brake or a specific hub gear.

## The rear engine: powerful and quiet

The rear motor is located in the rear wheel hub and drives the rear wheel directly. It is popular with sporty, urban bikes and with light Pedelecs where the motor should hardly be noticeable.

### Advantages

- **Good traction:** The driven rear wheel carries more weight than the front wheel, so it spins less often.
- **Quiet:** Many rear engines operate very quietly.
- **Protects the drive:** The chain only transmits your power, not that of the motor.
- **Inconspicuous appearance:** On light urban bikes, the engine is often barely visible.

### Disadvantages

- **Rear-heavy:** The weight is at the rear. This is noticeable when lifting, carrying and when luggage is on the luggage rack.
- **No use of gears:** The engine works at the wheel speed. On very steep mountains when driving slowly, it works less efficiently than a mid-engine.
- **Elaborate removal:** If the rear wheel gets a flat tire, the motor cable has to be loosened and the wheel is heavy.
- **Limited gear selection:** Hub gears usually cannot be combined because the motor is located in the rear wheel hub.

### Suitable for whom?

For commuters and city riders who are looking for a light, sporty and quiet Pedelec and who primarily travel on asphalt.

## The comparison at a glance

| feature | Mid-engine | Front engine | Rear engine |
|---|---|---|---|
| driving experience | very natural | pulling | pushing, sporty |
| Weight distribution | middle, deep | front | behind |
| Wet/gravel traction | good | weaker | good |
| Chain/sprocket wear | higher | low | low |
| Combination with hub gear | yes | yes | mostly no |
| Coaster brake possible | restricted | yes | rare |
| Changing tires | easy | Cable release at the front | Cable release at the back |
| Price | higher | low | medium |
| typical use | All-round, mountain, load | City, flat | City, commuting |

## How much engine do I need?

Many buyers overestimate how much engine power they need. For flat cities and occasional bridges, a light engine with 40 to 50 Newton meters is sufficient. Anyone who lives in low mountain ranges, regularly transports luggage or a child or simply likes to feel powerful support is well served with 60 to 85 Newton meters. Very powerful motors make sense for e-mountain bikes and heavy cargo bikes.

A more powerful motor doesn't automatically use more energy as long as you don't constantly use it at the highest setting. However, it is often heavier and more expensive.

## Further criteria when choosing the system

- **Service and spare parts:** Are there workshops near you that know and can diagnose the system?
- **Battery availability:** How long does the manufacturer offer replacement batteries?
- **Software and Updates:** Some systems can be adjusted and updated via the app.
- **Sound:** Try different engines when test driving, especially on hills.
- **Weight:** Important if you need to carry the bike or lift it onto a rack.
- **Freewheeling without motor:** How easy is the bike to ride without assistance over 25 km/h or with an empty battery? Some engines create noticeable resistance.

## Special case: transmission and automatic transmission

Some Pedelecs combine a mid-engine with a hub gear, a continuously variable transmission or a gear in the bottom bracket. Some systems even switch automatically, depending on speed and cadence. This is comfortable for people who don't want to think about aisles, but more expensive.

## Test drive: this is how you compare drives

1. **Starting from a standstill:** How gently or suddenly does the support apply?
2. **Middle gear gradient:** How powerful and natural does the engine feel?
3. **Above 25 km/h:** How gently is the support withdrawn? Does the bike feel heavy afterwards?
4. **Tight corners and walking pace:** How stable is the bike when driving slowly?
5. **Noise:** How loud is the engine under load?
6. **Raise:** Raise the wheel once, front and back.

## Engine care and maintenance

Pedelec motors are closed units that you should not open yourself. However, there are a few things you can do to ensure their longevity:

- **Keep clean:** Only wipe the motor housing with a damp cloth, do not use a water jet or high-pressure cleaner. Water in bearings and connectors is the most common cause of problems.
- **Check plug:** Occasionally check whether the plug connections between the motor, battery and display are tight and dry.
- **Update software:** During the inspection, the workshop often installs updates that improve driving experience, efficiency or fault diagnosis.
- **Take noises seriously:** New noises such as clicking or grinding indicate bearing or gear wear. Then get to the workshop early.
- **Shift carefully:** With a mid-engine and derailleur gears, briefly take the power out of the pedals when changing gears.

Many manufacturers recommend inspecting the engine system once a year or after a certain mileage. If there are any problems within the warranty, the dealer where the bike was purchased is the first point of contact.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can you retrofit a drive?** There are retrofit kits, usually with a front or rear engine, occasionally with a mid-engine. The conversion is demanding; the frame, fork and brakes must be suitable for the load, and the bike must then continue to meet the requirements of a Pedelec. For most people, a ready-made Pedelec is the safer and often cheaper solution.

**Can the motor be replaced if it is broken?** Generally yes, but usually only with a model from the same manufacturer that is compatible with the battery, display and frame. A new engine is expensive, so it's worth taking care of it.

**Is a mid-mounted motor louder than a hub motor?** Often yes, especially under load. But there are big differences between the models, which are best heard for yourself during a test drive.

## Conclusion

The mid-engine is the best choice for most people: natural driving feel, balanced weight and great versatility, at the expense of slightly more wear on the drive and a higher price. The front engine is cheap and simple, but less confident on slippery surfaces. The rear engine impresses with its traction and quietness, especially on light city bikes. What matters are your routes, your budget and how you feel during the test drive.
