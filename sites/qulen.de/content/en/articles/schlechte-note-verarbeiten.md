Mia saw the grade before Ms. Lefèvre had completely given her the work. A red five, circled at the top right. She quickly pushed the paper under her notepad so that her neighbor at the bench wouldn't see it. The rest of the hour flashed past her. She had learned. A whole week long, vocabulary and grammar every evening. And still a five. During break she went to the toilet and cried as quietly as she could.

## The evening after

Mia was fourteen and in the eighth grade of a high school. French was her second foreign language, and things had gone quite well until seventh grade. Things have gotten more difficult since this school year. New tenses, longer texts, more free writing. Mia's grades had slipped to a four, and for this paper she was determined to do better.

At first she didn't show the work to anyone at home. She put them in the desk and said at dinner that she hadn't gotten the work back yet. Afterwards she lay in bed and couldn't sleep. Thoughts swirled in her head: I'm just too stupid for French. Learning doesn't help anyway. I'll stay seated. My parents will be disappointed.

The next morning she felt exhausted. She didn't show up in French class, looked out the window and was no longer interested in anything that had to do with that subject.

## The truth at the kitchen table

At the weekend, her mother found work while looking for a charging cable in the room. Mia expected thunderstorms. Instead, her mother sat on the bed next to her and asked, “Why didn’t you tell me?”

Mia burst into tears. "Because I learned. And still. I'm just bad."

Her mother took her in her arms. When Mia had calmed down, she said, "A five is annoying, but it doesn't say anything about who you are. It just says that something didn't work in this job. Let's figure out what together."

They sat down at the kitchen table and laid out the work in front of them. For the first time, Mia really looked at her, not just at the grade. And she noticed something surprising: she got almost all the points on the vocabulary tasks. She also did quite well in the grammar exercises in which she was supposed to fill in the gaps. She lost most of the points in text comprehension and free writing.

## What the work really says

Mia, guided by her mother, made a small table. What tasks, how many points possible, how many achieved, what was the problem? The result was clear. Her learning had worked, but only for part of the work. She had studied vocabulary and grammar rules, but had never practiced reading a French text or writing anything herself.

“So you didn’t study badly,” said her mother. "You learned the wrong thing. That's a big difference."

On Monday, Mia went to see Ms. Lefèvre after class. It took a lot of effort for her. But Ms. Lefèvre took her time, looked through the work with her and confirmed what Mia had already suspected. "Your grammar is actually solid. But when you write, you translate sentence after sentence from German, and you get confused. And when you read, you get stuck on every unknown word instead of understanding the context."

She gave Mia simple French readings and suggested she write a short text every week that she would correct. "Start small. Five sentences about your weekend."

## Small steps back

In the weeks that followed, Mia changed the way she studied. In addition to vocabulary, she read ten minutes of simple reading every other day. She no longer marked every unknown word, but tried to guess the meaning from the context. At the weekend she wrote her five sentences and gave them to Mrs. Lefèvre.

The first texts came back with many corrections. But the corrections became fewer. And Mia found herself starting to think in simple French sentences instead of translating everything from German.

The next paper was a four plus. The one after that is a three. No brilliant notes, but a clear path to the top. Mia had stapled the five from the first work into her folder, right in front of the new work. “So I can see where I come from,” she said when her friend asked her why she kept the worst work.

## What you can take from it

A bad grade hurts, especially if you tried hard. Disappointment, anger, shame or sadness are normal reactions. What's important is what comes next: whether the grade paralyzes you or whether you use it as information. The following steps will help you deal with a bad grade and turn it into a fresh start.

### Allow feelings, but don't dwell in them

It's okay to feel sad or angry after a bad grade. Give yourself time to feel these feelings. Talk to someone about it, go for a walk or do some exercise. What doesn't help: hide your grade, insult yourself or immediately question everything.

Pay particular attention to thoughts that generalize:

| Generalizing thought | Realistic thought |
|---|---|
| “I’m too stupid for this subject.” | "This job went badly. I'm finding out why." |
| “Learning doesn’t help anyway.” | “My learning hasn’t yet matched this work.” |
| “I’ll stay put.” | “One grade doesn’t decide the whole year.” |
| “Everyone else is better.” | “I compare myself to my own progress.” |

### Evaluate the work honestly

Once the initial anger has passed, take a closer look at the work. Not the grade, but the details:

1. **Break down the tasks.** What tasks were there and how many points did you get for each?
2. **Identify patterns.** Where did you lose the most points? Is there a type of task that you find particularly difficult?
3. **Determine types of errors.** Were they careless errors, gaps in knowledge, problems with understanding or time problems?
4. **Compare with preparation.** Did you learn what was required in the job?

[Fynn's story about the error notebook](/lerngeschichten/mathe-ueben-mit-fehlerheft/) shows how you can systematically collect errors and learn from them.

### Talk to the teacher

A short conversation with the teacher is almost always worthwhile. She can tell you exactly where your difficulties lie and how you can improve. The conversation can look like this:

- **Come prepared.** Bring the work with you and mark the parts you don't understand.
- **Specific questions.** “What can I do to get better at freewriting?” is better than “Why do I have a five?”
- **Ask for materials.** Many teachers have practice materials or tips for specific weaknesses.

### Talk to the parents

Hiding a bad grade from your parents usually makes things worse. Sooner or later they find out, and then on top of that comes the anger about keeping it a secret. A few tips for the conversation:

- **Choose the right moment**, not between the door and the hinge.
- **Be honest** about the grade and how you feel about it.
- **Bring a plan**, even if it's small: "I'm going to talk to the teacher and practice writing from now on."
- **Ask for support** when you need it.

### For parents: How you can help

It is important for children and young people that a bad grade does not put a strain on their relationship. Stay calm, show understanding for the disappointment and help evaluate the work. Praise for effort and concrete progress usually has a stronger effect than punishment. And if bad grades pile up, a discussion with the teacher is often the best next step.

### Planning a restart

The evaluation creates a plan for the near future:

1. **Choose one or two areas of focus** that you want to work on specifically.
2. **Plan small, regular exercise sessions**, for example ten to twenty minutes on several days per week.
3. **Make progress visible**, for example through corrected exercises or small self-tests.
4. **Adapt your preparation for the next work**: Also practice the types of tasks that are difficult for you.

[Greta's story about the learning plan for class work](/lerngeschichten/lernplan-fuer-klassenarbeiten/) shows how you create a learning plan for the next work.

### When bad grades pile up

If several subjects are slipping or you feel constantly overwhelmed, sometimes there is more to it: too little sleep, problems at home or with friends, stress or depression. Then talk to a person you trust, the class teacher, the advisory teacher or school social work. Children and young people can also call the number against grief on 116 111 free of charge and anonymously. You can find further contact points on our [Help & Advice](/hilfe-und-beratung/) page.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Hide the grade.** This just postpones the problem and adds additional stress.
- **Give up immediately.** A bad grade is an interim result, not a final result.
- **Just look at the grade.** The valuable information is in the details of the work.
- **Learn more of the same.** If your method isn't right, more of the same won't help.
- **Comparing yourself to others.** Your path is your own.

## Briefly asked

### How long can I be angry about a bad grade?

Feelings take time. A day or two of anger or sadness is completely normal. But if after weeks you can't think of anything else or the grade is paralyzing you in all areas, talk to someone about it.

### Can I still make up for a bad grade?

In most cases, yes. Certificate grades are made up of several achievements, both written and oral. Ask your teacher how the grade is made up and where you can still collect points.

### What if I find the rating unfair?

Speak to the teacher in a friendly and objective manner and ask about the evaluation criteria. Sometimes a misunderstanding clears up. If you still have doubts, you can contact the class teacher or the student council.
