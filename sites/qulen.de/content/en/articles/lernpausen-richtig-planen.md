At a quarter past eight, Marie had secured her favorite spot in the reading room, second row, with a view of the trees in the inner courtyard. She set up her laptop, stacked three books next to it and got her exam notes ready. At half past four she packed up again. Eight and a half hours. On the screen was the beginning of her homework, two pages, and there were twenty-three tabs open in the browser. She had read the paragraph about educational aids in the textbook four times during the afternoon. She didn't keep it.

## Eight hours, two pages

Marie was twenty-two and was studying social work in her fourth semester at a university. The examination phase began in three weeks: an exam in the module on legal principles and a homework assignment that had to be completed by the end of the semester. Marie had decided to work early and hard this time. For her that meant sitting in the library for a long time.

She considered breaks to be wasted time. She only got up to go to the toilet and at lunch she ate a sandwich standing up in the foyer because eating was forbidden in the reading room. Then we immediately went back to the place. Towards midday she noticed that her eyes were shifting over the lines more and more often. But she persevered because she thought that just persevering would make the difference. In the afternoon she jumped back and forth between homework, exam material, emails and the learning platform and felt constantly busy.

In the evening she was exhausted and dissatisfied at the same time. She had worked all day and yet nothing felt accomplished.

## A conversation at the lockers

Ezra knew Marie by sight. She was studying in the same course, just three semesters further, and often sat two tables away. On a Wednesday, both of them were at the lockers at the same time. “You always come before me and go after me,” Ezra said. "How you do that?"

Marie laughed briefly. "To be honest, I don't do it very well. I sit for a long time, but in the end there is little." Ezra nodded. It was the same with her before. Then she started working in blocks: first using the well-known Pomodoro technique with 25 minutes of work and a 5-minute break, now usually 50 minutes of work and a 10-minute break, and after the third block a long lunch break. "And during breaks I actually go out. Not on my cell phone, but outside the door."

“Then I lose ten minutes every hour,” said Marie. Ezra shrugged. "Try it for a week. If you can't manage as much after that, you can switch back."

## The timer on your wrist

The next morning, before opening the laptop, Marie wrote a plan on a piece of paper. A single task for each block: Block one, structure chapter 2.1 of the homework, block two read and write out the appropriate passages in the textbook, block three answer exam questions on youth welfare law. She set the timer on her watch, which only vibrated.

The first breaks went wrong. Marie sat and opened her messages, and five minutes turned into fifteen. Afterwards, instead of feeling refreshed, she felt even more distracted. From the second day onwards, every break she went around the building or down the stairs to the water cooler, looked out the large window in the stairwell into the distance and then came back. At lunchtime she went to the cafeteria with Ezra for three quarters of an hour without talking about exams.

After a few days, Marie noticed something else. In the morning she was able to read difficult legal texts and specialist articles well. After lunch, her concentration dropped for about an hour. So she put reading in the morning and, after the cafeteria, did things that required less thinking: entering references, formatting quotations, writing emails to the lecturer. At four she stopped, wrote down on a piece of paper what she had accomplished and what she would start doing the next day, and left.

## Fewer hours, more accomplished

After two weeks, Marie was sitting in the library for about six hours a day instead of eight and a half. The raw text of Chapter 2 of her homework was finished, and for the exam she had a stack of question cards, most of which she was able to answer. There were also bad days when even 50 minutes seemed too long. Then she set the timer to 25 and started small. She had to remind herself for a while that breaks were part of working. But she saw it on her sides.

## What you can take from it

Concentration is not an achievement that can be achieved by sitting for long periods of time. It wears off over time and breaks bring it back. If you plan your breaks instead of taking them secretly from your feed, you can often get more done in fewer hours. This applies to studies as well as to school and training.

### Recognize signs of waning concentration

Your head often intervenes before you admit it to yourself. Pay attention to these signals:

| signal | What it tells you | What helps now |
|---|---|---|
| You read the same paragraph several times | The attention is gone, the eyes continue reading alone | Short pause, then formulate a question to the text |
| You open new tabs or apps for no reason | Your head is looking for variety | consciously end the block instead of continuing to click |
| You become restless or irritable | The tension increases | get up, take a few steps |
| Neck, back or eyes become tired | You've been sitting the same way for too long | Change posture, look into the distance |
| You sort, format and organize | You're avoiding the actual task | check what result this block should bring |

### Learning blocks with fixed breaks: the Pomodoro technique

The Pomodoro Technique is a well-known and simple method. Its name comes from the Italian word for tomato, after a tomato-shaped kitchen clock. This is how it works in its classic form:

1. **Set a task** and write it down.
2. **Set a timer for 25 minutes** and only work on that task.
3. **Take a 5-minute break after the bell rings,** even if you're in a good mood.
4. **After four blocks there is a longer break** of around 15 to 30 minutes.

The times are not a law. For long texts or writing a term paper, many people take longer to get into it and work with 45 or 50 minutes. Shorter blocks are easier for unpopular tasks or bad days. Try out what suits you and the task. The only important thing is that the end of the block is clear before you start.

If possible, don't use your cell phone as a timer, because if you unlock it to stop it, you'll quickly end up somewhere else. [Ben's story about cell phone distraction](/lerngeschichten/handy-ablenkung-beim-lernen/) shows you how to keep your cell phone out of studying entirely.

A library day with 50-minute blocks could look like this:

| time | What you do |
|---|---|
| 8:30 to 9:20 | Block 1: challenging task, for example developing a technical text |
| 9:20 to 9:30 | Break: get up, get water, look out the window |
| 9:30 to 10:20 | Block 2: the same task or the next difficult one |
| 10:20 to 10:30 | Break: walk around the building once |
| 10:30 to 11:20 | Block 3: Answer questions about the material from your head |
| 11:20 a.m. to 12:15 p.m | long lunch break away from the square |
| 12:15 to 1:05 p.m | Block 4: easier tasks such as literature references or emails |
| 1:05 p.m. to 1:20 p.m | Break in the fresh air |
| 1:20 p.m. to 2:10 p.m | Block 5: Review and plan for tomorrow |

### Which is a relaxing break

A good pause changes the channel. When you've read and looked at a screen, your head needs something else: movement, space, stillness.

- **Get up and leave your seat.** Just walking to the water cooler or into the stairwell has an effect.
- **Get some fresh air** if you have time.
- **Drink something** and refill your bottle.
- **Look into the distance**, for example out the window.
- **Stretch,** relax your shoulders and neck.
- **Talk to someone for a moment**, preferably about something other than exams.

What is not a break: the feed on social networks, news or videos. They continue to demand your attention, and because they don't have a natural ending, the break quickly becomes too long. Also, plan the break before you are exhausted. If you only get up when you can't do anything anymore, it will take longer to get back into it.

### Plan longer breaks and the end of the day

In addition to the short breaks, a learning day requires a long break, usually at lunchtime. Really take your time away from your desk and eat in peace instead of casually. On long days, a second, slightly longer break in the afternoon helps.

A fixed end to the day is just as important. Decide in advance when you will stop and end the day with a small ritual:

1. **Note what you have accomplished.** This shows you progress, even on tough days.
2. **Write down the first step for tomorrow.** Then the next morning you don't have to think about what to start with.
3. **Clean up your space** and leave. The way out of the library is your end of the day.

Plan free time during the week even during the exam phase. [Paula's story about the exam phase of your studies](/lerngeschichten/klausurenphase-im-studium/) shows how you can distribute several exams across your calendar in just a few weeks.

### One thing at a time

If you jump back and forth between tasks, you have to think about it anew each time. It takes energy and still feels like a lot of work. Therefore: one block, one task.

- **Before each block, specify what the result should be,** for example “Section 2.1 structured”.
- **Keep a parking space note.** Whatever comes to your mind from time to time, such as an email or an appointment, you write it down there and do it later.
- **Bundle small things.** E-mails, learning platforms and organizational matters get their own block, ideally at your lowest point of the day.

The location also influences how well you stick to something. You can find tips on this in [Aylin's story about the right place to study](/lerngeschichten/lernplatz-einrichten/).

### A daily rhythm that suits your daily form

Not everyone is most awake in the morning. For a week, observe how well you can concentrate in each block and give yourself a grade from 1 to 5. A pattern will quickly emerge. Place demanding tasks such as new texts or writing in your strong times and routine tasks such as formatting, copying references or sorting in your weaker times. Fixed appointments such as lectures, seminars or a part-time job set the framework within which you plan.

If you constantly feel exhausted despite taking breaks for a long time, talk to your family doctor. Many universities and student unions also offer advice on learning problems and exam stress. More about this under [Help & Advice](/hilfe-und-beratung/).

## Typical pitfalls

- **Confuse sitting time with study time.** Eight hours at your desk is not eight hours of studying.
- **Spend breaks on your cell phone.** Afterwards you are often more restless than before.
- **Skip breaks because it's happening.** A short stop keeps concentration at a good level for longer.
- **Packing too many tasks in one block.** One block, one result.
- **Don't plan an end.** Every day becomes too long without work.

## Briefly asked

### What if I'm just doing well when the timer goes off?

Then write down in one sentence where you are and what comes next, and still take a break. With this sentence you will find your way back more quickly. If you are writing for a long time, you can exceptionally extend the block by a few minutes.

### How many hours a day can I study meaningfully?

This is very different and depends on you, the task and your everyday life. Pay less attention to the hours and more to the results of your blocks and the signals from your head.

### Does this also work at home?

Yes. The blocks, the breaks and the solid finish are even more important when no one is working around you. You can find more stories about sticking with it in the chapter [Concentration & Motivation](/themen/konzentration-und-motivation/).
