Valentina stared at her list. Twelve components of the cell, each with its function. Mitochondria, ribosomes, Golgi apparatus, endoplasmic reticulum. She had already read the list five times today. She closed her eyes and tried to recite them. Cell nucleus, mitochondria... and then it was over. The rest was gone, as if someone had erased it from her mind. The bio test was tomorrow. Valentina pushed the paper away and sighed so loudly that her grandmother asked from the kitchen if everything was okay.

## A head like a sieve

Valentina was fourteen and in the eighth grade of a high school. She understood most of the topics in class well. Her problem was retention. Years in history, technical terms in biology, the order of the planets in geography: what she had learned in the evening was often only half there the next morning.

She learned as most do. She read the lists over and over again, wrote them down, marked important things with a highlighter. This felt diligent. But little stuck.

Her grandmother, Nonna Rosa, sat down with her at the kitchen table that evening. She had previously been a primary school teacher in Italy and had been living with her family in Germany for several years. She looked at the list and smiled. "You know how I used to memorize the rivers of Italy? Not through reading at all. Through stories."

## Images in your head

Nonna Rosa explained to her that memory retains images, stories and places much more easily than loose words. An abstract word like “mitochondrion” has no hook for the mind to hang on to. A vivid picture, on the other hand.

“What does a mitochondrion do?” she asked.

“It’s the powerhouse of the cell,” Valentina said. “It produces energy.”

"Good. Then imagine a tiny power plant with a smoking chimney. And in front of it is a man called 'Mito', sweating, shoveling coal."

Valentina had to laugh. But she noticed that the image remained. Together they invented an image for each component. The ribosomes became little chefs that cooked protein spaghetti from building instructions. The Golgi apparatus became a post office that packed and shipped packages. The cell nucleus was the library in which all the blueprints were located.

## A palace in your own home

Then Nonna Rosa showed her a technique that Valentina first thought was a fairy tale. “The ancient Greeks and Romans used this,” she said. "It's called the memory palace. Or the method of loci."

The idea: You imagine a place that you know very well, for example your own apartment. Then you mentally define a fixed path through this place, with clear stations. Front door, wardrobe, kitchen, refrigerator, dining table, living room and so on. At each station you place a picture for a term. Later you walk the path in your head and collect the pictures again.

Valentina tried the twelve cell components. The cell membrane stood at the front door as a bouncer, deciding who was allowed in. The cell wall hung on the coat rack like a stiff coat, only for plants. The ribosome chefs were cooking in the kitchen. The vacuole stored water and supplies in the refrigerator. Mito's little power plant stood on the dining table. And so on, throughout the entire apartment.

Then she closed her eyes and walked down the path. The images appeared station by station. She was able to list all twelve components, in the correct order. She could even count them backwards by walking the path backwards. Valentina was so taken aback that she tried again.

## More than just tricks

The next day the bio-test went well. But Nonna Rosa had given her something else: "Memorization techniques help you recall things. You still have to understand them." Valentina realized what she meant. One question asked her to explain why muscle cells have a particularly large number of mitochondria. The picture of the power plant helped her find the organelle. She had to explain herself that muscles need a lot of energy.

In the following months, Valentina built up a small collection of memory aids. For history, she used rhymes and pictures for years. For geography, she learned the mnemonic for the planets: “My father explains our night sky to me every Sunday.” For English, she made up little scenes with vocabulary. And for longer lists, she created additional memory palaces: the way to school, her aunt's house, the supermarket around the corner.

But she also noticed that not everything was working. For math formulas, it helped more to derive the formula yourself. And she learned vocabulary best with flashcards and regular repetition, with the pictures serving as additional support. Memorization techniques were not a magic wand. But they were a very good tool in her learning box.

## What you can take from it

Our memory is not good at retaining loose lists of abstract concepts. But it is excellent at remembering images, stories, places and feelings. Memorization techniques use exactly this strength. They associate new information with something that the mind easily remembers.

### Mnemonics: little helpers for solid facts

Mnemonics are mnemonics, rhymes or abbreviations that help you recall an order or a rule. Some have been known for generations:

| Mnemonic bridge | What you remember |
|---|---|
| My father explains our night sky to me every Sunday | Planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune |
| Never wash without soap | Directions clockwise: North, East, South, West |
| 333, at Issos Brawl | Battle of Issus in 333 BC |
| Anyone who writes with an h is stupid | “namely” is written without an h |

Mnemonics that you invent yourself work best. The more personal, funny or unusual they are, the better they stick. Here's how you can build your own:

1. **Note the first letters.** Write the first letters of the terms in the correct order.
2. **Make a sentence.** Find a word for each letter to create a sentence that you can imagine.
3. **Say out loud.** Repeat the sentence out loud a few times. Rhythm and sound help with memory.
4. **Test.** The next day try to derive the terms from the sentence.

### Pictures and stories

Images are suitable for terms without a fixed order. The rules for this are simple:

- **Exaggerate.** A huge, loud, or absurd image sticks better than a normal one.
- **Incorporate movement.** Something that moves is easier to remember than a still image.
- **Use your senses.** What does it smell like, what does it sound like, how does it feel?
- **Connect with meaning.** The image should fit the function, like the power plant fits the mitochondrion.

You can link several pictures to form a short story. Each image leads to the next. This way you can also remember longer sequences, such as the steps of a biological process or the causes of a historical event.

### The memory palace: step by step

The memory palace, also known as the loci method, is one of the oldest known memory techniques. It is particularly suitable for lists and sequences.

1. **Choose a familiar place.** Your apartment, your way to school or your classroom. You have to be able to imagine him in detail.
2. **Set a fixed route.** Designate ten to twenty stations that you always go through in the same order.
3. **Place pictures.** At each station you place a living picture for a term.
4. **Walk the path.** Close your eyes and walk the path in your head. Collect the pictures as you go.
5. **Repeat.** Walk the path a few times over the following days until it sits.

A location can be used multiple times. If you need it for a new topic, simply add new images. However, it is worth reserving separate locations for important topics so that the images do not mix together.

### Abbreviations and acronyms

Sometimes a single made-up word is enough. The first letters of a list create a word that you can easily remember. This works particularly well if the word is pronounceable or reminds you of something familiar.

### Where memory techniques reach their limits

Memorization techniques are powerful, but not designed for everything:

- **They are not a substitute for understanding.** You can remember that mitochondria provide energy, but you still need to be able to explain why this is important.
- **They initially take time.** Coming up with good images takes time. It's especially worth it for knowledge that you need for a long time.
- **They need repetition.** Even a memory palace fades if you never visit it again. [Hannah's story about spaced repetition](/lerngeschichten/verteiltes-wiederholen-statt-pauken/) shows you how to distribute repetitions wisely over time.
- **They do not fit every material.** For math and science, derivations and practice are often more helpful. Mind maps are suitable for connections, as [Lukas' story about mind maps and concept maps](/lerngeschichten/mindmap-erstellen/) shows.

### Combine memory techniques with other methods

Memory techniques work best when combined with other learning methods. For vocabulary, you can draw a small picture on the back of an index card, like in [Frida's story about the index box](/lerngeschichten/vokabeln-lernen-mit-karteikasten/). For study notes, you can write mnemonics in the margin, like in [Nele's story about study notes](/lerngeschichten/lernzettel-schreiben/). This is how individual tricks become a solid learning system.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Overly complicated mnemonics.** If the motto is longer than the list itself, it won't help.
- **Boring images.** A normal image fades quickly. Feel free to exaggerate.
- **Just learn by heart.** If you can recall the terms but cannot explain them, you will still lose points in exams.
- **Overcrowding the palace.** Too many images at one station mix together. Better to use more stations.
- **Do not repeat.** Even the best technique needs a few repetitions so that the knowledge is retained in the long term.

## Briefly asked

### Do memory techniques work for everyone?

Most people can benefit from images and places with a little practice. Some people find it easier to introduce themselves than others. Try different techniques and keep the ones that suit you.

### How much time should I invest in mnemonics?

For facts that take you a long time or that are particularly difficult for you, the effort is worth it. You don't need a mnemonic for things that you already remember easily.

### Can I write down memory aids in the exam?

You can of course use whatever you have in your head in the exam, and many people write down their mnemonics on a scratch paper at the beginning. Notes that you bring with you are only allowed if your teacher expressly allows this.
