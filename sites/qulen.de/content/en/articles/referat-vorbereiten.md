The paper had been on Paul's desk for half an hour. At the top, in his most beautiful writing, it said: “History Section: Castles in the Middle Ages.” There was nothing underneath. Paul had screwed the pen on and off three times, straightened the ruler and cut the eraser in half. He was supposed to speak for ten minutes, before the whole 6c, in three weeks. He didn't even know what to start the first sentence with.

## A topic as big as a castle

Paul was eleven and in sixth grade. When Mr. Hoffmann distributed the presentation topics in history, Paul answered immediately, as soon as the word “castles” was mentioned. In the summer he and his class visited a castle ruin and were thrilled.

That was exactly the problem now. He thought of so many things about castles that he didn't know where to start: sieges, jousting tournaments, catapults, castle ghosts. On the first afternoon he found videos about knights' armor on the Internet. On the second, he copied three pages from a non-fiction book, word for word. When he read the first page to his friend Merle during the break, she looked at him with pity. “That sounds like a book,” she said. Unfortunately, she was right.

## A question instead of a topic

During his lunch break, Paul sat in the school library with four books about knights and castles and the copied pages in front of him. Ms. Demir, who looked after the library, remained at his table.

“How long can you talk?” she asked.

“Ten minutes.”

“And what do you want to find out in ten minutes?”

Paul didn't understand. He wanted to talk about castles. “We could talk about castles for an entire school year,” Ms. Demir said. "You have ten minutes. So you need a question that fits in ten minutes. What have you always wanted to know?"

Paul thought of the tiny windows and the cold walls of the ruins. “Was it really as comfortable in a castle as it is in the movies?”

“This is a key question,” Ms. Demir said. She didn't cross out sieges and tournaments, but wrote them on a second piece of paper with the heading "Exciting, but not today." Then she showed him how to check a book before reading it: table of contents, index, the chapter on living and everyday life. Two of the four books remained, as well as the double page in the history book and an entry in a youth encyclopedia. For each source, Paul noted the title, author, and pages.

## Fifteen pieces of paper on the carpet

At home, Paul no longer copied anything. He read a paragraph, closed the book and wrote a single piece of information from his head on a small piece of paper, in his own words, with an abbreviation for the source in the corner. “Castle = residential building and fortress at the same time.” “Living and celebrating in the Palas, where there is often a fireplace.” “Keep = highest tower, last refuge.” “Small windows, often only with wooden shutters: dark and cold in winter.” “Loo = toilet bay window, a small porch on the outside of the wall.”

At the end there were fifteen pieces of paper on the carpet. Paul pushed them back and forth until three piles emerged: the castle as a building, the people and their everyday lives, plus cold, darkness and water. The note about the catapult didn't fit anywhere. With a heavy heart, Paul assigned it to “Exciting, but not today”. Suddenly the three piles formed an order, and the order became a structure.

To start with, he chose a question for the class: “Which one of you would like to live in a castle?” At the end he wanted to ask her again.

## The stopwatch doesn't lie

Four days before the appointment, Paul gave his talk out loud for the first time, alone in his room, with a stopwatch. After seventeen minutes he hadn't finished yet because he had told a little story about each piece of paper. In pencil he crossed out everything that didn't directly relate to the main question and summarized two sections. It took eleven minutes for the second run and ten and a half for the third.

On the day of the presentation, almost the entire class responded to his opening question. Ten minutes later, after freezing winters, sooty chimneys and the toilet bay window, only three hands went up and everyone laughed. Paul sight-read more often than he wanted. But at the top of his handout was his guiding question, and below that was an answer that he had worked out for himself.

## What you can take from it

In the beginning, Paul didn't have too little, but too much. He got further through a clear question and a path that puts a lot of information into a sequence. The following steps will show you how to prepare the content of a presentation, from the blank page to the handout. [Dominik's story about speaking freely](/lerngeschichten/frei-sprechen-beim-referat/) tells how you then recite it freely instead of reading it off.

### Step 1: Narrow down the topic and find a key question

A presentation topic is often just a generic term. Your task is to find a piece that fits your speaking time.

1. **Clarify the guidelines.** How long should you speak? Do you need a handout or slides? It's better to ask too much.
2. **Collect what interests you.** Write down everything that comes to mind on the topic for two minutes without sorting.
3. **Choose a section.** Circle the point that makes you most curious.
4. **Formulate a question.** A key question usually begins with “How”, “Why” or “What”. It gives your presentation direction.
5. **Discuss it.** Show your key question to the teacher before you continue working.

| Too wide | Limited, with a key question |
|---|---|
| Castles in the Middle Ages | How did people live in a castle and was it comfortable there? |
| Ancient Egypt | Why was the Nile so important to the people of ancient Egypt? |
| The Romans | What was everyday life like for a child in ancient Rome? |
| whales | How do whales manage to dive for so long even though they breathe air? |

Whatever you leave out when narrowing down goes on an extra piece of paper. You will need some of this later when the class asks questions.

### Step 2: Find and note reliable sources

A few sources are usually enough for a school report if they are good:

- **Your textbook.** It is tailored to your grade level and is a good starting point.
- **Non-fiction books from the school or city library.** Ask at the counter for books for your age group.
- **Encyclopedias for children and young people.** They explain terms briefly and understandably, even if a non-fiction book is too difficult.
- **Internet sites with caution.** Pay attention to who is behind a site, whether it remains factual and whether the information corresponds to your book. If two sources contradict each other, ask your teacher.

Check every book before you read it: the table of contents and indexes show you in a minute whether it contains anything related to your main question. Immediately note where information comes from: for books, the title, author and page number, for websites, the address and date of access.

### Step 3: Sort and structure information

Read a passage, close the book, and write down a piece of information in your own words on a separate piece of paper. This way you can see whether you have understood them and your presentation sounds like you.

Then sort the pieces of paper into piles. Each pile becomes a section of your main body, and for ten minutes two or three are usually enough. Anything that doesn't fit the main question goes on a separate piece of paper.

| part | What belongs in it | Paul's example |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Introduction, topic, key question, brief overview | Question to the class, key question, “I’ll tell you three things…” |
| Main part 1 | Basics that everyone needs | What a castle is, the most important buildings |
| Main part 2 | The core of your question | Who lived in the castle and how a day went |
| Main part 3 | Deepening or surprise | Cold, darkness, water and the toilet bay window |
| End | Answer to the key question, reference back to the introduction | Same question to the class again |

Also determine how you will get from one section to the next. A simple sentence is enough: "Now you know how a castle was structured. But who actually lived in it?"

### Step 4: Formulate the beginning and end specifically

The beginning and end are the most memorable, so prepare them sentence by sentence. Good places to start include:

- a question for the class to which everyone has an answer,
- a surprising detail from your sources,
- an object or picture that you show,
- a short scene: "Imagine it's January and you wake up in a stone tower..."

After getting started, name the topic and key question and say in one sentence what is coming. The conclusion answers the main question in two or three sentences. He appears round when he returns to the entrance, just like Paul's second question to the class. Avoid using “That’s it” as the last sentence. It's better: "That's the end of me. Do you have any questions?"

### Step 5: A schedule leading up to the appointment

A good presentation is not created in one evening. Plan backwards from the date. The example assumes three weeks:

| When | What you do | Done when... |
|---|---|---|
| Week 1, first days | Clarify specifications, narrow down the topic, agree on the key question | the key question is written on a piece of paper |
| Week 1, rest | Search and check sources | two to four good sources are noted |
| Week 2 | Read and write information sheets in your own words | every important piece of information is on a piece of paper |
| Beginning of week 3 | Sort, structure, write an introduction and conclusion | the structure fits on one page |
| Mid week 3 | Write a handout, create keyword cards, use slides only if requested | everything is printed out or saved |
| 4 to 2 days before | At least two test runs with a stopwatch | you stay within the given time |
| The day before | One last pass, packing up materials | the bag is packed |

During the test run, you speak loudly, standing up and with a stopwatch. Going through things quietly in your head is deceptive because you skip over gaps. If it's too long, cross out entire pieces of paper instead of speaking faster. If slides are required, [Kemal's story about presentation slides](/lerngeschichten/praesentationsfolien-gestalten/) shows how they accompany your presentation rather than replacing it.

### Step 6: A simple handout or thesis statement

A handout helps your audience think for themselves and later learn. In higher classes it is often called a thesis paper and contains pointed statements for discussion. One page is enough to get started:

- **Header:** your name, subject, class, date and topic
- **Key question:** clearly visible at the top
- **Outline:** the headings of your sections
- **The most important thing:** three to six short bullet points and the most important technical terms with explanations
- **Sources:** at the end, as you wrote them down

Don't write your entire presentation on the handout, otherwise the class will read instead of listening. Ask in advance if the teacher would like to see it in advance and how many copies you need.

## Typical pitfalls

- **The topic remains huge.** Without a key question, a presentation becomes a list. If you want to tell everything, you end up telling nothing correctly.
- **Copying instead of understanding.** Copied sentences sound foreign and are difficult to recite.
- **Look for sources at the end.** Write down each source as you use it.
- **No practice run.** Many first presentations are too long the first time they are read out loud. You notice this better at home than in front of the class.

## Briefly asked

### How many sources do I need for a school report?

Two to four sources that you have understood are worth more than ten that you have skimmed over. If your teacher gives a minimum number, stick to it.

### Can I use sentences from the internet?

Not just like that. You mark foreign sentences as quotes and cite the source. Your school decides whether and how you are allowed to use AI tools, so ask your teacher directly.

### What if I'm terribly nervous before the presentation?

You are not alone, and thorough preparation takes away a lot of uncertainty.
