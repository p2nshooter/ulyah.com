Ida read the first paragraph for the third time. “The rate of photosynthesis is influenced by limiting factors such as light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature, with the minimum factor determining the rate.” She understood each word individually. But by the end of the sentence she had forgotten the beginning. And by the end of the paragraph she no longer knew what the first sentence had said. There were six more pages in front of her. Tomorrow she should summarize the text in biology class.

## Reading without understanding

Ida was fifteen and in the tenth grade of a high school. She enjoyed reading, novels, fantasy, and sometimes comics. But technical texts were something completely different for them. The sentences were long, full of technical terms, and there was no plot to pull her along. She read page after page, and by the end she felt like she had walked through a fog.

Her method was simple: She read the text cover to cover, highlighted anything that sounded important with yellow highlighter, and then read it again. In the end the pages were almost completely yellow. And she still didn't know exactly what the main message was.

In biology class the next day she was supposed to summarize the text. She read a few sentences from her markers, out of context. Mr. Brandt asked: “And what is the core message now?” Ida didn't know.

## A five-step method

After the lesson, Mr. Brandt gave her a piece of paper with a reading method that he usually practiced with the upper class. “Reading is not the same as reading,” he said. "A novel pulls you along. You have to conquer a specialist text. You need a plan for that."

There were five steps on the piece of paper: get an overview, ask questions, read carefully, summarize what is important, repeat. The method is based on approaches that have been described in learning research for decades and is available in different versions. Mr. Brandt had simplified it for his students.

Ida tried out the text on photosynthesis that same afternoon.

## First skim, then ask

At first she didn't read the text, but skimmed it. She looked at the headings, the figures, the bold words, and the summary at the end of the chapter. After three minutes she knew: the question was what the speed of photosynthesis depends on. There were three factors and there was a chart showing how the rate changed.

Then she wrote down questions that the text should answer. “What factors influence photosynthesis?” “What does ‘limiting factor’ mean?” “What does the diagram show?” “Why doesn’t the rate increase indefinitely?”

With these questions in mind, she read the text paragraph by paragraph. And suddenly it was different. She no longer just read words, she looked for answers. When she found one, it was a small sense of achievement.

## Mark less, understand more

When reading carefully, she marked only sparingly, at most one or two places per paragraph, and only after the first reading of a paragraph, not during. She wrote down technical terms that she didn't know in the margins and clarified them in the book's glossary. “Limiting factor” meant that the factor that is most abundant limits the rate, even if all others are abundant.

After each paragraph, she paused briefly and wrote in one sentence in the margin what it was about. "Light is a factor. More light, more photosynthesis, but only up to a limit." This resulted in a summary of the entire text in my own words in the margins.

She now looked closely at the diagram that she had skipped over before. She described it to herself in words: The x-axis is the light intensity, the y-axis is the rate of photosynthesis. The curve first rises and then flattens. Why? Because then another factor, for example CO₂, becomes the limiting factor. Suddenly the sentence at the beginning made sense.

## The summary in the next hour

In the end, Ida wrote a half-page summary without looking at the text. She then compared with the text and added what was missing.

In the next biology lesson, Mr. Brandt again asked them to summarize a text, this time about cellular respiration. Ida had worked on him using the same method. She spoke calmly and clearly about the main message, the three phases and an example. Mr. Brandt nodded happily. "Just as."

Ida soon also used the method in history, geography and politics. It initially took more time than simply reading through, but she was less likely to have to read texts multiple times and retained more.

## What you can take from it

Technical texts are dense, full of technical terms and often without a narrative thread. They require a different way of reading than novels or news. With a fixed method, reading through becomes an active study and understanding becomes much easier.

### The Five Step Reading Method

1. **Get an overview (a few minutes):** View headings, subheadings, figures, bold terms, introduction and summary. What is it roughly about?
2. **Ask questions:** Write two to five questions that the text should answer. You can often turn headings into questions.
3. **Read Thoroughly:** Read paragraph by paragraph. Mark sparingly, clarify technical terms and look for answers to your questions.
4. **Summarize:** Write a sentence in your own words in the margin after each paragraph. At the end you summarize the entire text.
5. **Repeat:** Answer your questions from memory. You read up on what you don't know.

### Mark correctly

Many learners highlight too much and then end up with nothing highlighted. These rules help:

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| Read first, then mark | You only realize what is really important after reading it |
| One or two places per paragraph at most | only the essentials are noticed |
| Keywords instead of whole sentences | Markings remain clear |
| Two colors with fixed meaning | for example one for definitions, one for examples |
| Add marginal notes | a word in the margin often says more than a mark |

If you are not allowed to mark in your book, use sticky notes or write the notes in your notebook.

### Clarify technical terms

Unknown technical terms are often the reason why a text remains incomprehensible. Look them up in a glossary, an encyclopedia or in teaching materials. Create a list or index cards with technical terms and their explanations. [Frida's story about the index box](/lerngeschichten/vokabeln-lernen-mit-karteikasten/) shows you how to keep them permanently.

### Read figures, diagrams and tables

Graphics often contain the most important information in a text. This is how you access them:

- **Read titles and captions:** What is it about? What's on the axles?
- **Describe in words:** What does the graphic show? Is something rising, falling or staying the same?
- **Connect to the text:** Which statement in the text is supported by the graphic?
- **Explain:** Why is the curve like this?

### Reading slowly is not a mistake

You can read technical texts slowly. Pauses after each paragraph, going back and looking up are part of it. Anyone who reads a specialist text at the same speed as a novel usually understands less. Therefore, plan enough time and do not read difficult texts under time pressure.

### From reading to learning

What you have read is best consolidated if you process it further:

- **Write summaries**, as in [Nele's story about study notes](/lerngeschichten/lernzettel-schreiben/).
- **Represent connections**, for example in a concept map, like in [Lukas' story about mind maps](/lerngeschichten/mindmap-erstellen/).
- **Explain to someone** what you read.
- **Answer questions from memory**, even a few days later.

### Technical texts in studies and training

The method works just as well for scientific essays during your studies or for specialist texts at vocational school. When studying, it is particularly worthwhile to first read the introduction and conclusion of an essay and then decide which parts you need to read exactly. How this helps with homework is shown in [Jana's story about her first homework](/lerngeschichten/hausarbeit-schreiben-im-studium/).

### Reading texts in exams and exams

In exams you have less time, but the method can be shortened. Read the assignment first so you know what you are looking for in the text. Then skim the text to get an overview. When reading carefully, you specifically mark the passages that are important for the task and write down keywords in the margins. This saves you time when writing because you can quickly find your receipts. Pay particular attention to the operators in the task: whether you should “summarize,” “analyze,” or “evaluate” a text changes what you pay attention to when reading.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Started reading without an overview.** Then there is no framework into which you can classify the details.
- **Everything marked.** When everything is important, nothing is important anymore.
- **Overlook technical terms.** A misunderstood term can make an entire paragraph incomprehensible.
- **Skip graphics.** They often contain the core message.
- **Read only, don't process.** Little sticks without summarizing or querying.

## Briefly asked

### How long should it take me to write a technical text?

That depends on the length and difficulty. A textbook text of just a few pages can take thirty to sixty minutes if you work through it thoroughly. This isn't a waste of time because you'll have to read it again less often afterwards.

### What if I don't understand a paragraph even after reading it several times?

Read the paragraph before and after again, clarify any technical terms and try to explain the content out loud. If it still doesn't work, write down a specific question and ask it to your teacher or in your study group.

### Does it help to read texts out loud?

When reading difficult passages, reading aloud can help you read more slowly and more accurately. It's usually too time-consuming for entire texts, but a good trick for individual paragraphs.
