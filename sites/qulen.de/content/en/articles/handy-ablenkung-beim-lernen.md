Ben had sat at his desk from half past four to half past seven. Three hours of biology, nervous system topic, test the next morning. When he closed the book and wondered what exactly a synapse does, the first thing that came to mind was a video of someone throwing a basketball backwards over their shoulder into a basket. Then the discussion in the class chat about who will bring the grill on Friday. The synapse came third, and only roughly.

## Three hours at the desk, hardly anything on my mind

Ben was fifteen and in the ninth grade of a community school. He wasn't lazy, or so he told himself. He was sitting there. Yes, he read. Except the cell phone was next to the book and it kept vibrating. The 9b chat had over two hundred new messages that afternoon. Every time the display lit up, he “just took a quick look.” And sometimes he looked even when it hadn't lit up at all.

The next day's test went poorly. He confused terms that he had read three times the night before. When he got it back, next to a task it said in red letters: “We had that in detail!” Ben was annoyed, especially with himself. He had learned. Or?

## The tally sheet

The following week the class had a lesson on the topic of “learning to learn” with Ms. Jahnke, their class teacher. She didn't give a lecture about cell phones, but instead handed out blank pieces of paper. “An experiment,” she said. “Next time you study, make a line every time you pick up your phone and write why.”

Ben joined in, more out of curiosity. After an hour of English, the paper was full of lines. Next to it were reasons: “Chat vibrates.” “Check the time.” "Boring." “Task difficult.” As he read through it, he noticed something that honestly surprised him. Quite often he didn't pick up his cell phone because it had answered, but rather when a task became difficult. The cell phone was his emergency exit.

## The cell phone goes into the hallway

His first attempt was to place the phone face down on the table. It hardly helped. He reached for it anyway, just for no reason. So the next time he put it in his backpack and the backpack in the hallway after turning on Do Not Disturb mode. Calls from home continued to come through, everything else waited. The first twenty minutes felt like something was missing. Twice he thought he heard it vibrate. Then he forgot about it.

The chat remained. Ben wrote in: "I'll be in study mode until six, then I'll be back." A few jokes came back, a thumbs up, and the next day Can wrote something similar. For questions about homework, a few people in the class started their own little chat, and Ben muted the big one. For the vocabulary app he needed for Spanish, he rearranged the start screen: learning apps to the front, everything else to a folder on the last page.

He had to reinvent the reward twice. First, he allowed himself five minutes on his cell phone after each study block. Five minutes turned into twenty because one video ran into the next. After that, the rule was: use your cell phone only after two blocks, with a kitchen timer, and only in the hallway, while standing. It sounded strict, but it was surprisingly pleasant. After the last block, the evening was his, without a guilty conscience.

## An hour at a time

It didn't go straight. The week before the school trip, the chat exploded and Ben found himself doing math with his phone on his lap again. Before he would have thought: I just can't do that. This time he kept a tally sheet again for two evenings, saw that he had once again escaped from difficult tasks, and started again with shorter blocks.

About three weeks after the bio-test, Ben was sitting on a Tuesday evening doing a history assignment on the Weimar Republic. When the alarm went off, he looked up in surprise. An hour had passed and he hadn't thought about the cell phone once. He hadn't changed a person and continued to enjoy chatting in the evenings. But now he decided when.

## What you can take from it

Ben hasn't banned his cell phone. He took it out of learning and gave it a permanent place afterwards. You can implement the following steps on your own, without anyone else having to set the rules.

### Why interruptions make it so difficult to think ahead

When you learn, you hold several things in your mind at once: the question, the steps you've already taken, and an idea of where you're going. A look at the cell phone pushes that aside. Then you have to pick up the thread again, and often you only succeed halfway. You then read lines without them getting through. In addition, part of your thoughts remain with the chat: What do I answer right away? Many small interruptions feel like learning, but are more like a chain of restarts.

What's particularly tricky is that you often reach for your cell phone right when things get stressful. This is the moment when you learn the most because your head is working right now.

And something else is deceptive: spending a long time at a desk feels like a lot of learning. You won't notice whether something has stuck until you check yourself. So at the end of each block, write down three things you just learned. If you can't think of anything, the block was too busy and you know what you can work on.

### Put your phone out of reach and bundle notifications

1. **Out of sight, out of reach.** Another room, the backpack, a drawer. Turning it upside down on the table is usually not enough because your hand knows the way.
2. **Turn on do not disturb or focus mode.** Almost all smartphones have such a function. You can usually set exceptions for certain contacts so that important things get through. Where exactly this works can be found in the settings of your device.
3. **Bundle notifications.** Turn off game and social notifications, mute large groups, and check in at set times instead of every sound.
4. **A short starting ritual.** Put your cell phone away, put the water down, set the alarm, go. The more equal the beginning, the less you have to negotiate with yourself every time.

### Use your cell phone as a learning tool without ending up in the feed

Sometimes you really need your cell phone, for example for a vocabulary app, an explanatory video or a photo of the blackboard picture. Then a clear intention helps:

- **Determine what for beforehand.** “I’ll watch the Synapse video and then close it.” Not: “I’ll see what’s available.”
- **Clean up the home screen.** Put learning apps at the front, distracting apps in a folder at the back.
- **Prepare material in advance.** Download worksheets, choose the video, open the board picture and then put your cell phone away again.
- **Use an alarm clock or a clock to keep the time.** If you unlock your cell phone to stop the time, you will quickly end up somewhere else.
- **Paper is sometimes better.** Vocabulary can also be learned without a screen, as [Frida's story about the index box](/lerngeschichten/vokabeln-lernen-mit-karteikasten/) shows.

### Arrangements in the group chat

The fear of missing out is very real in class chats. Arrangements help more than secretly staying away:

- **Let me know.** “I’ll be offline until 6 p.m., then I’ll get in touch.” Most people understand this immediately.
- **Separate school and chatter.** A separate chat just for questions about assignments, the big one can remain silent.
- **Study at the same time.** If you arrange for everyone to study from 4 p.m. to 5 p.m., no one misses out.
- **Collect questions.** Write down whatever comes to your mind while studying on a piece of paper and put it all at once after the block.

### Cell phone time as a reward after the learning block

Cell phone time can be a good reward if it comes after work and ends. This is what a learning afternoon could look like:

| time | What you do | Where the cell phone is |
|---|---|---|
| 4:00 p.m. to 4:30 p.m | Block 1: Query technical terms | in the hallway, do not disturb mode |
| 4:30 p.m. to 4:35 p.m | short break: get up, drink, open the window | stays in the hallway |
| 4:35 p.m. to 5:05 p.m | Block 2: Calculate tasks | in the hallway |
| 5:05 p.m. to 5:20 p.m | Longer break with 10 minutes on cell phone, alarm set | briefly in the hand, while standing |
| 5:20 p.m. to 5:50 p.m | Block 3: Write a summary from your head | back in the hallway |
| from 5:50 p.m | free | wherever you want |

If 30 minutes seems too long at first, start with 15 or 20 and increase slowly. Messages first, feed last, and you set the alarm before you unlock. [Marie's story about learning blocks](/lerngeschichten/lernpausen-richtig-planen/) tells more about meaningful breaks.

### Dealing with relapses

Relapses are part of it. A habit you've had for years won't go away in a week. What you do afterwards is important:

- **Look for the trigger.** Bored, a difficult task, tired or worried about missing something? A tally sheet like Ben's shows you your pattern.
- **Make an if-then plan.** “When I want to pick up my phone, I write down the thought and finish the task.”
- **Help yourself differently for difficult tasks.** Mark the spot, write down your question and move on to the next task instead of running away.
- **Restart with the next block** not tomorrow or Monday.

If you notice that your cell phone is having a major impact on your sleep, friendships, or school as a whole and you can't get it changed on your own, talk to someone you trust. You can find contact points under [Help & Advice](/hilfe-und-beratung/).

## Typical pitfalls

- **Just turn the phone over.** As long as it is within reach, it will continue to pull on you.
- **Start with your cell phone.** “Just take a quick look and then we'll get started” often postpones the start by half an hour.
- **Endless reward.** Without an alarm clock, the break turns into the evening.
- **Too long blocks at the beginning.** If you plan on spending two hours without your cell phone, you'll give up more quickly.
- **Putting yourself down.** A relapse doesn't mean you can't, it means you can evaluate it.

## Briefly asked

### Can I listen to music from my cell phone while studying?

Try it. Some learn well with quiet music without singing, others get distracted, especially when reading. If you listen to music, put the playlist together beforehand so you don't have to touch your phone during the block.

### What do I do if I really need to be available?

Then in Do Not Disturb mode, set exactly these contacts as exceptions, and only them. Alternatively, take a quick look every time you take a break and tell the person when you'll look again. Being available does not mean that every message needs an immediate response.

### How long does it take until it gets easier?

This is different for everyone. The first few days are usually the toughest, then you get used to the new routine.
