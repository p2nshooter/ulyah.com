When Ms. Lindner handed out the papers, Pia felt it before she had read the first task. First there was a tightening in her stomach, then her hands became cold, and when she looked at the sheet, the numbers became blurry. “A bicycle dealer reduces the price by 15 percent…” Pia read the sentence three times. He didn't make any sense. Lotta was already scribbling down the first bill next to her. Pia heard her own heart and thought only one thought, over and over again: I can't do this. I've never been able to do that.

## A compartment like a locked door

Pia was fourteen and in the eighth grade of a high school. She wrote essays in German, which the teacher sometimes read out, and she was one of the best in English. But since fifth grade, math was like a door behind which something threatening waited. When her father tried to help at the kitchen table, it usually ended in tears. “I was never good at math either,” her mother said consolingly. “It runs in our family.”

Pia had long since come up with an explanation. There were math people and language people, and she just wasn't a math person. That sounded logical and was somehow reassuring. But it also meant that nothing would ever change.

What Pia didn't realize was that she had started avoiding math. She put off homework until shortly before going to bed, then she copied the answers from Lotta. She never spoke up in class and hoped not to be picked on. She hardly studied before work because just looking at the book made her stomach ache. And the less she practiced, the less she understood, and the less she understood, the greater the fear.

## The conversation after the lesson

The work on percentage calculation came back with a five. Pia hadn't written anything down on several tasks. Ms. Lindner asked her to stay briefly after class. Pia expected a lecture. Instead, the teacher turned the work upside down on the table and asked: “What happens to you when you have a sheet like this in front of you?”

Pia didn't want to say anything. Then she told the truth. The pounding heart, the cold hands, the empty head. That she felt stupid whenever numbers were involved.

Ms. Lindner nodded as if she had heard that many times. "Do you know what I believe? Your head is not empty at this moment. It is full. Full of worries and the sentence 'I can't do this'. There is simply no room left for the task."

She showed Pia a circle of four words on the back of the work: fear, avoidance, gaps, failure. And then an arrow back to fear. "You can break the circle at any point. We'll start with avoidance because that's easiest."

## Small steps through the door

The plan the two wrote down was so small that it almost seemed ridiculous to Pia. Ten minutes of math every day, no more. Always at the same time, right after lunch, before the anxiety has had time to build up. The first tasks should be so easy that she would be able to complete them. Only when that worked would things become more difficult.

There was also an exercise that Ms. Lindner called “the worry sheet.” Before every math unit and before every paper, Pia wrote down what was on her mind for two minutes. “I’m afraid I won’t know anything again.” “Lotta is definitely faster again.” It felt silly. But afterwards it actually felt a little quieter in her head, as if she had made some space.

The first small breakthrough came in the second week. Pia calculated a percentage problem using the rule of three, line by line, without any hassle. The result was right. She stared at the paper for a while. That hadn't been so difficult after all.

Ms. Lindner also recommended that she attend the math remedial lesson on Thursday. There were only six students sitting there and no one laughed when asked a question. Pia noticed that others were also afraid. A ninth grade boy once said bluntly, “When I see a system of equations, it makes me want to jump out the window.” Everyone laughed, and Pia laughed with them, for the first time in a math lesson.

## The next job

Pia wrote the next paper on assignments and percentages six weeks later. The night before, she packed her bag, laid out her calculator and set square, and went to bed early. In the morning in the classroom, she wrote down her worries on a scrap paper, folded it up and put it under the pencil case. Then she exhaled slowly three times and read the first task.

The heart palpitations still came. But this time Pia knew what it was and that it would pass. She started with the task that seemed easiest to her and worked her way forward. She got stuck with one task and left it alone for the time being. In the end she had completed everything except one sub-task.

Under the work there was a three minus. For Pia it was the best grade in math in two years. She still wasn't a math person, she said. But the door was no longer locked.

## What you can take from it

Math anxiety is common, and it has nothing to do with a lack of intelligence. Many people who struggle in math are extremely smart and hard-working in other areas. In the exam situation, fear uses up some of the attention that you actually need for the task. That's why the head feels empty even though the knowledge is there. The good news: Fear can be reduced, step by step.

### Recognize the cycle

Math anxiety often works like a self-reinforcing cycle:

1. **Fear:** Even the thought of math causes anxiety.
2. **Avoid:** You put off homework, copy or don't study for work.
3. **Gaps:** Because you practice less, gaps in knowledge arise that pile on top of each other.
4. **Failure:** Bad grades seem to confirm that you can't do it.

And then everything starts again, only stronger. Becoming aware of this cycle is the first step. Because it shows: The problem is not that you are “not a math person”, but that a pattern has emerged that can be changed.

### Question the sentence “I’m not a math person.”

Many people with math anxiety carry a similar sentence with them. Often it comes from the family or from early bad experiences. The problem is that it seems like an excuse for the future: If you don't have any talent anyway, practicing isn't worth it.

Try rephrasing the sentence. Instead of “I can’t do math,” it’s better to say “Math is harder for me than other subjects and I still have gaps.” That sounds less dramatic and more honest. And it leaves room for change. Math is a skill that grows with practice, like swimming or an instrument.

### Create small, secure experiences of success

Fear becomes smaller when you have experiences that contradict it. That's why you start with tasks that you can confidently solve:

- **Ten minutes a day** is better than two hours on the weekend. Short units don't overwhelm you and build up a routine.
- **Start with easy tasks.** The first two or three tasks of each unit should work safely. This warms you up and gives you courage.
- **Slowly increase the difficulty.** Only when you have completed one step do you take the next one.
- **Record successes.** A tick in the calendar for each day of practice makes progress visible.

If there are real gaps from previous school years behind your fear, it's worth taking a look at [Jannik's story about math gaps](/lerngeschichten/mathe-luecken-finden-und-schliessen/). There you will find out how to find the cause and take corrective action.

### The worry sheet: thoughts from your head onto paper

A simple exercise that helps many: Write down what worries you for two to three minutes just before studying or before doing a job. Honestly, without censorship. Then you put the sheet away. Many people find that their heads feel a little clearer afterwards. Try it out and see if it helps you.

### Become calm during the exam

When anxiety strikes at work, a few steps can help:

| situation | What helps |
|---|---|
| Heart palpitations before the start | exhale slowly, longer than one, three to five times |
| The first task seems unsolvable | First find an easier task and start there |
| Head feels empty | Write down everything that comes to mind on the topic, including formulas |
| Stuck in the middle of the task | Mark task, continue, come back later |
| Thought “I can’t do this” | consciously replace it with “I’m taking the next step now” |

[Elias' story about the blackout during the exam](/lerngeschichten/blackout-in-der-pruefung/) tells you in detail what you can do if you suddenly can't think of anything during an exam.

### Getting help is a sign of strength

You don't have to deal with fear alone. These people and places can help:

- **Your math teacher:** Many teachers know math anxiety and have ideas on how they can support you. A short conversation after class is often enough to get you started.
- **Remedial lessons and learning times:** Questions are easier in small groups.
- **Advisory teachers and school social work:** They listen and know other offers.
- **School psychological advice:** If the fear is very strong or bothers you outside of math, school psychologists can help. You can get in touch through your school.
- **The number against grief:** Children and young people can speak to someone free of charge and anonymously on 116 111.

You can find further contact points on our [Help & Advice](/hilfe-und-beratung/) page.

### For parents: How you can support

Parents mean well when they say “I was bad at math too.” But the sentence can reinforce the impression in children that math talent is inherited and that nothing can be changed. It is more helpful to praise effort and small progress, to stay calm when things don't work, and to talk to the school together if you are very afraid.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Wanting too much at once.** A math marathon on the weekend often reinforces the aversion.
- **Just look at grades.** Progress is first shown in feelings and in small successes, the grade follows later.
- **Write-off as a permanent solution.** It provides short-term relief, but increases the gaps.
- **Compare yourself with others.** Your benchmark is your own progress, not the pace of your neighbor at the bench.

## Briefly asked

### Is math anxiety a disease?

Math anxiety is not a diagnosis in itself, but rather a strong tension related to math that many people experience. However, if the fear is very great, stresses you out in many situations or you no longer want to go to school, talk to a person you trust or the school psychologist.

### Can I get rid of math anxiety completely?

For many people it becomes significantly smaller over time. There is often a bit of nervousness before work, and that is normal. The important thing is that fear no longer prevents you from practicing and tackling your tasks.

### What if I have really big gaps?

Then it is even more important to start early and get help. Gaps can be closed, even if they go back several school years. A clear plan makes the task manageable and takes away a lot of the anxiety.
