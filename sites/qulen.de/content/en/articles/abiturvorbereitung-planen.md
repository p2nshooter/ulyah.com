The Abitur plan has been hanging on the notice board since the first day of school of the semester. Milan stood in front of it and read the dates: German, mathematics, English, history, plus an oral exam. Everything should be over in just under four months. He thought about the folders at home, filled with worksheets from two years of high school, and felt his stomach tighten. Where should he start? His friend Jacob patted him on the shoulder. "Don't panic. I won't start until March anyway." Milan nodded, but the panic didn't go away.

## Two years of material

Milan was eighteen and in the senior year of a high school. He was a good but not an outstanding student. In history and English he usually had ten to twelve points, in German a little less, and in math he fluctuated between six and nine. He didn't need a special grade point average for his desired course of study, social sciences. But he wanted to pass his high school diploma well, for himself.

The problem was the sheer volume. Two years of material in four subjects. And the memory of the tenth grade final exams, which he had started three weeks earlier and had been completely exhausted by the end. He wanted to do it differently this time, but didn't know how.

## A conversation with the tutor

Milan's tutor, Mr. Kowalski, offered talks to the high school graduates in the weeks before the exams. Milan spoke up. Mr. Kowalski listened and then took out a sheet. "I recommend thinking about your preparation in three phases. First phase: overview and finding gaps. Second phase: work on material and practice. Third phase: exam training and repetition."

He drew a timeline. "You have about sixteen weeks until the first exam. Plan about four weeks for phase one, eight weeks for phase two and the last four weeks for phase three. And don't forget that you still have classes and exams at the same time."

Then he gave Milan an important tip: "Look at the requirements for the Abitur in your country. It tells you which topics can appear in the exams. And get the tasks from the last years. They show you better than anything else what awaits you."

## Phase one: Overview

In the first four weeks, Milan took stock. For each examination subject, he created a list of all topics that were relevant to the examination according to the specifications. He put a traffic light color behind each topic: green for “sits”, yellow for “I know this, I have to repeat it”, red for “big gap”.

The result was sobering but helpful. There were a lot of red fields in math, especially in stochastics. Almost everything in history was yellow. In English and German it was primarily the methods, i.e. text analysis and interpretation, that he had to practice.

He also sorted his folders. For each subject he created a new, slim folder in which only the most important materials ended up: learning sheets, overviews, sample solutions. He put the rest on the shelf.

## Phase two: Develop

In the second phase, Milan worked through the yellow and red topics. He created a weekly schedule in which each subject appeared several times. Math on Mondays and Thursdays, history on Tuesdays, German on Wednesdays, English on Fridays, a day off and a revision day on the weekend.

For math, he signed up for a high school diploma preparation course that his school offered in the afternoon. For history, he wrote study notes on each topic and created a large timeline that he hung on the wall. For German and English, he practiced analyzes on old exam texts and gave some of them to his teachers for correction.

He also met once a week with two classmates for a history study group. They quizzed each other and discussed assessment tasks.

Not everything went according to plan. One week he was sick, another week he had two exams coming up. Milan learned to adapt his plan instead of abandoning it.

## Phase three: exam training

Four weeks before the first exam, Milan began exam training. He calculated entire old high school math exams under real conditions: with a clock, with the permitted aids, without breaks. He then compared the expectations that his teacher had distributed.

He wrote two complete mock exams in German and English. The first took significantly longer than allowed. On the second one he made it in time. He repeated history with his study notes and explained the causes of the First World War to his mother over dinner until she laughed and begged for mercy.

In the last week before the first exam he didn't learn anything new. He repeated, got enough sleep and went for a run every day.

## The exams

The exams were strenuous, but Milan felt prepared. In math there was a stochastics problem that was similar to a problem from an old year. In history he was able to support an assessment task with many examples. The end result was a high school diploma that he was very happy with. With eight points, math wasn't his strongest test, but it was better than feared.

Jakob, who had only started in March, later told him that he had hardly slept in the last few weeks. Milan thought about his timeline on the wall and was glad he did it differently.

## What you can take from it

The Abitur is an examination over a long period of time and with a lot of material. If you start early and plan in phases, you can distribute the preparation evenly and avoid the exhaustion of the last few weeks. The following steps will help you.

### Clarify the framework conditions

The Abitur examinations are regulated differently depending on the federal state. Find out early:

- **Which subjects** do you examine in writing and which orally?
- **Which topics** are relevant to the exam according to your country's requirements?
- **Which aids** are permitted, such as a calculator, formulary or dictionary?
- **How ​​long** do the exams last and what task formats are there?
- **Where can you find old exam tasks** and expectation horizons? Many education ministries and schools make them available.

### Three phases of preparation

| phase | Period (example 16 weeks) | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Overview | Weeks 1 to 4 | Collect topics, identify gaps, sort material |
| Elaborate | Weeks 5 to 12 | Close gaps, write learning notes, practice |
| Exam training | Weeks 13 to 16 | old tests under real conditions, repetition |

Adjust the periods to suit your high school graduation plan. If you can start earlier, even better.

### The topic list with traffic lights

A traffic light list shows you at a glance where you are:

- **Green:** The topic is correct. A short repetition is enough.
- **Yellow:** You know the topic, but you have to repeat and practice it.
- **Red:** There is a larger gap here. These topics take the most time.

Update the traffic lights every few weeks. Seeing how red turns into yellow and yellow turns into green is extremely motivating.

### A realistic weekly plan

A weekly plan for Abitur preparation should:

1. **every examination subject regularly included** so that nothing is forgotten,
2. **schedule difficult subjects more often**,
3. **Take lessons, exams and appointments into account**,
4. **have at least one day off per week**,
5. Leave **buffer** for the unforeseen.

[Greta's story about the study plan for class tests](/lerngeschichten/lernplan-fuer-klassenarbeiten/), and for even longer periods of time [Paula's story about the exam phase in her studies](/lerngeschichten/klausurenphase-im-studium/) shows how you can build a study plan for several exams.

### Use old exams correctly

Old Abitur exams are the most valuable practice material:

- **Write under real conditions**: with a time limit and only the permitted tools.
- **Compare with the expectation horizon**: What is expected? Where did you lose points?
- **Collect errors** and rework them in a targeted manner, like in [Fynn's story about the error book](/lerngeschichten/mathe-ueben-mit-fehlerheft/).
- **Ask teachers for correction**, especially for essays and analyses.

### Healthy through the exam period

A long preparation can only be sustained if you take care of yourself:

- **Get enough sleep**, especially in the weeks before and during exams. More about this in [Luis' story about sleep and learning](/lerngeschichten/schlaf-und-lernen/).
- **Regular exercise** to compensate for sitting too much.
- **Breaks and free times** that you can enjoy without feeling guilty.
- **Social contacts**, even when time is short.

If the fear of taking the Abitur is weighing heavily on you, read [Yusuf's story about test anxiety](/lerngeschichten/pruefungsangst-bewaeltigen/) and talk to your tutor, guidance teacher or school psychologist.

### Don't forget the oral exam

Many people focus on the written exams and underestimate the oral ones. Schedule time for them too and practice out loud. [Hamid's story about the oral exam](/lerngeschichten/muendliche-pruefung-vorbereiten/) shows how this works.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Start too late.** Two years of material cannot be repeated in three weeks.
- **Only learn your favorite subject.** The difficult subjects take the most time.
- **Learn without instructions.** If you don't know the topics relevant to the exam, you may be learning the wrong thing.
- **Don't write old exams.** Without exam training, there is no sense of time and task format.
- **Delete rest.** Exhaustion costs points in trials.

## Briefly asked

### When should I start preparing for the Abitur?

Many people begin structured preparation around four to six months before the exams. It is important that you regularly revise and create learning notes in parallel to the lessons. Then the mountain at the end is smaller.

### How many hours per day should I study?

That depends on your status and your plan. In addition to lessons, one to three hours is realistic on most days, more in the weeks without lessons before exams. Regularity is more important than as many hours as possible.

### Is a high school diploma preparation course worth it?

For subjects with large gaps, a course can be helpful, especially if the school offers it free of charge. Commercial courses often cost a lot of money. Check beforehand whether they fit your requirements and whether independent practice with old exams will help you as well.
