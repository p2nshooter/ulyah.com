Fynn was sure he had done everything right. Before working on quadratic equations, he had practiced for three evenings, an hour each, with the solution book next to him. He had calculated over fifty tasks. When work came back there was a four underneath. And as he looked through the mistakes, he felt sick: They were almost exactly the same mistakes he had made while practicing. Sign in the midnight formula. A forgotten plus-minus. An incorrectly resolved binomial formula.

## Practice without effect

Fynn was sixteen and in the tenth grade of a high school. He was not a student who refused math. He did what he was told: practice, practice, practice. But something wasn't right. He felt like he was running on a treadmill. A lot of effort, but he couldn't move.

His practice routine was always the same. He calculated a problem, looked at the solution booklet, and if his result was wrong, he read through the solution, nodded and thought: “Oh, sure.” Then came the next task. Sometimes he would look halfway through the solution if he didn't know what to do next. At the end of an evening he had “done” a lot of tasks and felt good.

His math teacher, Ms. Okafor, had the class write a correction after work. Fynn wrote it listlessly. But Ms. Okafor sat down with him briefly afterwards. “Fynn, can I see your exercise book?” She flipped through the pages full of bills and ticks. Then she asked, “What did you do when an assignment was wrong?”

“Read the solution.”

“And then?”

“Carry on.”

Ms. Okafor nodded slowly. "The feeling of 'Oh, sure' is deceptive. When you read a solution, it seems logical to you. But that doesn't mean that you find it yourself. Your head recognizes the path, it doesn't follow it. That's a big difference."

## A booklet just for mistakes

Ms. Okafor suggested something he initially found strange: a separate notebook just for mistakes. Every time he made a mistake in practice, homework, or tests, he was supposed to record the problem there. Not just the right solution, but four things: the task, its wrong path, the place where it went wrong, and a sentence as to why.

"And after a few days you calculate the problem again. Without looking. Only when you get it right twice in a row can you check it off."

Fynn bought a plain checkered notebook and wrote on the front: “Error Notebook”. He found it a bit embarrassing. But he had nothing to lose.

## Patterns emerge

After two weeks, Fynn had twelve entries. As he leafed through them, he noticed something he had never noticed before. Five of the twelve errors had the same cause: he made sign errors when there was a minus in front of a bracket. Three more came about because he misapplied the binomial formulas, especially the second one. Only four errors were truly isolated cases.

That changed everything. Instead of randomly practicing other quadratic equations, Fynn specifically focused on the minus-before-the-bracket cases. He wrote on an index card: “Minus before the brackets: every sign in the brackets turns around” and added ten short exercises just for that. For the binomial formulas, he derived the second formula himself by multiplying out (a − b) · (a − b). Since then he no longer had to memorize it, but knew where it came from.

His handling of the solution booklet also changed. Fynn set a new rule: before looking at the solution, he had to think for himself for at least ten minutes. When he got stuck, he wrote down what he knew and where exactly he got stuck. Only then was he allowed to look, and then only the next step, not the entire solution. That was more strenuous. But that, Ms. Okafor said, is what learning is.

## The next job

Before the next work on parabolas and quadratic functions, Fynn did not calculate fifty problems, but rather went through his error book. He recalculated all the problems that were not correct twice. There were also a few new tasks that he intentionally worked on in a mixed order.

At work he stumbled upon a minus in front of a bracket during a task. But this time he noticed. A little warning signal in your head, almost like a voice from the error book. He calculated more slowly, deliberately reversed each sign and got the correct result.

Under the work there was a two minus. Fynn had spent less time practicing than before the last work, but much more specifically. Since then, the error book has always been next to his math notebook. “My personal trainer,” he once said half-jokingly to his friend Can. “He knows my weaknesses better than I do.”

## What you can take from it

Practicing a lot is not the same as practicing well. Those who calculate tasks, read the solution and carry on often have the feeling that they have achieved a lot but retain little. Mistakes are not defeats, but rather the most valuable indications of where you can still learn. An error log makes these notes visible and usable.

### Why mistakes are so valuable

Every mistake shows you a place where your understanding is not yet certain. If you just correct it and move on, that spot will stay wobbly. If you examine it, understand it and practice with purpose, it will become solid. Many learners don't make a hundred different mistakes, but rather the same few over and over again. Anyone who finds these can improve a lot with little effort.

### This is how you create an error log

You will need your own booklet or section in your binder. For each error you create an entry with these parts:

| part | What you write in it |
|---|---|
| Date and topic | e.g. B. 12.3., quadratic equations |
| task | the complete task |
| My way | your bill, even the wrong steps |
| Fault point | the line where it first became incorrect is highlighted in color |
| Error type | e.g. E.g. sign, formula, calculation errors, misunderstood task |
| Why? | a sentence in your own words |
| Right way | the correct solution, calculated yourself |
| Repeatedly | two ticks if the task was correct twice without help |

The most important part is the sentence under “Why?” It forces you to really understand the mistake. “I forgot that all signs are reversed when there is a minus in front of the brackets” is much more helpful than “arithmetic error”.

### Recognize and sort error types

After a few weeks it's worth taking a look at all the entries. Count which types of errors occur most frequently. Typical categories in math are:

- **Caresional errors:** Typographical errors, transposed numbers, forgotten units.
- **Rule error:** A rule is applied incorrectly, for example with signs, brackets, powers or fractions.
- **Comprehension error:** The concept behind it is unclear, for example what a function actually expresses.
- **Reading error:** The task was misunderstood or a part was missed. How to proceed here is shown in [Sara's story about word problems](/lerngeschichten/textaufgaben-in-mathe-loesen/).
- **Gaphole:** An older topic on which the new one is based is missing. Then [Jannik's story about math gaps](/lerngeschichten/mathe-luecken-finden-und-schliessen/) will help.

Each category needs a different countermeasure. A fixed control routine helps against careless errors, targeted short exercises against rule errors, and a new explanation against errors in understanding.

### Don't look into the solution too early

The solution booklet is a valuable tool, but only if you use it correctly. A simple rule helps:

1. **Try for yourself.** Work seriously on the task for at least ten minutes before checking.
2. **Write down where you hang.** What do you know? What are you missing? Which step is unclear?
3. **Watch the next step only.** Cover the rest of the solution and then try to move forward on your own.
4. **Repeat later.** Calculate the problem again after a few days without help.

This feels more strenuous than reading solutions, but it is precisely this effort that makes the knowledge stick in your memory. Why recall from memory is so effective is explained in [Hannah's story about spaced repetition](/lerngeschichten/verteiltes-wiederholen-statt-pauken/).

### Use the error book regularly

An error log only works if you open it again. Here's how to incorporate it into your week:

- **After each homework or practice session** you enter new errors.
- **Once a week** you recalculate all tasks that do not yet have two ticks.
- **Before each work** you go through the entire booklet on the topic.
- **After each work** you copy the errors from the correction into the notebook.

### A check routine against careless errors

Many points are lost in work due to small mistakes that you could actually avoid. A fixed routine at the end of each task helps:

- Is the sign correct?
- Did I copy correctly, including from the assignment?
- Does the scale make sense?
- Does the unit fit?
- Can I do a sample?

Write these questions on a card and keep it next to your notebook as you practice. Over time you will go through them automatically.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Enter only the correct solution.** Without your wrong path and the error point, the most important thing is missing.
- **Never open the booklet again.** An error booklet in the closet doesn't help anyone.
- **Be ashamed of mistakes.** The magazine is just for you. The more honest it is, the more useful it is.
- **Enter too many at once.** It's better to have ten well-investigated errors than fifty written-off solutions.
- **Check off after the first correct attempt.** Only twice correctly, at a distance, shows that it works.

## Briefly asked

### Does an error book also work in other subjects?

Yes. In languages ​​you can collect grammatical and spelling errors, and in physics or chemistry you can collect typical errors in formulas and units. The principle is always the same: make mistakes visible, understand them and repeat them in a targeted manner.

### How much time does that take?

A little more at the beginning because you look at every mistake more closely. You save time because you no longer have to calculate a lot of tasks at random, but instead work specifically on your weak points.

### What if I don't understand why something is wrong?

Then this is exactly the right question for your teacher, a classmate or the tutor. Bring the entry from the error book with you. With such a specific question you will usually get a much better answer than with “I don’t understand the topic”.
