The certificate was on the kitchen table and no one said anything. English: poor. Including the sentence that Emil had already feared: “The transfer is at risk if the performance in English does not improve.” Emil's mother read it twice. Then she sighed and said, "Now what? Six weeks of vacation?" Emil stared at the page. He was so looking forward to the holidays. Two weeks of camp with the Boy Scouts, outdoor pool, bike tours. Now it looked like he would be sitting at a desk for six weeks.

## A compartment that had slipped

Emil was thirteen and was in the seventh grade of a secondary school. He actually liked English in elementary school and fifth grade. But in sixth grade the grammar became more difficult and Emil lost touch. First with the tenses, then with irregular verbs, then with the if-clauses. He always learned vocabulary the night before a test and then forgot it again. By seventh grade, a three had become a four and then a five.

Emil's mother initially wanted a tutoring plan for the entire vacation. Two hours every day for six weeks. Emil protested. There were arguments, doors slammed. In the evening Emil, his mother and his father sat down at the table together. The father had an idea: “Let’s talk to Mr. Bauer before we plan anything.”

## A conversation before the holidays

Mr. Bauer, Emil's English teacher, took ten minutes on the last day of school. He looked at his notes and said: "Emil, you have two main problems. Firstly, you are missing a lot of vocabulary, especially from sixth grade. Secondly, the tenses are not correct, especially simple past and present perfect. If you catch up on that during the holidays, you will start the new school year completely differently."

Emil's mother asked for two hours a day. Mr. Bauer shook his head. "That's too much. Then Emil is exhausted after the holidays and hates English even more. Twenty to thirty minutes on five days a week is enough if he works specifically. And I would leave the first and last week of the holidays free to relax. He has four weeks in between. That's a lot of time if you use it properly."

He gave Emil a list of the most important vocabulary from the last two school years and recommended practice pages from the textbook on the tenses. "And read something in English that interests you. Comics, a simple read, anything."

## A plan with gaps

At home, Emil sat down with his father to make the weekly holiday calendar. The first week: free. The second and third weeks: camp. The fourth and fifth weeks: English on five days, thirty minutes each, in the morning after breakfast. The sixth week: free, with a small repetition at the end.

“Wait,” said Emil, “that’s only two weeks of English.” His father laughed. "You can take vocabulary cards with you at the camp. Ten minutes a day, in the evening in the tent." Emil grimaced but nodded.

He wrote the vocabulary on index cards, sorted by topic. He created a small notebook for the tenses. On the first page he wrote a table: When do you use which tense? How do you recognize them? What signal words are there?

## Camp with index cards

At the camp, Emil was initially embarrassed when he took out his index cards in the evening. But his tentmate Jonas, a boy from another school, was curious. It turned out that Jonas had lived in Ireland for a year and spoke English well. The two quizzed each other, and Jonas taught Emil a few expressions that weren't in the textbook. It almost became a game.

After the camp came the two weeks with fixed learning times. Every morning after breakfast, Emil sat at his desk for thirty minutes. Ten minutes of vocabulary, ten minutes of grammar exercises, ten minutes of reading. After that he had the rest of the day free. Some days he didn't feel like it, but he knew it would be over after thirty minutes.

While reading, Emil discovered an English comic series about a detective. He didn't understand every word, but the pictures helped. He wrote out unknown words that appeared over and over again. At some point he realized that he was no longer thinking about school when he was reading.

## The start of the new school year

In the first English lesson of the new school year, Mr. Bauer wrote a short test on tenses and vocabulary to see where the class stood. Emil got a three. For him it was like winning the lottery. In the first half of the year his grade stabilized at a four plus, later at a three. The transfer was no longer an issue.

And the best thing: Emil still had a vacation. Tent camp, outdoor swimming pool, bike tours. He just had a little bit of English built in every day.

## What you can take from it

Holidays are primarily for relaxation. If you don't switch off after a strenuous school year, you'll start the next one exhausted. But if there are gaps that would hinder you in the new school year, the holidays can be a good opportunity to catch up in peace. The key is an easy, realistic plan.

### Rest first

Before you even make a study plan, plan for rest. What has proven itself:

- **Keep the first week of vacation free.** After the school year, your head and body need a break.
- **Make the last week before school starts relaxed.** A little repetition is enough.
- **Don't sacrifice vacations, trips and special experiences.** Learning can be planned around this.
- **Plan free days.** Even during learning weeks, the weekend should remain free.

### Identify gaps specifically

Holidays are too precious to study aimlessly. Find out exactly where your gaps are:

1. **Talk to the teacher.** Before the holidays, ask which topics are most important and what you should catch up on.
2. **Look through the certificate and work.** What mistakes kept occurring?
3. **Make a list.** Write down the two or three most important topics. No longer.

How you can systematically find gaps is shown using the example of math [Jannik's story about math gaps](/lerngeschichten/mathe-luecken-finden-und-schliessen/). The principle works in every subject.

### An easy holiday learning plan

A good holiday learning plan is short and regular:

| Element | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Duration per day | twenty to thirty minutes, at most an hour |
| days per week | four to five, weekends off |
| point in time | in the morning before the day is filled with other plans |
| Content | one or two topics, no more |
| Variety | Combine vocabulary, exercises and something easy like reading |

Short, regular units are more effective than long learning days because the knowledge is remembered through repetition over many days. More on this explained [Hannah's story about spaced repetition](/lerngeschichten/verteiltes-wiederholen-statt-pauken/).

### Integrate learning into everyday holiday life

Not every learning unit has to take place at a desk:

- **Take vocabulary cards with you**, to the outdoor pool, on a trip, to the camp.
- **Use learning apps or audio books** in the foreign language for journeys if you like.
- **Read something that interests you**, in a foreign language or on the topic of a subject.
- **Watch films or series** in the original language with subtitles.
- **Calculate in everyday life**, for example when shopping, planning a bike ride or while cooking.

### Stay motivated

During the holidays it is tempting to skip studying. These tricks help:

- **Fixed time**, for example directly after breakfast.
- **Short sessions with a clear ending** so the rest of the day is free.
- **Visible progress**, such as ticks in the calendar.
- **Rewards**, for example an ice cream after a week of following the plan.
- **Learn together**, with a friend, a girlfriend or a sibling.

### Tutoring and holiday courses

Some schools, adult education centers or educational institutions offer holiday courses to catch up. They can be helpful, especially if there are large gaps. Make sure that there is enough free time and that your weaknesses are specifically worked on during the course. Under certain conditions, families with little money can receive support for learning support through the education and participation package. Information about this is available at your school or at the responsible office in your city or district.

### For parents

Parents often worry when a transfer is at risk. Nevertheless, a holiday schedule that is too strict is usually counterproductive. It is more helpful to make a realistic plan that includes rest together with the child and, if possible, with the teacher. Praise for small progress and a calm tone have more effect than pressure.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Plan the entire vacation.** Without rest, you start the new school year exhausted.
- **Too many topics at once.** It's better to cover two topics thoroughly than five superficially.
- **Postpone everything until the last week.** Then the pressure is on and time is short.
- **Learn without a goal.** If you don't know what you want to catch up with, you'll quickly lose interest.
- **Worksheets only.** Reading, listening and everyday applications make learning more lively.

## Briefly asked

### Should I also study during the holidays if I don't have any gaps?

Not necessarily. Rest is important. If you feel like it, you can read or try something new, but it's not mandatory. Some people briefly review the most important topics in the last week of the holidays to make it easier to start the new school year.

### How much can I realistically make up during the holidays?

Twenty to thirty minutes four to five days a week for three to four weeks can accomplish a lot, especially with vocabulary and basics. However, large gaps over several school years usually require further attention during the school year.

### What if I can't motivate myself during the holidays?

Start with five minutes a day. It is often easier to continue than to start. [Lasse's story about procrastination](/lerngeschichten/aufschieben-ueberwinden/) shows how you get into action.
