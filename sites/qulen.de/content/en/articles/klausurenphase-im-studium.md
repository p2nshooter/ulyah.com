Paula sat in the cafeteria in front of her calendar and felt like she was going to drown. Five exams. Microeconomics, statistics, accounting, business law and English for economists. All within three weeks, the first in exactly a month. She had somehow managed it in the first semester by studying day and night for the last two weeks. But back then there had only been three exams and she had barely passed statistics. Now there were five, and Statistics II was considered the most difficult subject in the entire bachelor's degree.

## The pattern from the first semester

Paula was twenty and was in her second semester studying business administration at a technical college. She had done well in school without having to try particularly hard. Everything was different during my studies. There was no homework that anyone checked, no class tests in between, just lectures, exercises and the exams at the end of the semester that decided everything.

In the first semester, Paula had attended the lectures, more or less attentive, and told herself that she would learn later. Later came in the last two weeks before the exams. She studied from morning to night, drank a lot of coffee, and slept little. She passed all the exams, but she was so exhausted afterwards that she spent the first few days of the holiday sick in bed. And after a few weeks there was hardly anything left of what she had learned.

Her roommate Sophie, who was already in her fifth semester of studying social work, sat down with her plate. She glanced at the calendar and at Paula's face. “Exam panic?” Paula nodded. Sophie put down her plate. "Come into the kitchen tonight. I'll show you how I do it."

## A calendar on the refrigerator door

That evening, Sophie spread out a large monthly calendar on the kitchen table. “First you enter all the exam dates. Then we’ll see how much time you really have.”

Paula entered the five dates. Then they marked all the days when she couldn't study: two evenings when Paula worked in the café, a family birthday, the lectures and exercises that were still running. What was left was less than Paula had thought, but more than she had feared.

“Now the most important question,” said Sophie. “Which exam is the most difficult for you and which one counts the most?” Paula thought about it. Statistics was difficult and required a lot of credit points. Accounting was easy for her. English was more of a formality. Microeconomics and business law were somewhere in between.

Sophie drew a small table with three columns: difficulty, weight in credits, date. This resulted in a sequence. Statistics got the most space even though the exam was only in the second week. English and accounting were given less time.

## Plan backwards

Then Sophie showed her a method that Paula didn't know yet: the backwards plan. For each exam, they started on the day of the exam and worked back. The last two days before each exam were reserved for repetition and old exams. Before that came the thematic blocks, spread out over the weeks.

“And you don’t learn one subject at a time,” Sophie said. "Otherwise you'll have forgotten statistics until the exam comes. You alternate. Two or three subjects per day, in blocks."

Paula built her plan. Statistics came in the morning when she was most awake. After lunch a lighter subject. In the late afternoon repeat of the previous day. Each block lasted approximately ninety minutes, with breaks in between. There was no more learning in the evenings after 8 p.m. Half a day a week was completely free.

At first, Paula thought the free times were excessive. “I don’t have any time for breaks.” Sophie shook her head. "You don't have time to get sick. Breaks are part of the plan."

## Old exams and a study group

For the first few days, Paula stuck to the plan and it felt good. But she quickly reached her limits when it came to statistics. She understood the formulas from the lecture, but often didn't know how to start with the exercises.

She remembered that the student council was collecting old exams. She looked at the exams from the last semester and noticed that certain types of tasks kept coming up: confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, regression analysis. She specifically targeted these guys.

She also joined a small study group that met twice a week in the library. Four people, each prepared a topic and explained it to the others. Paula took over the regression analysis. As she prepared her explanation, she herself understood the topic properly for the first time.

## The three weeks

The exam phase was strenuous, but different than in the first semester. Paula slept at least seven hours every night. She went for a walk every now and then and cooked with Sophie in the evenings. The day before each exam, she just did a light review and packed her bag.

In the end, she passed all five exams. She got a 2.3 in statistics, much better than she ever imagined. And the best thing: When she came across regression analyzes in market research in her third semester, she still remembered almost everything. Since then, the calendar has hung on the refrigerator door every semester.

## What you can take from it

An exam phase with several exams in a short period of time cannot be managed with spontaneous cramming. She needs a plan that distributes time realistically, sets priorities and includes rest. The following steps will help you approach multiple exams more calmly.

### Step 1: Make all appointments and obstacles visible

Enter all exam dates in a calendar that you can see at a glance. Then mark all the times when you can't study: lectures, job, appointments, family. This way you can honestly see how much time you have left. A realistic view prevents you from overextending yourself later.

### Step 2: Prioritize exams

Not every exam requires the same amount of preparation. Rate each according to three criteria:

| criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Difficulty | How well do I already master the material? |
| Weight | How many credit points or what proportion of the grade? |
| Scope | How much material do I have to learn? |

Exams that are difficult, important, and extensive get the most space in your plan. Exams that are easy for you get less time, but not no time at all.

### Step 3: Create a backwards plan

Plan each exam backwards from the exam day:

1. **The last one or two days** before an exam are reserved for repeating and old exams.
2. **Before that** you distribute the subject blocks of the subject.
3. **At the very beginning** there is an overview: What topics are there? What can I do, what is missing?

The school version also shows how to set up a learning plan for several exams [Greta's story about a week with three papers](/lerngeschichten/lernplan-fuer-klassenarbeiten/). The principle works the same way when studying, just over longer periods of time.

### Step 4: Alternate subjects

If you complete one subject completely and then start the next, you often forget the first one until the exam. It is better to learn several subjects alternately. This way you repeat each subject regularly and the change keeps your brain alert. A typical day might look like this:

| time | Content |
|---|---|
| morning | most difficult subject, new content |
| Noon | Break, eat, exercise |
| afternoon | second subject, exercises |
| Late afternoon | Repeat from the previous day, flashcards |
| evening | free |

Why spaced repetition is so much more effective than cramming is explained in [Hannah's story about spaced repetition](/lerngeschichten/verteiltes-wiederholen-statt-pauken/).

### Step 5: Use old exams

Old exams are one of the most valuable learning tools in your studies. They show you which types of tasks occur, how questions are worded and how much time you have per task. Ask the student council, the department or older students. Calculate them under exam conditions, i.e. with a time limit and without any aids that are not permitted in the exam.

### Step 6: Learn together

Study groups are particularly helpful with difficult subjects. If everyone prepares a topic and explains it to the others, everyone learns twice: by preparing and by listening. Make sure that the group stays small, has fixed appointments and works in a focused manner.

### Step 7: Schedule relaxation

An exam period is a marathon, not a sprint. Without rest, concentration and memory performance decline. Therefore, plan consciously:

- **enough sleep**, even if the temptation is to study at night,
- **regular breaks** in and between learning blocks, as in [Marie's story about learning breaks](/lerngeschichten/lernpausen-richtig-planen/),
- **Exercise**, such as a walk or a bike ride,
- **at least half a day off per week**,
- **real meals** instead of just snacks and coffee.

### Make the day before the exam calm

The day before an exam you won't learn anything fundamentally new. Repeat the most important points, pack your things, find the way to the exam room and go to sleep on time. How you spend the last 24 hours is described in [Elif's story about the day before the exam](/lerngeschichten/tag-vor-der-pruefung/).

## Typical pitfalls

- **Start too late.** If you only start two weeks before five exams, you hardly have any leeway.
- **One subject at a time.** The first subject is forgotten before the exam comes.
- **Disregard easy subjects.** Even easy exams require some preparation.
- **Delete breaks and sleep.** Exhaustion ends up costing more time than it saves.
- **Rely only on scripts.** Old exams and exercises show what is really required.

## Briefly asked

### How many hours a day should I study during the exam phase?

This varies from person to person. Many students do well with four to six hours of concentrated learning per day, spread over several blocks. More is not automatically better if concentration wanes.

### What do I do if I realize that there isn't enough time?

Re-prioritize. Focus on the topics that appear most frequently in old exams and the exams that carry the most weight. Also find out whether and how you can postpone an exam or retake it later, and if in doubt, ask the student advisor.

### Where can I find support when stress overwhelms me?

Many universities offer psychological counseling, learning coaching or time management workshops. Student advisory services can also help.
