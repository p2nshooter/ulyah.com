“Matilda, your homework?” Ms. Kramer stood next to the table and waited. Matilda leafed through the English notebook, then the notebook, then the English notebook again. The page was blank. She had been so sure that the exercise wasn't due until Thursday. Ms. Kramer made a small mark in her notebook, not in an angry way, more like the way one notes a rainy day. It was the third sign that month, and Matilda felt her face heat up.

## Many subjects, many teachers

Matilda was ten and had been in fifth grade since the summer holidays. In elementary school she had had almost everything with Mrs. Liebig, and Mrs. Liebig had written her homework in the same place on the blackboard every day, in green chalk. Now she had a different teacher for almost every subject, often a different room, and new habits to learn every week. Mr. Ruhl announced the math problems when the bell rang. In German they were on worksheets. The bio just said: “The poster by next Thursday.”

Matilda sometimes wrote in her homework book, sometimes on a piece of paper, sometimes nothing at all because she thought she would remember it. She didn't notice. In the evening she sat in front of her satchel and wasn't sure about anything. She thought of the organic poster at eight o'clock on Wednesday evening and stuck up pictures that turned crooked until half past nine. What annoyed her most was that she could do the tasks. She just never knew which one was on.

## A godmother with a worn planner

At Matilda's school, every fifth grade had sponsors from the eighth grade. Matilda's godmother was called Zoe and she sat in the godmother's corner in front of the library during her lunch break on Tuesdays. Matilda showed her the homework book. Some entries were on the wrong day, many pages were blank, and there was no date next to “Poster.”

“That’s what mine looked like in fifth grade,” Zoe said, placing her planner next to it. The corners were worn and the sides were full of small snags. "The booklet isn't the problem. You need a few rules that you follow the same way every time. Then you don't have to think anymore."

The first rule: The booklet is open on the table at the beginning of each lesson. If you only look for it when the doorbell rings, you won't write anything down. The second: A task is entered on the day it is due, not the day it was posted. The third: If there is no homework, you “don’t write anything down.” Then in the evening you know that you haven't forgotten it. And the fourth: You only check off the task when the task is finished and in your pocket.

Then Zoe asked when Matilda did her homework. “Someday,” Matilda said honestly. After dinner, after an episode of her series, sometimes after dinner. Zoe nodded. "Set a fixed time. For me it's half past three, after a break. Then it's no longer an argument with myself, it's just off."

## One week in advance

Two weeks later, Mr. Ruhl announced that the magazines would be collected the following week. They should be complete and neat. In the past, Matilda would have done this the night before. This time she sat down with Zoe and broke down the task: on Monday copy missing entries from her bank neighbor Ella, on Tuesday add headings and data, on Wednesday recalculate two tasks that she had skipped back then. Thursday remained free. She wrote each part in the planner on each day.

On Thursday she realized that there was no solution to a task in the booklet because she didn't understand it. In the past she would have simply left the gap. Now she wrote underneath: “I don’t understand why people are sharing here.” The next morning she asked Mr. Ruhl before class. He explained it in two minutes and she added the solution later.

She also tidied up her satchel. There were loose sheets of paper collected inside, a geography worksheet was folded three times and stuck to a lunch box. Now each compartment was given a color, and there was a transparent folder in the front just for papers that still needed to be done or turned in.

## Friday belongs to the planner

Zoe's last tip was the one that Matilda initially found most boring: a ten-minute weekly check-in every Friday after school. Browse through planners, look at next week, file loose papers, refill pens. After three Fridays, she noticed that it made Mondays easier. She already knew what was coming at the weekend.

It wasn't completely error-free. Once she forgot her sports equipment and once she forgot the signed consent form for the trip. But no new characters were added to Ms. Kramer's notebook until the fall holidays. And when Matilda saw a fifth-grader from the parallel class sitting in the sponsor's corner after the holidays, desperately leafing through an empty homework book, she knew exactly what she was going to say to her.

## What you can take from it

Matilda didn't get neater because she tried harder. She has introduced a few fixed routines that relieve her of the burden of remembering. The following steps are suitable for switching to secondary school, but will also help later when the tasks become more.

### Keep your homework book or school planner correctly

Regardless of whether you use a simple notebook or a school planner with a weekly view, the important thing is that you always proceed in the same way.

1. **Open the booklet at the beginning of the lesson.** This way you can write straight away, even if the task doesn't come until the bell rings.
2. **Enter on the due date.** If the task is where it needs to be completed, you can see at a glance in the evening what is due tomorrow.
3. **Write down exactly.** Not “Math”, but “Book p. 34, No. 5 a to c, in the notebook”.
4. **Also write down “nothing on”.** An empty field makes you doubt, a “nothing on” doesn’t.
5. **Don't check off until everything is ready and packed.**
6. **Write down oral tasks.** “Learning Vocabulary Unit 1” is just as much a homework task as a written exercise.

This is what a day can look like in the planner:

| compartment | task | Posted on | Done |
|---|---|---|---|
| Math | Book p. 34, no. 5 a to c in the booklet | Monday | ✓ |
| English | Learn vocabulary unit 1, page 2 | Monday | ✓ |
| German | Parts of speech worksheet, tasks 2 and 3 | Tuesday | |
| Organic | Poster, part 2: Outline the heading and layout | Tuesday | |
| Geography | nothing on | – | – |

### A fixed homework time and a sensible order

With a fixed time, you no longer have to negotiate with yourself every day. Choose a time when you are not completely exhausted and plan a short break beforehand to eat and breathe. If your school offers study time or homework help, this can also be your set time.

This order helps many people:

- **Start with something short.** An easy task gets you going.
- **Then the hardest part.** At the beginning you still have the most strength.
- **Oral at the end.** Checking vocabulary or re-reading a text is also possible when you are more tired.

Take a short break between two subjects, get up and have a drink. And pay attention to how long it takes you overall. If you regularly sit for much longer than your classmates, talk to your class teacher. Many schools have agreements about how much time homework should take. [Aylin's story about the right place to learn](/lerngeschichten/lernplatz-einrichten/) shows where you can work in peace.

### Spread larger tasks over several days

A poster, a reading diary or an announced vocabulary test cannot be completed in one evening. Break them down as soon as you find out about them:

1. Think about what parts the task consists of.
2. Write each part on its own day in the planner, with an activity word.
3. Plan the final part a day before submission so that you have a day off in case something goes wrong.

| day | Part of the task “Poster about the hedgehog” |
|---|---|
| Thursday | Read the pages about the hedgehog in the bio book and note five key points |
| Friday | Borrow a non-fiction book from the school library and add two key points |
| Monday | Divide the poster: headline, four boxes, sketch in pencil |
| Tuesday | Write texts in the boxes and add drawings |
| Wednesday | free if something takes longer |
| Thursday | Submit poster |

If you notice that you always leave big tasks until the last evening, [Lasse's story about procrastination](/lerngeschichten/aufschieben-ueberwinden/) will help you get started.

### What you do when you get stuck

Getting stuck is part of learning. It's important that a difficult task doesn't take up your entire afternoon.

- **Read the task again slowly.** Often the solution is in a word that you missed.
- **Look for a similar example in the notebook.** Something similar has usually already been calculated or discussed in class.
- **Set a time limit.** If you get stuck after about ten minutes, move on to the next task.
- **Write down the question.** Not “I can’t do this,” but exactly: “I don’t understand why people are sharing here.” A question like this can be answered quickly.
- **Ask classmates.** Ask for directions, not the solution. Copying won't help you in your next work.
- **Speak to the teacher.** It is best to do this before the lesson or immediately after. Show what you tried. An honest attempt with a clear question is worth more than a blank page.

### Organize folders and notebooks per compartment

Organizing your satchel saves you time and many lost tasks every morning.

- **One color per compartment.** Booklet, folder and cover in the same color. Then you grab it blindly.
- **File worksheets immediately.** Still in the lesson, with the date at the top right. Loose leaves disappear.
- **A folder for outstanding items.** Everything that still needs to be done, signed or handed in goes in its own folder at the front of the satchel.
- **Pack your satchel the evening before.** According to the next day's timetable, including sports equipment and special materials.

### The short weekly check on Friday

Ten minutes at the end of the week will keep your system running. Post this list wherever you do homework:

| Question | What you do |
|---|---|
| Is everything that has been checked off really done? | Browse through this week's planner, carry over what is open to the next week |
| What's next week? | View tests, assignments and special dates, break down large tasks |
| Is there something loose lying around? | File the sheets and empty the folder for open items |
| Am I missing something? | Write down any missing entries and ask your classmates on Monday |
| Do I have all the material? | Check pens, glue, notebook pages, sports equipment |

If you are further along and have several class tests coming up, [Greta's story about the learning plan](/lerngeschichten/lernplan-fuer-klassenarbeiten/) shows you how to build a plan for several weeks.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Rely on memory.** “I’ll remember that” works for one task, but not for ten subjects.
- **Enter on the day you give it up.** Then you'll be looking in the wrong place in the evening.
- **Make the planner colorful instead of using it.** A simple notebook that you always keep is better than a nice one that's at home.
- **Stick to a task.** It's better to write down a clear question and move on.
- **Skip the Friday check.** Especially if the week has been stressful, it shows you what was left behind.

## Briefly asked

### Booklet or app, which is better?

Both can work. You can write on paper in every lesson, even if cell phones are not allowed in class. It is important that you only use one system and not three halves.

### What do I do if I haven't written down a task?

Ask a classmate who reliably takes notes. Some classes also have a designated contact person for homework. If you can't reach anyone, do what you know for sure and tell the teacher honestly the next day.

### I was sick. How do I catch up on the tasks?

Have a classmate show you the entries from a planner and discuss with the teachers what you should catch up on by when. You can find more stories about structure and everyday learning in the chapter [Learning plan & homework] (/themen/lernplan-und-hausaufgaben/).
