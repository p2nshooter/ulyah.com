It was just after two in the morning when Luis closed the biology book. His eyes were burning, his neck was stiff, and there were two empty cans of energy drink on the desk. Tomorrow at eight there was the organic exam on genetics. Luis felt like he had squeezed everything he could into his head. He went to bed, but no sleep came. His heart was pounding, his thoughts were racing. At four he looked at the clock. The alarm clock rang at half past six.

## The blackout after the long night

Luis was seventeen and was in the eleventh grade of a comprehensive school with a high school level. He was a typical night owl. In the evening he felt awake and focused, but in the morning he could hardly get out of bed. That's why he preferred to study late, often until midnight, or longer before exams.

Then something happened in the biology exam that he didn't understand. He was sitting in front of a problem on Mendelian inheritance, a topic he had repeated three more times that night, and his head felt like it was being packed with cotton wool. He remembered learning it, but he couldn't get to the knowledge. He read the assignments several times, made careless mistakes, and didn't finish on time.

The exam came back with four points. Luis was stunned. He had never studied so much for an exam.

## An uncomfortable explanation

Ms. Yilmaz, his biology teacher, noticed the look on his face when he returned it and asked him to stay for a moment. As Luis talked about his long night, she nodded slowly.

"Do you know what happens to what you've learned when you sleep?" she asked. Luis shook his head.

"Your brain continues to work while you sleep. According to everything research has known so far, new memory content is consolidated during sleep and linked to what you already know. If you hardly sleep the night before the exam, this processing is missing. And at the same time, your brain is tired the next morning, concentration drops and you make more mistakes."

She smiled. "Sleep is part of studying for your memory. The night before the exam is not wasted time, but an important part of your preparation."

Luis was skeptical. He had always thought sleep was wasted time that could be better used for studying. But the four points spoke clearly.

## An experiment

Ms. Yilmaz suggested an experiment. For the next biology exam, he should start his preparation earlier, spread over two weeks, and sleep at least eight hours the night before. “Then compare yourself.”

Luis was a night owl, and that couldn't be changed overnight. But he could do some things differently. He continued to enjoy studying in the evenings, but no longer after 10 p.m. After that, there were no more books and screens. He put his cell phone in the hallway to charge, bought a simple alarm clock and read a few pages of a novel before going to sleep. Energy drinks were only available in the morning, and soon not at all.

The first few evenings were difficult. Luis lay awake and wasn't used to being in bed so early. But after about a week, he noticed that he was getting tired earlier in the evenings. It was a little easier for him to get up in the morning. And he was more alert in class.

## The next exam

He prepared the next organic exam on protein biosynthesis over two weeks, a little bit every day, with flashcards and exercises. The evening before the exam, he only briefly repeated the most important points, packed his bag and went to bed at half past ten. He didn't fall asleep right away, but eventually he did, and only woke up when the alarm clock sounded.

In the exam his head was clear. He read the tasks once, understood them and began to write. When faced with a difficult task he faltered, but he was able to think calmly. At the end he still had time to read through his answers.

The result: eleven points. Luis couldn't believe it. He had learned less during the night and achieved more. Since then, the night before an exam has been a night of sleep for him. He's still a night owl, but one who knows when it's over.

## What you can take from it

Sleep is not a luxury, but a foundation for good learning. Those who don't sleep enough have difficulty concentrating, make more mistakes and have a harder time retaining what they've learned. Especially before exams, the temptation to study through the night is great. The better strategy is almost always to start earlier and get enough sleep.

### Why sleep helps with learning

Sleep research shows that sleep plays an important role in memory. To put it simply:

- **What you have learned is consolidated while you sleep.** New content is processed and linked to existing knowledge.
- **You learn better when you're well rested.** Concentration, attention and the ability to absorb new things depend heavily on sleep.
- **Fatigue causes errors.** Anyone who is overtired reads less accurately, calculates more incorrectly and reacts more slowly.
- **Mood and stress depend on sleep.** Lack of sleep makes you more irritable and prone to exam anxiety.

### How much sleep do teenagers need?

Specialist societies usually recommend eight to ten hours of sleep per night for young people between the ages of 13 and 18. Many young people get significantly less in everyday school life. In addition, the internal clock shifts backwards during puberty: many people get tired later in the evening and have difficulty getting out of bed in the morning. This isn't laziness, it's biology. This makes it all the more important to consciously make sure you get enough sleep.

### The night before the exam

If you can't do everything the evening before an exam, a night of studying won't usually save you. Better:

1. **Stop studying on time**, about one to two hours before bed.
2. **Just repeat lightly**, for example reading through a learning sheet.
3. **Pack your bag** so you don't forget anything in the morning.
4. **Do something relaxing**: read, listen to music, take a short walk.
5. **Go to bed at your usual time.**

### Fall asleep better

Many sleep problems are related to evening habits. These tips help many:

| habit | What helps |
|---|---|
| Cell phone in bed | Charge your cell phone outside the room, simple alarm clock |
| Caffeine in the afternoon or evening | Coffee, cola and energy drinks only in the morning |
| Study until just before bed | a buffer time of at least half an hour |
| Irregular times | If possible, go to bed and get up in a similar way every day |
| circles of thought | Write thoughts and tasks for tomorrow on a piece of paper |
| Bright light in the evening | Dimmed lights, turn off screens in good time |

### Energy drinks and caffeine

Many students drink energy drinks or lots of coffee during exam periods. Caffeine can make you awake for a short period of time, but it disrupts sleep if consumed too late or in large quantities. Energy drinks often contain a lot of caffeine and sugar and are not recommended by experts for children and adolescents. Water, enough sleep and exercise are better awakeners.

### Coordinate learning times and sleep rhythms

If you're a night owl, you don't have to completely change your routine. But you can use it sensibly:

- **Difficult tasks** when you are most awake.
- **Set a fixed learning end time** after which no more learning will take place.
- **Don't sleep in too late on the weekend** so that your rhythm doesn't get completely disrupted.
- **Include short breaks** when studying to maintain concentration. More about this in [Marie's story about study breaks](/lerngeschichten/lernpausen-richtig-planen/).

### When sleep is consistently poor

Occasional bad nights before exams are normal. However, if you have trouble falling asleep for weeks, wake up often at night, are constantly tired during the day or your sleep is causing you a lot of stress, talk to your parents and get advice from a doctor's office. Exam anxiety can also disrupt sleep. [Yusuf's story about exam anxiety](/lerngeschichten/pruefungsangst-bewaeltigen/) shows what helps.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Study the night before the exam.** This usually costs more than it brings.
- **Energy drinks as a learning aid.** They disrupt sleep and do not solve the actual problem.
- **Cell phone in bed.** News, videos and games keep you awake.
- **Irregular sleeping times.** Constant changes mess up your internal clock.
- **Start studying too late.** If you start early, you don't have to sacrifice the night before the exam.

## Briefly asked

### Can I learn in my sleep if I play audio books?

New, complex content cannot be easily absorbed while sleeping. What your brain does while you sleep is primarily to consolidate what you learned during the day. Audiobooks can help you fall asleep, but they are not a substitute for learning.

### Does a nap help with learning?

A short afternoon nap of around twenty minutes can improve concentration in the afternoon for many people. It usually shouldn't be longer so that you can still fall asleep well in the evening.

### What do I do if I can't fall asleep before an exam?

Don't put any extra pressure on yourself. Lying quietly is also relaxing. If you lie awake for a long time, get up briefly, do something quiet in dim light, and go back to bed when you feel tired. A single restless night doesn't ruin good preparation.

### Should I catch up on sleep on the weekend?

Sleeping a little longer on the weekend can make up for a small deficit. But if you sleep very little during the week and stay in bed until midday on the weekend, your internal clock will shift further backwards and Monday morning will be even harder. It is better to go to sleep a little earlier during the week and to sleep a maximum of one to two hours longer on the weekend.
