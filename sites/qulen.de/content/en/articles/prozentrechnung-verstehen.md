"A jacket costs 80 euros. In the sales it is reduced by 25 percent. How much does it cost now?" Finja did the math: 80 times 25 divided by 100 equals 20. She wrote: “The jacket now costs 20 euros.” Her friend Merle looked over her shoulder and laughed. "A quarter less, and then it only costs a quarter? That would be a bargain." Finja blushed. She had calculated the discount, not the new price. Again. It was like magic. With each percentage problem, she didn't know exactly what she was supposed to calculate.

## Three terms, one mess

Finja was thirteen and was in the seventh grade of a comprehensive school. Percentage calculation was the new topic, and Mr. Wolff had written three terms on the board: basic value, percentage value, percentage. The formula: Percentage equals basic value times percentage divided by one hundred.

Finja had copied the formula and memorized it. But in the tasks she never knew which number was which term. Was 80 euros the basic value or the percentage value? And what was wanted? She somehow plugged the numbers into the formula and hoped for the best. Sometimes it was true, often not.

In the percentage calculation test she got a four minus. Mr. Wolff wrote underneath: “You often confuse what is given and what is wanted.” Finja knew that herself. She just didn't know how to change it.

## One cake and a hundred pieces

Finja's grandma, who used to work in a bank, noticed the math problems on the table at the weekend. She sat down next to Finja and asked: “What does ‘percent’ actually mean?”

Finja shrugged her shoulders. “Something about a hundred.”

"Exactly. 'Per cent' means 'out of a hundred'. Imagine a cake that you cut into a hundred equal pieces. One percent is one piece. 25 percent is 25 pieces, a quarter of the cake."

Then she asked: “And all the cake, they always are?”

“One hundred percent.”

"Right. And the whole pie is the base value. What you start from. For your jacket, that's the original price, 80 euros. The percentage tells you how many pieces you take, so 25 out of 100. And the percentage is how much those pieces are worth, so 20 euros."

Finja looked at her task. The discount was 20 euros. These were the 25 pieces that were taken away. But the question was what was left. 75 pieces. 80 minus 20, i.e. 60 euros.

## First ask, then calculate

Grandma Gerda gave her a habit that Finja applied to every percentage task from then on. Before she calculated, she wrote down three lines: G equals, p equals, W equals. After each line she entered either a number from the task or a question mark.

“How do you recognize the basic value?” Grandma asked. "It is the whole, the 100 percent. It often comes after the word 'of'. 25 percent of 80 euros. The 'of' points to the basic value."

Finja practiced this on ten tasks from the book. For some the basic value was sought, for others the percentage. With the three lines she immediately saw what was missing. Then she used the rule of three that she already knew, or the appropriate change in the formula.

## The rule of three as a friend

In the next lesson, Mr. Wolff showed the class that every percentage problem can also be solved using the rule of three, without a formula. Finja liked that. She knew the rule of three assignment tasks and felt more confident with it.

For the jacket it looked like this: 100 percent is 80 euros. 1 percent is 0.80 euros. 75 percent is 75 times 0.80, i.e. 60 euros. The new price. And the nice thing was: With the rule of three, she didn't have to think about how to change the formula. She just needed to know what the 100 percent was.

Finja also discovered a shortcut: a 25 percent reduction leaves 75 percent remaining. So she was able to calculate 80 times 0.75 directly. This also resulted in 60. Mr. Wolff called this the growth or decrease factor.

## The next test

In the next test on percentage calculation, Finja first wrote the three lines G, p, W for each task. In a task about a salary increase, she immediately recognized that the old salary was the basic value. In another task, in which the price was given after an increase and the old price was sought, she hesitated for a moment. Then she remembered: The new price is 100 plus the increase, for example 110 percent. And the old price sought was the basic value.

The test got a two. Finja showed it to Grandma Gerda, who smiled proudly and said: “Now you can check when you go shopping to see whether the discounts are right.” Finja actually did that the next time she shopped in the sales. And it was true.

## What you can take from it

Percentage calculation is essentially simple: it is always a proportion of a whole. It becomes difficult when you don't know which number plays which role. Anyone who first clarifies what is given and what is needed can solve most tasks with little effort.

### The three basic concepts

| term | Abbreviation | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core value | G | the whole, corresponds to 100% | Original price €80 |
| Percentage | p% | the share in percent | 25 % |
| Percentage | W | the proportion as a size | €20 discount |

A helpful reminder: The percentage value is the percentage of the basic value. “25 percent of 80 euros is 20 euros.” The word “of” often points to the core value.

### First organize, then calculate

For each percentage problem, first write down:

1. **G =** What is the whole thing, the 100 percent?
2. **p % =** What percentage is given or sought?
3. **W =** What proportion is given or sought?

Enter the numbers from the task and put a question mark after what you are looking for. That's half the job done.

### Solve with the rule of three

With the rule of three you can solve any basic problem without changing the formula:

- **Percentage wanted:** 100% is €80. 1% is €0.80. 25% is €20.
- **Percentage wanted:** €80 is 100%. €1 is 1.25%. €20 is 25%.
- **Basic value wanted:** 25% is €20. 1% is €0.80. 100% is €80.

The rule of three works because percentage calculation is a proportional assignment: twice as many percents mean twice as large a share.

### Solve with the formula

If you prefer to work with formulas, there are three variants:

- **Percentage:** W = G p / 100
- **Basic value:** G = W · 100 / p
- **Percentage:** p = W · 100 / G

All three are conversions of the same formula. If you only want to remember one, take the first one and rearrange it as needed.

### Discounts and price increases

For changes by a percentage, the factor helps:

| situation | What is left or added | factor |
|---|---|---|
| 25% discount | 75% of the price | 0,75 |
| 10% discount | 90% of the price | 0,90 |
| Add 19% VAT | 119% of the net price | 1,19 |
| 5% salary increase | 105% of the old salary | 1,05 |

This is how you calculate the new value in one step: new price = old price · factor. And vice versa: old price = new price : factor.

**Caution:** If a price first increases by 10 percent and then decreases by 10 percent, you will not arrive at the starting price. €100 · 1.10 = €110, then €110 · 0.90 = €99. The reason: The second percentage refers to a different basic value.

### Percentage calculation in everyday life

You come across percentages all the time:

- **Discounts** in final sales.
- **VAT** on invoices.
- **Interest** on savings accounts or loans.
- **Statistics** in news, such as election results or surveys.
- **Battery level** on the phone.

If you understand percentages, you can compare offers, classify news better and protect yourself from misleading numbers. So practice in everyday life: How much do I really save? ## Typical pitfalls

- **Discount instead of new price.** Read carefully whether the question is about the deduction or the final price.
- **Use the wrong base value.** The base value is always the initial value to which the percentage refers.
- **Confuse “at” and “up”.** “Decreased by 20%” means something different than “decreased to 20%”.
- **Simply add the percentages.** Two 10% discounts in a row are not 20%, but 19%.
- **Calculate without any calculation.** A quick calculation shows whether the result is plausible.

## Briefly asked

### What is the difference between percent and percentage points?

Percentage points describe the difference between two percentages. If voter turnout increases from 60% to 66%, it has increased by 6 percentage points, but by 10 percent relative to the initial value of 60%.

### Can I also calculate percentages as fractions?

Yes. 50% is a half, 25% is a quarter, 20% is a fifth, 10% is a tenth. With such percentages, calculating with fractions is often faster than with the formula.

### How do I remember which formula I need?

The easiest way is to use just one formula or the rule of three. More important than the formula is that you know which number is the basic value. Then the rest almost falls into place.
