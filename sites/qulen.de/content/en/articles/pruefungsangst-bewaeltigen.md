It was just after three in the morning and Yusuf lay awake. He went through the binomial formulas in his head. The first came immediately. He faltered on the second one, and everything else slipped into this little hole: the comma rules, the English tenses, the poetry analysis. “I’m forgetting everything,” he thought. “I’m sitting there and it’s all gone.” There were still six weeks until the first final exam.

## Nights with your eyes open

Yusuf was sixteen and in the tenth grade of a secondary school. He wasn't a high flyer, but he was solid: twos and threes, sometimes better in English. It had started with the test exam in math. During a task on probability, he couldn't think of anything for several minutes, his heart was pounding, and the sentence “Here we go, now you're failing” was stuck in his head. The fact that he continued to calculate and solved most of the problems was somehow lost.

Since then he hasn't slept well. He could barely get anything down in the morning and felt queasy on the bus. But the strangest thing was: the closer the exams got, the less he learned. As soon as he opened the math book, his shoulders hunched and he closed it again after five minutes. Better not to look.

Daria from his class noticed it first. “You’re totally through it,” she said during the break. "Go to Ms. Albers. She has office hours on Thursdays in the small room next to the library." Yusuf waved his hand. On Thursday he was still at the door.

## A conversation in a small room

Ms. Albers, a guidance counselor and actually responsible for German and history, initially just listened. Then she said something Yusuf hadn't expected: "Excitement before exams is normal. A little of it even helps because you're awake and focused. The only question is whether it drives you or whether it paralyzes you." With Yusuf it was the latter, they both saw that.

She asked about his body. Heart pounding, shallow breathing, a knot in my stomach, shoulders hunched. “This is your alarm system,” she said. "It prepares you for danger. It means well, it just exaggerates." Yusuf found the thought strangely comforting.

Then she had him write down the sentence that kept him up at night: “I forget everything in the exam.” Together they tested it like an assertion in a discussion. What spoke in favor of it? One task in the mock exam. What spoke against it? That he continued after that. That in the school year's work he almost always knew more than he previously thought. At the end there was a new sentence on the note: "I'll be excited. Maybe I'll get stuck on one task. Then I'll move on to the next one, and I'll know a lot of things."

## Practice until you become comfortable with the test

Ms. Albers suggested an experiment: test runs under real conditions. Mr. Petersen, his math teacher, gave him problems from previous years. On Saturday morning at nine, the same time as the real exam, Yusuf sat down at the desk, put only the permitted tools, the cell phone in the next room, and set an alarm clock.

The first run was unpleasant. In the first ten minutes his heart raced and he had to read from the beginning twice. Then something happened that he remembered: as soon as he started a task he could do, it got quieter. On the second run a week later the wave came again, but shorter. By the third time he knew she was passing.

To do this, he practiced a simple breathing exercise that Ms. Albers had shown him for two minutes every evening and placed a small notebook next to the bed. He wrote down what was going through his head before he fell asleep: “Probably again with Mr. Petersen,” “Comma rules on Monday.” The worries didn't go away, but they had a place until the next day. Sleep didn't get good right away, but it got better. Ms. Albers had advised him to go to his family doctor if he was still barely sleeping after two weeks. It didn't get that far, but he knew this path existed.

On the morning of the first exam, his hands were cold. He breathed a few times the way he had practiced: in slowly, and out even more slowly. Then he read through all the tasks once and started with one that he was sure he could do. At another he hung briefly, marked her and came back later. Yusuf passed all three tests. The fear hadn't gone away. She was just no longer behind the wheel.

## What you can take from it

Exam anxiety can rarely be easily switched off. But you can learn to understand her, give her less food and prepare yourself so that she no longer directs you. The following steps are intended for the weeks before an exam. [Elias' story about the blackout](/lerngeschichten/blackout-in-der-pruefung/) tells you what you can do if everything suddenly disappears in the middle of the exam.

### Normal excitement or paralyzing fear?

The first step is an honest assessment. This comparison does not replace a diagnosis, but it will help you take a look:

| Normal excitement | Fear that paralyzes you |
|---|---|
| Especially right before the exam | It's there almost every day for weeks |
| Sleep is sometimes restless | You sleep poorly for a long time |
| You still keep learning | You avoid studying because just the thought of it scares you |
| It gets quieter as soon as you start | It also blocks you from practicing |
| “Hopefully it works.” | “I’m sure to fail and then it’s all over.” |

If you find yourself mostly in the right column, it's not a sign of weakness, but a good reason to get support. Where you can find them is below.

### Understanding body signals

Heart palpitations, rapid breathing, sweaty hands, a queasy feeling in the stomach: this is how the body reacts to stress. He goes into a kind of alert mode. This is unpleasant, but a normal reaction. It is helpful to know your own early signs. With Yusuf it was the hunched shoulders. If you notice your signal early, you can take countermeasures before the wave gets big.

If you are unsure whether your symptoms are really caused by excitement, or if they are severe and occur frequently, have them checked out at a GP practice. This is not an exaggeration, but rather sensible.

### Examine and rephrase catastrophic thoughts

Fear likes to tell stories with the worst ending. This is how you take away their power:

1. **Write the thought down verbatim.** A sentence written on paper seems smaller than one that circles around in your head at night.
2. **Examine it as a claim.** What's in favor of it and what's against it? What really happened in previous work? What would you say to a friend in the same situation?
3. **Rephrase it realistically.** Not overly positive, but in a way that you can believe.
4. **Clarify the real consequences.** Ask your teacher what your final grade is made up of and what happens if an exam goes worse. The situation is usually less dramatic than your head imagines at night.

| Catastrophic thought | Realistic sentence |
|---|---|
| “I forget everything.” | “I’m excited, but I still know a lot.” |
| “Everyone else is relaxed.” | “Many people are nervous, you just can’t tell by looking at them.” |
| “If this goes wrong, my future will be ruined.” | “A test is important, but it is not my whole life.” |
| “I definitely can’t get nervous.” | "It's okay to be nervous. I can still work." |

### Preparation and test runs as a brake on fear

Fear grows where there is uncertainty. Good preparation is therefore perhaps the most effective counter-strategy. Spread the material out over the weeks, as [Greta's story about the learning plan](/lerngeschichten/lernplan-fuer-klassenarbeiten/) shows, instead of packing everything into the last few days. And make the exam situation familiar:

- **Ask for practice materials.** Teachers know where to find assignments from previous years or official sample assignments.
- **Simulate the real exam.** Same time, alarm clock, only the permitted aids, cell phone out of reach, and only then look at the solutions.
- **Repeat the run.** The second and third time you often notice that the tension eases sooner.
- **Create a hang-up plan.** For example: “If I get stuck on a task, I mark it and move on to the next one.”
- **Give sleep a chance.** Fixed times, no studying until the last minute in the evening, and a notebook next to the bed in which worries can wait until tomorrow.

[Elif's plan for the last 24 hours](/lerngeschichten/tag-vor-der-pruefung/) shows how you plan the day right before the exam.

### Simple breathing and grounding exercises

These exercises are not a magic trick. They help you get out of the carousel of thoughts and back into the room. They work best if you have practiced them in quiet moments beforehand.

- **Exhale longer than you inhale.** Breathe in through your nose and slowly count to four. Breathe out through your mouth and count to six. Repeat this five times. If you feel dizzy, just breathe normally again.
- **5-4-3-2-1.** Mentally name five things you see, four you hear, three you feel, two you smell, and one you taste. A short version is sufficient for the exam.
- **Floor under your feet.** Press both feet firmly on the floor, place your hands on the table and feel the pen between your fingers.

### When and where you can get help

Get support if fear dominates your everyday life for weeks, you hardly sleep or eat, have severe anxiety attacks with a racing heart or would prefer not to go to school at all. Good places to start are:

- **Trusted Source Teacher:** knows your school and will treat your concerns confidentially.
- **School social work:** in many schools there is pressure, worry and conflict.
- **School psychology:** The school psychology counseling centers also support students with test anxiety. You can find out how to contact them at the office or at school social work.
- **General practice:** if sleep problems or physical complaints persist. There they can rule out physical causes and refer you if necessary.
- **Number against grief:** anonymous advice for children and young people on the phone and online.

You can find the contact details on our [Help & Advice](/hilfe-und-beratung/) page. If you're stuck or thinking about harming yourself, don't wait: talk to an adult you trust right away or use the [Emergency Contacts](/hilfe-und-beratung/#im-notfall).

## Typical pitfalls

- **Avoid studying.** Looking away feels better in the short term, but the anxiety grows every day you don't look.
- **Fight against fear.** The sentence “I can't be nervous” usually makes you more nervous. Accepting and starting anyway works better.
- **Study through the night.** Fatigue increases tension. When you're well rested, you'll gain more of what you've learned.
- **Wanting to do everything alone.** If you talk to someone early on, you won't have to spend so much time later.

## Briefly asked

### Will exam anxiety ever go away completely?

For many people it gets smaller with every test they pass. There is often some residual excitement, and that's okay. The goal is not zero fear, but rather a fear that you can work with.

### Should I tell my teachers about this?

Often yes. Many teachers can explain to you how the exam works, and that alone takes away the uncertainty. You decide who you confide in.

### Do sedatives help?

Do not take any medication or medication on your own, or from friends. If you feel like you can't get through the exam period without help, talk to your family doctor.
