Lea typed the numbers into the calculator: 48.5 times 19.8. 9603 appeared on the display. She wrote the result in her notebook, underlined it twice and moved on to the next task. It was only when Mr. Becker compared the homework that it became apparent. “Lea, how much is about 50 times 20?” Lea thought for a moment. "Thousand." “And your result?” Lea looked into her notebook. 9603. Almost ten times as much. She forgot the comma when typing and entered 485 instead of 48.5. And she hadn't noticed.

## Trust in the machine

Lea was fourteen and in the eighth grade of a secondary school. Since seventh grade, she was allowed to use a calculator in math class, and since then she has typed almost every calculation, even simple ones. She completely trusted the device. What was on the display was correct.

The problem was that the calculator only calculated as well as the numbers she typed in. A forgotten comma, a swapped digit, a wrong key, and the result was nonsense. Lea didn't notice because she had no idea how big the result would have to be.

In her last class test she lost points in three tasks because her results were completely unrealistic. A bike that weighed 150 kilograms. An apartment with 8,500 square feet. A distance to school of 0.02 kilometers. Every single invoice was basically correct, but something had gone wrong somewhere during the typing process.

## The two-second rule

Mr. Becker gave Lea a tip after the lesson. "I call it the two-second rule. Before you type a calculation into the calculator, you estimate the result in your head in two seconds. Using round numbers. Then you compare. If the estimate and the result are far apart, something is wrong."

He explained to her how it works. "From 48.5 you make 50, from 19.8 you make 20. 50 times 20 is 1000. So the real answer has to be close to a thousand. If your calculator shows 9603, you know immediately that you typed something wrong."

Lea found this annoying at first. It took time, and she wasn't particularly good at mental arithmetic. But Mr. Becker insisted that she try it on every homework assignment for two weeks.

## Practice mental arithmetic

During her first attempts, Lea noticed that her mental arithmetic was rusty. Even 7 times 8 took a moment. She decided to train that. Every morning at breakfast, she practiced for five minutes using a mental arithmetic app her teacher recommended. The small multiplication tables became fluent again, then calculations with tens.

She also learned tricks. Times 5 is the same as times 10 and then half. Multiply 25 times 100 and then divide it by 4. Percent of round numbers she soon realized in her head: 10 percent of 80 is 8, so 20 percent is 16.

And she practiced rounding numbers sensibly. Not too rough, but rough enough to do the math in your head. 387 became 400, 0.48 became 0.5, 12.9 became 13.

## Orders of magnitude in everyday life

Mr. Becker gave the whole class another idea. In an hour he had them estimate: How heavy is a bicycle? How big is our classroom? How many liters does a bathtub hold? How long is the way to school? The estimates were sometimes far apart. Then they measured where possible or did research.

Lea realized that she had no idea about many things. A classroom was about 60 square meters, not 8,500. A bicycle weighed about 10 to 20 kilograms, not 150. She began to memorize a few reference values. One door is about two meters high. One liter of water weighs about one kilogram. A football field is approximately one hectare.

## The next job

In the next math work on areas and volumes, Lea first wrote an outline in the margin of each problem. When doing a task about an aquarium, she came up with 1,440 liters using the calculator. Her rollover said about 140. She checked her calculation and discovered that she had mixed up centimeters and decimeters. She corrected the result to 144 liters.

There was a two under the work. And Mr. Becker had written in the margin: “Great, your rollovers!” From then on, Lea also used the two-second rule outside of school, when shopping, when calculating her pocket money and when she was discussing the costs of a family vacation with her father.

## What you can take from it

The calculator is an excellent tool, but it doesn't think for itself. If you estimate beforehand, you will immediately recognize typos, unit errors and incorrect approaches. Rough calculations and solid mental arithmetic are therefore not old-fashioned exercises, but rather important control instruments.

### What is a rollover?

With a rollover, you round the numbers in a calculation so that you can easily calculate them in your head. The result is not exact but gives you the order of magnitude. This is how you do it:

1. **Round numbers.** Round to a place you can easily calculate, such as tens, hundreds, or a round decimal number.
2. **Calculate in your head.** Do the calculation with the rounded numbers.
3. **Calculate exactly.** Calculate the task exactly, in writing or with the calculator.
4. **Compare.** Are the rollover and result close to each other? If not, check your bill.

### Examples of rollovers

| task | rollover | Accurate result |
|---|---|---|
| 48,5 · 19,8 | 50 · 20 = 1000 | 960,3 |
| 387 + 512 + 96 | 400 + 500 + 100 = 1000 | 995 |
| 7.920 : 39 | 8.000 : 40 = 200 | about 203.1 |
| 0,48 · 62 | 0,5 · 60 = 30 | 29,76 |
| 18% of €245 | 20% of €250 = €50 | 44,10 € |

Important: When rounding, the rollover may differ slightly from the exact result. That's normal. It's just a matter of detecting large deviations like a factor of 10 or 100.

### Train mental arithmetic

Confident mental arithmetic makes rollovers quick and effortless. These areas are particularly worthwhile:

- **The small multiplication table** should sit without thinking.
- **Calculating with tens**, such as 30 x 40 or 600:20.
- **Halving and doubling**, for example for bills of 5, 25 or 50.
- **Simple percentages**: 10%, 25%, 50% of round numbers.
- **Simple fractions**: one half, one quarter, one third of known quantities.

Five minutes a day is enough to noticeably improve. Mental arithmetic apps, games with dice or cards or arithmetic in everyday life while shopping are good ways to practice.

### Useful mental arithmetic tricks

- **Times 5:** Times 10 and then halves. 48 5 = 480 : 2 = 240.
- **Times 25:** times 100 and then by 4. 36 · 25 = 3600 : 4 = 900.
- **Times 9:** times 10 and then subtracts the number once. 7 9 = 70 − 7 = 63.
- **Times 11 for two-digit numbers:** Add the digits together and write them in the middle. 43 · 11 = 4 (4+3) 3 = 473 as long as the sum of the two digits is less than 10.
- **Divide:** 6 47 = 6 40 + 6 7 = 240 + 42 = 282.

### Estimate magnitudes

Many mistakes are noticeable when you have an idea of how big typical things are. A few reference values will help:

- An adult is around 1.5 to 2 meters tall.
- A door is about 2 meters high and almost 1 meter wide.
- One liter of water weighs about 1 kilogram.
- A classroom has around 50 to 70 square meters.
- A football field is approximately one hectare, or 10,000 square meters.
- You can walk about 4 to 5 kilometers per hour.

If your result deviates far from such values, it's worth taking a second look. This is particularly helpful with word problems, as [Sara's story about word problems](/lerngeschichten/textaufgaben-in-mathe-loesen/) shows.

### Keep an eye on units

Many large deviations arise from confused units. Therefore, always check whether all sizes are in the same unit before you calculate. Caution is particularly advised when it comes to areas and volumes: one square meter is 10,000 square centimeters, one cubic decimeter is one liter. If such conversions keep tripping you up, record the errors in your error book, like in [Fynn's story about the error book](/lerngeschichten/mathe-ueben-mit-fehlerheft/).

### Rollover in the exam

The rollover is particularly worthwhile in class tests and exams. It only takes a few seconds and can save a lot of points. Write it briefly in the margin or under the task. Some teachers even specifically require it. And if you still have time at the end, check your results a second time with a quick check.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Trust the calculator blindly.** It only calculates as well as you type.
- **Round too roughly.** If you round 14 to 0, the rollover will no longer help.
- **Round too precisely.** If you can't do the math in your head, the rollover is too complicated.
- **Units overlooked.** Centimeters, meters and kilometers cause errors by a factor of 100 or 1000.
- **Only do the rollover for difficult tasks.** Typing errors often occur, especially with simple tasks.

## Briefly asked

### Is mental arithmetic still worth it when calculators exist?

Yes. Mental arithmetic helps you to check results, calculate quickly in everyday life and develop a feeling for numbers. In addition, many exams have parts without a calculator.

### How accurate does a rollover have to be?

It just needs to be accurate enough for you to catch big mistakes. If your rollover is 1000 and the exact answer is 960, that's fine. If the exact answer is 9600 or 96, something is wrong.
