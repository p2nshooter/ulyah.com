It was Thursday, 4 p.m., and the study group met at Noah's house like every week. Five people from the tenth grade, chips on the table, physics books open. After ten minutes everyone was talking about the weekend football game. After twenty minutes, Emma showed a video on her phone. After an hour they had calculated a single problem and it was wrong. When the others had left, Noah looked at the mess on the table and thought: This was the third week in a row. And in twelve days we will write the physics paper.

## Good intention, little result

Noah was sixteen and in the tenth grade of a comprehensive school. The idea for the study group came from him. Many people in the class had done poorly on the last physics test, and Noah had thought that together they could do better. Five friends agreed immediately.

The first meetings were fun. But they were more like afternoons with friends than study sessions. Nobody prepared anything. Nobody knew exactly what was supposed to be done that day. When someone had a question, everyone tried to answer at the same time, or no one knew the answer. And because the atmosphere was so good, no one wanted to be a spoilsport by calling for people to learn.

Noah realized that after the meetings at home he had to learn everything again on his own. The study group cost him time instead of saving it.

## A look at another group

Noah's cousin Amira was studying medicine and talked about her study group at a family dinner. Four people who had met twice a week since the first semester. “I would never have done anatomy without them,” she said.

Noah asked how they did it. Amira laughed. "We just chatted at the beginning. Then we made rules." She counted on her fingers. "Every meeting has a clear topic. Each person prepares a part and explains it to the others. We start on time, study in concentration for ninety minutes, and then we are allowed to chat as much as we want. Cell phones are in a box during the study time."

“What if someone doesn’t prepare?”

"Then we'll say it politely but clearly. If you come unprepared twice, you'll have to bring cake with you next time." Amira grinned. “Works amazingly well.”

## The restart

At the next meeting, Noah put a piece of paper on the table. "I wrote down a few rules. Say what you think about them." The others looked a little skeptical, but they were reading.

The rules were simple. Meetings on Tuesday and Thursday, ninety minutes each. Each meeting has a topic from physics class. Two people prepare the topic and explain it to the others, with a self-calculation task. Cell phones in the box. The last ten minutes: What have we achieved, what will happen next time? And then time for chips and conversation.

Emma thought the cell phone box was overkill, but after a brief discussion everyone agreed to try it out for two weeks. They divided the topics up to the physics work: electrical power, resistance, series and parallel connections, energy conversion.

## To explain is to understand

At the first real meeting, Noah and Lina explained series and parallel connections. It had taken Noah almost an hour to prepare the day before. He had read the topic in the book, done a few calculations and drawn a sketch on the blackboard that his mother had brought him from the basement.

As he explained, Noah realized where he was still unsure. When Tim asked why the total resistance becomes smaller when connected in parallel, Noah paused. Together they looked for the answer in the book, and Lina found a picture of water pipes that made it clear. In the end everyone, including Noah, understood it better than before.

The others received a worksheet with three tasks. Whoever was finished helped the others. After ninety minutes they had worked through an entire topic. In the last ten minutes they noted what remained unclear and wrote down these questions for the physics teacher.

## The physics work

They met three more times before work. Not every meeting went perfectly. One time Tim didn't prepare and brought a marble cake with him on Thursday. Once the discussion about one task took so long that they had to postpone the second topic. But overall they made progress.

In the physics paper, the group did significantly better than the last. Noah got a two, Emma, ​​who previously had a five, got a three. After the return, Ms. Schröder, the physics teacher, asked what they had done differently. Noah talked about the study group. Ms. Schröder nodded and said she would like to hang up the piece of paper with the rules in the classroom.

## What you can take from it

A study group can be a great help: you explain things to each other, supplement your knowledge, motivate yourself and notice that others have the same questions. To prevent a learning group from becoming just a chat group, it needs structure, clear agreements and a common goal.

### The right group size and composition

Three to five people are ideal for most study groups. Fewer people bring too few different perspectives, more people make coordination difficult and invite digression. Pay attention to the composition:

- **Common Goal:** Everyone should prepare the same exam or subject.
- **Similar seriousness:** When some want to learn and others are just looking for company, frustration arises.
- **Mixed Strengths:** It helps if not everyone is unsure about the same topics.
- **Friendship is not a must:** It's sometimes harder to learn with good friends because chatting is so close.

### Rules that work

Clear agreements avoid conflicts. These rules have proven themselves in many learning groups:

| Rule | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| Fixed dates and fixed duration | makes the meetings binding and plannable |
| Every meeting has a topic | prevents you from scrolling around aimlessly |
| Each person prepares something | distributes the work and ensures commitment |
| Start on time | shows respect for other people's time |
| Cell phones away during study time | prevents constant distraction |
| Chatting after study time | gives the social its place without disrupting learning |

Write down your rules together. Rules that everyone has a say in are more likely to be followed.

### Structuring a meeting

A meeting of around ninety minutes might look like this:

1. **Arrival and overview (5 minutes):** What is the topic today? Who explains what?
2. **Explain (25 minutes):** The people preparing the topic explain the topic with examples and sketches.
3. **Clarify questions (10 minutes):** Everyone asks questions, the group looks for answers together.
4. **Practice yourself (30 minutes):** Everyone solves tasks, whoever finishes helps the others.
5. **Queries (10 minutes):** You quiz each other, for example with index cards.
6. **Review and Planning (10 minutes):** What did we accomplish? What remains open? Who will prepare what next time?

Plan to take a short break after about 45 minutes. [Marie's story about learning breaks](/lerngeschichten/lernpausen-richtig-planen/) shows how you can make breaks meaningful.

### Why explaining is so effective

Anyone who explains something to someone else has to organize the topic themselves, understand it and put it into their own words. Gaps are immediately noticeable. That's why the person explaining often learns the most. So take turns so that each person regularly takes on the role of explainer.

Questions from listeners are helpful: “Why is that like that?” “Can you give an example?” "What if …?" Such questions make everyone think.

### Distribute roles

In larger or longer learning groups, fixed roles that change regularly help:

- **Moderation:** pays attention to the time and that everyone has a chance to speak.
- **Experts:** prepare and explain the topic.
- **Question collector:** notes open questions for the teacher.
- **Protocol:** records what was discussed in a few bullet points.

### Online learning groups

If you don't live in the same place or appointments are difficult to find, you can also meet via video call. The rules remain the same, but some things need special attention: cameras on so everyone is involved, a shared digital whiteboard or shared documents for sketches, and clear facilitation so everyone doesn't talk at the same time.

### Typical conflicts and solutions

Even in good learning groups there are sometimes tensions:

- **Someone never prepares.** Be open about it. A friendly but clear rule helps, such as the cake rule.
- **One person is constantly talking.** The moderator makes sure that everyone has their say.
- **The level varies greatly.** The stronger ones explain, the others practice. Both benefit.
- **The group constantly wanders.** A timer and visible schedule help you stay on track.
- **Appointments don't work.** A shared calendar and a fixed day of the week create reliability.

### Study group and own learning

A study group does not replace your own learning. She complements it. Plan time aside from meetings to review and practice alone. ## Typical pitfalls

- **No preparation.** Without prepared content, the study group quickly turns into a chat circle.
- **Group too large.** If there are six people or more it often becomes confusing.
- **No time structure.** Without a fixed routine, time flies.
- **Want to do everything together.** Some things, like learning vocabulary, are better done alone.
- **Ignore conflicts.** Unspoken anger destroys the group in the long term.

## Briefly asked

### How often should a study group meet?

Once or twice a week is enough for most groups. Additional meetings may be useful before important exams. Regularity is more important than frequency.

### What if I'm always the one in the group who explains everything?

It can be tiring, but you benefit from it because explaining consolidates your own knowledge. Speak up anyway if you feel like you're being taken advantage of and make sure to switch roles.

### Can a learning group also work with two people?

Yes, a learning partner can be very helpful, especially for questioning each other. Sometimes different perspectives are missing when there are two people, but coordination is easier.
