Kemal clicked on the next slide. Like all the others, it was full to the brim with text. Twelve bullet points, each almost a whole sentence, in small font so that everything fit. Kemal read the bullet points one by one. After the third slide, he noticed someone in the second row yawning. In the back row, someone was typing on their cell phone under the table. And Ms. Wagner, the teacher, had stopped looking at him and was reading along silently instead. Kemal spoke faster. He just wanted to finish.

## Hardworking but boring

Kemal was fifteen and in the ninth grade of a community school. He had done a lot of research for his presentation on renewable energy in physics, more than most in his class. He wanted to show how much he knew. So he had packed everything into the slides: definitions, numbers, explanations, advantages and disadvantages of wind, sun, water and biomass.

Fifteen slides, each full of text. He'd been proud of it when he'd clicked through it at home. But in the classroom he noticed that something was wrong. The class could not read and listen at the same time. And because Kemal read what was on the slides anyway, many seemed to think they could read it themselves. Or not at all.

After the lesson he got a C plus. “The content is very well-founded,” said Ms. Wagner, “but your slides stole the show.” Kemal didn't quite understand what she meant.

## The foil as a stage set

Ms. Wagner took a few minutes during the next break. She opened Kemal's presentation on her laptop and pointed to a slide with twelve bullet points about wind energy. “What do you want the class to see on this slide?”

Kemal thought about it. “Everything about wind energy?”

“And what should you say?”

“What’s on the slide.”

Ms. Wagner smiled. "Then you don't need it at all. Imagine you're an actor in the theater. The foil is the stage set. It helps the audience to imagine something. But you tell the story. If the entire script is already written on the stage set, no one looks at the actor anymore."

She showed him how she would create a slide about wind energy herself: a large photo of a wind farm on the sea, with a single short sentence above it. And next to it perhaps a single number, large and clear. “You tell me everything else.”

## One slide, one message

For his next presentation, this time in geography about water scarcity, Kemal tried something different. Before he even opened a slide, he wrote what he wanted to say on index cards. Only when the lecture was finished did he think about each section: What should the audience see so that they can better understand what I'm saying?

He made a rule for himself: one slide, one message. Each slide was given a heading that contained the core message. Not “water consumption”, but “thousands of liters of water are needed to make one t-shirt”. This includes a picture, a simple diagram or a maximum of three short keywords.

Looking at the numbers, he realized how much of a difference a good diagram can make. Instead of a table with ten countries and their water consumption, he showed a bar chart with five countries, highlighting Germany in color. He explained the rest verbally.

He also paid attention to readability. Large font, a clean, simple font, dark text on a light background. No playful transitions or flying letters. For each image, he noted the source on the last slide.

## The new lecture

This time, Kemal only had nine slides, and most of them consisted primarily of a picture and a sentence. While practicing in front of his sister, he realized that he could no longer read because there was hardly anything written on the slides. He had to tell the story freely, using his cue cards. That was unusual, but it made his talk more lively.

Something happened in class that surprised Kemal. When he showed the slide with the T-shirt, a murmur went through the class. Someone shouted, “Really now?” And suddenly everyone was listening. He explained how much water is used for cotton, dyeing and processing, and then pointed to the next slide, which showed a dry riverbed.

At the end there were so many questions that Mr. Schulze had to stop the discussion in order to distribute the homework. The rating was a one minus. “Slides provide excellent support for the lecture,” Mr. Schulze noted. Kemal grinned. This time the slides didn't steal the show. They had set the stage for him.

## What you can take from it

Presentation slides are a tool, not a replacement for your presentation. Good slides support what you say: they show pictures, organize numbers, highlight the most important things. Bad slides compete with you for the audience's attention. With a few simple rules, you can design slides that make your presentation stronger.

### First the lecture, then the slides

Many people start with the presentation program and fill slide after slide with content. The reverse order is better:

1. **Determine content and structure.** What is your core message? What sections does your presentation have? How you do this is shown in [Paul's story about his first presentation](/lerngeschichten/referat-vorbereiten/).
2. **Write keyword cards.** Write down what you want to say about each section.
3. **Plan slides.** For each section, think about: What will help the audience understand this? A picture, a diagram, a concept?
4. **Design slides.** Only now open the program.

### One slide, one message

Each slide should convey exactly one idea. A headline that formulates this message as a short statement is helpful:

| Weak headline | Strong headline |
|---|---|
| Wind energy | Wind power supplies a large part of our electricity |
| Water consumption | A T-shirt requires thousands of liters of water |
| Causes | Three reasons led to the First World War |
| Results | Our survey shows: Most people come by bike |

A strong headline immediately tells the audience what it's about, even if they were briefly distracted.

### Little text, large font

Text on slides should be as concise as possible:

- **A maximum of three to five bullet points** per slide, often even less is better.
- **No full sentences** except in the headline or a short quote.
- **Large font:** Headings significantly larger than the rest of the text, and even the smallest text must be readable in the last row.
- **Simple font:** Clear, sans serif fonts are usually easy to read on screens. Playful fonts quickly appear restless.
- **Strong contrast:** Dark font on a light background or vice versa. Avoid text in busy photos.

A simple test: If you can no longer read your slide on your screen from three meters away, the font is too small or the text is too long.

### Pictures that tell something

Pictures often have a stronger effect than words if they are chosen well:

- **Meaningful rather than decorative.** An image should contribute to the content, not just fill the slide.
- **Show big.** It's better to have one big picture than four small ones.
- **Good quality.** Pixelated or distorted images appear unprofessional.
- **Cite sources.** Note where your images come from and use images that you are allowed to use, for example from free image databases or your own photos. If you are unsure, ask your teacher.

### Numbers as a diagram

Tables full of numbers are difficult to capture on slides. Diagrams show at a glance what is important:

- **Bar charts** are suitable for comparisons, for example between countries or groups.
- **Line charts** show developments over time.
- **Pie charts** are only suitable for parts of a whole, and even then only with a few parts.

Show only the values ​​that are important for your statement and highlight the crucial ones in color. Label axes and identify the source of the data.

### Uniform design

A calm, uniform design allows the content to have an impact:

- Use **one template** for all slides.
- Limit yourself to **two or three colors**.
- Avoid **wild transitions and animations**. They are distracting and waste time.
- Make sure that headings are always in **the same place**.

### Film and language in interaction

During the lecture, you speak to the audience, not to the slide. Just look briefly at the screen to check whether the correct slide is running. Explain what is on the slide instead of reading it out loud. When you show a new slide, give the audience a moment to absorb it before continuing. [Dominik's story about speaking freely](/lerngeschichten/frei-sprechen-beim-referat/) shows you how to speak freely without reading.

### Check technology beforehand

Nothing is more annoying than a presentation that doesn't start. Check beforehand whether your file is running on the computer at school, whether fonts and images are displayed correctly and whether you have a replacement, for example the file as a PDF or in a second storage location. And in an emergency, plan to be able to continue speaking without slides. That's what your cue cards are for.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Slides as lecture scripts.** If you write everything on the slide, you just read it.
- **Too many slides.** As a rough guide, one slide per one to two minutes of lecture time is often sufficient.
- **Small font to accommodate more.** Then the text is too long, not the font is too big.
- **Colorful effects instead of content.** Animations and sound effects rarely look professional.
- **Lack of sources.** Teachers expect sources to be cited, especially when it comes to pictures and numbers.

## Briefly asked

### How many slides do I need for a ten minute presentation?

This depends on your content, but six to ten slides including the title and source slide are often enough. More important than the number is that each slide has a clear purpose.

### Can I create slides using AI tools?

Your school or your teacher decides whether and how AI tools are allowed. Ask beforehand. Even if they are allowed, you should check each statement and design the slides so that they fit your presentation.

### What should I do if I am very nervous before the lecture?

Good foils give you support, but practice and a few simple techniques help against nervousness. [Leyla's story about stage fright](/lerngeschichten/lampenfieber-vor-dem-referat/) tells more about this.
