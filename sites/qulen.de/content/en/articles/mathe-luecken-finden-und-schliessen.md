Jannik calculated the problem for the third time. 4(x − 2) − 3 = 2x + 5. Solve the bracket, sort, divide by two: x = 5. At the bottom of the worksheet was the solution for checking, printed upside down: x = 8. He erased until the paper was almost transparent, then he pushed the notebook away so hard that the set square slipped off the desk. “I just don’t check equations,” he wrote to Malik. The answer came after ten seconds: “Welcome to the club.”

## A test full of red squiggles

Jannik was fifteen and in the ninth grade of a secondary school. Math had never been his favorite subject, but he had somehow gotten through seventh grade. Since the autumn holidays it was all about equations, systems of equations were soon to follow, and Jannik had the feeling that he was slipping a little further every week. In class, he nodded when Mr. Kowalczyk reshaped on the blackboard. At home something different came out of him.

The short test before the Christmas holidays came back with lots of red squiggles. Jannik came to the conclusion that seemed most logical to him: practice more equations. At the weekend he calculated twenty problems from the book. Eleven of them were wrong. More of the same had done nothing except make him feel even worse.

## “Your equations aren’t the problem at all.”

After the next hour, Jannik remained at the desk, the test in his hand. Mr. Kowalczyk looked at him for a long time. Then he took a pencil and circled not the wrong tasks, but individual places in them. “Look where it happens.”

It was always the same three places. From 4(x − 2) Jannik made 4x − 2 and only multiplied the four with the x. −3x = 12 had become x = 4 instead of x = −4. And at ¾x = 6 he had multiplied by ¾ instead of dividing by ¾ and got 4.5 instead of 8.

“You can do the forming yourself,” said Mr. Kowalczyk. "You know that you have to do the same thing on both sides. But along the way you stumble across things that came much earlier: brackets, accidentals, breaks. If the chain keeps falling off on your bike, pedaling faster doesn't help."

He turned the page over and drew a chain of four links. At the top: equations. Below: terms and brackets. Among them: calculating with negative numbers. At the bottom: fractional calculation. "Go backwards down the chain until you get to a link that holds. That's where you start."

## A box under the bed

At home, Jannik pulled out the box from under his bed that contained his old school supplies. He never threw them away, more out of laziness than intention. In his seventh grade notebook he found the chapter on terms. Most of it was correct, only the brackets with a minus in front of them always showed improvements. In the sixth grade notebook it became clearer. Entire pages of “Dividing Fractions” and “Calculating with Negative Numbers” were red.

He even remembered it. At that time he had memorized two sentences without understanding why they were true: “Minus times minus equals plus” and “Multiply by the reciprocal.” He later used the first one when subtracting, where it doesn't apply, and simply turned −3 − 5 into 8. He had twisted the second one at some point. When the topics were over, he was relieved and no one asked about it anymore. Until now.

Mr. Kowalczyk gave him copies of the review pages from the sixth and seventh grade math books. Jannik worked on them over three afternoons, without a book, without a cell phone, ten minutes per topic. He drew a hook, a wave or a cross behind each task. Then his gap was in front of him in black and white. She was smaller than feared, but she sat at the bottom.

## Twenty minutes a day

The crosses and waves became a list, sorted from bottom to top: first negative numbers, then fractions, then brackets with minus, and finally equations again. From Monday to Thursday, Jannik planned twenty minutes for the old stuff, before the normal homework. On Friday there was a mixed mini-test consisting of five tasks. On Tuesdays he also went to the math remedial class that his school offered in the seventh period. The teacher there showed him his list and picked out suitable tasks.

The first week was tough. Jannik was ashamed to expect fifteen problems from the sixth grade and preferred not to open the booklet on the bus. Then came the moment on the number line. The special education teacher made him think of negative numbers as debts: If you have 3 euros in debt and add 5 euros in debt, you are not at plus eight, but at minus eight. A single question helped with division: How often does ¾ fit into 6? Jannik drew quarter boxes, counted and came up with 8. Exactly the number that was missing from his test.

Five weeks later the class wrote a paper on systems of equations. Jannik made another sign error, but he found it himself when he calculated the sample at the end. Under the work there was a “satisfactory” rating. He didn't become a math whiz. But he now knew where his ground was and that it was supporting.

## What you can take from it

Jannik didn't work harder than before, but in the right place. Math builds on each other more strongly than most subjects: what wobbles in grade 6 can cause an equation to collapse in grade 9. The good news is that such gaps can be found if you search specifically. You can take the following steps alone or together with your teacher.

### Distinguish symptom and cause

The topic on which you are currently failing is often just the place where an older gap becomes visible. To find out, don't look at which tasks are wrong, but rather where within the task the error occurs.

1. **Gather materials.** Take two or three corrected tests, papers, or homework assignments from the current topic.
2. **Circle the location of the error.** Not the entire problem, but the line where it first becomes incorrect.
3. **Give each error a name.** For example, “sign,” “bracket,” “fraction,” or “unit.”
4. **Count.** Mistakes that keep coming back are your trail. It's usually not a single careless mistake.

| What you just notice | Where the cause often lies |
|---|---|
| Equations go wrong | Resolve brackets, signs, fractions |
| Percentage tasks don't work | Fractions and decimals, rule of three |
| The Pythagorean theorem gives incorrect results | Squaring, roots, rearranging equations |
| Functions seem confusing | Coordinate system, inserting numbers into terms |
| Areas and volumes are never correct | Convert units |
| Word problems remain a mystery | Translate text into calculation steps |

The table shows typical connections, not hard and fast rules. Your own path can be completely different. If you are particularly struggling with translating texts, [Sara's story about word problems](/lerngeschichten/textaufgaben-in-mathe-loesen/) will help you.

### Follow topic chains backwards

Once you have found your error types, trace them back: What topic do you need to be able to do this step? And what do you need for that? Write down the chain like Mr. Kowalczyk did on the back of the test. A limb lasts if you can solve most of the tasks without help and explain in your own words why your approach is correct.

Look for topics, not grade levels. The school year in which fractions or negative numbers are used depends on the federal state, the type of school and the textbook. And just go along the chain that leads to your mistake. You don't have to repeat all the material from the lower classes.

### Self-test with old magazines and review pages

Before you practice, check where you really stand. This is how an honest self-test works:

- **Look for old magazines and books.** Your magazine entries show you how the topic was explained back then, and the improvements reveal where there were problems back then.
- **Use the pages at the end of the chapter.** Many math books have pages with names like “Review,” “Test Yourself,” or “I Can Do That.” They summarize a topic in a few tasks and are well suited for diagnosis. Older volumes can often be loaned to you by your teacher or the school library.
- **Work without help and for a limited time.** About ten minutes per topic, without book, solutions and cell phone.
- **Three characters given.** Tick for sure, wave for unsure, cross for wrong or no idea. Hits scored count as a wave.

Important: The self-test is not yet practice. It just shows you where to start.

### Create and organize a gap list

Waves and crosses become your gap list. Sort them from bottom to top so that the basic topics come first. Within a level you prefer what your current topic needs most. This is what Jannik's list looked like:

| No. | gap | Self-test | Used for | Done |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Add and subtract negative numbers | Cross | Everything with signs | |
| 2 | Divide fractions | Cross | Equations with fractions | |
| 3 | Multiply fractions | wave | Equations with fractions | |
| 4 | Remove the parenthesis with a minus in front of it | Cross | Terms and equations | |
| 5 | Multiply out | wave | Terms and equations | |
| 6 | Equations with brackets and fractions | wave | Systems of equations | |

You only fill out the last column when you are confident about a topic in the mini-test, not after the first good day.

### A realistic catch-up plan

Closing gaps is a project over weeks, not a weekend. Short daily units are more effective than a long math Saturday because you recall the material more often and there is time to set in between. A unit of around 20 minutes might look like this:

1. **Warm-up (3 minutes):** two tasks from the previous day's topic, from memory.
2. **Understanding (10 minutes):** read the explanation in the old notebook or book, calculate an example step by step and ask yourself at each step why it is allowed.
3. **Calculate yourself (7 minutes):** three to five tasks without help, then compare.

Schedule such units four days a week, in addition to your normal homework, and reserve the fifth day for a mixed mini-test from all the gaps you have completed so far. Allow one to two weeks per link in the chain. Holds a limb, calculate a task from your current topic as a reward. That's where you'll notice most clearly that the work is worth it. [Fynn's story about the error book](/lerngeschichten/mathe-ueben-mit-fehlerheft/) shows how you learn from mistakes when practicing instead of looking at the solution too early.

### Support from a teacher, remedial lessons or tutoring

Searching alone is a good place to start. With help it often goes faster.

- **Your math teacher:** Bring your blank list with you. A specific question like "I think my problem is dividing fractions. Do you have any practice material for this?" is much easier to answer than “I can’t do math.”
- **School support offers:** Many schools have support hours, learning times or projects in which older people help younger people. Ask your class teacher or the office what you have available.
- **Tutoring:** If you're thinking about it, make sure that your gap list is worked on, that you do the math yourself, and that ways are explained instead of just doing homework. A trial lesson shows whether it's a good fit. You clarify costs and conditions beforehand. Families with little money can receive a grant to support learning under certain conditions, for example through the education and participation package. You can find out whether this is an option at your school or at the responsible office in your city or district.

Tutoring doesn't replace your twenty minutes a day, it just makes it easier. And if the word math paralyzes you, it's worth taking a look at [Pia's story about fear of math](/lerngeschichten/angst-vor-mathe/).

## Typical pitfalls

- **Just practicing more of the current topic.** This was Jannik's first attempt. As long as the gap underneath is open, you will mostly repeat your mistakes.
- **Start at the beginning.** If you want to go through all the material from grade 5 onwards, you will lose interest before you get to the actual gap.
- **Ashamed of old topics.** Almost everyone has a gap somewhere. Nobody has to see which book you are calculating from.
- **Just learn the rules by heart.** Memorizations help, but only if you know when they apply. For each rule, ask: Why is this true?
- **Forget the current lesson.** The catch-up plan is added to the homework. He doesn't replace them.

## Briefly asked

### How long does it take to close a math gap?

This depends on how deep the gap is and how regularly you practice. One to two weeks with short daily units is often enough for a single topic. If the chain goes back over several school years, plan on a few weeks to months.

### I don't have my old notebooks anymore. What now?

Ask your teacher or school library for older volumes of your math book. Classmates with careful notebooks can also help out. The tasks from the end of the chapter are often enough for the self-test.

### Is it embarrassing to admit that I'm missing material from grade 6?

No. This is valuable information for your teacher because it explains why you are stuck on the current topic. If you can say exactly where the problem is, you will usually get more precise help.
