Mr. Arslan asked the question: “Why did Romeo kill Tybalt if he wanted to reconcile with him beforehand?” Sofia knew it. She had read the scene the night before and thought about it. Her hand twitched. But then she thought: What if I say something wrong? What if everyone looks at me? What if my voice sounds weird? Her hand remained on the table. Maximilian spoke up and said almost exactly what Sofia had thought. Mr. Arslan praised him. Sofia looked at her notebook and was annoyed.

## Good grades on paper, quiet in the room

Sofia was fifteen and in the ninth grade of a high school. She was strong in written work. In German she usually got a B and in English she even got a minus one. But the report card only showed a C in both subjects. The reason was her oral grade. She almost never contacted me.

It wasn't that she didn't have anything to say. On the contrary, her head was full of thoughts. But there was an invisible wall between the thought and the raised hand. The longer she waited, the higher the wall became. And if she did get picked up without having spoken, she would blush and speak so quietly that Mr. Arslan had to ask.

At the parent-teacher conference, Mr. Arslan explained that oral participation in German made up about half of the grade. “Sofia has so much on her,” he said. “I’d just like to hear it more often.”

## A suggestion in private

After the parent-teacher conference, Mr. Arslan asked Sofia to stay briefly after class. He didn't blame her. Instead, he asked, “What’s holding you back?”

Sofia hesitated. Then she talked about the fear of saying the wrong thing and the feeling that everyone was staring at her. Mr. Arslan listened and nodded. "It's like that for many people. I'll make you a suggestion. We'll start small. In every German lesson you'll contact us once. Only once. It doesn't matter if it's with an answer, a question or a guess. And I promise you that in the first two weeks I'll only take you up on it if you get in touch."

Sofia was unsure if she could do it. But once an hour sounded doable.

## The first message

The first week was difficult. On Monday, Sofia waited the entire hour for the right moment and only spoke up five minutes before the end with a short answer to an easy question. Her heart was pounding so loudly that she thought the others had to hear it. But when she finished, Mr. Arslan just said, “Exactly.” Nobody laughed. Nobody stared.

On Wednesday she reported in the middle of the lesson. Twice on Friday. She noticed that it became a little easier with each report.

Her big sister Laura, who was studying to be a teacher, gave her another tip. "Prepare for the lesson. If you know what it's about, you'll have some ideas ready. Before the lesson, write down a question or observation that you can bring in."

Sofia tried it. Before each German lesson, she looked at her notes from the last lesson and read the homework again. She wrote down a question that interested her and an observation she had made. She went to class with these two anchors in her head.

## Questions also count

What particularly helped Sofia was the realization that collaboration doesn't just consist of the right answers. In a lesson about a poem by Rilke, she didn't understand a verse at all. Before, she would have stayed silent and hoped someone else would ask. This time she spoke up and said: "I don't understand why the panther suddenly takes up the image in the third verse. What does that mean?"

Mr. Arslan was visibly happy about the question and passed it on to the class. A discussion arose in which several people took part. At the end of the lesson he said: “Thank you Sofia, that was a very good question.” Sofia realized that sometimes a question has more impact than an answer.

She also learned to deal with mistakes. She once said something wrong about the narrative perspective of a short story. Mr. Arslan kindly corrected her, and a classmate added. Sofia blushed, but only briefly. She noticed that no one held it against her. And the correction helped her understand the topic better.

## The new testimony

At the end of the semester there was a two in German and a minus one in English. Mr. Arslan wrote in his assessment: “Sofia now participates in lessons regularly and with thoughtful contributions.” Sofia read the sentence several times. The wall between thought and hand had not completely disappeared. But it had gotten so low that Sofia simply stepped over it.

## What you can take from it

Oral participation makes up a large part of the grade in many subjects. Anyone who rarely contacts you is wasting points, even if they know a lot. Reaching out is a skill that can be practiced. With small steps, good preparation and a different perspective on mistakes, it becomes easier.

### What counts as cooperation

Oral participation is more than correct answers:

| Type of contribution | Example |
|---|---|
| Answer to a question | “Romeo wants to avenge Mercutio’s death.” |
| Own observation | “I noticed that the word ‘night’ appears a lot in the text.” |
| Question of understanding | “What exactly is meant by this term?” |
| Guess | “I think the character is lying here because…” |
| Supplement | “The passage on page 34 also fits in with this.” |
| contradiction | “I see it differently because…” |
| Summary | “So we found three reasons…” |

Many teachers particularly value contributions that show that you think for yourself. A good question can be worth more than a short correct answer.

### Start small

If you find it difficult to report, set small, achievable goals:

1. **Week One:** Report once per hour to a subject you feel comfortable with.
2. **Week Two:** Once per hour in two or three subjects.
3. **Week three:** Twice per hour, also with a question or guess.
4. **After:** Gradually increase until participation becomes a habit.

Reporting early in the lesson is often easier than late because the tension has not yet become so great. Easy questions at the beginning of a lesson are ideal ways to get started.

### Well prepared for the lesson

If you know what it's about, it's easier to say something:

- **Read through notes from the last lesson.** This way you know where to go next.
- **Do your homework thoroughly.** It is often the basis for class discussions.
- **Write down a question and an observation.** Two anchors that you take with you into the lesson.
- **Preview the text or material** when announced.

### Dealing with the fear of making mistakes

Fear of saying the wrong thing is the most common reason for not coming forward. It is helpful to see errors differently:

- **Mistakes are part of learning.** Classes are the place where you can try things out.
- **A false contribution is better than none.** It shows that you are thinking and often helps the whole class.
- **Most classmates quickly forget mistakes.** They are busy with themselves.
- **Teachers evaluate cooperation over time.** A single mistake is hardly significant.

If the fear of speaking in front of the class is really bothering you, the tips from [Leyla's story about stage fright](/lerngeschichten/lampenfieber-vor-dem-referat/) will also help.

### Formulate well

A few sentence starters can help if you find it difficult to get started:

- “I believe that… because…”
- “I noticed that…”
- "I'm not sure, but maybe..."
- “I want to follow up on what…said.”
- “I have a question about…”

Speak loudly and clearly enough for those in the back rows to hear you. When speaking, look not only at the teacher, but also at the class.

### Talk to the teacher

If you are having difficulty, a short conversation with the teacher can help. Be honest about what is holding you back and ask how your participation is being evaluated and what you can improve. Many teachers are happy to support you when they know you are making an effort.

### Combine taking notes and collaboration

Good cooperation and good notes complement each other. If you write carefully, you will have keywords ready for your own contributions. [Ole's story about taking notes](/lerngeschichten/richtig-mitschreiben/) shows you how to take notes sensibly, and [Clara's story about concentration in class](/lerngeschichten/konzentration-im-unterricht/) tells you how to stay focused in class.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Wait for the perfect answer.** By the time it's formulated, the opportunity is often gone.
- **Only report in the last few minutes.** This is when the tension is at its highest and the opportunities are at their lowest.
- **Only let answers count.** Questions and guesses are also valuable input.
- **Withdraw after a mistake.** Especially then it helps to get back in touch soon.
- **Go into the lesson unprepared.** Without prior knowledge it is difficult to contribute something.

## Briefly asked

### How many times should I check in per hour?

There is no fixed number. Quality is more important than quantity. Regular, well-thought-out posts every hour have a better effect than many short messages in one and then nothing for weeks.

### What if the teacher never takes my turn?

Hang in there and talk about it kindly in a quiet moment. Sometimes teachers don't even notice who is checking in and how often. A short conversation can change that.

### Do contributions in group work also count towards the oral grade?

Often yes. Many teachers also observe how you participate in group work, present results or speak at presentations. Ask your teacher how she composes the oral grade.
