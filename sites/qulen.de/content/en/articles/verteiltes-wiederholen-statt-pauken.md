“What happens during osmosis?” Ms. Wendt leaned against the desk and let her eyes wander through the biology course. Hannah lowered her head a little. She knew that she knew that. Exactly fourteen days ago she had written a whole page about it in the exam, including a sketch. Now she could only think of three words: water, membrane, something with concentration. Luckily, someone else answered.

## Three rounds in one night

Hannah was sixteen and in the eleventh grade of a high school. She liked biology because it was about things that happened in her own body. Her study system for exams had been fixed since middle school: she started two days beforehand, and the last night was long. She read her binder cover to cover, then the worksheets, then the pages in the book. Then everything again. And around 2 a.m. a third time, faster, with the highlighter in my hand.

By the third pass, everything felt familiar. She knew every illustration, every heading. It was exactly this feeling that had always given her security. She got nine points on the cell biology exam, a decent result for a night with four hours of sleep. She accepted that she would walk through school the next day in a daze.

Then came the hour with the osmosis question. During the break afterwards, Hannah realized that it wasn't just osmosis. Diffusion, active transport, the structure of the biomembrane: everything had converged into an indistinct pulp. The new topic, enzymes, was based on exactly this.

## The feeling of knowing something

After the lesson, Hannah stopped at Ms. Wendt's desk. “I really studied that for the exam,” she said. "Honestly. Half the night."

“I believe you,” said Ms. Wendt. “How exactly did you learn?”

Hannah talked about the three rounds. Ms. Wendt nodded and pulled a blank sheet of paper out of her folder. "Then do a little experiment. Write down everything you know about biomembranes. Without a stapler. Five minutes."

Hannah wrote. Phospholipids, two layers, proteins. After that it got thin. When the five minutes were up, there was barely half a page on the paper, and half of it she was unsure about. “You see,” said Mrs. Wendt kindly. "When you read it again, you recognize everything. It feels like knowledge. But in the exam, the stapler isn't next to you. You have to get it out yourself. And that's exactly what you've never practiced."

She drew a line on the back of the page that sloped steeply and then became flatter. "That's pretty much how forgetting works. Every time you successfully get something out of your head, the curve falls more slowly afterwards. A night counts as one time, no matter how often you read."

"And what should I do instead? Study bio every day?"

“Query a little bit every day,” said Ms. Wendt. "With increasing intervals. Try it out until the next exam. We'll be writing about enzymes in four weeks."

## A calendar full of small appointments

That evening, Hannah opened her planner. For bio, she created a stack of index cards, not with terms, but with questions. “Why does enzyme activity decrease at very high temperatures?” “What does substrate specificity mean?” “What distinguishes a competitive from a non-competitive inhibition?” On the back she wrote the answer in two or three sentences. She also included questions about cell biology so that they wouldn't fade any further.

Then she created a repeat calendar. After every biology lesson: on the same evening, ten minutes of questions about the new material without looking at the folder. Two days later again. Then after a week, then after two. Each card was given the next date in pencil in the bottom right corner. On Sunday there was a round of mixing: all the cards that were due, well mixed, old and new topics mixed up.

The first week was sobering. Things that she had actually understood in class were missing from the blank page. But Hannah quickly realized that these gaps were particularly useful. They showed her where she needed to read specifically instead of reading everything again. Her friend Jule shook her head when she saw the cards. "Are you studying already? The exam isn't for another three weeks." – “No more than usual,” said Hannah. “Just distributed differently.”

In the third week there was a math exam and Bio stayed down for four days. The stack of due cards was then thick. Instead of catching up on everything at once, she tackled the oldest appointments first, doing one quick round a day until she was back on track.

## The question about the exam

The night before the enzyme exam, Hannah did one last round of shuffling, then closed the planner and went to bed at ten-thirty. It almost felt forbidden, so early. She received eleven points in the exam. That made her happy, but it wasn't the moment she remembered later.

That came three weeks later. The course began with genetics, and Ms. Wendt asked why an enzyme can no longer work properly if something changes in its blueprint. Hannah spoke up and explained: Then the shape of the active site can change and the substrate no longer fits. It wasn't until she was finished that she realized she hadn't looked anywhere for this. And when Ms. Wendt explained how substances actually get into the cell, she also knew that.

## What you can take from it

Hannah hasn't learned any more than she used to. She distributed her time differently and got into the habit of getting knowledge from her head instead of just recognizing it. Both are among the well-documented findings of learning research, and both work in any subject with a lot of specialist knowledge, from biology to history to economics in vocational schools. For vocabulary, there is a separate, proven form of it that explains [Frida's story about the index box](/lerngeschichten/vokabeln-lernen-mit-karteikasten/).

### Why forgetting is normal

Forgetting is not a sign that there is something wrong with you. Your memory is constantly sorting out what it no longer needs. As early as the 19th century, the psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus described in self-experiments how quickly what was learned fades away without repetition. This is where the idea of ​​the forgetting curve comes from: A lot is lost shortly after learning, and then forgetting progresses more slowly.

The good news is in the second part of the idea. If you repeat something before it's completely gone, it will stay longer afterwards. If you repeat it again, it will last even longer. Therefore, the intervals between repetitions are allowed to increase over time. And something else helps: sleep. When you sleep, your brain consolidates what you learned during the day. An all-nighter takes away exactly the time in which what you have learned could sink in.

### Retrieval instead of rereading

Rereading and highlighting feel productive because everything looks familiar. What feels more strenuous is more effective: getting the knowledge from memory before you look it up. These forms work in almost every subject:

| Method | That's how it works | Example from organic |
|---|---|---|
| Blank sheet | Write down everything you know about a topic in five to ten minutes. Then compare with the stapler and add a different color. | “Everything I know about enzymes” |
| Self-test | Answer questions in writing or out loud and only then check the solution. | Exercises from the book without first looking at the solution section |
| Own questions | Make each heading in the binder a question and write the answer on the back of a card. | “How does pH affect an enzyme?” |
| Explain | Explain the topic out loud as if you were teaching it to someone in a younger class. | Explain the process of osmosis using your own sketch |

Good questions focus on connections, not just concepts. “What is an enzyme?” is a start. “Why can an inhibitor block an enzyme even though it is not the substrate?” forces you to connect knowledge, which is what exams often ask. If you can't think of an answer, that's not a failure, but rather information: now you know what to read. [Nele's story about learning notes](/lerngeschichten/lernzettel-schreiben/) shows how you can make concise study notes from the material instead of copying the book.

### Create a recurring calendar with increasing intervals

1. **Collect questions.** After each lesson, write down a few questions about the new material on cards or in a list.
2. **The first round on the same day.** Ten minutes of reading in the evening is worth more than an additional hour of reading just before the exam.
3. **Write down the next appointment.** Write on each card or next to each question when it's time again.
4. **Adjust spacing.** What you knew for sure will come back later. What you didn't know will come back soon.
5. **Only what is due every day.** This keeps each round short and you don't have to think about what to start with every time.

As a rule of thumb, you can follow this pattern. It's not a hard and fast rule, but a starting point that you adapt to your schedule:

| round | When | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | on the day of the lesson | Answer questions about the new material, read up on gaps |
| 2 | two to three days later | the same questions without looking in the folder |
| 3 | about a week later | the questions mixed with older topics |
| 4 | about two weeks later | blank page on the whole topic |
| 5 | before the exam, then at long intervals afterwards | Mixed round with everything that comes with it |

Whether you do this with paper cards, a table in the planner or an index card app is a matter of taste. The only important thing is that you don't have to decide every day what's wrong. That's already certain.

### Mix topics instead of practicing in blocks

Many people learn one topic after the other: first everything about membranes, then everything about enzymes. It feels fluid because you always know what it's about. But in the exam this is not the priority. There you have to recognize for yourself what knowledge is required.

If you mix up your questions, you're practicing exactly that. Include questions from different topics, including older subject areas, in your revision rounds. At the beginning it seems bumpier and you are slower. That's okay, because it's this effort that makes your knowledge more agile. In subjects with arithmetic tasks the same applies: different types of tasks mixed together instead of twenty of the same tasks in a row.

### Build repetition into the week

Distributed repetition rarely fails because of the method, mostly because of everyday life. These habits help:

- **A solid anchor.** The daily round right after homework, after dinner or on the train.
- **Keep it short.** Ten to twenty minutes per subject is usually enough. Better short and regular than long and rare.
- **A mixed session on the weekend.** All due questions from all topics once a week.
- **Take cancellations in stride.** After missing days, go to the oldest appointments first, not all at once.

## Sample plan for the four weeks before an exam

This is what a plan could look like if the lesson brings new material until shortly before the exam. Allow about a quarter of an hour per day for the subject, a little more on weekends:

| period | New material | Repeat | Self-test |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 weeks before | create questions after every hour | daily round with what is due | blank page on the weekend |
| 3 weeks before | create further questions | cards due plus older topics | Mixed round on the weekend |
| 2 weeks before | Read the gaps from the self-tests specifically | Distances grow, laps remain short | Exercises without solution part |
| last week | no longer develop anything new yourself | all cards mixed, emphasis on uncertain ones | a test exam under time pressure |
| day before | none | a short mixing session | go to sleep early |

It's not over after the exam. Leave the cards in the stack, just well spaced apart. Especially in high school, a lot of things come up again in later topics and exams.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Check immediately.** Anyone who reads the answer before thinking about it is just practicing recognition again.
- **Too big questions.** “Explain cell biology” cannot be answered honestly. Break big questions into small ones.
- **Query terms only.** Also ask why and how.
- **Discard cards you know.** They are dealt less often, but they remain in the deck.
- **Sacrificing sleep.** A night without sleep is usually more loss than gain.

## Briefly asked

### Isn't it enough to read everything again the night before?

Sometimes for the next morning. But if you need the knowledge again in later topics, exams or final exams, a single night will rarely go that far.

### What do I do if the exam is already in a week?

Start retrieving anyway. Do a short round every day with a blank sheet and questions, read only your gaps and distribute the material over all the remaining days. [Elif's plan for the day before the exam](/lerngeschichten/tag-vor-der-pruefung/) shows how you plan the last few hours.

### Does this also work alongside many other subjects?

Yes, just then. Short sessions fit more easily into a full day than long study evenings. You can find further methods in the chapter [Vocabulary & Learning Methods](/themen/vokabeln-und-lernmethoden/).
