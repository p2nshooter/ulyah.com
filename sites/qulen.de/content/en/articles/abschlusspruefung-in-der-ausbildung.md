The invitation from the Chamber of Commerce and Industry was on Selin's desk in the office. Exam dates, times, room information, the list of permitted aids. In ten weeks the time had come: the second part of her final exam as an office management clerk. She was less worried about the written exams. But it also said: “Specialized task in the elective qualification, case-related technical discussion.” A conversation with an audit committee. Selin placed the invitation upside down on the table and tried to concentrate on the bills in front of her.

## Three years of training, one exam

Selin was twenty-one and in her third year of training at a medium-sized medical technology company. She started training after graduating from high school and enjoyed working in the office. She organized appointments, processed orders, wrote invoices and looked after personnel files. Her instructor, Mr. Lang, often praised her for her reliability.

The vocational school ran well, with grades in the two to three range. She passed the first part of the final exam in her second year of training with “satisfactory” results. But the technical talk scared her. She was rather reserved when talking to strangers. And she knew that the examiners would ask where she was unsure.

## A plan with the instructor

Selin spoke to Mr. Lang. He took half an hour and went through the exam components with her. “Let’s first clarify what exactly is required,” he said. “Then let’s make a plan.”

Together they looked at the board's examination information. There was a written exam on customer relationship processes, one on economics and social studies and the case-related technical interview in her elective qualification, which was human resources management. For the technical discussion, she had to prepare reports about specialist operational tasks that would then be discussed.

Mr. Lang suggested that Selin get two hours at work every Friday afternoon for the next few weeks to prepare for the exam. “And once a week we do a trial Q&A session about your reports.”

## In writing: old exams and study sheets

For the written parts, Selin obtained old exam questions from the vocational school and from a colleague who had taken the exam the previous year. She quickly noticed that certain topics came up again and again: disruptions in the sales contract, customer communication, labor law, social security.

She created a learning sheet for each topic. For economics and social studies, where there were lots of facts, she used flashcards that she looked through on the train on the way to work. And every Sunday she took an old test under real conditions, with a clock and the permitted aids.

The first test tests went mediocre. Selin noticed that she was often unsure when it came to tasks relating to labor law. She spoke to her vocational school teacher, who gave her additional material and went through some questions with her during a break.

## Oral: practice, practice, practice

Selin prepared the technical discussion differently. She read her reports on operational tasks again and again, but not just to know the content. She thought about every step: Why did I do it like that? What alternatives were there? Which regulations played a role? What would I do differently today?

Mr. Lang played the examiner every Thursday. He asked questions, sometimes friendly, sometimes persistent. “Why didn’t you reject the applicant immediately?” “What are the deadlines for termination during the probationary period?” “What happens if the works council is not consulted?” The first laps were bumpy. Selin faltered, became confused, and didn't know some of the answers.

But with each passing week she became more confident. She learned to listen to questions calmly, think briefly and then answer in a structured manner. If she didn't know something, she was honest about it and explained where she would look. Mr. Lang nodded. "That's exactly what examiners want to see. Nobody expects you to know everything by heart. They want to see that you know your way around your job."

Selin also practiced with her roommate, who knew nothing about office management. If she could explain a process to her in a way she understood, Selin knew she could do it herself.

## The exam weeks

The written exams went well. During a task on the purchase contract, Selin immediately recognized the task type from the old exams. During the technical interview, she was nervous when she entered the room. Three people sat at the table, friendly but serious. Selin took a deep breath, sat down and answered the first question.

The examiners asked about one of their reports about organizing an application process. Selin explained the steps, justified her decisions and answered questions. She was unsure when asked about the General Data Protection Regulation, but said so openly and explained which principles she knew. The examiner smiled and added a point.

At the end of the day she received the message: passed, with an overall grade of “good”. Mr. Lang gave her a bouquet of flowers and offered her a job in the human resources department. Selin accepted.

## What you can take from it

The final exam is the highlight of your training. Depending on the profession, it consists of written, practical and oral parts. With early, structured preparation and support for your business, you can master them well.

### Know the exam exactly

The content and forms of the examination are set out in the training regulations for your profession. Find out early at your chamber, in your company and at the vocational school:

- **What parts of the exam** are there: written, practical, oral?
- **Is there an extended final exam** where the first part counts earlier?
- **Which aids** are allowed?
- **How ​​are the parts weighted** and what do you need to pass?
- **What deadlines** apply for registration and for submitting documents such as reports, project work or proof of training?

### A preparation plan

| Period before the exam | Focus |
|---|---|
| about three to four months | Clarify exam content, create a list of topics, identify gaps |
| about two to three months | Develop topics, learning sheets and index cards, study groups |
| about four to six weeks | old exams under real conditions, mock interviews |
| last week | Repetition, organization, recovery |

### Prepare written exams

- **Old exams** are the most important practice material. Ask the chamber, vocational school and company about it.
- **Recognize task types**: Many topics come back in a similar form.
- **Practice under time pressure** so that you are not surprised in the exam.
- **Close gaps in a targeted manner**, for example with the vocational school teacher or a learning group.

### Prepare practical and oral exams

- **Being able to explain your own work**: Why did you do something like that? What alternatives were there?
- **Conduct trial interviews**, ideally with trainers or experienced colleagues.
- **Use technical terms confidently** and be able to explain them.
- **Practice thinking out loud**, as in [Hamid's story about the oral exam](/lerngeschichten/muendliche-pruefung-vorbereiten/).

### Learn together with other trainees

There are people in vocational school who are facing exactly the same exam. Take advantage of this. A small learning group of three or four trainees can share the work: Each person prepares a topic and explains it to the others, together you calculate old exams and compare your solutions. For oral parts of the exam, you can question each other and take turns taking on the role of the examination committee. Those who explain learn the most, and those who listen discover gaps that would have gone unnoticed on their own. [Noah's story about the study group](/lerngeschichten/lerngruppe-organisieren/) shows how a study group works well and does not become a chat circle. Trainees from other companies also often bring new examples because every company organizes processes slightly differently. This is particularly helpful for case-related questions in which you have to transfer your knowledge to an unfamiliar situation.

### Involve the business

Your training company has an interest in you passing the exam. Be open about your preparation. Many companies allow learning periods, test exams or discussions with experienced employees. On the day before the final written examination, trainees are generally entitled to time off work according to the Vocational Training Act. Clarify the details with your company.

### The formalities at a glance

In addition to learning, don't forget about organization:

- **Register** for the exam in good time via the company and the chamber.
- **Proof of training** complete and signed, as it is usually a requirement for admission. How you lead it regularly is shown in [Tobias' story about learning in training](/lerngeschichten/lernen-in-der-ausbildung/).
- **Get permitted aids** and try them out beforehand.
- **Clarify exam location and directions**.

### Dealing with nervousness

Excitement before the final exam is normal. Thorough preparation, enough sleep and a few breathing exercises help. [Yusuf's story about exam anxiety](/lerngeschichten/pruefungsangst-bewaeltigen/) tells you how to deal with severe exam anxiety, and [Elif's story about the day before the exam](/lerngeschichten/tag-vor-der-pruefung/) shows what helps the day before.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Don't know the type of exam.** If you don't know what's coming, you may be preparing incorrectly.
- **Only practice the writing test.** Practical and oral parts require separate preparation.
- **Neglect the proof of training.** If it is missing, your approval may be at risk.
- **Learn alone.** Trainers, vocational schools, colleagues and learning groups are valuable help.
- **Start too late.** The final exam covers material from the entire training.

## Briefly asked

### What happens if I fail the final exam?

As a rule, you can repeat the exam, usually up to twice. If desired, the training can be extended until the next re-examination. You can find out the exact rules from your chamber.

### Can I use notes in the oral exam?

This depends on the form of examination and the examination regulations. Sometimes your own documents such as reports or presentations can be used, sometimes not. Clarify this with your chamber or instructor beforehand.

### Where can I get help if I have problems with my training?

The first points of contact are your trainer and the vocational school. The chambers have training advisors who can help with questions and conflicts. The employment agency offers support during training under certain conditions.

### How much time should I allow for the exam outside of work?

That depends on your profession and your status. Many trainees plan two to four short learning units per week in the last three months before the exam, plus a longer block on the weekend. In the last few weeks it usually increases, especially due to trial exams. It is important that you study regularly instead of packing everything into the last few days.
