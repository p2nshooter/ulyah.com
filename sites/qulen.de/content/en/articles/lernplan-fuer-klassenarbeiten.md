Greta discovered the problem on a Wednesday during the big break. She was sitting with her friend Lina on the wall in front of the cafeteria, the homework book on her knees, and wanted to enter the date that Ms. Brandt had just announced in German: class work on Friday in two and a half weeks. There was already something on this week's page. Monday: English. Wednesday: Math. And now Friday: German. Three jobs in five days.

“How is that supposed to work?” she asked. Lina shrugged her shoulders. "It's just before the certificates. Everything always comes at once."

## Three appointments and an empty head

Greta was fourteen, in the eighth grade of a comprehensive school and was not a bad student. She usually studied the evening before work: reading through notebook entries, doing a few calculations, and going to bed early. So far that had somehow been enough. But in a single evening for three subjects? Even she knew that this didn't work out.

In the afternoon she put all three notebooks on the desk and opened the math book. Linear functions, slope, intercept. After a quarter of an hour she remembered that she didn't even know which units were coming up for English. Then she thought about the German work, a discussion, and how her last one had failed precisely because of the structure. After an hour and a half, she had skimmed through three subjects and gotten nothing done in any of them. That evening in bed she counted: seventeen days, three compartments, a huge mountain. Her stomach clenched.

## Thinking backwards with Ms. Brandt

The next day the class had study time. Ms. Brandt, Greta's class teacher, walked through the rows and stood next to her. There was only one word on Greta's sheet, underlined three times: LEARNING PLAN.

“Three jobs in one week?” asked Ms. Brandt. Greta nodded. Ms. Brandt pulled up a chair. "Then we do it like packing our suitcases for a school trip. First put everything on the table, then sort it, then pack it. And we start thinking about the day of departure."

First, Greta wrote down what would appear in the work for each subject. When it came to math, she found the topics in the booklet: reading the slope from the graph, setting up functional equations, and word problems. In German: Structure of a discussion, supporting arguments with examples, introduction and conclusion. When it came to English, she didn't know exactly and honestly wrote next to it: "Ask Mr. Yıldız."

Then came the part that surprised her. Ms. Brandt had them break each topic down into chunks that fit into about half an hour and write an activity word for each chunk. Not “learn math”, but “calculate eight slope problems and compare them with the solutions”. Not “English”, but “cover vocabulary Unit 4, query, mark incorrect ones”.

“And now the real trick,” Ms. Brandt said, drawing a timeline from today to Friday of exam week. "You don't distribute from the front, but from the back. What do you do the day before each work?"

"Learn?"

"Repeat. Nothing new anymore, just go through what you can already do and do a trial task for a while. So these days belong to the respective subject." Greta entered: Sunday for English, Tuesday for math, Thursday for German. Before that, in the almost two weeks up to that point, she distributed the snacks so that every day there were two, at most three, mixed from different subjects. English and Math were supposed to be finished a little earlier because their work came first. But German couldn't just stay there until the end.

"And here," Ms. Brandt tapped two empty boxes, "there's room. If something takes longer, it will slide in there. If not, you're free." Greta liked that. For the first time the mountain looked like a path.

## The week she was afraid of

The first few days went better than expected. After the lesson, Mr. Yıldız revealed that Units 3 and 4 were coming up, as well as the Present Perfect. Every evening, Greta checked off what she had accomplished and realized how good a crossed out box felt.

On the fourth day it stalled. The math word problems took twice as long as planned and one box remained open. In the past, she would have thrown the whole plan away at this point. This time, as agreed with Ms. Brandt, she sat down for ten minutes on Sunday evening and honestly examined: What was done, what took longer, what could be smaller? The two open math morsels went into a buffer. For German, she did not write a complete discussion of the exercise, but only an introduction and two developed arguments, which Ms. Brandt looked at during the learning time.

During the exam week itself, the schedule was almost boring, and that was a good thing. On Tuesday afternoon she went through the math topics again, timed a page of self-test from the book and stopped at six to go to handball training. When a text assignment came up at work on Wednesday about two cell phone plans with a basic fee, she immediately recognized the pattern.

When the three papers came back, the English score was a two, the math score was a three plus, and the German score was a two minus. No miracles. But for the first time, Greta knew exactly why things had turned out this way. She hung the plan above her desk, with all the crossing outs and arrows.

## What you can take from it

Greta's plan wasn't complicated. Three things made the difference: she had everything on one page, she thought backwards from the day of the exam, and she left room for the unexpected. This is exactly how you can proceed if you have several tasks to do in the next two to three weeks.

### Step 1: Collect dates and material

Enter all of the work and tests for the next few weeks in one place, on a sheet or in a calendar. Add fixed appointments such as training, tutoring, birthdays or your part-time job, because you are missing this time when learning.

Then write a list of materials for each subject. Good sources include the teacher's announcement, your notebook entries, worksheets, and the book pages provided. If something is unclear to you, ask in the next lesson: “What topics are covered and are there any exercises?” If you are missing entries because you were sick, get them now from classmates and not on the weekend before work.

Then mark each topic with a traffic light color: Green means “I can”, yellow “fairly”, red “no idea”. This way you can see at a glance where your time needs to go.

### Step 2: Divide the material into small portions

“Learning math” is not a plan, but a feeling of pressure. Divide each topic into portions of approximately 20 to 45 minutes and give each portion an action word and a visible result. Portions in which you do something yourself are most beneficial: calculating, quizzing yourself, writing down key points from memory, explaining a task to someone. Just reading through feels good, but often doesn't stick.

| Thinking too big | Well portioned |
|---|---|
| Learn math | 8 tasks to calculate the slope, compare with the solutions, note down errors |
| English vocabulary | Cover vocabulary from Unit 4, query, mark incorrect ones |
| Review grammar | Write down the present perfect rule from your head, then do 10 practice sentences |
| Practice German | Write an introduction to the discussion and compare it with the information sheet |
| Go through organic | Explain the technical terms of the chapter without a book and read the gaps |

### Step 3: Plan backwards from exam day

Take your timeline and start at the back. The day before each assignment belongs to the respective subject: repetition, a test task under time pressure, nothing new. [Elif's plan for the last 24 hours](/lerngeschichten/tag-vor-der-pruefung/) shows how you plan the last hours before an exam.

Before that, distribute the portions. A few rules will help:

- **Red topics forward.** What you don't understand belongs at the beginning. Then there is time to ask questions in class.
- **Mix subjects.** Two or three portions of different subjects per day are often more enjoyable than a whole afternoon of math, and no subject is forgotten.
- **The first job is not the only one.** Portions for the last job of the week should also be planned early on.
- **Think about everyday life.** Homework continues. It's better to plan less on normal school days, but be reliable. [Matilda's story about self-organized homework](/lerngeschichten/hausaufgaben-selbst-organisieren/) shows how you can keep your daily stuff under control.

### Step 4: Build in buffer and repeat days

No plan survives reality unchanged. A task takes longer, you get sick, training gets postponed. Therefore, consciously plan empty boxes, perhaps one every two to three days or one free afternoon per week. If a buffer remains empty, it is not a lost day, but a day earned.

Also include short repetitions. What you learned on Monday you look at again on Thursday for ten minutes before it fades away. [Hannah's story about spaced repetition](/lerngeschichten/verteiltes-wiederholen-statt-pauken/) explains why repeating at intervals is worthwhile. And give yourself free time and enough sleep: you learn more easily when you're rested.

### Step 5: Honestly adjust the plan after a few days

Set yourself a fixed test date after three or four days, like Greta did her Sunday evening. Ask yourself three questions: What have I accomplished? What took longer than expected? What did I leave out and why?

Then you adjust: open things go into a buffer, portions that are too big become smaller, red topics take priority, and things that would be nice are thrown out. What you shouldn't do: punish yourself with long night shifts or completely rewrite the plan every day. If you notice that there is a big gap in a subject, talk to your teacher early and not just the day before work.

## Your weekly template for copying

Draw this table on a piece of paper and post it wherever you study. The example shows the week that Greta planned before her exam week.

| day | Portion 1 (approx. 30 min.) | Portion 2 (approx. 30 min.) | Repeat briefly (10 min.) | Done? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Monday | Math: Slope from graphs, 8 tasks | English: Vocabulary Unit 3 | – | |
| Tuesday | German: Collect and organize arguments | English: Present Perfect, 10 sentences | Math mistakes from Monday | |
| Wednesday | Math: Set up a functional equation | Buffer | Vocabulary Unit 3 | |
| Thursday | English: Vocabulary Unit 4 | German: write an introduction | Present perfect | |
| Friday | Math: two word problems | free (training) | Vocabulary Unit 4 | |
| Saturday | German: expand two arguments | Buffer | Math error list | |
| Sunday | English: repeat everything, sample text for a limited time | – | – | |

Cross out what you have done instead of erasing it. This way you can see what you have already accomplished before you start work, which is reassuring. An honest “yes”, “partly” or “no” belongs in the last column. This is exactly the column you need for step 5.

## Typical pitfalls

- **The plan is too full.** If you plan four hours every day, you usually give up after three days. It's better to plan less and then do it.
- **Planning instead of learning.** A plan with five colors and stickers looks nice. After twenty minutes of planning, the first portion should still begin.
- **Read only.** Reading through gives the illusion of security. Every portion needs something you can actively do and check.
- **Forgot the last paper.** If you only start with the third subject after the second paper, you only have one day left to do it.
- **Give up after the first slip-up.** A missed day is a case for the buffer, not the end of the plan.

## Briefly asked

### How early should I start the learning plan?

Preferably on the day you know the dates. Two to three weeks’ notice is usually sufficient for normal class work. If there are larger gaps in a subject, start earlier.

### What do I do if I miss a day completely?

Don't try to catch up on everything the next day. Push the most important portion into the next buffer and eliminate what does the least good.

### Is a learning plan worth it for just one piece of work?

Yes. It's just shorter: a list of ingredients, a few portions, a repeat day and a buffer.

### And if I just don't start despite the plan?

Then it's rarely according to plan. [Lasse's story about procrastination](/lerngeschichten/aufschieben-ueberwinden/) shows how to make the first step so small that it's easy.
