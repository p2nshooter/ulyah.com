Two days before the presentation, the four met for the first time to put their pieces together. Ahmet had prepared the introduction about climate change, Lena the causes, Jonas the consequences and Elif the solutions. Everyone had made their own slides. As they clicked through them one after the other on Lena's laptop, there was silence. Four different designs, four different font sizes. Lena and Jonas had both explained the melting glaciers. Elif's part lasted fifteen minutes, Jonas' part three. And no one had closure. “This is a disaster,” said Jonas. Nobody objected.

## Sharing is not the same as collaborating

Ahmet was fifteen and in the ninth grade of a secondary school. The group presentation on geography was supposed to last twenty minutes and counted as a class test. Ms. Neumann put the groups together and specified the topic: “Climate change: causes, consequences, solutions”.

At the first meeting after the lesson, the group quickly decided to divide the topic into four parts. Everyone should prepare their part alone. That seemed fair and practical. After that, they only wrote in the group chat, mostly about other things.

Now, two days before the deadline, they realized that sharing wasn't the same as collaborating. Her presentation was four small presentations that stood next to each other, overlapped and didn't fit together.

## A rescue evening

Ahmet suggested meeting again the next evening for three hours. The others groaned, but they agreed. Ahmet's older brother Yusuf, who was studying in the second semester and had already given several group presentations, listened to the problem that evening.

“You started dividing,” he said. "You should have started with the goal. What do you want the audience to understand at the end? That's your common thread. And then you hang the pieces on that."

He gave Ahmet three questions that the group should clarify first: What is our core message in one sentence? How do we guide the audience from start to finish? And who is responsible for what, not just in terms of content, but also for what we have in common?

## The core message

The next evening the four of them sat in Lena's kitchen. Ahmet wrote the first question on a large piece of paper. “What do you want the class to know at the end?” After a quarter of an hour of discussion, they had one sentence: “Climate change is primarily caused by humans, is already having noticeable consequences, and there are solutions that everyone can take part in.”

With this sentence they could suddenly check what belonged in the presentation and what didn't. They deleted the double explanation of the glaciers for Lena and left it for Jonas, where it fit better as a result. Together they shortened Elif's long part to what contributed to the core message. Jonas supplemented his short part with an example from Germany, the hot summers of recent years.

Then they built crossings. Ahmet would say at the end of the introduction: "But why is the climate actually changing? Lena will now explain that to you." Lena would transition to Jonas at the end of her part, and so on. Everyone took responsibility for the conclusion together: everyone said a sentence about a solution that they could implement themselves.

## A common face

Elif, who was well versed in presentation programs, took on the task of bringing all the slides into a consistent design. She didn't have to prepare any additional content for this. Jonas created the handout with the most important points and sources. Lena took care of the timing at the dress rehearsal.

They rehearsed twice in a row, with a stopwatch. In the first round, the presentation lasted twenty-seven minutes. On the second twenty-two. They cut back again and distributed the time more evenly.

They were finished shortly after ten. Tired but satisfied.

## The report

The next day the group gave a presentation that seemed to be all of a piece. The transitions worked, the slides looked consistent, and the timing was right. They complemented each other’s questions when asked by the class. When someone asked about electric cars, Elif answered and Jonas added an example.

Ms. Neumann gave the group a B plus. In her feedback she wrote: “Clear common thread, good transitions, fair distribution.” When assessed individually, each member received similar marks because everyone had made a visible contribution.

Ahmet decided to proceed differently from the beginning of the next group presentation. First the goal, then the plan, then the division.

## What you can take from it

Group presentations are an opportunity: you can share work, complement each other and learn from each other. At the same time, they involve risks: unequal distribution of work, misunderstandings, parts that don't fit together. With clear agreements from the start, four individual parts become a joint presentation.

### Start with the goal

Before you assign tasks, clarify together:

1. **Key message:** What should the audience know at the end? Put it in one sentence.
2. **Outline:** What parts does it take to lead to this statement?
3. **Time frame:** How long can the presentation last and how much time is given to each part?
4. **Requirements:** What does the teacher expect? Slides, handout, sources, individual assessment?

[Paul's story about his first presentation](/lerngeschichten/referat-vorbereiten/) shows how to narrow down and structure a topic.

### Distribute tasks fairly

Fair doesn't always mean that everyone does exactly the same thing. It is said that the effort is similar. In addition to the content parts, there are common tasks that are often forgotten:

| task | What goes with it |
|---|---|
| Content parts | research, keyword cards, your own slides |
| Foil design | uniform template, font sizes, images |
| Handout | Summary, sources, layout |
| Organization | Appointments, reminders, contact with the teacher |
| Timing and rehearsal | Plan a dress rehearsal, stop time, give feedback |
| Introduction and conclusion | make the common thread visible |

Those who have less content take on a shared task. This way the effort is balanced. Write down the distribution in writing, for example in a table that everyone has access to.

### Make a shared schedule

A schedule with intermediate steps prevents unpleasant surprises:

- **First meeting:** Goal, structure, distribution of tasks.
- **Intermediate meeting:** Everyone presents their stand. Overlaps and gaps become visible.
- **Combine:** Unify slides, formulate transitions.
- **Dress rehearsal:** talk through the entire thing at least once, with time measurement.
- **Buffer:** be ready a few days before the appointment.

### Transitions and common thread

A group presentation only looks like a whole when the parts are connected to each other:

- **Introduction** introduces the topic, the structure and the people.
- **Transitions** lead from one part to the next: “Now that we know the causes, let’s now look at the consequences.”
- **References** connect the parts: “As Lena showed earlier…”
- **Conclusion** summarizes the core message and rounds it off.

### Resolve conflicts

Conflicts sometimes arise in groups. Typical situations and possible solutions:

- **Someone doesn't participate.** Speak up early and directly. If nothing changes, inform the teacher in good time, not after the presentation.
- **Someone wants to decide everything.** Agree that important decisions will be made together.
- **Different ideas about quality.** At the beginning, clarify what your goal is.
- **Scheduling issues.** Use a shared calendar and keep meetings short and efficient.

The rules from [Noah's story about the learning group](/lerngeschichten/lerngruppe-organisieren/), which can be easily transferred to presentation groups, are also helpful.

### Collaborate digitally

Many groups today work with shared documents and presentations. This can be very practical if a few rules apply. Creates a shared file instead of each person building their own presentation that has to be stitched together later. Agree who will edit which slides so that no one overwrites each other. Use a fixed folder for images and sources and name files clearly. And record important decisions in writing, for example in a short note at the beginning of the document: core message, structure, distribution of tasks, dates. This means everyone can check what has been agreed at any time and no one has to rely on their memory or a long chat history. Also check which programs your school allows and be careful not to share personal information unnecessarily.

### Present together

During the lecture itself:

- **Everyone stands at the front**, even if someone else is speaking.
- **Listen carefully** when others speak instead of looking at your own cards.
- **Support each other**, for example with questions from the audience.
- **Uniform slides**, as in [Kemal's story about presentation slides](/lerngeschichten/praesentationsfolien-gestalten/).

## Typical pitfalls

- **Split immediately, with no common goal.** Then the pieces don't fit together.
- **Communicate in chat only.** Important agreements require a real meeting or a video call.
- **No intermediate meetings.** Otherwise, overlaps will only be noticed shortly before the appointment.
- **Forget common tasks.** Design, handout and rehearsal need people in charge.
- **No dress rehearsal.** Without a rehearsal, the timing and transitions are rarely correct.

## Briefly asked

### How is a group presentation evaluated?

That is different. Some teachers give a common grade, others grade each person individually or combine both. At the beginning, ask how the assessment will be done so that you can distribute the tasks accordingly.

### What if a group member is sick on the day of the presentation?

Plan for this: Everyone should know each other's parts roughly so someone can step in. Inform the teacher immediately and clarify together whether you should postpone the presentation or give it without the person.

### How do I deal with it if I work more than the others?

Speak it in a friendly but clear manner to the group. If nothing changes, talking to the teacher may help. Many teachers appreciate when problems are addressed early rather than after assessment.

### How many meetings does a group presentation need?

Three meetings are often enough for a twenty-minute presentation: one for planning and dividing, one for comparing the interim results and one for putting things together and doing the dress rehearsal. In between, each person works on their part and reports to the group if they have any questions.
