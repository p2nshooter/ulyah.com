“Task 1: A plate capacitor with circular plates…” Elias read the sentence once, twice, three times. Each word made sense on its own, but together they made nothing. He knew he could do something like that. At the weekend he had expected exactly such tasks. Now there was just a smooth white surface where the formulas were supposed to be. His heart was pounding in his throat and Jonas's pen was already scratching across the paper next to him.

## A white space in the head

Elias was seventeen and in the twelfth grade. He liked physics and had prepared thoroughly for this exam on electric fields. What was happening now frightened him all the more. He looked at the clock: five minutes had passed and there was only his name on his sheet. My thoughts raced: If I can't do the first task, I can't do the rest either. That hardly gives you any points. What if this happens to me in high school? His hands were clammy and the pen almost slipped from his fingers.

He read the problem a fourth time, more quickly now, as if he could force the solution. Things only got worse.

## Bounce three times, exhale once

Then he remembered, of all things, the gym. Elias played basketball, and his coach had taught them a routine for making free throws in close games: bounce the ball three times, exhale once, look at the hoop, then throw. “When things get loud in your head, you need something to hold on to,” he had said.

Elias put down the pen. He placed both feet firmly on the floor, placed his hands flat on the table and breathed out as long as he could. Then again. The task was still not clear afterward, but his heart beat a little slower.

Instead of reading task 1 a fifth time, he turned the page. Task 3 was an explanatory question: Why is an electron in the field of a plate capacitor accelerated towards the positively charged plate? He knew that. The electron is negatively charged, a force acts on it in the field and it points towards the positive plate. He wrote three sentences, and with each sentence he became calmer.

He then took the concept paper and wrote down everything he could think of about the topic, out of order: C = Q / U. E = U / d. W = ½ C U². Farad as a unit. A sketch with two plates and field lines between them. When I looked at the sketch, the formula that was missing before appeared again: C = ε₀ · εr · A / d. Elias went back to task 1 and was able to solve part a. He didn't quite get to the end of part c, but he wrote down his approach.

## Then: evaluate instead of pondering

The exam came back with nine points. Less than he had planned, but a far cry from the disaster he had seen in the first five minutes. Still, the question lingered in his mind: What if this happens again?

He spoke to Ms. Sander, his physics teacher. She didn't make a drama out of it. “Many people experience a moment like this,” she said. “What’s important is what you make of it.” They went through the exam together. Elias noticed something: he hadn't skimmed through all the tasks first, but immediately started with the longest one. And he hadn't had a plan in case he got hanged. He came up with the free throw routine by accident.

So he wrote a plan, short enough to fit on an index card, and practiced it. With practice tasks at home, with an old timed exam that Mrs. Sander gave him, and once very consciously by starting with the most difficult task to practice switching. In the next exam the wave at the beginning came again, short and weaker. Elias put down the pen, exhaled, scanned the tasks and started with one he was confident about. After a few minutes the wave was over.

## What you can take from it

A blackout feels like all knowledge has been erased. That's not it. It's just hard to achieve for now. The following steps will help you remain able to act in this moment. [Yusuf's story about exam anxiety](/lerngeschichten/pruefungsangst-bewaeltigen/) tells you how you deal with exam anxiety in the weeks beforehand.

### What roughly happens during a blackout

If you suddenly feel very threatened, your body goes on alert. The heart beats faster, the breathing becomes shallow, the muscles tense. This reaction is designed to quickly run away or fight back. Unfortunately, it doesn't help with calm reflection. When you're under so much stress, it's harder to concentrate and recall what you've learned.

The trigger is often a single thought such as “I can’t do that” or “It’s all over now”. It increases the tension, the tension confirms the thought, and the spiral turns. The good news: When your body calms down a bit, access to your knowledge usually returns. A blackout doesn't mean you're unprepared either. It can affect those for whom the exam is particularly important.

### Your emergency plan during the exam

The simpler the plan, the better it works under pressure. You can remember these steps:

1. **Put down pen.** This is your stop signal. You stop forcing the task.
2. **Exhale calmly.** Feet on the floor, hands on the table, exhale slowly three to five times, longer than you inhale.
3. **Change task.** Mark the difficult task with a mark in the margin and continue browsing.
4. **Start with something easy.** A task you are confident in will give you back points and peace of mind.
5. **Write down key words.** Write down everything that comes to mind about the topic on the concept paper: terms, formulas, a sketch. One keyword often leads to the next.
6. **Return with a partial approach.** Write down what is given and what is sought, and what formula or idea might fit. One approach can also bring points.

| What you notice | What you do |
|---|---|
| You read an assignment for the third time without understanding it | Put down the pen, breathe out, mark the task and change |
| Your heart is racing, your hands are shaking | Feet on the floor, exhale slowly and long |
| “I can’t do anything” runs in my head | Replace the thought: "I'm stuck on one task. I'll look at the rest." |
| You are missing a formula or a term | Keywords and sketch on the concept paper, then return |
| You realize that time is running out | Focus on the tasks with the most achievable points |

### Time reserve and order of tasks

Many blackouts occur at the beginning when you immediately jump into a difficult task. A few habits take pressure off:

- **First skim through all the tasks.** Take two to three minutes, read each task briefly and mark the ones that are easy for you.
- **Divide time according to points.** An example: If you have 90 minutes for 60 points and plan 15 minutes in reserve, 75 minutes remain. That's 1.25 minutes per point, so for a 12 point task it's about 15 minutes. Write the planned end time next to each task.
- **Start with a safe task** if the order is optional. That gets you going.
- **Leave space.** If you skip a task, leave space on your paper so you can continue writing properly later.
- **Protect the reserve.** It is intended for returning to open tasks and a quick check at the end.

[Elif's plan for the last 24 hours](/lerngeschichten/tag-vor-der-pruefung/) shows how you can approach the first few minutes of an exam calmly and plan the day before.

### Practice an emergency card beforehand

An emergency plan only works if you don't have to invent it during the exam. Write it on an index card, as short as possible, for example:

**STOP:** Pen away. **BREATHE:** exhale three times. **CHANGE:** Sign to the edge, next task. **COLLECT:** Keywords on the concept paper. **BACK:** given, sought, approach.

Practice the card in normal situations so that it comes to mind on your own during the exam:

- when doing homework, when you are stuck on a task,
- for an old exam or timed practice tasks,
- consciously start with the hardest task first in order to practice switching.

Important: The card belongs in your head, not in your pencil case. Only take what is expressly permitted into an exam. Your teacher will tell you which aids are permitted.

### Evaluate after the exam instead of pondering

After a blackout, many people tend to spend hours wondering why it happened to them. That rarely helps. A short, sober evaluation is better, ideally on the same day. Elias answered it like this:

| Question | Elias' answer |
|---|---|
| When did it start? | After three minutes, on the first task |
| Which thought came first? | “If I can’t do this, I can’t do the rest.” |
| What helped? | Breathe out, change tasks, put formulas on the concept paper |
| What didn't help? | Always read the task faster |
| What do I do differently next time? | First, skim everything and start with a safe task |

Then you put the piece of paper away. When the exam comes back, you review it with these answers in mind and speak to your teacher if necessary. Pondering asks “Why is this happening to me of all people?” Evaluating asks “What do I do next time?” Only the second question will get you further.

### When help makes sense

A single blackout is unpleasant, but usually nothing to worry about. Get support if blockages repeat themselves in many exams, if you would prefer to avoid exams altogether out of fear, if you hardly sleep for days or if severe physical complaints occur.

Good first contacts are your trusted or advisory teacher and school social work. School psychological counseling also supports students with exam anxiety. Physical complaints such as a racing heart or nausea that occur again and again should be clarified by a family doctor. You can find contact points and contact details on our [Help & Advice](/hilfe-und-beratung/) page.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Wanting to force the task.** Reading faster and faster increases the pressure instead of reducing it.
- **Fight the excitement.** “I can’t be nervous now” usually makes you more nervous. Breathing out and continuing to work helps more.
- **Start without an overview.** If you start immediately with task 1, you might miss the easy points later on.
- **Turn in blank sheets.** A noted approach shows what you can do and often earns partial points.
- **Never practice your emergency plan.** You won't come up with a plan you've only read when you're under stress.

## Briefly asked

### Can I prevent a blackout completely?

Not always. However, you can reduce the risk by preparing well, practicing exam situations and starting with a safe task. And you can learn to end a blackout faster.

### Can I go outside for a moment during the exam?

Schools regulate this differently, so ask beforehand. In many cases it is easier to stay put and use the emergency plan.

### Should I tell the teacher I blacked out?

This can make sense, especially if it happens often. Many teachers will give you practice material or help you become more familiar with the exam situation. You can find more stories about exams and exams in the chapter [Exams & Exam Anxiety](/themen/pruefungen-und-pruefungsangst/).
