"A swimming pool is filled with two pumps. The first pump alone would take six hours, the second alone four hours..." Sara didn't read any further. She read the beginning again, then again. The words made sense individually, but together they formed a wall. On the page in front of it were twenty equations, all of which she had solved correctly. But there was no equation here. Just words. Sara closed the book and said out loud to no one, “Why can’t they just write this down normally?”

## Calculate yes, understand no

Sara was fifteen and in the ninth grade of a secondary school. When Mr. Peters wrote a calculation on the board, she was often the first to get the result. Her arithmetic skills were good. But as soon as a task came in text, it was like someone turned off the lights.

In her work, she often left out word tasks entirely in order to have time for other tasks. This cost a lot of points every time, because the word problems often gave the most. Her grade fluctuated between three and four, even though she could have gotten a two in pure arithmetic.

Her friend Johanna didn't understand that. “You just have to write the numbers out and do the math,” she said. Sara tried that. She wrote out all the numbers and did some math with them, mostly what was currently in class. Sometimes it was true, most of the time it wasn't. She had no idea why.

## A detective without a magnifying glass

The turning point came during a substitute lesson. Ms. Albers, who actually taught German, was supposed to supervise the class in math and have them complete tasks from the book. As she walked through the rows, she stopped at Sara, who was sitting in front of another word problem and chewing on her pen.

“What does it say?” asked Ms. Albers. Sara read the assignment. “And what are we looking for?” Sara hesitated. She didn't know exactly. In the end, she didn't even read the question correctly.

Ms. Albers smiled. "It's like a crime story. Before the detective looks for the perpetrator, he first has to know what actually happened. Read the text again, slowly, and then tell me in your own words what it's about. Without numbers."

Sara read. Then she said: "Someone buys a cell phone on installments. They first pay a down payment and then a fixed amount every month. And the question is how much they have paid in total at the end." Ms. Albers nodded. "And now? What do you need?"

Suddenly it was clear. Down payment plus monthly rate times number of months. Sara wrote down the bill. She agreed.

## Five steps on a piece of paper

After the lesson, Sara went to Mr. Peters and told him about it. He was pleased and gave her a piece of paper that he usually only gave out during special education classes. It had five steps for word problems. Read. Understand. Plan. Calculate. Check.

Sara stuck the note inside her math notebook. Over the next few weeks, she worked through each word task in these five steps, even if it took longer at first. She underlined what was given, marked the question with a different color and wrote down in one sentence what it was about. For many tasks she made a small sketch.

The sketches became her favorite tool. In the pump task, she drew a basin and two pipes, and suddenly she saw that both pumps were working at the same time. The first filled a sixth of the pool in an hour, the second a quarter. In total, five twelfths per hour. Sara sat with this realization for a while and was almost a little proud.

She also made a list of signal words that she collected from the tasks. “Total”, “together”, “per”, “ever”, “more than”, “less than”, “increased by…”, “decreased to…”. Mr. Peters warned them that signal words are only clues, not a recipe. But they helped her initially understand.

## Working with three word problems

The next paper on linear functions contained three word problems, including one about a cell phone plan with a basic fee and a cost per gigabyte. In the past, Sara would have skipped over them. This time she read the task twice, marked the basic fee and the price per gigabyte and wrote below: “Wanted: From how many gigabytes is tariff B cheaper?”

She created an equation for both tariffs, equated them and did the math. In the end she tested it by putting her number in both tariffs. The results matched. She wrote an answer sentence underneath and moved on to the next task.

There was a two under the work. She got almost all the points on the word problems. Mr. Peters wrote in the margin: “It’s great how you structure the tasks.” Sara showed the comment to Johanna. “You see,” said Johanna. “Just write out the numbers.” Sarah laughed. It wasn't that easy after all. But it was doable.

## What you can take from it

Many people who struggle with word problems are actually good at math. The problem lies in translation: turning a situation into a mathematical calculation in words. This skill can be learned like a language. With a fixed approach, the puzzle becomes a solvable task.

### The five steps for every word task

These steps work for almost all word problems, from elementary school to final exams:

1. **Read.** Read the entire task calmly once without doing any calculations straight away. Then read it a second time, marking as you go.
2. **Understand.** Explain to yourself what it's about in your own words, preferably without numbers. What happens in the story? What is given, what is sought?
3. **Plan.** Think about which calculation steps you need and in which order. Write down a short plan, for example “first the total price, then the discount”.
4. **Calculate.** Do the calculation cleanly, with units.
5. **Check.** Does the result make sense? Is the size right? Is the sample correct? Write an answer sentence.

### Make given and sought visible

Mark everything in the text with one color and what you are looking for with another. Write both below in a small list:

| Given | Wanted |
|---|---|
| Deposit: €80 | Total price |
| Monthly rate: €25 | |
| Term: 24 months | |

This way you can see at a glance what you can work with. Also pay attention to hidden information, such as “a dozen” for twelve or “a week” for seven days. And sometimes there are numbers in the text that you don't even need. This is intentional to check close reading.

### Work with sketches and tables

A drawing suddenly makes many tasks more understandable. It's not about art, but about overview:

- **For movement tasks** a line with start, finish and arrows for directions helps.
- **For geometry** you draw the figure and enter all known lengths and angles.
- **For percentage and interest problems**, a bar that represents the basic value and shows shares helps.
- **For tariffs and comparisons**, a table with several values ​​next to each other is suitable.
- **For assignments**, a two-column table helps, like the rule of three.

### Use signal words as clues

Certain words often indicate certain types of calculations. But they are only hints, not hard and fast rules, because the meaning always depends on the context.

| word in the text | Common meaning |
|---|---|
| together, overall, sum | addition |
| difference, less than, left | Subtraction |
| each, per, each, three times | Multiplication |
| distribute, share, a third of it | Division or fraction |
| by…increased, by…decreased | Change by an amount or percentage |
| to… increased, to… lowered | new value after the change |

The difference between “around” and “up” is particularly tricky. “The price will be reduced by 20 percent” means something different than “the price will be reduced to 20 percent.” Read these places particularly carefully.

### Always check the result

Many errors in word problems are noticeable if you look at the result with common sense:

- **Check the order of magnitude.** Can a bicycle really go 4,000 kilometers per hour? Can a person be 300 years old?
- **Check units.** Do meters fit meters and minutes fit minutes? Do you have to convert?
- **Take a test.** Insert your result into the task and check whether everything works out.
- **Write answer sentence.** It forces you to look at the question again and often brings a point of its own in exams.

### Practice targeted

Word problems become easier with practice, especially if you work on them consciously:

1. **Few tasks thoroughly instead of many quickly.** Three word problems with all five steps produce more than ten frantically calculated ones.
2. **Invent your own word problems.** When you write your own story about an invoice, you understand the path in both directions.
3. **Think out loud.** Explain to someone else how you do it. You will quickly notice where your understanding is still shaky.
4. **Collect errors.** Note which step you failed at. Reading, planning or arithmetic? [Fynn's story about the error booklet](/lerngeschichten/mathe-ueben-mit-fehlerheft/) shows how this can be done systematically.

If the same errors keep cropping up when calculating, for example with fractions or signs, there may be an older gap behind them. Then [Jannik's story about math gaps](/lerngeschichten/mathe-luecken-finden-und-schliessen/) will help you.

### In the exam

Word problems take time, but often bring a lot of points. So don't read it last, but get an overview at the beginning of your work. If you don't get anywhere right away, at least write down Given and Wanted and make a sketch. Often there are already sub-points for this, and sometimes the route comes to you while you are working on another task.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Start calculating immediately.** Anyone who writes out the numbers before understanding the situation often makes the wrong calculations.
- **Skip the question.** Some tasks ask for an intermediate result, others for something completely different than expected.
- **Forget units.** Minutes and hours, centimeters and meters are often mixed up.
- **Follow signal words blindly.** “More” does not always mean plus. The context decides.
- **Do not write an answer sentence.** This often costs points and a final check option.

## Briefly asked

### Why are word tasks more difficult for me than normal tasks?

Because they require two skills: close reading and translation in mathematics. If you only practice arithmetic, you won't practice the second part. However, with a firm approach and a little practice, translating becomes much easier.

### Will it help if I read more?

Yes, close reading is very important for word problems. Anyone who reads texts carefully and understands connections also has an advantage in math. Therefore, practice reading slowly and with a pen in your hand when doing word problems.

### What if German is not my first language?

Then unknown words can make understanding even more difficult. Make a small list of typical words from word problems and ask questions if any terms are unclear. Many teachers like to explain a word without revealing the solution.
