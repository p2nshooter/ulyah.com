Fifteen pages. Jana read the task in the university's learning portal for the third time. “Homework in the introductory seminar introduction to political science, 12 to 15 pages, due six weeks after the end of the lecture.” She was allowed to choose the topic herself, as long as it fit the seminar. At school she had never written more than five pages at a time, and then only for a technical paper in which her teacher had specified every step. Now there was no one to tell her what to do and when. Jana closed the laptop. Six weeks after the end of lectures. That was still a long time coming.

## Six weeks that go by quickly

Jana was nineteen and was studying political science and sociology in her first semester. The first few weeks of studying were a whirlwind of new impressions, lectures, first-year parties and the search for a part-time job. She pushed the housework to the back of her mind.

After the lectures ended, she had two exams and took a week's break. When she wanted to start doing the housework, there were only four weeks left. And she still didn't have a topic.

She spent two days clicking through the Internet without knowing what she was actually looking for. She read newspaper articles, watched videos and ended up feeling more confused than before. On the third day she wrote a desperate message to her seminar leader, Dr. Hartmann, and asked for a consultation appointment.

## The consultation hour

Dr. Hartmann received them warmly in a small office full of books. “Many first semester students are exactly where they are now,” she said. "That's normal. The most important step is to turn a topic into a question."

Jana said she was interested in voter participation, especially among young people. Dr. Hartmann nodded. "That's a topic, but not a question yet. What exactly do you want to know?"

Jana thought about it. “Why are young people less likely to vote?”

"It's too big for fifteen pages. There are entire books about it. Can you narrow it down? To one country, one election, one specific statement?"

After ten minutes they had a question that Jana could answer: “What role does political interest play in the voter turnout of young adults in Germany?” Dr. Hartmann recommended two introductory works and showed her how to search for articles using the library catalog and specialist databases. “And make a schedule,” she concluded. “Backwards from the deadline.”

## A plan on the wall

At home, Jana took a large sheet of paper and drew a timeline of the remaining four weeks. She planned backwards from the deadline:

Last three days: proofreading, formatting, checking bibliography. The week before: writing the main part and revising. The week before: outline and first drafts of the introduction and theory part. The first week and a half: searching for literature, reading, excerpts.

She entered a small goal for each day. Not “write a term paper,” but “read three essays and take notes” or “write section 2.1, about two pages.” This suddenly made the huge task manageable.

## Reading systematically

In the library, Jana found more literature than she could read. She quickly learned that she didn't have to read every book from cover to cover. She first looked at the table of contents, read the introduction and conclusion of essays, and then decided which parts were important to her question.

For each text she created a note in a document: a complete reference, the most important statements in her own words, direct quotations with page numbers, and a sentence about how the text related to her question. These notes, called excerpts, later became her most important tool.

After a week and a half, she had worked through twelve texts and had an initial overview. She noted that there were various explanations in research for the lower voter turnout among young people, and that political interest was just one of them. This became an important part of her work.

## Write without being perfect

Writing was the hardest thing for Jana. She sat in front of a blank page and wanted the first sentence to be perfect. After an hour she had written three sentences and deleted them all.

Her fellow student Merve, with whom she sometimes sat in the library, gave her a tip: "Write poorly first. The main thing is that there is something there. You can revise it later." Jana tried it. She wrote the body first, not the introduction, and allowed herself to write clumsy sentences. The next day she read her text and corrected it. It happened much faster.

She wrote the introduction at the very end, when she knew what was in her work. She introduced the question, explained why it was relevant, and gave an overview of the structure. The conclusion summarized the results and answered the question.

## The levy

Jana finished two days before the deadline. She read the work out loud again, checked every reference and asked Merve to proofread the work. Merve found three typos and one paragraph that was unclear. Jana revised it and submitted the work a day before the deadline.

A few weeks later she received a grade of 2.0. Dr. Hartmann had noted in the margin: “Clear question, good literature work, argumentation can still be deepened in some places.” Jana was proud. And she knew now that next time she would start earlier.

## What you can take from it

A term paper is a project over several weeks, not an essay that you write in a weekend. With a clear question, a realistic schedule and a step-by-step approach, the big task becomes a series of achievable steps.

### From topic to research question

A topic is an area, a research question is a specific question that you answer in your work. Here's how to get from one to the other:

1. **Find interest.** Which topic from the seminar interests you?
2. **Narrow down.** To a time period, a country, a group, an aspect or a theory.
3. **Formulate it as a question.** Not “voter turnout”, but “What role does political interest play in the voter turnout of young adults in Germany?”
4. **Check feasibility.** Is there enough literature? Can the question be answered within the given scope?
5. **Discuss with the teacher.** A short consultation saves many hours in the wrong direction.

### Building the schedule backwards

Plan backwards from the deadline. A rough plan for a term paper of around fifteen pages could look like this:

| phase | Proportion of time | Tasks |
|---|---|---|
| Topic and question | about 10% | Find a topic, formulate a question, consultation hours |
| Literature | about 30% | search, read, excerpt |
| Outline | about 10% | Determine the structure, plan the argument |
| Write | about 35% | Main part, then introduction and conclusion |
| Revise | about 15% | Correction, formatting, bibliography |

Always plan a buffer. Illness, part-time work or other exams can get in the way.

### Search and read literature

You can find scientific literature primarily through the catalog of your university library and through specialist databases that your library offers. Good starting points are also introductory works and bibliographies of texts that you have already found. A targeted approach helps when reading:

- **Get an overview.** Read the table of contents, introduction and conclusion first.
- **Select.** Read carefully only the parts that are important to your question.
- **Excerpt.** Write down important statements in your own words, record quotations with page numbers.
- **Note down sources immediately.** Write down every reference in its entirety, not just at the end.

[Ida's story about reading technical texts](/lerngeschichten/fachtexte-lesen-und-verstehen/) also shows how you can access specialist texts in a targeted manner.

### Create an outline

A typical term paper consists of an introduction, main part and conclusion. The main part is often divided into a theoretical and an analytical part. Your outline should show how you can get to the answer to your question step by step. A mind map can help with organization, as in [Luke's story about mind maps](/lerngeschichten/mindmap-erstellen/).

### Write in stages

- **Start with the main part.** You write the introduction and conclusion at the end.
- **Write first, then improve.** A rough draft is better than a blank page.
- **Set daily goals.** For example, one to two pages per day.
- **Fixed writing times.** Mornings or at your most productive time.
- **Prove.** Every statement that does not come from you needs to be cited as a source.

### Quote correctly

Scientific work means identifying alien thoughts. Place direct quotations in quotation marks and cite the source with the page number. Appropriate transfers also require a source citation. The citation method required is usually stated in a guide from your institute. If you are unsure, ask. Your university regulates whether and how you can use AI tools. Find out beforehand, as unauthorized use can be considered an attempt to deceive.

### Revise and submit

Allow enough time for revision. Read your work from a distance, preferably out loud. Check whether each section contributes to the question, whether the argument is logical, and whether all sources are correctly cited. If possible, have someone else proofread it. Finally, check the formal specifications: scope, font size, cover page, bibliography and, if necessary, a declaration of independence.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Starting too late.** Time flies faster than you think.
- **Too broad a topic.** A big question cannot be answered in a few pages.
- **Read endlessly.** At some point you have to start writing, even if you haven't read everything.
- **Do not note sources.** In the end, page numbers are missing and the search takes hours.
- **Perfectionism in the first draft.** It prevents writing.

## Briefly asked

### How many sources do I need for a term paper?

This depends on the subject, the topic and the requirements of your institute. More important than the number is the quality: scientific literature that fits your question. Ask your teacher what is expected.

### Can I quote Wikipedia?

This is usually not common in scientific work. However, Wikipedia can serve as an introduction to getting an overview and using the literature references there to access scientific sources.

### What if I don't make the deadline?

Contact your teacher or the examination office early on. In justified cases, such as illness, extensions are sometimes possible. The rules for this are in the examination regulations.
