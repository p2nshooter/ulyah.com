Lukas leafed through his folder. Fourteen pages of notes on the French Revolution, neatly copied, with headings and dates. Estates General, Ballhaus Oath, Storming of the Bastille, Declaration of Human Rights, Reign of Terror, Napoleon. He knew each event individually. But when Mr. Demir asked during the lesson how it all connected, Lukas couldn't utter a single sensible sentence. The exam was in two weeks. And he knew that a question like that would come up in the exam.

## Lots of trees, no forest

Lukas was seventeen and was in the eleventh grade of a vocational high school with a focus on economics. For him, history was a compulsory subject that he neither loved nor hated. He studied conscientiously, took notes, read up. But his notes were like a long road without intersections: one event after another, in chronological order, without connections.

In the exams he usually received full points for pure knowledge questions. However, in tasks such as “Explain the causes…” or “Assess…” he was missing the connections. His answers remained lists, and each time Mr. Demir wrote in the margin: “Make connections!”

His classmate Johanna, with whom he often worked together in economics classes, once showed him her study materials. Instead of long pages, it had large leaves with a word in the middle and branches extending in all directions. “Mind maps,” she said. “I can then see everything at a glance.” At the time, Lukas thought it was painting. Now he was ready to try it out.

## The first attempt

Lukas took a large sheet of paper, laid it across and wrote in the middle: “French Revolution”. Then he drew main branches for the major areas that came to mind: causes, course, consequences, people. Smaller branches sprouted from each main branch. Under “causes” he wrote: national debt, class society, enlightenment, bad harvests.

After half an hour the paper was full. It looked chaotic, but for the first time Luke saw the whole topic on one page. He noticed that some branches were much thicker than others and that he had little knowledge of the consequences. He never noticed this gap in the fourteen pages of notes.

The next day he showed the mind map to Johanna. She nodded approvingly, then pointed to two branches. "The national debt and the convening of the Estates General are directly related, aren't they? The king needed money and therefore had to convene the Estates." Lukas looked at the paper. In his mind map, the two terms were on completely different branches. The connection between them was missing.

## From the mind map to the concept map

Mr. Demir, to whom Lukas showed the mind map after the lesson, had an idea. "A mind map is good for collecting and organizing a topic. But there is another form for connections: the concept map, sometimes also called a conceptual network."

He drew a few terms in boxes on the board and connected them with arrows. He wrote a verb on each arrow: “National debt – leads to – the convocation of the Estates General.” “Third Estate – declares itself to be – National Assembly.” “Enlightenment – ​​provides ideas for – explanation of human rights.”

"The difference is," said Mr. Demir, "that a mind map grows from the inside out like a tree. A concept map is a network. Any term can be connected to any other, and the label on the arrow tells you how."

Lukas tried it at home. He took the twenty most important terms from his mind map, wrote each one on a small sticky note, and spread them across his desk. Then he moved them back and forth until connections became visible, and finally drew everything on a large sheet of paper with labeled arrows.

This was significantly more strenuous than the mind map. With some arrows he didn't know what to write on them. He looked up exactly these passages in the book. After two evenings, he had a network that showed how economic hardship, political ideas and social tensions interacted. Lukas realized that he could now explain why the revolution broke out, not just when.

## The exam

In the exam, the second task was: “Explain how economic and political causes contributed to the outbreak of the French Revolution.” Lukas closed his eyes briefly and saw his concept map in front of him. The national debt in the left corner, the arrow to the Estates General, from there to the Third Estate and the National Assembly. He wrote a short outline on his concept paper and began.

His answer was no longer a list, but a chain of reasons and consequences. Underneath the exam there was a B plus and the comment: “Very good connection of the causes.” Lukas hung the concept map over his desk. For the next topic, industrialization, he started with a large sheet.

## What you can take from it

Long linear notes are good for capturing information. However, they are often too bulky to provide an overview and connections. Mind maps and concept maps make the structure of a topic visible. As you create, you also have to decide for yourself what is important and how it is related. And it is precisely this reflection that is already learning.

### Mind map or concept map?

Both forms have their strengths:

| | Mind map | Concept map |
|---|---|---|
| Construction | a central theme, branches outwards | Terms as a network, connected in any way |
| Connections | mostly unlabeled, just superordination and subordination | labeled arrows explaining the relationship |
| Good for | Collecting, organizing, overview, brainstorming | Causes, effects, processes, complex relationships |
| Effort | low to medium | medium to high |
| Typical use | Plan a presentation, repeat the topic, structure the essay | Prepare for an exam in history, biology, politics or economics |

You can also combine the two, like Lukas: first collect and organize with a mind map, then combine the most important terms in a concept map.

### How to create a mind map

1. **Lay the sheet horizontally.** A large, unlined sheet in landscape format gives you space in all directions.
2. **Topic in the middle.** Write the central topic in large letters in the middle and circle it.
3. **Draw main branches.** Think of four to seven general terms and draw a thick branch for each.
4. **Add sub-branches.** Thinner branches with sub-points branch off from each main branch.
5. **Keywords instead of sentences.** Only one word or a very short term belongs on each branch.
6. **Use colors and symbols.** Each main branch gets a color. Small drawings or symbols help remember.
7. **Mark gaps.** Where you have little idea, put a question mark. These are your learning tasks.

### How to create a concept map

1. **Collect terms.** Write out the ten to twenty most important terms on a topic, preferably on individual pieces of paper.
2. **Arrange.** Place the pieces of paper so that related terms are close together. Important or overarching terms are placed at the top or in the middle.
3. **Connect.** Draw arrows between terms that are related.
4. **Label arrows.** Write a verb or short phrase on each arrow: “leads to,” “is part of,” “influences,” “contradicts.”
5. **Look for cross-connections.** Are there connections between distant areas? These are often particularly revealing.
6. **Check.** Read each compound as a sentence. Does it make sense? Would your teacher agree?

The labeled arrows are the heart of it. If you don't know what goes on an arrow, you've found a place where your understanding is still patchy.

### Learn with maps, not just paint

A nice map on the wall doesn't help much if you just look at it. How to use them to learn:

- **Draw from memory.** Put the finished map away and try redrawing it on a blank sheet of paper. Compare afterwards. Whatever is missing, you repeat specifically.
- **Explain out loud.** Tell someone how the terms are related, following the arrows.
- **Derive exam questions.** Each connection can be a question: “Why did the national debt lead to the convocation of the Estates General?”
- **Add regularly.** As new content is added in class, expand your map.

Tracing from memory is particularly effective because you actively recall knowledge instead of just recognizing it. Why this is so important is explained in [Hannah's story about spaced repetition](/lerngeschichten/verteiltes-wiederholen-statt-pauken/).

### Analogue or digital?

Both can work well. You often think more freely on paper and retain more through drawing. Digital programs have the advantage that you can easily move, add and share terms. If you work digitally, make sure you don't spend more time designing than thinking about the content.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Too much text on the branches.** Complete sentences make a mind map confusing. Keywords are enough.
- **Unlabeled arrows in the concept map.** Without labels, the exact information you want to learn is missing.
- **Just copy.** Copying a map from the book is of little use. The benefit comes from organizing and linking things yourself.
- **Start too late.** A good concept map takes time. The evening before the exam it is usually too late for this.
- **Strive for perfection.** Your map doesn't have to be pretty, it has to help you. Crossing out and redrawing are included.

## Briefly asked

### Which subjects are mind maps suitable for?

For almost everyone. They are particularly helpful in subjects with many connections, such as history, biology, geography, politics, economics or German, when it comes to text analysis. In math, they help more with an overview of topics than with the calculation itself.

### How big should a mind map be?

So big that it remains clear. One sheet of paper in A4 or A3 format is usually sufficient for a lesson topic. If it gets too crowded, divide the topic into several maps.
