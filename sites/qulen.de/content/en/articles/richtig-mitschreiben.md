Ole's hand hurt. Mr. Neumann spoke quickly and Ole tried to write down every word. “Photosynthesis takes place in the chloroplasts, using light energy…” Ole scribbled as quickly as he could. While he was still writing the word “light energy,” Mr. Neumann was already two sentences further. Ole left out a few words, made dots and hoped to understand later what was meant. At the end of the lesson he had four pages full of writing. And when he read it that evening, he barely understood anything about it.

## Writing instead of listening

Ole was sixteen and had just moved on to the introductory phase of high school. In middle school, most teachers had written on blackboard pictures that you simply copied. Now many spoke freely, showed slides, discussed with the class and expected the students to capture what was important for themselves.

Ole had concluded that he had to write everything down if possible. That way he wouldn't miss anything. But the opposite happened. Because he was so busy writing, he didn't really listen. He wrote down sentences whose meaning he didn't want to understand until later. And when Mr. Neumann asked the class a question, Ole realized that he had no idea what it was actually about.

His notes were long but messy. Between important definitions there were asides, examples and half-sentences that he could no longer place. As the first biology exam approached, he realized that his notes were hardly useful for studying.

## A double lesson without a pen

The next week Mr. Neumann did something unusual. “Today, please all of you put down your pens,” he said. "I'll explain cellular respiration to you. You just listen. At the end you'll get five minutes to write down the most important things."

Ole felt uncomfortable without a pen in his hand. But after a few minutes he noticed something: he was following the explanations much better. He heard when Mr. Neumann raised his voice or repeated something. He noticed when a new section began. And he understood how the individual steps were connected.

In the five minutes at the end he wrote half a page: the three main steps of cellular respiration, where they take place, what comes out at the end. Short keywords, an arrow here and there. When he read half the page that evening, he understood it immediately.

In the next hour, Mr. Neumann explained what he wanted to show. "Taking notes doesn't mean copying. It means: listening, understanding, selecting, recording. Your head has to do the work, not just your hand."

## The divided sheet

Mr. Neumann also introduced the class to a method that he had used in his studies: the Cornell Method. Each page is divided into three areas. On the right there is a wide column for the actual notes during class. On the left there is a narrow column for keywords and questions that can be added after class. And below is a strip for a brief summary in your own words.

Ole tried it. He used the ruler to draw a line about a third of the way from the left edge and a line a few inches above the bottom edge. In class he wrote down on the right, but no longer everything, just definitions, main statements, examples that helped him and questions that occurred to him. He developed his own abbreviations: “PS” for photosynthesis, “ZA” for cellular respiration, an arrow for “leads to,” an exclamation mark in the margin for things that Mr. Neumann had emphasized as important.

In the afternoon he took ten minutes to follow up. He read through his notes, added what was missing, and wrote keywords and questions in the left column. “Where does cellular respiration take place?” “What’s the difference from photosynthesis?” Below he summarized the lesson in two or three sentences.

## Learn with the left column

Ole only discovered the best thing about the method before the exam. He covered the right column with a sheet and tried to answer the questions in the left column from memory. Wherever he didn't know what to do, he looked. So he tested himself, without any additional flashcards.

The exam was not an easy one, but Ole felt prepared. Not only did he have notes, he understood them. There was a two under the exam. But Ole found something else even more important: he no longer sat rushed in class. He listened, thought along and sometimes even spoke up. His hand didn't hurt anymore either.

## What you can take from it

Good notes are not created through speed, but through selection. Anyone who tries to write everything down no longer listens properly. Those who specifically take notes understand more and then have documents that are easy to learn from.

### Listen first, write second

The most important rule when taking notes is: first understand, then note. Listen to a thought all the way through before writing it down. Then put it in your own words and as briefly as possible. It's unusual at first because you feel like you're missing out on something. But you miss less than if you write in the middle of a sentence and don't hear the next thought.

### Recognize what is important

Teachers often give hints about what is particularly important. Pay attention to these signals:

| signal | What it means |
|---|---|
| “This is important”, “Remember…” | almost certainly relevant to the exam |
| Something is said repeatedly or more slowly | central content |
| Something is written on the board or on a slide | Core information |
| Lists like “There are three reasons…” | Structure you should adopt |
| Definitions and technical terms | Basis for understanding and testing |
| Summaries at the end of the lesson | the most important thing in short form |

Not everything that is said belongs in your notes. You can usually leave out anecdotes, repetitions and organizational notes unless they help you understand.

### Abbreviations and symbols

With your own abbreviations you can write faster without losing the meaning. A few examples:

- **Arrows:** → for “leads to” or “follows from this”, ← for “because of” or “caused by”
- **Mathematical symbols:** = for “is” or “means”, ≠ for “is not”, + for “and” or “in addition”
- **Frequent words:** e.g. B., or, v. a. for “above all”, ex. for “example”, def. for “definition”
- **Technical abbreviations:** own abbreviations for terms that occur often, such as “PS” for photosynthesis
- **Marginal note:** ! for important, ? for unclear, P for relevant to the exam

It is important that you still understand your abbreviations weeks later. Create a small legend at the front of your notebook or folder.

### The Cornell Method

The Cornell Method is a proven way to take notes so that you can study well later:

1. **Divide the page.** Draw a vertical line about a third of the way from the left edge and a horizontal line a few inches above the bottom edge.
2. **In class: right column.** Here you write down keywords, definitions, examples and sketches.
3. **After class: left column.** Add key terms and questions that relate to the notes.
4. **Summary below.** Summarize the lesson in two or three sentences in your own words.
5. **To study: cover the right column.** Answer the questions on the left from memory and check yourself.

The method combines note-taking, follow-up and self-testing in one sheet.

### Follow-up: ten minutes that are worth it

A note is only really useful if you revise it promptly, ideally on the same day. You can:

- complete incomplete sentences,
- Clarify any ambiguities with the book or classmates,
- Write down questions you want to ask in the next lesson,
- Mark important things in color,
- write a short summary.

The longer you wait, the less you remember what was meant between the lines. Well-prepared notes quickly produce good study notes before an exam. [Nele's story about learning sheets](/lerngeschichten/lernzettel-schreiben/) shows how this works.

### Take notes with a laptop or tablet

If your school allows digital notes, a tablet with a pen can be a good solution because you can write and draw in a similar way to paper. When typing on a laptop, however, there is a risk that you will try to write everything down verbatim because it happens so quickly. Also make sure notifications are turned off. [Ben's story about using cell phones while studying](/lerngeschichten/handy-ablenkung-beim-lernen/) tells how you can get distraction from devices under control.

### Taking notes in conversations and discussions

Not every hour is a lecture. In discussions or group work, it is worth noting down every contribution, but rather the most important arguments and results. A simple structure helps: arguments in favor on the left, arguments against on the right, and the result of the discussion below. If you think in a structured way, you can also have a better say and score points verbally.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Write everything down verbatim.** You stop listening and understand less.
- **Don't take notes at all.** If you only rely on your memory, you won't have much material before the exam.
- **Abbreviations that no one will understand later.** Not even you. Create a legend.
- **Never follow up.** Disorganized notes quickly lose their value.
- **Don't ask questions.** What remains unclear in the lesson rarely becomes clearer later. A question mark in the margin reminds you to ask.

## Briefly asked

### Should I take photos of slides instead of taking notes?

A photo can help capture a complicated graphic if your teacher agrees. However, it does not replace taking notes yourself. When you write, you have to choose and put it into your own words, and that's exactly what helps you understand.

### How much should I write down per hour?

That depends on the subject and the lesson. As a rough guide, one to two pages per hour is often enough for a lecture if you use keywords and abbreviations. What is more important than the quantity is that you have grasped the main points.

### What if I missed something?

Mark the spot with a question mark and ask a classmate or the teacher about the lesson. Many teachers also provide slides or materials that you can use to fill in gaps.
