“The beach,” it said. Frida stared at the two words as if they could tell her the solution on their own. She knew she knew that word. She had learned it three weeks ago for the vocabulary test for Unité 2, and it was correct in the test. Now there was just an empty feeling. “La… la…” She didn’t write anything down.

## Learned, mastered, forgotten

Frida was twelve and in the seventh grade of a high school. She had studied French since the beginning of the school year and actually liked the subject. She liked the sound of it, and Madame Lehmann, as the class called their French teacher, taught well. Only the vocabulary was always the same: On the Sunday evening before the test, Frida sat down with the book, covered the right column of the vocabulary list, mumbled, uncovered, mumbled again. An hour, sometimes more. On Monday she wrote a B.

Then Madame Lehmann announced a repeat test after the autumn break, vocabulary from Units 1 and 2. Frida got a four. She recognized most of the words immediately when she saw the solutions. "La plage! Of course!" But she hadn't thought of it herself. At home she slammed the magazine on the desk. “I learned it!”

## A box with five compartments

After the next hour, Madame Lehmann asked her to stay for a moment. She took a flat cardboard box out of her bag, divided into five compartments, the first narrow, the last wide. “You didn’t learn wrong,” she said. "You only learned once. Your memory remembers what it needs again and again. If you only see a word on a Sunday evening, it thinks: That can go away."

Then she explained the principle. Each vocabulary word gets its own card. New cards go into the first compartment. If you know a word, the card moves one slot further. If you don't know, it goes back to the beginning. You practice the first subject every day, the second subject less and less often. “So you spend your time on the words you don’t know yet and not on the ones you already know.”

“Every day?” asked Frida. “I still have math and bio too.”

“Ten minutes,” said Madame Lehmann. "No more. That's less than your Sunday hour when you add it up."

## Ten minutes after lunch

At the weekend, Frida built her own box out of a shoebox and four strips of cardboard. She bought flashcards in A7 format and wrote down the vocabulary from the current unit. That took longer than expected. After an hour she had twenty-five cards and her hand hurt. However, Madame Lehmann had said: “Writing is the first step in learning.” In fact, Frida already knew a few words afterwards.

Their first cards looked like this: “plage – beach”. When she showed Madame Lehmann a few of them on Monday, the teacher circled the beginning. “Without an article, you won’t know later whether it’s le or la, and then your adjectives won’t be right.” Frida rewrote the cards, this time with an article, a short example sentence and a note on pronunciation.

Then the routine began. Every day after lunch she set the kitchen timer for ten minutes and went through the first compartment, and on some days a back compartment too. She read the German page, said the answer out loud and only then turned the card over. On the bus, she initially only asked herself questions, until her friend Merle became curious and they asked each other questions. She wrote down words that she had to translate into French on a scrap paper, because accents also counted in the test.

After two weeks, the first cards were in compartment 3. Frida noticed that she really enjoyed pushing them along. And she started using the new words in her own sentences: "Le samedi, je vais au marché avec ma grand-mère. J'achète des pommes." Madame Lehmann corrected the sentences every now and then and put a small star under them.

## The next retest

Shortly before Christmas the next repeat test came, this time over three units. Frida was nervous, but different than usual. She knew that most of the words had already passed through her hands several times. When “the beach” was on the page, she wrote without hesitation: la plage. She got a B. And this time she knew the words would still be there in a few weeks.

On the Sunday evening before the next vocabulary test, she no longer sat with the list. The cards for the new Unité had long been in the box.

## What you can take from it

Learning from the list the night before works for the next morning, but rarely for the weeks after. This is exactly where the index box comes in. The principle goes back to Sebastian Leitner, who described it in a book in the 1970s: What you can do is done less often, and what you can't do more often. The fact that distributed repetition is better remembered than one-time cramming is one of the well-documented findings of learning research.

### Label index cards sensibly

A good card contains more than one pair of words, but not too many. The German word is on the front, and everything you need to know about the French word is on the back:

| What belongs on the card | Example | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Word with article | la plage (the beach) | Gender determines the endings of adjectives and articles |
| Part of speech and peculiarities | acheter (to buy), verb ending in -er, but: j'achète | You'll learn irregular shapes right away |
| Feminine form of adjectives | petit / petite (small) | You need both forms all the time |
| Short example sentence | En été, on va à la plage. | You see how the word is used in the sentence |
| Pronunciation note | plague: the g sounds soft like the second g in “Garage” | You learn the word as it is spoken |
| Marker for direction | a dot at the top right | Shows whether you are already practicing the card in both directions |

A few rules: Write only one word or fixed expression per card. Write by hand, because copying is the first step in learning. And practice both directions. Translating from French to German is easier because you just have to recognize the word. From German to French you have to pick it up yourself, and that's exactly what you need when speaking and writing. Many people therefore practice the easy direction first and then add the difficult direction as soon as a card has left the first compartment.

### The subject system explained

This is how a card goes through the box:

1. New cards go into compartment 1.
2. You take a card, read the front and answer out loud or in writing before turning over.
3. Answered correctly: The card moves one compartment further.
4. Wrong or only half-known: The card goes back into compartment 1. If that frustrates you too much, you can just put it back one compartment. The main thing is that she comes back more often.
5. Cards that successfully leave compartment 5 are placed in a “can-do” box, which you go through again before big tests.

How often you practice which subject is a matter of experience. This rule of thumb is a good start:

| compartment | What lies within | When you practice it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | New cards and any you had wrong | every day |
| 2 | Once you really knew it | about every two days |
| 3 | Knew it right twice | about twice a week |
| 4 | I knew it right three times | about once a week |
| 5 | Knew it right four times | approximately every two weeks or when the compartment is full |

Don't pick up more new cards than you can handle. When compartment 1 overflows, it's a sign to take a break with new words until it empties again.

### Ten minutes every day instead of an hour at a time

Short daily rounds have two benefits. Your memory keeps getting the signal that it needs these words. And ten minutes is so little that you hardly have to negotiate with yourself. Attach the round to something you do every day: after lunch, on the bus, before you sit down to do your homework. If you miss a day, just continue the next day without making up twice as much.

By the way, the principle doesn't just work for vocabulary. How to repeat subject matter in biology or history at intervals is told in [Hannah's story about spaced repetition](/lerngeschichten/verteiltes-wiederholen-statt-pauken/).

### Speak and write loudly

- **Answer first, then turn over.** Anyone who immediately turns over the card and thinks “Oh yes, I knew that” is deceiving themselves. Retrieving it from memory is the real learning moment.
- **Speak out loud.** This way you can practice pronunciation at the same time, and especially in French it helps to consciously leave out the silent letters.
- **Write if you are translating into French.** You can immediately tell from a scrapbook whether accents and double letters are correct.
- **Listen.** Many school books come with listening material. Ask your teacher where you can find it.

### Digital flashcard apps

There are many apps that work on the same principle. They can be practical: they often calculate the repetition intervals themselves, sometimes act out the pronunciation and are always there. But you should keep a few things in mind:

- **Cards you write yourself often work better than finished lists** because creating them is part of learning. Finished decks made by others may also contain errors.
- **Cell phone is distracting.** If you're constantly checking messages while checking, [Ben's Cell Phone Distraction Story for Studying](/lerngeschichten/handy-ablenkung-beim-lernen/) will help you.
- **Costs, Advertising, and Data.** Some apps are free, others cost money, show ads, or require an account. If in doubt, clarify this with your parents or teacher before you register.

A mix also works well: paper cards for the current Unité and an app for older words, or vice versa.

### Reuse vocabulary in your own sentences

A word that you only see on one card remains a card word. It's only really yours when you use it. Write two or three sentences about your own life for words that are in subject 3. A small notebook in which you write two sentences in French every day is enough. Also, try using new words in class and ask your teacher to look at your sentences occasionally.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Cards without articles or special features.** Then you will only learn half the vocabulary.
- **Too much on one card.** You can never honestly classify five words on a card as “known”.
- **Practice only one direction.** If you only recognize, you will get stuck when writing.
- **Turn around too quickly.** Always answer first, then check.
- **The box collects dust.** Without a fixed place in the daily routine, it ends up on the shelf after a week.

## Briefly asked

### Does the index box also work for English or Latin?

Yes, for every language. In Latin, for example, the stem forms of a verb and the genitive of nouns belong on the back.

### What do I do before an announced vocabulary test?

Add the new lesson words to the box as soon as you start. The night before the test, you can go through all the cards in this lesson again, regardless of which subject they are in.

### What if I just can't remember some words?

Memory aids help with particularly stubborn words. [Valentina's story about mnemonics and memory palaces](/lerngeschichten/merktechniken-eselsbruecken/) shows how this works. You can find further learning methods in the chapter [Vocabulary & Learning Methods](/themen/vokabeln-und-lernmethoden/).
