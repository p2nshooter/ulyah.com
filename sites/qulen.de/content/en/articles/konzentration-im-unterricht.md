Ms. Dietrich pointed to the board where there was a long calculation of the Pythagorean group of theorems. “Clara, what’s next?” Clara jumped up. She had just looked out the window, where a crow was hopping around on the school roof, and wondered whether crows can actually remember faces. Now she was staring at the board and had no idea what it was talking about. "Uh... times two?" she said. A few classmates giggled. Ms. Dietrich sighed softly and took someone else's turn.

## Anywhere but here

Clara was thirteen and in the eighth grade of a high school. She was a curious girl with many interests. She read a lot, loved to draw and could spend hours on a subject that fascinated her. But in class her mind kept wandering. Sometimes it was the weather outside, sometimes the conversation during the break, sometimes an idea for a drawing.

She often only noticed when she was called on or when the teacher was already on to the next topic. Then she tried to reconnect, but she was missing a piece and couldn't quite understand the rest. At home she had to learn a lot of things that she had missed in class.

When assessing work behavior, her report card said: “Clara could participate more in class and work with more concentration.” Clara thought that was unfair. She did make an effort. Her mind just did whatever it wanted.

## A conversation with the class teacher

At the parent-teacher conference, Ms. Dietrich, who was also Clara's class teacher, said that Clara often seemed absent in class. Clara's father asked if there was anything serious. Ms. Dietrich shrugged her shoulders. "There can be many reasons for this. Too little sleep, boredom, being overwhelmed, sometimes more. If it's very severe and is noticeable at home, it's worth talking to your pediatrician. But a few simple strategies often help."

She suggested that Clara get a different seat for the next few weeks, closer to the board and away from the window. And she gave Clara a tip that she had used herself as a student: "Ask yourself a question every lesson that you want to find the answer to. Then you will listen differently."

## Questions as anchors

Clara tried it. At the beginning of each lesson, she looked at the topic written on the board or that the teacher announced and wrote a question in the margin of her notebook. In History: “Why did people in the Middle Ages even want to move to cities?” In math: “Why do you need the Pythagorean theorem in real life?” In Bio: “How do fish actually breathe?”

It was amazing how much this helped. With a question in mind, Clara listened more carefully because she was waiting for the answer. Sometimes the answer came in class, sometimes not. Then she answered and asked. Ms. Dietrich was visibly happy when Clara asked a question for the first time.

Clara also developed a kind of early warning system. When she felt her mind wandering, she would make a small line in the edge of her paper. Then she consciously brought her attention back. In the first week she sometimes had ten strokes an hour. After three weeks there were still three or four.

## Small changes with a big impact

Her father also helped. He noticed that Clara often read or drew until late in the evening and was tired in the morning. Together they agreed on a more regular bedtime during the week. Clara also decided to eat breakfast in the morning instead of just drinking a quick sip of juice.

During breaks, she now walked around the schoolyard with her friend more often instead of sitting on her cell phone. The exercise and fresh air made her more alert for the next hour. And if a double lesson was particularly long, she would sometimes ask to go and get some water.

After two months, Clara noticed that she was getting a lot more out of class. She had to do less work at home. Her oral grade in math rose from a four to a three, and in history even to a two. And the crow on the roof? She now watched them during breaks. By the way, she discovered that crows can actually remember faces. She gave a short presentation in biology.

## What you can take from it

Attention is not a fixed quality that you have or don't have. It fluctuates, depends on many factors and can be trained. Wandering thoughts are normal; everyone experiences them. The important thing is that you learn to notice them and take your attention back.

### Why your mind wanders

There are many reasons why concentration decreases in class:

| Cause | What can help |
|---|---|
| Fatigue | Enough sleep, breakfast, exercise during breaks |
| Boredom | ask your own questions, think actively |
| Overwhelm | ask specific questions, close gaps |
| Distraction from the environment | Different seat, cell phone gone |
| Worry or stress | talk to someone you trust |
| Hunger or thirst | eat and drink regularly |

Often several causes come together. It's worth taking an honest look at what plays a role for you.

### Think actively instead of listening passively

Passive listening is tiring because the brain has little to do and quickly looks for other activities. Active thinking keeps things on point:

- **Ask one question for the lesson.** Start by writing down what you want to find out.
- **Write down in your own words.** If you write down thoughts, you process them. [Ole's story about taking notes](/lerngeschichten/richtig-mitschreiben/) shows how good note-taking works.
- **Make predictions.** Think about what the teacher will explain next and see if you were right.
- **Looking for connections.** What does the topic remind you of? Where does it occur in your everyday life?
- **Get in touch.** Anyone who participates will stay awake. Questions also count as participation.

### Notice digression and come back

The most important skill is not to never wander, but to notice it quickly. These exercises help:

1. **Target list:** Make a small mark in the margin when you notice that your thoughts are somewhere else. This will make you more aware of wandering off.
2. **Anchor word:** Choose a word that you say to yourself in your mind to come back to, such as “here” or “blackboard.”
3. **Briefly catch up:** If you missed something, look in the notebook of the person sitting next to you or ask instead of giving up the rest of the lesson.
4. **Park your thoughts:** If you think of something important, write it down on a piece of paper and take care of it after the lesson.

### Use the environment

Classroom space can make a big difference. Further forward, facing the board, there are fewer distractions than at the window or in the last row. The people sitting next to you also play a role. If you notice that you're constantly chattering next to a certain person, changing places can help. Feel free to talk to your teacher about it. And the cell phone should be kept switched off in the bag during class unless it is specifically needed.

### Body and concentration

Concentration is closely related to the body:

- **Sleep:** Sufficient sleep is the most important foundation. More about this in [Luis' story about sleep and learning](/lerngeschichten/schlaf-und-lernen/).
- **Breakfast and Drink:** If you come to school on an empty stomach, it is harder to concentrate.
- **Exercise:** Breaks in the fresh air make you more awake than breaks on your cell phone.
- **Short movements in class:** Place your feet firmly on the floor, sit upright, briefly relax your shoulders.

### Train concentration

Attention can also be practiced outside of school:

- **Reading** longer texts without interruption.
- **Single tasks instead of multitasking**, such as only doing one thing at a time when learning.
- **Short mindfulness exercises** in which you consciously pay attention to your breathing for a few minutes.
- **Limit screen time**, especially fast-paced videos that accustom the brain to constant change.

[Ben's story about cell phones while studying](/lerngeschichten/handy-ablenkung-beim-lernen/) tells how to reduce distraction from cell phones while studying.

### If the concentration problems are very severe

If you can hardly concentrate despite good strategies, the difficulties arise in many situations, including at home and in your free time, and they put a lot of strain on you or your family, talk to your parents and get medical advice. There are various causes that can be clarified professionally. School psychological counseling can also help. You can find further contact points on our [Help & Advice](/hilfe-und-beratung/) page.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Berate yourself for rambling.** This is even more distracting. Coming back friendly is better.
- **Give up the rest of the lesson.** Even if you missed something, you can get back in.
- **Just rely on willpower.** Sleep, seating and breakfast have a big impact.
- **The cell phone within reach.** Even a vibrating cell phone in your pocket can be distracting.
- **Don't ask.** If you don't understand, you'll switch off more quickly.

## Briefly asked

### Is it normal that I can't concentrate for the whole hour?

Yes. No one can concentrate equally for 45 or 90 minutes uninterrupted. Attention always fluctuates. The important thing is that you notice when you wander off and find your way back.

### Does it help to doodle in class?

For some people, light doodling helps maintain attention as long as it doesn't become their main activity. Try it out to see if it helps you, and if in doubt, ask your teacher if it's okay.

### What do I do if I'm really bored with class?

Ask your own questions, find connections to your interests and talk to your teacher if you constantly feel underchallenged. Sometimes there are additional tasks or projects that challenge you more.

### Can music help you concentrate?

Music is usually not an option in class. When learning at home, some people find quiet music without singing pleasant, while others find it easier to concentrate in silence. Music with lyrics that make you sing along is distracting for most people. Try what works for you and be honest with yourself.
