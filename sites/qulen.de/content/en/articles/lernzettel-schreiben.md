Nele's desk looked like a small stationery store. Brush pens in twelve colors, a ruler with a wavy edge, tape with stars. In front of her were six pages of a summary of the history chapter “Germany after 1945”, carefully written in beautiful letters, each heading in a different color gradient. The chapter in the book had eight pages. It had taken her all Sunday afternoon. Her mother glanced at it as she passed. “That looks like it was printed,” she said. Nele smiled. It felt like learning.

## Six pages for eight pages

Nele was fifteen and in the tenth grade of a community school. She was hardworking, everyone said that. Before each work, she created such summaries for history, biology and geography. She read it again the night before work.

In the history paper a week later, the third task was: “Explain how the currency reform of 1948 is related to the Berlin blockade.” Nele knew exactly that she had written about it. She saw the heading in front of her, turquoise with a light blue shadow. But she couldn't put together what was underneath. She wrote a few uncertain sentences and got a C plus for the work.

## “Where are you in this text?”

After returning it, Nele showed the six pages to her history teacher, Ms. Hartmann. She wanted to understand what went wrong. Ms. Hartmann leafed through the pages slowly and placed her finger on a paragraph. “That’s very neat,” she said. "But these are almost exactly the sentences from the book. Where are you in this text?"

Nele blushed. She had actually transcribed sentence by sentence, just shortened it a bit. Ms. Hartmann made her a suggestion for the next topic, the founding of the two German states: read a section first without writing. Then close the book and write down what was important, in your own words and in bullet points. Then open it up and compare. And only one page for each topic.

“One page?” asked Nele. “Nothing fits there.” – “That’s exactly what it’s about,” said Ms. Hartmann. "You have to decide what is important. That decision is learning."

## One page per topic

The first page using the new method took longer than expected and looked messy. In some sections, Nele knew shockingly little after closing them. Then she read again, more specifically. At the end there was no nice text on the page, but a column with questions on the left edge and next to it short answers with arrows: cause, event, consequence.

Her friend Emma asked her about it during the break. Nele covered the right column with a notebook and answered the questions. What she didn't know got a dot in the margin. Two days later she only asked about the points.

Nele didn't want to give up color completely. She agreed with herself on two colors with fixed meanings: years in red, technical terms in green. She had a rule for decorations: only when the content of the page was finished and queried once.

## The next job

Before the next history test, Nele had four learning sheets instead of twenty pages. She had spent less time writing and significantly more querying. There was a question in the paper about the differences between the two founding states. Nele wrote down three points without hesitation and realized that she had the arrows from her study sheet in her head. She got a B minus. She still kept the six colorful pages from earlier as a reminder of how much effort she had put in and how little effort alone brings.

## What you can take from it

A good study note is not a reduced-sized textbook. It is the result of your decisions about what is important and a tool you use to interrogate yourself. Both ensure that the material ends up in your head and not just on paper.

### Why copying is not learning

When you copy, your hand and your eyes are the main ones working. You transfer words without having to constantly decide what they mean and how they are related. This takes time and produces a beautiful result, so it feels productive. But you learn primarily when you select, rephrase, make connections and later retrieve the material from your head. A simple test: Can you explain the content of a paragraph without looking? If not, even the best summary won't help.

### Shorten it and put it in your own words

The method that helped Nele consists of four steps that you repeat for each section:

1. **Read.** Read a section to the next subheading without immediately highlighting or writing.
2. **Collapse.** Ask yourself: What is the core message of this section? Formulate them in a sentence.
3. **Write.** Write down the most important points from memory in keywords and in your own words.
4. **Check.** Open the book again, compare and fill in what is missing in a different color. This way you can see later what you forgot the first time.

A few rules will help you when shortening. Technical terms remain because they are expected in the work. You copy the years, names and numbers exactly. You keep one of several examples. You represent causes and consequences with arrows instead of subordinate clauses. This is what it looks like in an example:

| That's what it says in the book | That's what it says on the learning sheet |
|---|---|
| In June 1948, a new currency, the D-Mark, was introduced in the three western occupation zones. The Soviet Union responded, among other things, by closing the land and water routes to West Berlin. The Western Allies then supplied the city via an airlift. | June 1948: Currency reform in the western zones (D-Mark) → Soviet Union blocks routes to West Berlin (Berlin Blockade) → Western Allies airlift |

### Structure with headings, key terms and examples

A learning sheet is good if you can find your way around it at a glance. A solid structure ensures this:

- **Headline as a topic or question,** for example “Why was Germany divided in 1949?”
- **Few, clearly marked key terms.** When everything is highlighted, nothing stands out.
- **One example per term.** A concrete case makes a definition tangible.
- **Always the same arrangement.** If terms are always in the same place, your eye will find them more quickly.

Depending on the content, a different shape fits on the note:

| Type of content | This is how you hold on to it |
|---|---|
| Technical term | Term, explanation in one sentence, an example |
| event | Date, cause, process in keywords, consequence |
| sequence or process | Arrow chain with numbered steps |
| Comparison | small table with the comparison points as rows |
| Formula | Formula, meaning of the characters, units, when to use them |

If you prefer to represent connections as a network instead of a list, [Lukas' story about mind maps and concept maps](/lerngeschichten/mindmap-erstellen/) shows you a different form.

### The study sheet with one page per topic

Limiting it to one page is the most important trick because it forces you to choose. One A4 page per topic is enough for most teaching units. If it doesn't fit, the topic is usually too broad. Then divide it into two topics, each with its own page.

A tried and tested structure looks like this: The topic and date are at the top. On the left you draw a margin about a third of the width into which your questions will appear later. On the right are your keywords, arrows and examples. At the bottom you summarize the topic in a single sentence: “In one sentence: …” This sentence is often the most difficult and therefore the most valuable.

This is what an excerpt from Nele's learning sheet on the division of Germany could look like:

| Question on the sidelines | Keywords on the right |
|---|---|
| How was Germany divided after 1945? | four occupation zones: American, British, French, Soviet; Berlin in four sectors |
| What was the monetary reform? | June 1948, western zones only: D-Mark replaces Reichsmark |
| How did the Soviet Union react? | Berlin blockade from June 1948 to May 1949 → Supply via the airlift |
| When did the two states emerge? | May 1949: Basic Law, Federal Republic; October 1949: GDR |

At the bottom of the page is her sentence: “The occupied zones became two states in 1949 because the Western Allies and the Soviet Union went their separate ways in the early Cold War.”

### The learning sheet as a query tool

A study sheet only has its effect when you use it to quiz yourself. Reading alone is not enough:

1. **Write questions in the margins.** Each block on the right has a question on the left, for example, “What were the consequences of the currency reform?”
2. **Cover the right side** and answer the questions out loud or in writing.
3. **Mark what doesn't work** with a dot in the edge. On the next round, you first query the marked places.
4. **Turn it over.** Read a term from the right and think about which question it is the answer to.
5. **Let us quiz you.** A friend, a sibling or a parent can quiz you with your paper, even without knowing the subject.

Plan these rounds of queries at increasing intervals instead of reading the note once the evening before. [Hannah's story about distributed repetition](/lerngeschichten/verteiltes-wiederholen-statt-pauken/) tells why this works.

### How much design makes sense

Design can be fun, but it is not a learning step. It makes sense where it carries information:

- **Colors with fixed meanings.** Two to three colors that always mean the same thing help you find something again. Colorful headlines without meaning don't do that.
- **Sketches that show something.** A timeline, arrows, or small map can explain more than a paragraph.
- **Easily readable font** instead of artistic letters.
- **Decoration only at the end.** If you like designing, take this as a reward after the content of the page has been completed and queried once.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Mark almost everything.** If you mark every other line, you haven't yet decided what's important.
- **Don't write the study notes until the evening before.** Then there won't be any time left to test yourself with them.
- **Adopt someone else's summaries.** You save time, but also the very thinking work through which you learn. Use them only for comparison after you have written your own page.
- **Read only, never cover.** Recognition feels like knowledge, but is different than remembering.
- **Copy out what you don't understand.** If you can't say it in your own words, you should first clarify it, for example with your teacher.

## Briefly asked

### Can I also write learning notes on the computer or tablet?

Yes, if you really shorten and rephrase. Just be careful not to copy text from the book or the Internet. Many people find it easier to limit themselves to the essentials by hand because it takes longer to write.

### How long should it take me to complete a study sheet?

That depends on the topic. As a rule of thumb: most of your time should go into reading, selecting and querying, not writing and decorating.
