“Explain why the monsoon is so important for agriculture in South Asia.” Hamid knew the answer. He had them on his study notes, in his notes, in his head. But when Mr. Fischer looked at him during the practice exam and waited for an answer, nothing came. Hamid opened his mouth, closed it again. Then he said, “Um, about the rain.” And then it was quiet. Mr. Fischer waited friendly. Hamid felt the blood rush to his head. He knew so much more. Why didn't it come out?

## Knowledge in the head, not in the mouth

Hamid was eighteen and in the thirteenth grade of a high school. In a few months he was due to graduate from high school and geography was his oral exam subject. He chose it because he liked the subject and always got good grades in written exams. But he hadn't really thought about the oral exam.

Hamid was someone who learned in silence. He read, wrote summaries, memorized terms. In written exams he had time to organize his thoughts before writing them down. In an oral exam he had to speak immediately, in front of two or three teachers, and respond to questions. He had never practiced that.

During the practice test that Mr. Fischer offered to all examinees, he noticed how big this difference was. His knowledge was there, but it only came out in fragments. He spoke in half sentences, lost the thread when Mr. Fischer asked questions, and had the feeling that his answers sounded much stupider than he actually was.

## “Think out loud”

After the practice test, Mr. Fischer sat down with him. "You know a lot, Hamid. I can tell by the keywords you mention. But in an oral exam, it's not just what you know that counts, but how you show it. The examiners can't look into your head. You have to take them with you."

He suggested that Hamid practice a technique he called “thinking out loud.” "When you get a question, don't start with the solution. Start with your way of thinking. Say what you know about the topic, how you understand the question, what aspects you want to address. Then you work your way step by step to the answer."

He demonstrated it. "The question is why the monsoon is important for agriculture. To do this, I first have to explain what the monsoon is, namely a seasonally changing wind system. Then what consequences it has for precipitation. And then why agriculture in South Asia depends so heavily on this precipitation. So first about its origin..."

Hamid listened and realized that this was exactly what he was missing. Not the knowledge, but the structure to develop it in conversation.

## Practicing with your brother

At home, Hamid asked his older brother Karim, who was studying to be a teacher, to help him. Karim asked him questions from his documents and Hamid was supposed to answer out loud. At the beginning it was difficult. Hamid paused, repeated himself, lost the thread. Karim didn't interrupt him but took notes. Afterwards they discussed what went well and what didn't.

After a week, Hamid began to notice progress. He began his answers with a brief overview: “I would like to address three points.” This gave him a structure and showed the listeners where the journey was going. If he didn't immediately have a term ready, he would describe it instead of falling silent. And when Karim asked, he would take a moment, repeat the question in his own words, and then answer.

Karim sometimes deliberately played the role of strict examiner, asking for details or asking a question that went beyond the material. “In a real exam there will be questions that you don’t expect,” he said. “Then what counts is how you deal with it.” Hamid learned to be honest when he didn't know something and then to think about what he could infer from his knowledge.

Hamid also recorded himself practicing. He listened to the recordings on the way to school. He noticed that he said “um” a lot and that he often became quieter at the end of his answers. He was able to improve both over time.

## The test

On the day of the exam, during the preparation period, Hamid was given an assignment about urban development in Latin America, with a map and a table. He used the time as he had practiced. He read the tasks carefully, noted the most important points in an outline and marked what he could glean from the material and what he wanted to add to his own knowledge.

In the examination room he began with a sentence that he had prepared: “In my presentation I would first like to evaluate the material and then address the causes and consequences of urbanization.” Then he spoke, more calmly than expected. In the conversation that followed, an examiner asked a question about a topic that he had only learned in passing. Hamid hesitated for a moment. Then he said, "I've learned less about that, but I can try to deduce it from what I know about..." And then he thought out loud.

After the test he received twelve points. Mr. Fischer congratulated him in the hallway. “You took us,” he said. Hamid smiled. He felt like he had not only passed a test, but learned a new skill.

## What you can take from it

Oral exams require different skills than written ones. You not only have to have your knowledge, but you have to show it in the conversation: structured, understandable and flexible. The good news is that these skills can be practiced, just like content.

### How oral exams often work

The exact process depends on the type of school, federal state, training or university. Often there are these elements:

| part | What happens | What matters |
|---|---|---|
| Preparation time | you are given a task, often with materials, and prepare on your own | Read the task carefully, create an outline |
| Lecture | you present your results, usually for a few minutes at a time | Structure, technical language, free speaking |
| Exam interview | The examiners ask questions, often on other topics | answer questions, show connections |

Find out from your teacher or your examination regulations early on how exactly your exam will work, how long the individual parts are and what aids are permitted.

### Practice thinking out loud

Thinking out loud means making your way of thinking audible. This is how you take the examiners with you and show that you understand the connections:

1. **Take up the question.** Briefly repeat the question in your own words. This buys you time and shows that you understand them.
2. **Announce structure.** “I would like to address three aspects of this.” This organizes your thoughts and helps those listening.
3. **Develop step by step.** Work your way from the basic to the specific.
4. **Use and explain technical terms.** This shows specialist knowledge and understanding at the same time.
5. **Summarize.** A short final sentence completes your answer.

By the way, speaking freely in presentations works in a similar way, as [Dominik's story about cue cards](/lerngeschichten/frei-sprechen-beim-referat/) shows.

### Practice with others

An oral exam cannot be prepared in private. Find a practice partner:

- **Classmates:** Quiz each other, preferably with realistic exam questions.
- **Family and friends:** Even people without specialist knowledge can listen and provide feedback on whether your explanations are understandable.
- **Teachers:** Many offer mock exams or office hours. Take advantage of these opportunities.
- **Recordings:** Record yourself and listen to the recording. This is how you notice filler words, tempo and volume.

### Use the preparation time wisely

If you get preparation time during the exam, use it specifically:

1. **Read the task carefully.** Pay attention to the operators such as “describe”, “explain” or “assess”. They show you what is required.
2. **Evaluate material.** Write down the most important statements from maps, texts or tables.
3. **Create an outline.** Write the order of your points as keywords, not as complete sentences.
4. **Decide the beginning.** Think carefully about the first sentence. A secure beginning provides stability.
5. **Keep track of time.** Allow a few minutes at the end to review your notes.

### Deal with questions and gaps in knowledge

There will be questions in the interview that you don't expect. This is normal and desired, because the examiners want to see how you think. These strategies help:

- **Think for a moment.** A pause of a few seconds is completely fine and appears deliberate, not uncertain.
- **Ask if something is unclear.** “Do you mean the economic or social consequences?” is a good question.
- **Be honest.** If you don't know something, say it. Then try to derive an answer from your existing knowledge.
- **Relate to what is known.** Connect the question to a topic you know well without completely digressing.

### Body language and voice

How you speak also affects the examiners. Sit or stand upright, maintain eye contact with everyone present, speak clearly and not too quickly. Gestures can support your explanations. Nervousness is normal and is usually perceived with understanding by examiners. [Leyla's story about stage fright](/lerngeschichten/lampenfieber-vor-dem-referat/) tells you what helps against heart palpitations, and [Yusuf's story about exam anxiety](/lerngeschichten/pruefungsangst-bewaeltigen/) shows how you can get the fear of exams under control in general.

### Learn targeted content

For oral examinations, it is particularly important to understand connections because examiners often ask about causes, consequences and examples. The following are helpful:

- **Concept maps** that show connections between topics, like in [Luke's story about mind maps](/lerngeschichten/mindmap-erstellen/).
- **Collect examples** that you can use to illustrate general statements.
- **Practice technical terms**, not just by heart, but in a way that you can explain them.
- **Formulate exam questions yourself** and answer them out loud.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Only learn in silence.** If you never practice out loud, you will be surprised at how difficult it is to speak during a conversation.
- **Recite texts you have learned by heart.** This seems rigid and doesn't help when questions arise.
- **Speaking too fast.** Nervousness quickens the pace. Conscious breaks help.
- **If there is a gap, remain silent.** It is better to think out loud what you can derive from your knowledge.
- **Waste preparation time.** Writing down complete sentences takes time. Keywords are enough.

## Briefly asked

### How long in advance should I start preparing?

In terms of content, as early as for a written exam. You should start practicing out loud at least a few weeks in advance so that the new way of speaking can become established.

### Can I look at my notes during the exam?

Notes from the preparation period are generally permitted. Most of the time you don't bring your own documents. Clarify the exact rules in advance with your teacher or in the examination regulations.

### What if I have a blackout?

Ask for a moment to breathe calmly and look at your notes. It often helps to start with a simple part of the question. [Elias' story about the blackout in the exam](/lerngeschichten/blackout-in-der-pruefung/) tells you what else you can do in the event of a blackout.
