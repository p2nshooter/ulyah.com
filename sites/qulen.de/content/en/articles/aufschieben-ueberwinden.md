On Sunday afternoon, Lasse's room was tidier than it had been in months. The books were sorted by color on the shelf, the cables under the desk were bundled with Velcro, and the pens were in the drawer, sorted by length. Only the English folder was still exactly where he had left it on Friday: closed on the windowsill. It was twelve days until the exam. “Tomorrow I’ll really get started,” thought Lasse. “With a big block when I have more time.”

## Two weeks that suddenly became two days

Lasse was seventeen and in the eleventh grade, the first level of the upper secondary school at his comprehensive school. He liked speaking English and often spoke up in class. In writing it was something different. In the last exam he had to analyze a short story and received six points for it. In the margins were comments like “Receipt?” and “Content only, no analysis.”

Monday passed with football training and a video that he wanted to watch “just briefly”. On Tuesday he volunteered to do the math homework for the entire week. On Wednesday he felt too tired. Every evening he lay in bed, scrolling and felt a dull knot in his stomach when he thought about the exam. Every evening he promised himself that tomorrow would be the day.

## “What exactly does learning English mean?”

On Thursday, Lasse's class had a tutoring session with Mr. Asante, who also taught him English. At the end he casually asked how the preparation was going. Lasse wanted to say “pretty good,” but then said, “To be honest, I haven’t started yet.”

Mr. Asante nodded as if it were the most normal thing in the world. "What's on your list? What does learning English mean to you?" Lasse realized he couldn't say it. In his head the task was a huge gray block: everything, somehow, and ideally perfect.

They talked for ten minutes. Something else came out of this. Lasse was afraid that the six points hadn't been a slip-up. As long as he didn't learn, he could tell himself that he hadn't really tried. And he always waited for a free afternoon when he could do “everything properly.” That afternoon never came.

“You’re not lazy,” Mr. Asante said. "You have too big a task and you're a little afraid of it. Both can be made smaller." He gave him a first step, so small that Lasse almost had to laugh: Open the short story from class this evening and write three sentences about how the main character feels at the end. Not more.

## Three sentences are enough for today

Lasse wrote a sentence on a sticky note and stuck it to the screen: “When I come into my room after dinner, the first thing I do is open the English folder.” At ten past seven he was sitting at his desk. Three sentences turned into half a page and a list of references. After twenty minutes he stopped, although he was able to continue. It felt unusually good.

On Friday, together with Mr. Asante, he wrote down what had to happen before the exam, each with a separate deadline: clarifying the task words, collecting expressions for the analysis, writing a sample analysis paragraph, getting feedback, revising the paragraph. There was a fixed start time every day, a short session on training days and a longer session on days off.

On Tuesday of the second week the plan was canceled. After training, Lasse was exhausted and instead watched two episodes of a series. Previously he would have concluded that everything was lost anyway. This time he remembered a sentence from Mr. Asante: “A missed evening is a missed evening, not a missed plan.” On Wednesday he started again on time and only postponed one step by a day.

## A normal evening before the exam

The evening before the exam, Lasse went through his list of expressions and read his improved sample essay. At half past nine he closed the folder. He got eight points in the exam. No wonder, but a clear step. What surprised him most was something else: When the next topic was announced, he opened the folder that same evening. Three sentences. Not more. That was enough to start with.

## What you can take from it

Procrastination is not a character flaw. It is usually an attempt to avoid an unpleasant feeling: insecurity, excessive demands or fear of a bad result. In the short term, evasion feels better, but in the long term the pressure increases. This is about getting started. If your cell phone is what's taking you out of studying, [Ben's story about cell phone distraction](/lerngeschichten/handy-ablenkung-beim-lernen/) will help you.

### Why we procrastinate, without blame

If you know your own reason, you can take more targeted countermeasures. Often several come together:

| reason | That's how it feels | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| The task is too big | “I can’t do it today anyway.” | break it down into intermediate goals, a tiny first step |
| The task is unclear | “I don’t even know where to start.” | Write down the task and ask the teacher |
| Fear of failure | “If I learn and it still fails…” | Measure progress by doing, not by grade |
| Perfectionism | “I won’t start until I can do it right.” | consciously start imperfectly, limit time |
| The task is boring | “Everything else is more interesting right now.” | short sessions, reward afterwards |

For subjects that generally don't appeal to you, it's worth taking a look at [Dennis' story about motivation for unloved subjects](/lerngeschichten/motivation-fuer-ungeliebte-faecher/).

### Set the smallest first step

The first step must be so small that your head doesn't find any reason to move it. It begins with a verb, names a specific thing and lasts a maximum of ten minutes.

- Instead of “learn English”: “Read the task from the last exam and underline the task words.”
- Instead of “Repeat bio”: “Write the headlines from the last four hours in a list.”
- Instead of “Make a presentation”: “Write down five questions that my presentation should answer.”
- Instead of “practice math”: “calculate problem 3 on page 112.”

Lay out the material the evening before: folder open, pen next to it, the correct page marked. And agree on a five-minute rule with yourself. You work for five minutes, then you can honestly stop. Often you keep going because the hardest part has already been done. If not, you still started.

### If-then plans and fixed start times

An if-then plan links a situation that will happen anyway with a concrete action. This way you don't have to decide every time whether to start.

- “After I eat after school, I sit down at my desk at 3 p.m. and open the folder.”
- “When I get back from training and have a shower, I study vocabulary for twenty minutes.”
- “When I find myself wanting to clean up, I put it on the list for after studying.”
- “If I don’t know what to do next, I write down my question and move on to the next point.”

Treat start times like a training appointment. Put it on the calendar and set a reminder. It's better to plan short units that you actually stick to, rather than long ones that are just on paper.

### Break down large tasks into intermediate goals with their own deadlines

A deadline in two weeks feels far away. Intermediate goals bring you closer:

1. **Write down the goal and date.** For example: “English exam on Friday in two weeks.”
2. **Clarify what is required.** Ask about topics, task types, and permitted resources.
3. **Set intermediate goals with visible results.** A list, a sample paragraph, an answered question. “Reading something” is not a good intermediate goal because afterward you don’t know if you’re finished.
4. **Give each intermediate goal its own date.** Plan a buffer of one to two days before the exam.
5. **Check off what has been done.** The visible progress helps when starting next time.

This is what Lasse's plan looked like for the last eight days:

| day | intermediate destination | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Friday | Clarify task words such as “analysis” and “comment”. | List of explanations |
| Saturday | Collect expressions for analysis | a page with wording |
| Sunday | Write a sample analysis paragraph | a paragraph with evidence |
| Monday | Get feedback from Mr. Asante | Notes on strengths and flaws |
| Tuesday | Revise paragraph | improved version |
| Wednesday | Buffer | Make up for what was left behind |
| Thursday | Go through the expression list and sample paragraph again | short repeat, end of work early |
| Friday | Exam | go quietly |

### Deal with relapses in a friendly manner

Relapses happen to everyone. What matters is how you talk to yourself afterwards. Harsh self-criticism usually makes the next start more difficult because it makes learning seem even more unpleasant. A more sober question is more helpful: What happened right before the postponement? Were you tired, was the step too big, did something come up?

Then a simple rule applies: restart at the next scheduled appointment, not on Monday or after the holidays. Make the first step even smaller than usual. A missed evening postpones your plan, it doesn't make it worthless.

### When there is more to it

Sometimes procrastination is a sign of something bigger. Talk to your guidance counselor, teacher or school social worker if you are unable to do anything in almost all areas for weeks, if you feel depressed or empty for a long time, if fear of school dominates your everyday life or if you find it consistently difficult to concentrate despite all your attempts. If exhaustion or sleep problems persist, your family doctor's office is also a good place to go. You can find further ways under [Help & Advice](/hilfe-und-beratung/).

## Typical pitfalls

- **Wait for motivation.** Pleasure often only comes while working, rarely before.
- **Perfect the plan.** Spending an hour working on a study plan is more convenient than spending ten minutes on the material.
- **Procrastinate productively.** Cleaning up or other homework feels useful, but they put off exactly that one task.
- **All or nothing.** If you only count large learning blocks, you won't even start on short days.
- **Give up after a slip-up.** A day without studying is not proof that you can't do it.

## Briefly asked

### Am I just lazy?

Probably not. If you're lazy, you usually don't worry about the task. Those who procrastinate, on the other hand, often think about it all the time and feel bad about it. It's tiring and shows that the matter is important to you.

### I work best under pressure. Why should I change that?

You might be quick before the deadline. However, there is no time to repeat and ask questions for exams, and gaps are only noticed when it is too late. Try it out with distributed steps and see how you feel.

### What if I really stop after five minutes?

Then you'll have worked for five minutes and have an easier start tomorrow. Set the next start time now. You can find more stories about structure in everyday learning in the chapter [Learning plan & homework] (/themen/lernplan-und-hausaufgaben/).
