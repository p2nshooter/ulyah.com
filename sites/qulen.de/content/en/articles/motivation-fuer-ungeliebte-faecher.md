“Why do I even need this?” Dennis hadn't meant to ask the question out loud, but it had slipped out. Ms. Kühn stood at the front of the board and explained the electron configuration of sodium. Some in the class giggled. Ms. Kühn turned around, looked at him and said calmly: "That's a good question, Dennis. I'll be happy to answer it for you after class." Dennis blushed. He had expected detention, not an offer to talk.

## A subject without meaning

Dennis was fifteen and in the ninth grade of a secondary school. He was good at sports, liked history, and got a solid B in English. But for him chemistry was a collection of formulas, symbols and rules that had nothing to do with his life. He only studied when there was work to be done, and then usually the evening before. His grades fluctuated between three and five.

The problem wasn't that he couldn't understand chemistry. The problem was that he didn't want to. He left every chemistry homework until the end. When he sat down, his mind immediately wandered. After ten minutes he checked his cell phone, after twenty minutes he was in the kitchen. He called it “chemical paralysis,” and his friends laughed about it.

But his last report card showed a C in chemistry, and his parents weren't happy. Dennis also wanted to train as a vehicle mechatronics technician after school, and his uncle, who had a workshop, had told him that scientific grades would also be taken into account in applications.

## The conversation after the lesson

After the lesson, Dennis remained hesitantly at the desk. Ms. Kühn packed up her things and asked: “What do you like to do in your free time?”

“I work on cars and mopeds with my uncle.”

Ms. Kühn smiled. "Then you deal with chemistry every day. Why does metal rust? What happens in a car battery? Why does gasoline burn and what comes out the back of the exhaust? What does a catalytic converter do? It's all chemistry."

Dennis had never thought about it. Rust was just annoying to him. Batteries were just batteries. But now that Ms. Kühn said it, he remembered that his uncle had recently complained about the acid in an old battery.

“I’ll make you a suggestion,” said Ms. Kühn. "For every new topic, you choose a question from the workshop that has to do with it. And at the end of the topic, you explain the answer to me in two minutes."

## Questions from the workshop

The next topic was about redox reactions. Dennis wrote down his question: “Why does the body rust in some places faster than others?” He looked in the book, asked his uncle and did a little research on the Internet. Suddenly the topic made sense. Iron reacts with oxygen and water, and salt in winter speeds up the reaction. This is why cars rust faster in regions with a lot of road salt.

He explained the answer to Ms. Kühn in two minutes after the lesson. She nodded and added something about sacrificial anodes, which can be used to protect metal from corrosion. Dennis told his uncle over the weekend, who was impressed.

Of course, not every topic could be linked to the workshop. Dennis found little reference to the periodic table. To do this, he developed a different strategy that his big brother had recommended to him: not having to love the subject, but simply creating a solid framework.

## A fixed framework instead of enthusiasm

Dennis made it a point to study chemistry for twenty minutes three days a week, always right after lunch, before thinking about other things. He set a timer and put the phone in the kitchen. When the timer went off, he was allowed to stop, no matter how far he had gotten.

After each session, he rewarded himself with something small: an episode of his favorite series, a ride on his bike, a snack. And he kept a simple tally on the bulletin board. After four weeks there were twelve lines there. Dennis was almost a little proud of this series.

What surprised him most was that the more he knew, the less bad he found the subject. It wasn't that chemistry suddenly became his favorite subject. But it was no longer the wall he ran into every day.

## The next job

In the next paper about acids and bases there was a task about batteries in cars. Dennis smiled as he read it. He wrote almost half a page. Underneath the paper was a B minus, his best chemistry grade in years.

At the end of the semester there was a three on the report card. For many this would not have been a special note. For Dennis, it was proof that he could achieve something even without a love for the subject. And when he wrote his application for an internship in a car repair shop in the spring, he mentioned in the cover letter that he was interested in the chemistry behind corrosion. At the interview, the master asked exactly this.

## What you can take from it

Nobody has to love every subject. But for almost every subject, there are ways to make it more bearable and even interesting. Motivation often doesn't arise before learning, but during it: those who start and experience small successes become hungry for more. The following strategies will help you stick with subjects you don't like.

### Look for a personal connection

The question “Why do I need this?” is justified. Instead of using it as an excuse, you can ask it seriously and look for an answer:

- **Combine hobbies.** Anyone who plays sports can discover physics in movements. Anyone who cooks finds chemistry in the kitchen. Anyone who gambles finds math in game mechanics.
- **Check career aspirations.** Many training courses and degree programs require the basics of subjects that you don't like at school.
- **Ask everyday questions.** Why does a cake solidify in the oven? Why is the sky blue? Why is there inflation?
- **Ask the teacher.** Many teachers are happy to be asked about the meaning and have exciting answers.

Not every topic can be connected to your everyday life. But just a few connections can make the whole subject appear in a different light.

### Motivation from within and without

There are two types of motivation. Inner motivation occurs when you are genuinely interested in something. External motivation comes from goals, rewards or consequences. Internal motivation is rare for unloved subjects. Then it's completely okay to work with external motivation.

| Art | Examples |
|---|---|
| Goals | a certain grade, a degree, a training position |
| Rewards | an episode of a series, a snack, time with friends after studying |
| Visible progress | Tally list, calendar with check marks, learning plan to check off |
| Community | learn together, question each other |
| Curiosity | a specific question to which you want to know the answer |

External motivation often develops into a bit of internal motivation over time. Those who understand something better often find it more interesting.

### Create a solid framework

Anyone who waits for pleasure often waits in vain for subjects they don't like. A fixed framework replaces desire with habit:

1. **Fixed times.** Set specific days and times for the subject, for example three times a week after lunch.
2. **Short sessions.** Twenty to thirty minutes is enough. Short sessions are easier to start with.
3. **Set a timer.** When the timer goes off, you can stop. This lowers the inhibition threshold.
4. **Remove distractions.** Move phone to another room, turn off notifications. More on this in [Ben's story about cell phone distraction](/lerngeschichten/handy-ablenkung-beim-lernen/).
5. **Start with the easiest.** A quick success at the beginning makes you want more.

[Lasse's story about procrastination](/lerngeschichten/aufschieben-ueberwinden/) shows how you get started when you keep putting off a task.

### Small goals and visible progress

Big goals like “getting a B in chemistry” are far away. Small goals are tangible: “Today I understand what an acid is.” “This week I’m learning ten elemental symbols.” Every small goal achieved is a sense of achievement.

Make your progress visible: a tally list, a calendar with ticks, a list of completed topics. Especially with unloved subjects, it helps to see that you have already accomplished something.

### Learn with others

Learning together is often easier, especially if the subject is boring alone. You can quiz each other, explain difficult topics or solve tasks together. When you explain something to someone else, you understand it better yourself. Just make sure that the study group doesn't turn into just a chat group.

### Focus on the whole

Sometimes it helps to see an unloved subject as part of a larger goal. Maybe you need the qualification for a course that interests you. Maybe you want to study later. The subject itself doesn't have to excite you if you know what you're doing it for. Write down your goal and hang it above your desk.

### When the unhappiness is deeper

Sometimes there is more to the dislike of a subject than just boredom. Maybe you have big gaps and that's why you feel overwhelmed. Then targeted steps help, as [Jannik's story about math gaps](/lerngeschichten/mathe-luecken-finden-und-schliessen/) shows. Maybe the subject scares you, like in [Pia's story about being afraid of math](/lerngeschichten/angst-vor-mathe/). And if you barely feel motivated in all subjects or feel exhausted or depressed, talk to someone you trust. You can find information on our [Help & Advice](/hilfe-und-beratung/) page.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Wait for motivation.** With unloved subjects, it often only comes after you have started.
- **Only study before working.** This creates stress and gaps that make the subject even less popular.
- **Belittling the subject.** Anyone who constantly says “It’s pointless” is making it harder for themselves.
- **Goals too big.** Going from a five to a one in a month is unrealistic and leads to frustration.
- **Comparing yourself to others.** If your girlfriend loves chemistry, that's her strength, not your standard.

## Briefly asked

### Do I have to like a subject to be good at it?

No. Many people do well in subjects they don't particularly like. A fixed framework, clear goals and regular learning are often more important than enthusiasm.

### What if I don’t like the teacher?

This can have a big impact on motivation. Try to separate the subject and the person. You learn for yourself, not for the teacher. If there are serious conflicts, a conversation with the class leader or the trusted teacher can help.

### How do I reward myself without it becoming too much?

Choose small rewards that fit the study time. An episode of a series after twenty minutes is appropriate, but a whole evening of gaming is not. The reward should come after learning, not before.
