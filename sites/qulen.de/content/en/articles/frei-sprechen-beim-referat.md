Dominik held four pages in his hand, tightly printed, font size eleven. He stood at the front next to the desk, the papers so close to his face that he only saw the class as a colorful border. “Structural change in the Ruhr area,” he read. “In the nineteenth century, the Ruhr area developed into one of the most important industrial areas in Europe thanks to hard coal mining and the steel industry.” This was followed by a sentence that lasted five lines. Then another. When he looked up after eleven minutes, Luis was in the back row staring out the window and two girls in the middle were whispering.

## Four pages, word for word

Dominik was sixteen and in the tenth grade of a comprehensive school. He worked on the report for two weekends. He had gotten books from the public library, built a timeline, and rearranged each sentence until he liked it. That's exactly why he wrote everything down. He didn't want to forget anything, and he wanted it to sound good.

After the lecture, Ms. Seidel, his geography teacher, handed out small feedback sheets as always. Dominik read it in the schoolyard during break. “Content was good, but I dropped out halfway through.” "Too fast." “You never looked up.” And one that hit him the most: “To be honest, no one was listening.”

Ms. Seidel said it more pleasantly, but in a similar way. "You did thorough research, it shows. But you spoke to your paper and not to us." Then she made him an offer. The unit on urban development began in three weeks. If he wanted, he could open the lesson with a short five-minute lecture on the same topic, this time spoken freely.

## A stack of index cards

At home, Dominik put his four pages on the desk and a stack of index cards next to them. Ms. Seidel had given him three questions: What should the others know after five minutes? How do you get from one thought to the next? And which sentence do you start with?

The first question was the hardest. On the four pages everything was placed next to each other with equal importance. He deleted until three core statements remained: Coal and steel made the region great. When mines and smelters closed, many jobs were lost. And the region has changed with new industries, universities and converted industrial facilities, even if this has not been done equally well everywhere.

He described a card for each core statement, one for the beginning and one for the end. Only keywords were put on the cards, written in capital letters, with a maximum of six lines. He only wrote down two things in full: his first sentence and his last. Between the cards he placed small pieces of paper with transitions, for example "That was the heyday. And then came the break."

## Practice out loud until it sounds like you're telling a story

The first pass in front of the mirror in the bathroom was so embarrassing for Dominik that he stopped after a minute. On the second try he talked to the bathroom wall, which worked better. Each time he worded the sentences slightly differently, and at first he thought it was a mistake. Until he realized that that was exactly the difference to reading: he told the story.

Then he recorded himself on his cell phone. He was frightened when he listened. He spoke far too quickly and there was almost always a long “um” between sentences. On each card he wrote “SLOW” in red at the top right. And in two places a thick pause mark, a vertical line.

He held the dress rehearsal at the kitchen table in front of his friend Jonas and his mother, standing up with a stopwatch. The thread broke on the third card. Dominik stood there silently, felt himself getting warm, looked at the map, read the keyword “mine closures” and was back inside. “I didn’t even notice,” Jonas said afterwards. “I thought it was a break.”

## Five minutes on Monday morning

On Monday Dominik was at the front again, this time with five cards instead of four pages. He started with a question: “Which one of you has ever been to a museum that used to be a mine or a steelworks?” A few hands went up. He spoke more slowly than felt right and looked at someone else after each sentence. Once he lost track for a moment, looked at the map and said: “Exactly, I was at the workplace.”

At the end, Luis asked how many people used to work in mining. Dominik didn't know exactly. Instead of guessing a number, he said, "I don't know for sure. I'll look it up and tell you on Thursday." He kept his word. The feedback sheet from that day said: “This time you could listen.”

## What you can take from it

Speaking freely does not mean speaking without any aids or knowing everything by heart. It means telling the story along a clear structure and using short keywords as a guide. This is about presentation technique. [Paul's story about the first presentation](/lerngeschichten/referat-vorbereiten/) shows how you develop and structure the content. If your main concern is nerves, read [Leyla's story about stage fright](/lerngeschichten/lampenfieber-vor-dem-referat/).

### Why reading doesn't resonate with the audience

Written language is made for the eye. Long sentences with inserts can be looked at twice when reading, but not when listening. Anyone who reads usually speaks too quickly and too evenly because their eyes follow the text and not the people in the room. There is no eye contact and therefore the feeling that someone is talking to you.

| Mode of presentation | Effect on the listeners | risk to you |
|---|---|---|
| Reading from the running text | monotonous, difficult to follow, little contact | you lose the line and find it difficult to find again |
| Learn everything by heart | often seems recited | one forgotten word and the whole chain breaks |
| Free with keyword cards | lively, at your own pace, with eye contact | initially unusual, requires loud practice |

### Keyword cards instead of running text

1. **Use solid cards.** Index cards in A6 or A5 format fit comfortably in the hand and shake less visibly than sheets of paper. Label only one side.
2. **Number each card.** If you drop the stack, it will be sorted again in seconds.
3. **One card per thought step.** This is usually an outline point, plus a card for the beginning and the end.
4. **Keywords only, capitalized.** Five to seven lines at most, so you can read them with a quick glance at arm's length.
5. **Write down exceptions.** Write down the first and last sentences, quotes, definitions as well as exact numbers and years in full. You can read it there and also say: “I’ll read the definition to you.”
6. **Incorporate stage directions.** A pause, a note like “Slide 3” or “SLOW” in a different color will help you in the middle of the lecture.

This is what the difference looks like in Dominik's example: The sentence about the nineteenth century became “19th century: coal + steel → large industrial area” on the map. He tells the rest from what he has understood.

### Determine key messages and transitions

Before you write cards, answer a question in writing: What do you want others to know after your talk? Formulate two to four key statements as complete sentences. Everything on your cards must support one of them, the rest can go.

Transitions are the places where listeners most often drop out. So determine them beforehand. Good transitions briefly summarize and announce:

- "That was the starting point. Now let's see what has changed."
- "So much for the causes. What consequences did that have for people?"
- “An example makes this clearer.”
- “Finally, I would like to return to the question from the beginning.”

You learn the beginning and end almost word for word. A confident first sentence carries you through the most exciting seconds, a clear last sentence prevents your presentation from fraying with “Yes, that’s it”.

### Practice out loud: mirror, voice recording, sample audience

Going through it in your head isn't enough. It's only when you speak out loud that you realize where you're missing words. Plan several rounds, each with its own focus:

| practice round | This is how you do it | You pay attention to that |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Alone, loud | Talk through card by card, preferably in front of the mirror or a wall | Can you find words for every keyword? |
| 2. Voice recording | Record and listen to it completely | Pace, filler words, pauses, length |
| 3. Sample audience | in front of friends or family, standing up | Eye contact, comprehensibility, questions |
| 4. Dress rehearsal | with slides, cards and a clock, just like in the classroom | Keep time, quiet start, clear end |

It's not a mistake that your sentences sound slightly different every time you read them. This is exactly how you know that you speak freely.

### Eye contact, pace and pauses

- **Look first, then speak.** Look at the card, pick up the keyword, raise your head, then say the sentence. You only speak when you look into the audience.
- **Switch the direction of your gaze.** Look at individuals in different corners of the room for a sentence, not just the teacher or your best friend.
- **Speak slower than it feels.** Up front, time passes faster for you than for everyone else.
- **Make pauses consciously.** After a core message and before a transition. A pause does not have an embarrassing effect on the listener, but rather a sorting effect.
- **Replace filler words with silence.** If you notice an “um,” make it a short pause next time.
- **Stand tight.** Both feet on the floor, cards in one hand, the other free for gestures.

### If you lose track

A hang-up is part of speaking freely and is much less noticeable than you think. This is how you come back:

1. **Stand and breathe calmly.** From the perspective of the class, this is a break.
2. **Look at the card.** The keyword usually brings the thought back.
3. **Summarize.** “I’ll briefly summarize where we are” gives you time and helps others too.
4. **Add later.** If you think of a point that was left out, simply say: “I would like to add one more thing.”
5. **Don't keep apologizing.** A quick “moment” is enough.

### Answer questions from the class

- **Listen to the question to the end** and briefly repeat it in your own words. This will save you time and make sure you understand it correctly.
- **Answer briefly** and then ask: “Does that answer your question?”
- **Prepare.** Think of three questions that are likely to come up and create a reserve card with answers.
- **Be honest if you don't know something.** "I'm not sure. I'll check and tell you next hour" seems more competent than a guessed number. Then keep the promise.
- **Remain objective when criticizing.** Thank you for the tip and explain how you came to your conclusion.

The conversation in an oral exam works in a very similar way, as [Hamid's story about thinking out loud answers](/lerngeschichten/muendliche-pruefung-vorbereiten/) shows.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Whole sentences on the cards.** Then you read them again, just smaller.
- **Just practice in your head.** When you speak out loud for the first time, the lecture sounds completely different than you thought.
- **Look at the slides instead of the audience.** The slides support you, they are not your cheat sheet.
- **Rewrite everything the night before.** You don't know new cards well enough on the last day.
- **Too much material for the time.** If you have too much time, you'll get hectic. It's better to have one less core statement.

## Briefly asked

### Am I not allowed to read anything at all?

But. You can read quotes, definitions and exact numbers; no one else expects that. It is important that you look up again afterwards and continue speaking freely.

### How many cards do I need?

That depends on your topic. As a rough guide: one card for each bullet point, one for the beginning and one for the end, and perhaps a reserve card for questions.

### Can I use my cell phone or tablet instead of cards?

Ask your teacher beforehand. Paper is usually more reliable because no screen locks and no message flashes. You can find more stories about lectures, slides and oral contributions in the chapter [Speaking & Presenting](/themen/referate-und-praesentieren/).
