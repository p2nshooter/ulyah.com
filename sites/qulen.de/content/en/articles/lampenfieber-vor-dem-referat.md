Leyla had practiced her presentation about bees eleven times. In front of the mirror, in front of her little sister, even in front of the cat, who fell asleep after the third minute. She knew every sentence. But when Ms. Brandt said, “Leyla, it’s your turn,” and she stood up, something strange happened. Her knees felt like pudding. The cue cards in her hand rustled because her fingers were shaking. And when she opened her mouth, her voice came out so thin that someone in the back row shouted “Louder!” shouted.

## When your body doesn't cooperate

Leyla was thirteen and in the seventh grade of a comprehensive school. She was not a shy student. During breaks she talked to everyone and liked to take the lead in group work. But standing alone in the front with thirty pairs of eyes on her was something completely different.

That morning she stumbled through the first few minutes. She read more from the cards than she wanted to, lost the thread once and jumped to a point that she actually only wanted to explain at the end. When she sat down, her face was glowing. The grade was a three. “The content is very good,” said Ms. Brandt’s comment. “Lecture still uncertain.”

At home, Leyla threw herself on the bed. What was that about? She had known everything. Her big brother Emre, who was currently training to become an industrial clerk, was leaning in the doorway. “Stage fright?” he asked. Leyla nodded into the pillow. "I did that too during my first presentation at work. In front of the whole team. I thought I was going to pass out."

## Heart palpitations are not the enemy

Emre told her what had helped him. His instructor had explained to him that stage fright is a completely normal stress reaction. The body releases hormones, the heart beats faster, breathing becomes shallow, the hands become clammy or tremble. “Your body thinks it’s about to run away from a bear,” Emre said. “He doesn’t know it’s only 7b.”

That sounded funny, but it actually helped Leyla. The shaking wasn't a sign of failure. It was just her body getting ready. And some people even used this energy to speak more vividly.

“The stage fright doesn’t go away completely,” said Emre. "But you can learn to deal with it. And the more you're in front, the smaller it becomes."

## Practice, but differently

The next presentation was in six weeks, this time on geography about volcanoes. Leyla decided to practice differently this time. Emre had given her advice that seemed strange to her at first: "Don't just practice the text. Practice the situation."

So when she practiced, she no longer stood comfortably at the desk, but instead stood upright like in front of the class. She spoke loudly, not in a murmur. She practiced in front of her family at dinner and asked her parents to deliberately look a little bored or ask questions. Once she made her sister cough loudly in the middle of a lecture. Leyla lost track for a moment, took a breath, looked at her map and continued. Afterwards she grinned. It hadn't been bad.

She also recorded herself on her cell phone. Watching the video was uncomfortable at first. But she noticed that she seemed much calmer than she felt. The shaking of the hands could hardly be seen in the video. And she realized that she spoke the first few sentences too quickly.

So she memorized the beginning, just the first three sentences, word for word. "Imagine it's bubbling under your feet. Rock that's a thousand degrees hot is pushing upwards. And one day it breaks through." If the beginning was right, Emre had said, the rest would follow more easily.

## The second attempt

On the morning of the volcano presentation, Leyla was nervous again. The heart was pounding, the stomach was tingling. But this time it didn't scare her. “Hello bear,” she thought and almost laughed.

During the break before, she briefly went into the hallway, stood up, pressed her feet firmly into the floor and breathed out slowly a few times, longer out than in. She took a sip of water. Then she went to the front, put down the cards, looked briefly at her friend Mira in the second row, who nodded encouragingly at her, and began with the three sentences she had memorized.

Her voice shook a little at first. After a minute she became calmer. During a slide about the eruption of Mount Vesuvius, she briefly forgot a year, looked at her map, found it and continued talking. Nobody seemed to have noticed. At the end, two classmates asked questions, and Leyla realized that she knew the answers.

“Very lively,” wrote Mr. Kaya in her review. Leyla read the sentence three times. She still had stage fright. But it no longer determined how her presentation went.

## What you can take from it

Almost everyone experiences stage fright, even those who are constantly in front of an audience for work. It can rarely be completely eliminated, but you can learn to understand it, minimize it and speak with it rather than against it. The following steps will help you before and during a presentation.

### Understand what is happening in the body

Stage fright is a stress reaction. Your body is preparing for a challenge: your heart beats faster, your breathing becomes shallower, your muscles tense. Typical signs are:

- Heart palpitations and a tingling sensation in the stomach,
- shaking hands or knees,
- a dry throat or a thin voice,
- sweaty hands or a red face,
- the feeling of having forgotten everything.

These reactions are unpleasant but not dangerous. They just show that you care about the situation. Many people find that their stage fright subsides significantly after the first minute or two of the performance.

### Prepare thoroughly

A large part of the nervousness comes from uncertainty. The better you know your topic and your process, the less room there is for fear. [Paul's story about his first presentation](/lerngeschichten/referat-vorbereiten/) shows you how to structure a presentation from the first keyword to the structure. And [Dominik's story about speaking freely](/lerngeschichten/frei-sprechen-beim-referat/) explains how you can detach yourself from a text you are reading.

### Practice the situation, not just the text

Anyone who only practices in their head or while mumbling at their desk is ill-prepared for the real situation. Therefore, make the practice similar to an emergency:

1. **Practice standing and out loud.** This will help your body get used to the posture and your own voice.
2. **Practice in front of an audience.** Family, friends or classmates are ideal test listeners. Ask them to ask a question every now and then.
3. **Incorporate disruptions.** A cough, an interjection, a ringing cell phone. This is how you learn to find the thread again after an interruption.
4. **Record you.** A video shows you what you really look like. Usually much calmer than you feel.
5. **Practice with timing.** If you know that the presentation is on time, you will be calmer.

### Memorize the beginning

The first few seconds are often the most difficult. Therefore, memorize the first two or three sentences verbatim. A strong introduction, such as a question, a surprising fact or a short picture, gives you support and at the same time arouses the interest of the class. Then you continue speaking freely with your cue cards.

### Shortly before the presentation: Calm your body

These steps help many people in the minutes before the lecture:

| What you do | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| Exhale slowly, longer out than in | calms heartbeat and breathing |
| Place your feet firmly on the floor | gives a feeling of stability and security |
| Raise your shoulders and let them fall | relieves tension in the neck and arms |
| Drink a sip of water | helps against dry mouth |
| Say the first sentences quietly to yourself | activates the introduction in the head |
| Find a friendly person in the audience | provides support at first eye contact |

Try out what helps you most beforehand. Not every exercise suits everyone.

### When it hits in the middle of the lecture

Even with good preparation, it can happen that you suddenly lose the thread or your voice wobbles. Then these steps will help:

- **Take a break.** A short pause seems much less long to the audience than it feels to you. Often it is not even perceived as a mistake.
- **Look at the card.** That's what cue cards are for. A calm look, find the next keyword, continue speaking.
- **Repeat the last sentence.** Sometimes the thread can be found by saying the last thought again in different words.
- **Be open about it.** A short sentence like “Wait a minute, I’ll take a quick look at my notes” is completely fine.

### Reduce stage fright over time

The most effective remedy for stage fright is experience. Every time you stand at the front and notice that things are going well, your body learns something new. Therefore, consciously look for small opportunities to speak in front of others: speak up more often in class, present a result from group work, say something to the class council. A theater group or a debating club can also help.

However, if the fear of lectures is so great that you don't want to go to school on those days, you can't sleep at night or the fear severely limits you in other situations, talk to a person you trust, your guidance teacher or school social work. You can find further contact points on our [Help & Advice](/hilfe-und-beratung/) page.

## Typical pitfalls

- **Don't practice at all so you don't feel nervous.** This just postpones the fear until the day of the presentation.
- **Memorize the entire text.** If you forget a word, you often lose the whole thread. Better: start by heart, the rest with keywords.
- **Speaking too fast.** Nervousness quickens the pace. Consciously plan breaks and mark them on your maps.
- **Apologizing for being nervous.** A sentence like "Sorry, I'm really nervous" draws attention to something that hardly anyone else would have noticed.

## Briefly asked

### Can you tell I have stage fright?

Usually a lot less than you think. You strongly feel trembling hands, heart palpitations or a tingling sensation in your stomach, but the audience sees little of it. A video of a practice round will often show you this clearly.

### Does it help not to look at the audience?

It feels easier in the short term, but eye contact makes your presentation more lively. A good compromise: First look at people who are friendly to you, and then slowly let your eyes wander around the room.

### What if everyone laughs because I make a mistake?

This happens less often than you fear, and when it does, it is usually not meant in a bad way. A quick smile and a calm carry on show confidence. Hardly anyone remembers a small slip of the tongue for long.
