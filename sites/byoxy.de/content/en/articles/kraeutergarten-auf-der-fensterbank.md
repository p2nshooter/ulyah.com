Harvesting fresh herbs straight from the kitchen is a small luxury that takes up little space. A sunny windowsill is enough for basil, chives, parsley, thyme and much more. At the same time, the windowsill is a demanding location: the light is weaker than outside, the air is dry in winter, and the containers are small. If you choose the right herbs and group them according to your needs, you will have fresh spice all year round.

This guide shows which herbs are suitable for the windowsill, how to combine them, which containers and soil are suitable and how to water, fertilize and harvest.

## The location

Most culinary herbs need **a lot of light.** A windowsill on a south or west window is ideal, an east window also works for many herbs. Only a few herbs grow satisfactorily on the north window.

| window | Suitable herbs |
|---|---|
| South | Basil, thyme, rosemary, oregano, sage, marjoram, savory |
| West | Basil, thyme, oregano, chives, parsley, coriander |
| East | Parsley, chives, mint, lemon balm, chervil, coriander, dill |
| North | Mint, chervil, parsley (limited), woodruff, cress |

**Temperature and air** are also important: The herbs dry out quickly directly above a hot heater, and heat-loving herbs such as basil suffer from a cold window in winter. A Styrofoam plate under the pots insulates against cold window sills.

## The herbal groups

Herbs have very different needs. If you put them together in groups, it will be much easier.

### Mediterranean herbs

Thyme, rosemary, oregano, sage, marjoram, savory, lavender.

- **Light:** Lots of sun
- **Soil:** Lean, permeable, with sand or pumice
- **Water:** Little, let the soil dry in between
- **Fertilizer:** Very little

### Moisture loving herbs

Basil, parsley, chives, coriander, dill, chervil, mint, lemon balm.

- **Light:** Bright, partial shade
- **Soil:** Rich in nutrients, humus
- **Water:** Evenly moist
- **Fertilizer:** Regularly

These two groups should **not be in the same container**. Rosemary and basil in one pot means at least one plant is suffering.

## vessels and earth

- **Pots with drainage hole**, at least 12 to 15 cm in diameter per plant. Herbs dry out quickly in very small pots.
- **Planters or coasters** to protect the windowsill. Drain off any standing water.
- **Herb boxes** for the windowsill, with separate compartments for Mediterranean and moisture-loving herbs.
- **Clay pots** for Mediterranean herbs because excess moisture evaporates.
- **Soil:** Herbal soil. For Mediterranean herbs, mix with a third of sand or pumice. See [Potting soil and substrate](/ratgeber/blumenerde-und-substrat/).

## Pouring

| group | Pouring |
|---|---|
| Mediterranean herbs | When the top 2 to 3 cm is dry; rarer in winter |
| Moisture loving herbs | When the surface is slightly dry |

It is best to water in the morning. It is better to water the basil from below. The most common mistakes are overwatering with thyme and rosemary and drying out with parsley and basil.

## Fertilize

Supply moisture-loving herbs such as basil, parsley and chives with organic herbal fertilizer at half the dosage every two weeks from spring to late summer. Fertilize Mediterranean herbs lightly no more than once a month. Too much fertilizer reduces the aroma.

## Harvesting

- **Harvest regularly:** This promotes bushy growth.
- **Shoot tips instead of individual leaves:** For basil, mint, oregano and sage, cut off entire shoot tips above a pair of leaves.
- **Parsley:** Cut off the outer stems close to the base, leaving the heart in the center.
- **Chives:** Cut about two to three centimeters above the ground. It sprouts again.
- **Never harvest more than a third** at a time.
- **Best Time:** Late morning, when the dew has dried and the essential oils are at their strongest.

## Herbs in winter

In winter there is little light on the windowsill and most herbs hardly grow.

- **Plant Lamp:** An LED plant light above the windowsill, 12 to 14 hours per day, allows for fresh growth even in winter.
- **Mediterranean herbs** such as rosemary and thyme are best kept bright and cool in winter at 5 to 12 °C, for example in an unheated room.
- **Chives** need a cold period. Put the pot outside for a few weeks in the fall and let it get some frost, then bring it inside the house. It then sprouts again.
- **Cress and microgreens** are the easiest winter herbs: quick harvest, little light required.
- **Water more sparingly** and do not fertilize.

## Practical example: The kitchen window sill

A kitchen windowsill facing west housed basil, rosemary, thyme and parsley together in a long box. The basil and parsley developed brown tips, the rosemary dried out on the inside and rotted at the bottom. The box was dissolved: rosemary and thyme went into clay pots with herb soil and sand, basil and parsley went into plastic pots with nutrient-rich herb soil. The two groups were cast separately. In winter, the rosemary moved into the cool bedroom window, and an LED strip was installed above the kitchen window sill. Since then, fresh herbs have been available all year round.

If you're growing herbs on your windowsill for the first time, it's best to start with three or four species from the same group. This way you get to know their needs before adding other herbs with different requirements. A good start for a sunny window is thyme, oregano and rosemary in clay pots, for a bright east window chives, parsley and mint in plastic pots.

## Growing herbs from seeds

Many herbs can be sown cheaply yourself. Cress is the fastest success: it germinates on damp cotton or kitchen paper in two to three days and is ready to harvest after about a week. Basil is a light germinator whose seeds are only pressed on. Parsley, on the other hand, needs patience because it often takes two to four weeks to germinate; Soaking the seeds overnight can speed up the start. Coriander and dill germinate reliably, but bloom quickly when it is warm.

For a consistent harvest, subsequent sowing is worthwhile: Sow fast-growing herbs such as cress, coriander or chervil in a new pot every two to three weeks, instead of a large quantity once. This means that a young, productive pot is always available while the older one is being harvested.

## Microgreens: Harvest after a few days

Microgreens are young seedlings that are harvested as soon as they have formed their first leaves, around one to three weeks depending on the species. They taste intense and require hardly any space: a flat bowl with soil or a growing fleece, densely sown seeds and a bright place are sufficient. Radishes, mustard, rocket, broccoli, peas and sunflowers are well suited.

Make sure the containers are clean and the seeds are fresh, water moderately and provide air movement to prevent mold from forming. Harvest with clean scissors just above the ground. After that, most microgreens do not grow back and the tray is reseeded.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I keep herbs from the supermarket permanently?

Yes, lots of them. It is important to divide and repot densely sown pots.

### Which herbs are easiest?

Chives, mint, thyme and cress are considered particularly easy to care for.

### Do I need a plant lamp?

Usually not in summer. It helps a lot in winter and on darker windows. See [Houseplant Light](/ratgeber/licht-fuer-zimmerpflanzen/).

### Why are my herbs getting long and thin?

Long, thin and pale shoots are a typical sign of a lack of light, especially in winter or on north-facing windows. The plants stretch towards the light and become soft and lacking in aroma. Place it in the brightest window or add a plant lamp and cut back the decayed shoots so that the plant sprouts again compactly.

### Dry or freeze?

Robust herbs with firm leaves such as rosemary, thyme, oregano and sage are easy to dry. Delicate herbs such as parsley, chives, dill and basil lose a lot of flavor when dried and are better frozen finely chopped.

## Conclusion

A herb garden on the windowsill is successful if you group herbs according to their needs: Mediterranean herbs sunny, lean and dry, moisture-loving herbs bright, nutrient-rich and evenly moist. Choose sufficiently large pots with a drainage hole, regularly harvest entire shoot tips and supplement the light in winter. This means you have fresh seasoning at hand all year round.
