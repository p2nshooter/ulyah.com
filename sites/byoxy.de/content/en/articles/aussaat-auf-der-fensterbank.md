Growing tomatoes, peppers, chili, basil or summer flowers from your own seeds is one of the most beautiful experiences for balcony and plant lovers. A tiny seed turns into a strong young plant within a few weeks. At the same time, you have access to a much larger variety of varieties than in the garden center. The windowsill is the nursery. If you follow a few basic rules, you can avoid typical problems such as long, thin seedlings or mold.

This guide explains the right time, the right material, how germination works, how to care for, prick out and harden seedlings.

## The schedule

Many people sow seeds too early. In February, the light on the windowsill is still weak and the seedlings are rotting. Plants sown too early are often too large and weakened by mid-May, when they are allowed outside.

| plant | Sowing on the windowsill | Outside |
|---|---|---|
| Chili and peppers | End of February to mid-March | After the Ice Saints, mid-May |
| Tomatoes | Mid-March to early April | Mid-May |
| eggplant | End of February to mid-March | End of May |
| Basil | April | At the end of May, or left on the windowsill |
| Cucumbers and zucchini | End of April to beginning of May | End of May |
| Nasturtium | April | Mid-May |
| Summer flowers such as zinnias, marigolds | April | Mid-May |
| Salad | From February, ongoing | From April, frost hardy |

In mild regions it can start a little earlier, in harsh regions a little later. If you have a plant lamp, you can sow earlier.

## material

### potting soil

Special potting soil is **low in nutrients, fine and germ-free.** This is important: few nutrients force the seedlings to form strong roots, and germ-free soil reduces mold and damping off. Normal potting soil is too rich in nutrients and too coarse.

### vessels

- **Growing trays** with transparent hood (mini greenhouse)
- **Multi-pot plates** with individual cells, practical for larger seeds
- **Peat or coconut spring pots** that can be planted later
- **Recycling:** Yogurt cups with holes in the bottom, egg cartons (soak quickly), toilet paper rolls

Always use clean containers and clean used bowls with hot water.

### Seeds

High-quality, fresh seeds germinate more reliably. Most seeds remain viable for a few years, exceptions such as parsnips or chives only last one to two years. Seed-resistant varieties are suitable for growing your own seeds the following year.

## Germination: Light and dark germinators

| Type | Meaning | Examples | sowing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light germinator | Need light to germinate | Basil, lettuce, parsley (partly), lots of summer flowers, strawberries | Just press it down, don't cover it or barely cover it |
| Dark germinator | Only germinate without light | Tomato, pumpkin, nasturtium, marigold | Cover with soil |
| Neutral | Germinate regardless of light | Lots of vegetables | Cover lightly |

As a rule of thumb: **Cover seeds with soil about twice to three times as thick as they are large.** Fine seeds are only pressed down.

## Temperature

| plant | Germination temperature |
|---|---|
| Chili, peppers, eggplant | 25 to 28°C |
| Tomato | 20 to 25°C |
| Basil | 20 to 25°C |
| Salad | It germinates poorly at temperatures of 15 to 18 °C and above 20 °C |
| Summer flowers | 18 to 22°C |

Heat-loving species such as chili germinate very slowly or not at all if the temperature is too low. A place on a warm shelf or a heating mat helps. **Important:** As soon as the seedlings appear, they should be kept brighter and a little cooler, otherwise they will rot.

## Sowing step by step

1. **Fill the container:** Loosely fill in the potting soil and press it down lightly.
2. **Moisten:** Moisten the soil evenly with a spray bottle or from below before sowing.
3. **Sowing:** Distribute seeds evenly or place two to three seeds in cells. Don't sow too densely.
4. **Cover** depending on germ type.
5. **Label:** Note the variety and date.
6. **Cover:** With a clear cap or film to maintain even moisture.
7. **Set up warm and bright.**

## Seedling care

### Ventilate

Remove the hood for a few minutes every day to prevent mold. After germination, ventilate longer and longer and finally remove completely.

### Pouring

Keep the soil evenly slightly moist, but never wet. It is best to water with a spray bottle or from below. Too much moisture leads to mold and the dreaded **damping-off disease**, in which seedlings shrink at the base and fall over. See [Mold on Potting Soil](/ratgeber/schimmel-auf-blumenerde/).

### Light

Light is the most critical factor in spring. Seedlings need the **brightest spot** directly on the south, east or west window. Rotate the bowls every day to prevent the plants from growing crooked.

**Growing:** Long, thin, pale stems with small leaves show lack of light when there is too much heat. A plant lamp, 10 to 20 cm above the seedlings, for 12 to 14 hours per day can help. More about this in [Light for houseplants](/ratgeber/licht-fuer-zimmerpflanzen/).

## Prick

Pricking out means transferring the seedlings from dense sowing into individual pots. This gives them more space, light and nutrients.

### When?

As soon as the **first real pair of leaves** appears after the cotyledons. Cotyledons are the first, usually simply shaped leaves. The correct leaves have the typical shape of the plant.

### How?

1. Fill small pots (6 to 9 cm) with a mixture of potting soil and some herb or vegetable soil.
2. Drill a planting hole with a pricking stick, pencil or spoon handle.
3. Carefully lift the seedling out of the soil with the stick and hold it **by a cotyledon**, never by the delicate stem.
4. If necessary, shorten long roots slightly.
5. Place the seedling in the hole. **Tomatoes** can be planted deep down to the cotyledons; they form additional roots on the stem.
6. Press the soil lightly and water carefully.

## Fertilize

About two to three weeks after planting, you can start with a low-dose liquid fertilizer, once a week in a quarter to half of the package instructions. See [Fertilizing Houseplants](/ratgeber/zimmerpflanzen-duengen/).

## Hardening

Plants grown on the windowsill are not used to wind, sun and temperature fluctuations. Before they finally go outside, they are hardened over **one to two weeks**:

1. On mild days, initially place in a protected, shady place for one to two hours.
2. Increase the duration every day and slowly get used to more sun.
3. Bring it in at night while it's cold.
4. After the Ice Saints (mid-May), heat-loving plants are allowed outside permanently.

Without hardening off, the leaves will burn in the sun or be damaged by the wind.

## Typical mistakes

| problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| No germination | Too cold, sown too deeply, too dry, old seeds | Check temperature and sowing depth, fresh seeds |
| Long, thin seedlings | Too little light, too warm | Place the plant lamp brighter and cooler |
| Seedlings fall over, stems contracted | Damping-off due to wetness | Water less, ventilate, germ-free soil |
| Mold on the earth | Too humid, too little air | Remove the hood and ventilate |
| Yellow cotyledons | Of course or nutrient deficiency after weeks | Prick out, fertilize lightly |
| Leaf damage after being put out | Not hardened | Slowly get used to the sun and wind |

## Practical example: Growing tomatoes

In the first year, tomatoes were sown at the end of February on a north windowsill above the heater. They germinated quickly, but within two weeks they became long, thin and bright green and collapsed. In the second year, sown were sown at the end of March, germination took place warm on a shelf, and immediately after emergence the trays were placed in a south-facing window in a cooler room, supplemented by an LED lamp. After pricking out the tomatoes were planted deep. In mid-May, strong, compact plants were ready and, after hardening off for a week, they were moved to the balcony. More about this in [Tomatoes on the Balcony](/ratgeber/tomaten-auf-dem-balkon/).

## Store seeds and check germination

You don't have to throw away any leftover seeds. Seeds remain germinable for the longest time if they are stored in a cool, dry and dark place, for example in paper bags in a well-sealed jar in the cellar or refrigerator. Moisture and heat are the biggest enemies, which is why seed bags don't belong on the warm windowsill.

How long seeds remain viable varies greatly. Tomato, pumpkin and cabbage seeds often germinate well after several years, while parsnips, chives and parsley decline significantly after just one or two years. For older seeds, a simple germination test is worthwhile: Place ten seeds on damp kitchen paper, roll them up loosely, put them in a freezer bag and keep it warm. After one to two weeks, count the seeds that have germinated. If seven out of ten germinate, the germination rate is around seventy percent, and you simply sow a little more densely.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I sow without potting soil?

It works, but it's worse. Normal soil is often too nutrient-rich and not germ-free. Potting soil is worthwhile for beginners.

### Do I absolutely need a plant lamp?

Not always. If you sow later from the end of March on a bright south-facing window, it often works without it. A lamp is very helpful for early sowing and dark windows.

### How many seeds per pot?

For larger seeds, two to three per cell; later the strongest seedling is kept. Sprinkle fine seeds thinly in a bowl.

### Why are my seedlings suddenly falling over?

When young seedlings become thin and glassy at the base of the stem and fall over, damping-off disease is usually the cause, caused by soil fungi. It is promoted by cultivation that is too wet, cool and poorly ventilated. Infected plants cannot be saved; remove them immediately. Clean containers, fresh potting soil, economical watering from below and daily ventilation help preventatively.

### When can pre-grown plants go outside?

You should only plant frost-sensitive plants such as tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers and basil outdoors after the Ice Saints in mid-May, or a little later in rough locations. They must first be hardened for one to two weeks. Robust species such as lettuce, kohlrabi or peas, on the other hand, can tolerate a sheltered place outside in April if you cover them overnight when frost is forecast.

## Conclusion

Sowing on the windowsill is successful with the right schedule, germ-free potting soil, the right germination temperature and, above all, plenty of light after emergence. Prick out the seedlings as soon as the first pair of true leaves appear, fertilize sparingly and harden the plants before planting out. Small seeds grow strong plants for the balcony and garden.
