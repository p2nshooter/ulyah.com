Houseplants live in a limited volume of soil. The nutrients that are in the soil when you buy or repot are used up or washed out after a few weeks to months. Without replenishment, new leaves become smaller and paler and growth stops. At the same time, too much fertilizer is more damaging than too little: salts accumulate, roots burn, leaf tips turn brown.

This guide explains which nutrients plants need, what types of fertilizer there are, when and how much you should fertilize and how to recognize deficiencies and over-fertilization.

## What nutrients do plants need?

Plants need a variety of nutrients. The three most important nutrients are listed as **NPK** on every fertilizer package:

| nutrient | characters | task | Deficiency symptoms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | N | Leaf and shoot growth, green color | Older leaves turn uniformly light green to yellow, growth is weak |
| Phosphorus | P | Roots, flowers, energy balance | Dark, partly reddish-purple leaves, few flowers |
| Potassium | K | Water balance, strength, resistance | Brown edges on older leaves, soft tissue |

There are also **secondary nutrients** such as magnesium, calcium and sulfur as well as **trace elements** such as iron, manganese, zinc and boron. Iron deficiency is shown, for example, by yellow young leaves with green veins, magnesium deficiency by yellow areas between the veins of older leaves.

A statement like **7-3-6** on the package means: 7 percent nitrogen, 3 percent phosphorus and 6 percent potassium. Green plant fertilizers usually contain more nitrogen, flowering plant fertilizers more phosphorus and potassium.

## Types of fertilizer

### liquid fertilizer

The most commonly used fertilizer for houseplants. It is added to the irrigation water and is immediately available.

- **Advantages:** Easy to dose, quick effect, adaptable to the season.
- **Disadvantages:** Must be given regularly.

### Long-term fertilizer

Available as sticks, cones or coated beads. They release nutrients over weeks to months.

- **Advantages:** Convenient, ideal for people who often forget to fertilize.
- **Disadvantages:** The release depends on humidity and temperature and cannot be stopped. Fertilizer sticks release the nutrients very selectively, which can cause roots in their vicinity to burn.

### Organic fertilizers

For example, liquid plant manure, worm humus or organic liquid fertilizers based on vinasse.

- **Advantages:** Promotes soil life, gently.
- **Disadvantages:** Often associated with odor indoors, works slower, can attract fungus gnats.

### Special fertilizer

- **Cactus fertilizer:** Little nitrogen, more potassium and phosphorus.
- **Orchid fertilizer:** Very low concentration, for salt-sensitive roots.
- **Citrus fertilizer:** With more iron and magnesium, for lime-sensitive potted plants.
- **Hydrofertilizer:** Especially for hydroponics and mineral substrates.

## When to fertilize?

| time | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| March to September | Fertilize regularly, every 1 to 4 weeks depending on the plant |
| October to February | Pause or greatly reduced |
| After repotting | No fertilizer for 6 to 8 weeks, fresh soil is pre-fertilized |
| Sick or stressed plants | First fix the cause, then fertilize again |
| Freshly rooted cuttings | Only after a few weeks, in low doses |

In winter, most houseplants barely grow because of the lack of light. What they don't use remains in the earth as salt. Exception: Plants under a plant lamp that grow vigorously even in winter can continue to be fertilized.

## How much to fertilize?

- **Package information as an upper limit:** Many experienced plant lovers prefer to fertilize more often at half the dosage than less often at full dosage.
- **Only fertilize in moist soil:** Fertilizer on bone-dry soil can damage roots. If the soil is very dry, water with clear water first, then fertilize.
- **Dose measuredly:** Use a measuring cup or syringe. “One shot” is often way too many.

### Practical calculation aid

If the bottle says “5 ml to 1 liter of water” and you are using a 2 liter watering can, add 10 ml of fertilizer. For half the dosage it is 5 ml to the full pot.

## Recognize over-fertilization

- **Brown, burnt leaf tips and edges,** even though the plant is getting enough water.
- **White or yellowish crusts** on the surface of the soil or on the edge of the pot.
- **Plant wilted despite moist soil** because the roots are damaged by too high a salt concentration.
- **Fast, soft, masty growth** with long leaf spacing.
- **Increased susceptibility** to aphids and other pests.

### What to do if you over-fertilize?

1. **Rinse:** Place the pot in the shower or sink and slowly pour in three to four times the amount of lukewarm water in the pot. Drain well.
2. **Remove crusts:** Scrape off the top layer of soil and replace it with fresh soil.
3. **Repot if there is severe damage:** Remove old soil, rinse roots, place in fresh soil.
4. **Break:** Do not fertilize for at least four to six weeks.

## Detect nutrient deficiencies

| Symptom | Possible cause |
|---|---|
| Older leaves are uniformly light green to yellow | Nitrogen deficiency |
| Young leaves yellow, veins green | Iron deficiency, often due to too high a pH value (calcareous water) |
| Older leaves yellow between the veins | Magnesium deficiency |
| New leaves significantly smaller than old ones | General lack of nutrients, often also lack of light |
| Hardly any flowers on flowering plants | Lack of phosphorus or too little light |

Deficiency symptoms are often confused with casting defects. Before fertilizing more, check light and water first. [Yellow leaves: causes] (/ratgeber/gelbe-blaetter-ursachen/) provides detailed information.

## Home remedies: coffee grounds, eggshells, banana peels

- **Coffee grounds** contain some nitrogen, potassium and phosphorus. Mixed in small quantities it can be useful. It molds easily on the surface of the earth and encourages fungus gnats.
- **Eggshells** provide calcium but decompose very slowly. They hardly play a role for houseplants.
- **Banana peels** contain potassium, but they decompose slowly in the pot and attract flies.

Home remedies cannot completely replace fertilizer. Anyone who uses them should use them sparingly and carefully.

## Fertilize by plant group

| Plant group | Recommendation during the growing season |
|---|---|
| Fast-growing leafy plants (Monstera, ivy) | Green plant fertilizer every 2 to 3 weeks |
| Slow growing (lucky feather, bow hemp) | Every 4 weeks, half dose |
| Cacti and succulents | Cactus fertilizer every 4 weeks |
| Orchids | Orchid fertilizer, weak, every 2 to 4 weeks |
| Calathea, ferns | Every 3 to 4 weeks, half dose |
| Flowering plants | Fertilize flowering plants every 1 to 2 weeks |
| Herbs and vegetables in the pot | Weekly organic or mineral, depending on the type |

## Practical example: More is not better

An ivy plant should grow faster, so the owner added a heavy shot of liquid fertilizer to the water every time she watered, about weekly, even in winter. After a few months, brown leaf tips appeared, a white crust formed on the edge of the pot, and several leaves turned yellow. The plant was thoroughly rinsed in the shower, the top layer of soil replaced, and no fertilizer was applied for six weeks. In the spring it was fertilized again, this time every three weeks at half the dosage. The new leaves remained healthy.

## Store and dose fertilizer correctly

Liquid fertilizers usually last for several years when stored sealed, cool and dark. They should not be left in the sun or near food and should be kept out of the reach of children and pets. Shake the bottle briefly before each use because components may separate. Store long-term fertilizer and fertilizer sticks in a dry place, as moisture attacks the shell of the beads. A simple habit helps against over-fertilization: Use the fertilizer only on one fixed day of the month or every two weeks and note on a calendar or in an app which plants were fertilized. This means you neither forget to fertilize nor accidentally fertilize twice.

## Read the label correctly

Almost every fertilizer package has three numbers on it, for example 7-3-6. They indicate the proportion of the three main nutrients in percent: nitrogen, phosphate and potassium, abbreviated NPK. Nitrogen promotes the growth of leaves and shoots, phosphorus supports roots and flowers, and potassium strengthens resistance and water balance. Green plant fertilizers usually emphasize nitrogen, flowering plant fertilizers contain more phosphorus and potassium.

Also pay attention to trace elements such as iron, manganese, zinc, copper and boron. In addition to the main nutrients, a complete fertilizer also contains these substances in small quantities. If they are missing, symptoms of deficiency will appear after some time, especially in plants that have not been repotted for a long time. The dosage information on the label is an upper limit; For most houseplants, half the amount is enough.

## Fertilize after repotting and on sick plants

Fresh potting soil is usually pre-fertilized and nourishes the plant for the first four to eight weeks. Therefore, do not start fertilizing immediately after repotting. Even weakened plants, for example after root rot, severe pest infestation or complete drying out, should only be fertilized once they have recovered and are forming new leaves again. Fertilizer is not a cure: A plant with damaged roots cannot absorb the nutrient salts and they put additional strain on the roots.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to fertilize purchased plants immediately?

No. Plants from the trade are usually well taken care of. Start fertilizing about four to six weeks after purchase, during the growing season.

### Are fertilizer sticks bad?

They are comfortable, but distribute nutrients unevenly. If you use them, you should put them at a sufficient distance from the trunk and not directly on thick roots.

### Do hydroponic plants need different fertilizer?

Yes, special hydro fertilizer. Normal fertilizers are designed for soil in which some of the nutrients are bound.

### Can I use lawn or garden fertilizer on houseplants?

Better not. Garden fertilizers are tailored to other plants and soils, and their grains often release too many nutrient salts at once in small pots. This can damage the roots. For houseplants, liquid fertilizers that you dilute with water are easier to dose.

Garden soil and garden fertilizer therefore belong on beds and balcony boxes, not in houseplant pots.

For houseplants, a complete liquid fertilizer is the easiest choice.

## Conclusion

Fertilizing is important, but in moderation. Fertilize regularly from spring to late summer with a suitable fertilizer, preferably weakly rather than rarely heavily, only in moist soil, and give the plants a break in winter. Watch for signs of deficiency or over-fertilization and always combine fertilizing with proper light and watering. Nutrients alone do not make a plant healthy.
