Some houseplants do not grow from a single stem, but from many shoots that come directly from the soil. Over time they will completely fill the pot. For these plants, dividing is the quickest and easiest method of propagation: one large plant turns into two, three or more finished plants with their own roots in just a few minutes. At the same time, dividing rejuvenates the plant and gives it new space.

This guide explains which plants can be divided, when the right time is, how to proceed step by step and how to care for the sections afterwards.

## Which plants can be divided?

Plants that form **several shoots from the soil**, grow with rhizomes, clumps or tubers or have many children gathering around them are suitable.

| plant | Growth habit | division |
|---|---|---|
| Bow hemp (Sansevieria) | Rhizomes | Very light, with knife |
| Lucky Feather (Zamioculcas) | tubers | Easy, with knife |
| Calathea | Rhizomes, bushy | Easy, carefully pull apart |
| Peace Lily (Spathiphyllum) | Horst | Easy |
| Ferns (e.g. sword fern) | Horst, rhizomes | Easy |
| spider plant | Clump with thick roots | Very light |
| Aloe, Echeveria, Haworthia | Rosettes with children | Easy |
| Cobbler palm (Aspidistra) | Rhizomes | Light but slow growth |
| Strelitzia | Horst | Medium, powerful tool |
| Mountain palm (Chamaedorea) | Several stems in the pot | Possible, sensitive |
| Herbs like chives, mint | Horst, foothills | Very light |

**Not suitable** are plants with a single trunk, such as rubber tree, fiddlehead fig, monstera with only one shoot or dragon tree. They are propagated via cuttings. See [Cuttings in a Water Jar](/ratgeber/stecklinge-im-wasserglas/).

### Several plants in the pot

Many commercial plants, such as ivy, philodendron, mountain palm or monstera, consist of several cuttings or seedlings that have been placed together in a pot. These can also be carefully separated when repotting. Whether this makes sense depends on whether you prefer one large, bushy plant or several smaller ones.

## The best time

- **Spring** is ideal, just before or as growth begins. The sections then have the entire season to form new roots.
- **When repotting:** Dividing and repotting are best combined.
- **Not during flowering**, not in winter and not on stressed or sick plants.

Signs that division makes sense: The pot is overfull, shoots are crowding the edge, roots are growing out of the drainage hole, the plant is becoming bare in the middle or has simply become too big.

## Tools and material

- Sharp, clean knife or garden knife; for strong roots, an old bread knife or spade
- Clean scissors
- New pots with a drain hole to match the size of the pieces
- Fresh soil to suit the plant. See [potting soil and substrate](/ratgeber/blumenerde-und-substrat/)
- Pad, gloves
- Maybe a spray bottle to keep exposed roots moist

## Step-by-step instructions

### 1. Prepare

Water the plant lightly the day before so that the root ball is supple. Exception: Dry dividing succulents, arched hemp and lucky feathers.

### 2. Unpotting

Carefully remove the plant from the pot. If the pot is heavily rooted, run a knife along the edge of the pot. Plastic pots can be cut open if necessary.

### 3. Loosen soil

Gently shake or pluck out the old soil with your fingers until the individual shoots and their roots become visible. This way you can see where natural dividing lines are.

### 4. Share

- **Loose clumps** such as calathea, ferns or peace lilies: carefully pull apart with your hands. Grab the base, not pull the leaves.
- **Firm rhizomes and tubers** such as bow hemp or lucky feather: Cut through the connection points with a sharp knife.
- **Very dense root balls** like the Strelitzia: Cut through the ball with a strong knife or spade.

Each section needs **at least one, preferably several shoots and a healthy proportion of roots.** It is better to have a few large parts than many tiny parts.

### 5. Check roots

Remove rotten, soft, brown roots and dead plant parts. Very long roots can be shortened slightly. For succulents, arched hemp and lucky feathers, allow the interfaces to dry for a day.

### 6. Planting

Place each piece in a suitable pot. The pot shouldn't be too big: a little space around the root ball is enough. Plant the sections at the same height as before, fill with soil and press down lightly.

### 7. Pour on

Pour in thoroughly and allow excess water to drain off. Only water succulents after a few days.

## Examples in detail

### Bow hemp

The rhizome is divided with a knife so that each part has at least one or two rosettes of leaves and a piece of rhizome with roots. Allow to dry, place in succulent soil and do not water for a week. See [Caring for Bow Hemp](/ratgeber/bogenhanf-sansevieria-pflege/).

### Calathea

Carefully pull the ball apart so that each part has at least three to four leaves and rhizomes. Then pay particular attention to consistent moisture and high humidity, perhaps under a transparent hood for a few days. See [Maintaining Calathea](/ratgeber/calathea-pflege/).

### Peace Lily

The nest can usually be easily pulled into two to four parts with your hands. Drooping leaves in the first few days are normal.

### Ferns

Large ferns such as sword fern are cut into several pieces with their own roots. Remove old, brown fronds. Then place it brightly, without sun and with even moisture.

## Aftercare

| period | Care |
|---|---|
| First 2 weeks | Bright, without direct sun, warm, even humidity, high humidity in tropical species |
| Week 2 to 6 | Water normally, pay attention to new shoots |
| From week 6 to 8 | Start fertilizing at half the dosage |

Some drooping leaves or a yellow leaf after division is normal because roots have been damaged. The plant compensates for this in the following weeks.

## Typical mistakes

1. **Too small sections** without enough roots or shoots.
2. **Pull on leaves** instead of grabbing the base.
3. **Divide in winter** when the plant is barely growing.
4. **Pots too big** for the cuts.
5. **Fertilize immediately**, although fresh soil contains nutrients.
6. **Bright sun** immediately after sharing.

## Practical example: One peace lily becomes three

A five-year-old peace lily had become so dense that water was barely absorbed when watered and the leaves were turning yellow in the middle. She was potted out in April. The ball consisted almost entirely of roots. Using hands and a knife, it was separated into three parts, each with six to ten shoots. Dead leaves and brown roots were removed. The three plants were placed in 15 cm pots with fresh soil and left brightly without sun for two weeks. After an initial droop, the leaves straightened up and by summer all three bloomed.

## Sharing as a rejuvenation treatment

Many plants that can be divided become bare or unsightly in the middle over the years, while strong young shoots grow around the edges. Sharing is then not only a method of reproduction, but also a method of rejuvenation. The young, outer parts usually grow best, while the old, woody or worn-out center can be disposed of.

Especially with ferns, spider plants, chives or peace lilies, a plant often appears healthier and more compact after dividing than before. Therefore, plan divisions not only if you want new plants, but also if a plant, despite good care, is weakening and has completely filled the pot.

## Divide perennials on balconies and terraces

Perennials in pots and boxes can also be divided, for example hostas, daylilies, grasses, cranesbills or chives. A tried and tested rule of thumb is: Divide perennials that bloom in spring after flowering or in fall, while perennials that bloom in summer and fall in spring. This gives the sections time to grow before they form their next flower.

## When sharing doesn't work

Not every plant can be divided. Plants with only a single trunk or growing point, such as fiddleleaf figs, rubber trees, yuccas, many palms or monsteras, would be destroyed if the root ball was cut. You propagate them via cuttings or moss. Phalaenopsis orchids also grow from a single heart and cannot be divided.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many parts can I make from a plant?

That depends on the size. Each part should have enough shoots and roots to grow independently. For medium-sized plants, two to three parts are common.

### Can I share with a spade?

For very large, robust plants such as Strelitzia or large grasses, yes. A knife is enough for most houseplants.

### What do I do with sections without roots?

With succulents you can let them dry and root. Other plants often root in water or under a hood. See [Offshoot and Kindel](/ratgeber/ableger-und-kindel/).

### How long does it take for divided plants to become bushy again?

That depends on the vigor. Fast-growing plants like spider plants or chives often look full again after just a few weeks, while slower ones like bowed hemp take a whole season or longer.

### Do I have to fertilize after dividing?

No. Fresh soil contains enough nutrients for the first few weeks, and the injured roots still have to grow back. Do not start fertilizing again until after four to six weeks at the earliest.

### Can I share a flowering plant?

Better not, because flowering takes a lot of energy. Wait until after flowering. If you have to, cut off the flowers so that the pieces can put their energy into new roots.

### How do I prevent rot at the interfaces?

Instead of ripping the roots, use a clean, sharp knife. Large cut surfaces on fleshy rhizomes, such as bow hemp, are allowed to dry for a few hours before planting, and water sparingly in the first few days.

## Conclusion

Dividing is the quickest way to propagate and rejuvenate bushy houseplants at the same time. Divide in the spring when repotting, make sure that each section has enough shoots and roots, place the parts in suitable pots with fresh soil and care for them particularly carefully for a few weeks.
