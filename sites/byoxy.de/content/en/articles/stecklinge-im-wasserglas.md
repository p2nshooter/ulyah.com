Cut off a shoot, place it in a glass of water and watch how white roots grow after a few weeks: Propagation in a glass of water is the easiest and most illustrative way to grow many plants from one plant. It works with many popular houseplants, costs nothing and is fun, especially with children. If you follow a few basic rules, you will almost always be successful.

This guide explains which plants are suitable, how to cut cuttings correctly, how to care for them in water and when and how to plant them in soil.

## Suitable plants

| plant | Rooting in water | Duration until the first roots |
|---|---|---|
| Ivy plant | Very light | 1 to 3 weeks |
| Philodendron (climbing) | Very light | 2 to 3 weeks |
| Monstera | Lightweight, knots required | 2 to 6 weeks |
| Tradescantia | Very light | A few days to 2 weeks |
| Coleus | Very light | 1 to 2 weeks |
| Spider plant (kindel) | Very light | 1 to 2 weeks |
| Rubber tree | Means | 4 to 8 weeks |
| Fiddle fig | Means | 4 to 8 weeks |
| Basil, mint | Very light | 1 to 2 weeks |
| Hoya | Means | 3 to 6 weeks |
| Begonia | Easy | 2 to 4 weeks |
| Cacti, succulents | Not suitable, rot easily | Better in dry substrate |

In principle, plants with soft to semi-woody shoots and nodes are particularly suitable. Succulents are better propagated using dry methods. See [Propagate Succulents Using Leaf Cuttings](/ratgeber/sukkulenten-vermehren-blattstecklinge/).

## What is a knot?

A node is the point on the stem where a leaf attaches. There are sleeping buds and, in many climbing plants, aerial roots. New roots and shoots grow from the nodes. **For most plants, a cutting without a node will not form roots** or, if it does, will never produce a new shoot. In the case of Monstera and Philodendron, a leaf without a stem piece with nodes is therefore not successful in propagation.

## The right time

Rooting works best **in spring and summer** when the plants are actively growing and there is plenty of light. In winter it is possible, but it takes longer and the risk of rot is higher.

## Cut cuttings

### material

- Sharp, clean scissors or knife (wipe with alcohol)
- Glass, vase or bottle, transparent or colored
- Water at room temperature, preferably stale or filtered

### Step by step

1. **Choose healthy shoots:** Strong, without pests, not flowering.
2. **Length:** Usually 10 to 15 centimeters with two to four knots. For Monstera, a piece with a node, an aerial root and a leaf is sufficient.
3. **Cut:** Cut just under a knot, about half an inch to a centimeter. The cut should be smooth, not bruised.
4. **Remove lower leaves:** Pluck off any leaves that would stand in water. Leaves in the water rot and spoil the water.
5. **Shrink large leaves:** For large-leaved plants, you can leave one or two leaves on top or cut large leaves in half to reduce evaporation.

## Care in the water

### water level

The knot or knots should be in the water with the leaves above it. For very small glasses, a knot under water is often enough.

### Light and warmth

- **Bright, but without direct sun.** In the blazing sun, the water heats up and algae grows.
- **Warm:** 20 to 25 °C is ideal. A place on a warm shelf or near, but not directly over, the heater will speed rooting.

### Water change

Change the water every three to seven days, immediately if it becomes cloudy. Fresh water contains more oxygen and fewer bacteria. Rinse the glass while doing this. If algae forms, use a dark or opaque container.

### Patience

Depending on the plant, the first roots appear after a few days to eight weeks. Nothing can happen for a while. As long as the cutting is green and firm, everything is fine.

## When to put in soil?

The best time is when the roots are **three to five centimeters long** and ideally have formed the first lateral roots. If you leave the cuttings in water for too long, they form pure “water roots” that first have to adapt to the soil.

### Implement step by step

1. **Choose a small pot:** 8 to 12 cm in diameter, with a drain hole.
2. **Loose soil:** Houseplant soil with some perlite or coconut fiber.
3. **Use carefully:** The delicate water roots break easily. First fill in some soil, hold the cutting in and fill it carefully.
4. **Pour well.**
5. **Transition period:** In the first two to three weeks, keep the soil evenly slightly moist so that the roots can adapt. Then water normally.
6. **Placing several cuttings** together in a pot will result in a bushy plant more quickly.

## Alternative: permanently in water

Ivy, philodendron, spider plant and some other plants can grow permanently in water. What is then important is:

- Regular water changes
- Occasionally some low concentration hydro fertilizer
- A bright location

The plants usually grow slower than in soil, but are decorative.

## Typical mistakes

| problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Cutting rots from below, becomes black and mushy | Leaves in water, water not changed, too cold | Cut off the rotten part, fresh water, remove leaves |
| No roots after weeks | No knot, too dark, too cold | Cut new cuttings with nodes, place warmer and brighter |
| Leaves turn yellow | Of course, the plant uses reserves | Wait while the stem is firm |
| Green algae in a glass | Too much light on the water | Dark glass or change more frequently |
| Cutting wilts after being placed in soil | Roots damaged, conversion | Keep evenly moist, be patient |

## Rooting aids

Rooting powder or gel with plant hormones can help with difficult-to-root plants such as rubber tree or fiddleleaf fig. It is unnecessary for easy-rooting plants such as ivy. Willow water, i.e. water in which willow branches have stood, is used as a natural home remedy.

## Practical example: One ivy plant becomes five

A lush ivy plant with long tendrils was cut back in May. The tendrils gave rise to twenty cuttings, each with two to three nodes, distributed across five jars on a light shelf. The water was changed twice a week. After two to three weeks, almost all cuttings had four centimeter roots. Four cuttings were placed together in a 12 cm pot. After one summer, it had grown into five bushy plants that were given as gifts to friends.

## The right vessels

| vessel | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Clear glass | Root growth clearly visible, decorative | Algae formation in high light |
| Brown or green glass, e.g. B. old apothecary bottles | Less algae | Roots less visible |
| Test tube stand | Space-saving, several cuttings next to each other | Little water, frequent refills |
| Wall vases | Decorative | Difficult to clean |
| Jam jars | Free, stable | Wide opening, cuttings tip easily |

It is important to have an opening that is narrow enough so that the cutting stands upright without leaves hanging into the water. Heavy vases with a narrow neck are suitable for cuttings with large leaves such as Monstera. Several small cuttings can be kept stable in a jar with a piece of chicken wire or a perforated lid.

## What roots badly in water

Not every plant is suitable for the water glass. Succulents and cacti rot easily in water because their water-rich tissue decomposes quickly without oxygen. They root more reliably if the interface dries first and the cutting is then placed on dry, mineral substrate. Many cuttings of ornamental trees and herbs with woody shoots also grow more safely in moist potting soil than in water.

## Water roots and earth roots

Roots that arise in water are adapted to life in water. When they are transplanted into soil, they have to adjust and some cuttings then stall for a few weeks. You'll make the change easier if you don't wait too long: roots about two to five centimeters long are ideal. Keep the soil evenly slightly moist for the first few weeks after transplanting so that the transition is not too abrupt.

## A project for children

Cuttings in a glass of water are a wonderful project for children because you can watch the roots grow every day. In addition to ivy and coleus, kitchen scraps are also suitable: an avocado pit, held over a glass with toothpicks so that the bottom is in water, will form roots and a shoot after a few weeks to months. A pineapple crown, from which the lower leaves have been removed, also roots in the water, and a sweet potato sprouts long, decorative tendrils in the glass.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use tap water?

Yes. Stale, room temperature tap water works well. If the water is very hard, filtered water can reduce the limescale on the glass.

### Do cuttings in water need fertilizer?

Not in the rooting phase. Only if they stay permanently in water, occasionally a little hydro fertilizer.

### How many cuttings should I take?

More than you need. Not every cutting will root, and several in a pot will produce a prettier plant.

### Why does the water become cloudy or smell unpleasant?

Leaves that have been submerged in water usually decompose or bacteria multiply in the warm water. Remove any leaves below the water line, rinse the glass and stems, and change the water more frequently. Cut rotten, mushy stems back to the healthy tissue.

### How long does rooting take?

That is very different. Ivy, coleus and tradeskantia often form their first roots after one to three weeks, while monstera, rubber tree and other strong shoots usually take several weeks. Warmth and a bright place speed up rooting, but everything takes longer in winter. As long as the cutting remains firm and green, patience is worth it.

A transparent glass shows you every progress.

## Conclusion

Propagation in a glass of water is easy if you follow a few basic rules: cut cuttings with nodes, remove the lower leaves, place in a bright and warm place without direct sun, change the water regularly and plant in loose soil when the roots are three to five centimeters long.
