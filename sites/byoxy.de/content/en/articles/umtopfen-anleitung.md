Repotting is one of the most important care measures for houseplants. It gives the roots new space, replaces used soil and allows a look at the health of the roots. At the same time, it is an intervention that causes stress to plants. If you repot too early, too often or in a pot that is far too large, you can do more harm than good.

This guide explains when repotting is necessary, which pot size is right, which tools you need, how to proceed step by step and how to care for the plant afterwards.

## When is repotting necessary?

Clear signs are:

- **Roots grow out of the drainage hole** or push out of the top of the earth.
- **The root ball is completely rooted:** When you pull it out, you can hardly see any soil, just roots, which often grow in circles.
- **Water runs through immediately** or is no longer absorbed at all.
- **The soil dries out very quickly** because there is hardly any soil left in the pot, just roots.
- **The plant is top heavy** and will tip over.
- **The soil is worn out:** compacted, bagged, with salt crusts or a musty smell.
- **Suspected root rot or root pests.**

However, the mere age of a plant or the purchase are not compelling reasons. Many freshly purchased plants can remain in the pot for months.

## The best time

**Spring, around March to May,** is ideal. The days become longer, the plant begins to grow and can quickly form new roots. Repotting is also possible in summer. In autumn and winter, repotting should only be done in emergencies, such as in the event of root rot. Flowering plants are better repotted after flowering.

| Plant type | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Young, fast growing plants | Annually |
| Mature leafy plants | Every 2 to 3 years |
| Cacti, succulents, arched hemp | Every 3 to 4 years |
| Large container plants | Every 3 to 5 years, replacing the top layer of soil in between |
| Orchids in bark | Every 2 years |

## The right pot size

The most common mistake when repotting is a pot that is too large. It contains a lot of soil into which no roots yet reach. This soil remains wet for a long time after watering and is at risk of root rot.

- **Rule of thumb:** The new pot should be **two to four centimeters larger** in diameter than the old one, up to five centimeters for large plants.
- **Trigger hole:** Indispensable.
- **Material:**

| material | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Plastic | Lightweight, inexpensive, retains moisture evenly | Less breathable |
| Clay and terracotta | Breathable, heavy and stable, water evaporates through the wall | Dries out faster, limescale edges |
| Glazed ceramics | Decorative, stable | Often without a trigger hole, heavy |
| Textile or fiber pots | Lightweight, good ventilation | Short-lived |

Many people use a simple plastic culture pot with a drain hole that is placed in a decorative planter. This is practical as long as no water remains in the planter.

## Tools and material

- New pot with drain hole
- Fresh, suitable soil (see [potting soil and substrate](/ratgeber/blumenerde-und-substrat/))
- Possibly expanded clay or a piece of clay for the vent hole
- Clean scissors or sharp knife
- Gloves, especially for poisonous or prickly plants
- Newspaper base or a repotting mat
- Watering can with room temperature water
- Possibly a wooden stick to loosen the soil

## Step-by-step instructions

### 1. Prepare

Water the plant the day before if the soil is very dry. Slightly moist root balls loosen better and the roots are less likely to tear. For succulents and cacti, however, repot them dry.

### 2. Remove the plant from the pot

Hold the pot at an angle, grab the plant at the base and carefully pull it out. If they get stuck, run a knife along the edge or press lightly on plastic pots. Never pull on the leaves.

### 3. Check roots

| Root condition | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Solid, white, light beige or green for orchids | Healthy |
| Brown, mushy, comes off easily, smells putrid | Lazy, cut off |
| Dark, dry, hollow | Dead, cut off |
| Dense felt in a circle | Balls matted, loosen carefully |

### 4. Loosen root balls

Gently pull apart matted roots with your fingers. Loosely shake off old, used substrate. Do not forcefully remove all soil unless there is root rot. Cut off rotten and dead roots with clean scissors. If you have root rot, the instructions in [Detect and save root rot](/ratgeber/wurzelfaeule-erkennen-und-retten/) will help.

### 5. Prepare new pot

Optionally place a piece of clay over the vent hole or fill in a thin layer of expanded clay. Then fill in enough soil so that the plant sits at the same height in the new pot as before and leaves two centimeters of watering edge at the top.

### 6. Planting

Place the plant in the middle and fill with soil all around. Tap the pot lightly on the table to allow the soil to settle. Just press gently; the soil should remain loose. Do not place the trunk deeper than before, otherwise it may rot.

### 7. Pour on

Water thoroughly until water runs out the bottom. The earth will sink a little, if necessary top up. Remove excess water. Exception: Only water cacti, succulents, arched hemp and plants with cut roots after a week.

## Care after repotting

- **Location:** Return to the usual spot, but protect from direct sun for one to two weeks.
- **Watering:** More carefully than usual because the larger amount of soil stays moist longer.
- **Fertilizing:** Pause for six to eight weeks, fresh soil is pre-fertilized.
- **Patience:** Drooping leaves or a yellow leaf in the first few days are normal. The plant usually recovers within two weeks.

## Special cases

### Large container plants

Very large plants can often no longer be repotted. Instead, remove the top five to ten centimeters of soil every spring and replace it with fresh soil.

### Divide plants

Many plants with several shoots from the ground, such as spider plants, arched hemp, calathea or ferns, can be divided when repotting. See [Dividing Plants](/ratgeber/pflanzen-teilen/).

### Orchids

Phalaenopsis are placed in coarse bark substrate instead of soil. See [Caring for Orchids](/ratgeber/orchideen-phalaenopsis-pflege/).

### Root pests

White, wooly masses on the roots indicate root lice. Then remove all of the old soil, rinse roots and clean the pot thoroughly. See [Mealybugs and Mealybugs](/ratgeber/wollaeuse-und-schmierlaeuse/).

## Typical mistakes

1. **Pot too large:** Soil remains wet, risk of rot.
2. **Pot without drain hole.**
3. **Plant planted too deep.**
4. **Soil pressed too tightly:** The roots don't get any air.
5. **Fertilize immediately:** Fresh soil contains enough nutrients.
6. **Repot without necessity in winter.**
7. **Reuse old, dirty pots** without cleaning.

## Practical example: The rubber tree in a giant pot

A small rubber tree in a 14cm pot was repotted directly into a decorative 30cm pot “so that it has room”. After three months, the lower leaves fell off and the soil smelled musty. The roots only reached ten centimeters into the ground, and everything underneath was wet and dark. The plant went into a 17 cm pot with fresh, loose soil. After a month it grew new leaves again, and a year later it went into a 20cm pot.

## Choose the right pot: material and shape

In addition to the size, the material also determines the well-being of the plant. Clay pots are breathable, the soil in them dries faster, and they are stable thanks to their weight. This makes them ideal for cacti, succulents and all plants that do not tolerate wet feet. Plastic pots are lightweight, inexpensive, and retain moisture longer, which suits ferns, calatheas, and other moisture lovers. Glazed ceramic without a drain hole is only suitable as a planter.

The shape also counts. Shallow bowls suit succulents with shallow roots, tall and narrow pots suit plants with long roots such as palm trees. Orchids like Phalaenopsis thrive in clear pots because their roots use light and you can see their condition at all times.

## Fresh soil without a new pot

Some plants are too large or heavy to repot regularly. Then it helps to carefully remove the top three to five centimeters of soil in spring and replace it with fresh soil. This means the plant receives new nutrients without disturbing the root ball.

If you want a plant to stay in the same pot and stop growing, you can also carefully shorten the roots when repotting. To do this, cut off about a quarter of the outer roots all around with a clean knife, put the plant back into the cleaned pot with fresh soil and water it. This cut is suitable for robust plants such as rubber trees or linden trees, not for sensitive species.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to repot immediately after purchase?

No, unless the plant is significantly too big for its pot or the soil is in poor condition. First give her a few weeks to get used to the new location.

### Can I repot into a smaller pot?

Yes, if many roots were rotten and were removed or the plant was divided. The pot should fit the remaining root ball.

### Should pots be cleaned beforehand?

Yes, clean used pots with hot water and a brush to remove limescale, pathogens and pests.

### Can I also repot in winter?

Only in emergencies, such as root rot, root pests or a broken pot. Otherwise you wait until spring, when the plant grows again and quickly pushes new roots into the fresh soil.

### Why does my plant droop its leaves after repotting?

Slight weakening is normal after repotting because fine roots have been injured. Place the plant in a bright place for one to two weeks, but without direct sun, water moderately and do not fertilize yet. It usually straightens out again within a few days.

Then the usual watering rhythm continues.

## Conclusion

Repotting is necessary when roots no longer have room or the soil is used up, ideally in spring. Choose a pot that is just a little larger with a drainage hole, check the roots, use fresh, suitable soil, do not plant the plant deeper than before and then give it a little rest without fertilizer and blazing sun.
