For a houseplant, the soil in the pot is a habitat, water reservoir, source of nutrients and support at the same time. In nature, roots grow in a vast, living soil. They only have a few liters of substrate available in the pot, which has to work for years. That's why it's worth taking a closer look at the earth. Many problems with waterlogging, rot and fungus gnats start with unsuitable or worn out soil.

This guide explains what makes good potting soil, what additives there are, how to use peat-free soil correctly and which mixture suits which plants.

## What does a good earth have to do?

| task | Why it is important |
|---|---|
| Store water | So that the plant is supplied between two watering sessions |
| carry air | Roots need oxygen, otherwise they will suffocate and rot |
| hold nutrients | So that fertilizer is not washed out immediately |
| Structural stability | The earth should not collapse after a few months |
| give support | Large plants need stability in the pot |
| Appropriate pH value | Usually slightly acidic to neutral so that nutrients are available |

The most important thing is the balance between **water and air.** A very fine, dense soil stores a lot of water but little air. A coarse, mineral mixture contains a lot of air but little water. Each group of plants needs a different ratio.

## Commercially available soils

- **Universal or potting soil:** Intended for balconies and gardens, often relatively fine. It works for houseplants, but is better loosened up with coarse components.
- **Houseplant or green plant soil:** Slightly more structurally stable, a good basis for many leafy plants.
- **Cactus and succulent soil:** With sand or mineral components, lean and permeable. Often too fine and can be improved with pumice.
- **Orchid soil:** Coarse pine bark, not real soil. Only for epiphytic orchids and some bromeliads.
- **Herb and vegetable soil:** More nutrient-rich, for balcony boxes and pots with crops.
- **Growing soil:** Fine, low in nutrients and germ-free, only for sowing and cuttings.

Cheap soils sometimes contain a lot of fine, quickly decaying material. A good soil feels loose, contains noticeably coarse particles and smells pleasantly woody, not musty.

## Peat and peat-free soil

Classic potting soil consists largely of peat. Peat stores a lot of water and is light, but its mining destroys peatlands, which are valuable habitats and large carbon sinks. Peat-free and peat-reduced soils are widespread today. Instead, they contain bark humus, wood fibers, coconut fibers, green waste compost or mineral components.

Peat-free soil works well, but behaves a little differently:

- **The surface dries faster** while it is still damp underneath. Therefore, make the finger test a little deeper.
- **Wood fibers initially bind nitrogen.** Start fertilizing a little earlier than with soil containing peat.
- **Storage:** Buy peat-free soil as fresh as possible and do not store it in direct sunlight for months.

## Aggregates at a glance

| Aggregate | Effect | Suitable for |
|---|---|---|
| Perlite | White, very light, loosens up, retains some water | Almost all houseplants, cuttings |
| Pumice | Mineral, porous, structurally stable, heavier than perlite | Succulents, aroids, bonsai |
| Lava granules | Mineral, very stable, gives weight | Cacti, large potted plants |
| expanded clay | Coarse, light, as drainage or in mixtures | Drainage stories, hydroponics |
| Pine bark | Organic, coarse, airy | Monstera, Philodendron, Orchids |
| Coconut fibers or coconut chips | Store water, loosen up, peat-free | Calathea, ferns, aroids |
| Quartz sand (coarse) | Mineral, makes soil more permeable and heavier | Cacti, succulents |
| Sphagnum moss | Stores a lot of water | Orchids, cuttings, moss sticks |
| Activated carbon | Binds odors, often used in Aroideen mixtures | Optional |

Fine play sand is unsuitable for mixtures: it tends to compact the soil rather than loosen it up.

## Proven mixtures

The following mixtures are guidelines, not rigid recipes. Parts refer to volume.

| Plant group | Mixture |
|---|---|
| Robust leafy plants (ivy plant, rubber tree, spider plant) | 3 parts houseplant soil, 1 part perlite |
| Aroids (Monstera, Philodendron, Anthurium) | 2 parts soil, 1 part pine bark, 1 part perlite or pumice |
| Moisture-loving plants (calathea, ferns) | 3 parts soil, 1 part coconut fiber, 1 part perlite |
| Succulents and cacti | 1 part cactus soil, 1 part pumice or lava |
| Bow hemp, lucky feather | 2 parts earth, 1 part pumice or lava |
| Orchids (Phalaenopsis) | Fine to medium pine bark, possibly some sphagnum |
| herbs (Mediterranean) | 2 parts herb soil, 1 part coarse sand or pumice |

## Drainage

A drainage layer of expanded clay or clay shards at the bottom of the pot is often recommended. It prevents soil from trickling out of the drainage hole and keeps roots above any standing water in the planter. But what is much more important is the **drainage hole itself** and a **loose soil mixture.** A thick drainage layer in a pot without a hole does not prevent waterlogging, it only shifts the wet zone upwards.

## When will the earth be used up?

Over time, the organic components break down, the earth becomes finer and denser, stores more water and less air. In addition, salts from tap water and fertilizer accumulate. Indications of spent soil:

- Water runs in very slowly when watering or stands at the top.
- The earth has sunk in and the edge of the pot is well above the surface of the earth.
- White or yellowish crusts on the surface or on the edge of the pot.
- The earth smells musty or sour.
- The plant hardly grows despite fertilization.

Then it's time for fresh soil. You can find instructions in [Repotting step by step](/ratgeber/umtopfen-anleitung/).

## Store and reuse soil

- Roll up **open bags** and store in a cool, dry and dark place to prevent fungus gnats from nesting. See [Get Rid of Fungus Gnats](/ratgeber/trauermuecken-loswerden/).
- **Old soil** from healthy plants can be reused in the garden or compost. It is better to use fresh soil for new houseplants.
- **Dispose of soil from diseased plants** with root rot or pests in the household waste, do not reuse.

## Mold on the earth

A white coating on the ground is often harmless mold caused by moisture and organic components. Whitish, hard crusts, on the other hand, are usually lime or fertilizer salts. How to distinguish between and treat the two can be found in [Mold on Potting Soil](/ratgeber/schimmel-auf-blumenerde/).

## Practical example: The earth is too dense

A philodendron had been standing in the same soil for four years. When watering, the water barely came in and instead stood at the top for several minutes. The lower leaves turned yellow. Repotting revealed a dense, black, crumbless mass in which many of the roots were brown and soft. In a new mixture of houseplant soil, pine bark and perlite in a ratio of 2 to 1 to 1, the plant formed new, white roots within a few weeks and the irrigation water was absorbed immediately.

## Mix your own soil: step by step

1. **Prepare the work area:** Lay out a large tub, bucket or tarp.
2. **Measure the base:** Using an old pot or measuring cup, measure out the portions of the base soil.
3. **Add additives:** Perlite, pumice, bark or coconut according to recipe.
4. **Mix thoroughly:** Using your hands or a small scoop until evenly distributed.
5. **Lightly moisten:** Dry soil, especially peat-free, absorbs water better if it is slightly moist before planting.
6. **Store leftovers:** Store in a closed container or bag in a cool, dry place.

Perlite creates dust when mixed. It's best to work outside or lightly moisten the perlite beforehand. If you have a lot of plants, you can prepare two or three basic mixes in advance, such as an airy aroid mix, a mineral succulent mix and a moisture-retaining fern mix.

## Special earths: when they are worthwhile

In addition to regular potting soil, there are a number of specialty soils, and not all of them are just marketing. Cactus and succulent soil contains a lot of mineral material such as sand, pumice or lava and allows water to drain away quickly, preventing rot. Orchid substrate consists predominantly of coarse bark, because most indoor orchids grow in nature on trees and need air at the roots. Rhododendron or ericaceous soil is acidic and is suitable for blueberries, azaleas and other lime-sensitive plants.

Herbal soil is usually lower in nutrients than potting soil, which suits Mediterranean herbs, and sowing soil is fine, low in nutrients and largely germ-free, so that sensitive seedlings do not burn or rot. Potted plant soil is particularly structurally stable because it is intended to remain in the container for several years. For most green plants, however, a good universal soil is sufficient, which you can loosen with pumice or perlite if necessary.

## Soil and pH

Most houseplants grow best in slightly acidic to neutral soil. Hard, calcareous tap water raises the pH value of the soil over time, which you can sometimes recognize by white lime crusts on the surface of the soil and on the edge of the pot. Plants sensitive to limescale react to this with chlorosis: the leaves turn yellow while the leaf veins remain green because the plant can no longer absorb certain nutrients such as iron well. Rainwater, filtered water and regular repotting in fresh soil prevent this.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use garden soil for houseplants?

Better not. Garden soil compacts heavily in the pot, can contain pests, weed seeds and pathogens and is too heavy for pots.

### How often should I change the soil?

Every one to three years for most houseplants, less often for slow-growing succulents. For large tubs, simply replacing the top layer is often enough.

### Is expensive soil really better?

Not always, but high-quality soils are usually more structurally stable and contain fewer fine components. A favorable soil can be significantly improved with perlite or pumice.

### How do I recognize good soil by smell?

Fresh, healthy soil smells pleasantly of the forest floor. If, on the other hand, it smells putrid, sour or musty, it has been wet for too long and poorly ventilated, or roots are decomposing in it. You should no longer use such soil. Musty-smelling bags with mold inside also don't belong in pots.

### What are the white balls in the soil?

It is usually perlite, a foamed volcanic rock that loosens the soil, or, if the soil is cheap, Styrofoam beads. Round, even granules in yellow, green or blue are often slow-release fertilizers. Snail eggs look different: They are translucent, lie in clutches and are found directly beneath the surface of the earth.

## Conclusion

The right soil is the basis of healthy roots. Make sure you have a loose, structurally stable mixture with the right ratio of water and air for your plant, use additives such as perlite, pumice or bark, use pots with a drainage hole and replace used soil in a timely manner. This will prevent many problems before they even arise.
