Silvery, shiny spots on the leaves, tiny black dots and new leaves that come out of the heart in a crippled state: these are typical traces of thrips. These pests are so small and agile that many plant owners overlook them for a long time and only notice them when a plant has already suffered significantly. At the same time, thrips are among the most stubborn pests in the home because their eggs in the leaf tissue and their pupae are often protected from many substances in the soil.

With the right knowledge of their life cycle and a little perseverance, thrips can be reliably gotten rid of. This guide shows you how to recognize an infestation, which methods work and what a treatment plan looks like over several weeks.

## What are thrips?

Thrips, also called fringed wings or bubble-footed insects, are slender insects that are usually only one to two millimeters long. The adults have narrow wings with fine fringes and are light, brownish or almost black, depending on the species. The larvae are pale yellow to whitish and have no wings. To the naked eye they appear as tiny, fast-moving lines on the surface or underside of the leaf.

There are several types of houseplants. The Californian flower thrips, which also attacks flowers, and a species that primarily colonizes large-leaved green plants such as ivy, monstera, philodendron and anthurium are particularly widespread. Thrips are not very picky and can quickly spread from one plant to its neighbors.

## Damage: This is how you recognize thrips

Thrips scratch the plant cells with their mouthparts and suck them out. The empty cells fill with air, which creates the typical damage pattern:

- **Silvery or light gray spots and streaks** on the leaves that shine in slanted light.
- **Tiny black dots** in the speckled areas. This is thrips droppings and is a very reliable identification feature.
- **Stunted, spotted new leaves** when the thrips are already sucking on the young shoots.
- **Brown, dried out spots** in severe infestation, later leaves fall off.
- **Spotty, misshapen flowers** on flowering plants, often with brown edges.

To check, hold a white sheet of paper under a shoot and tap it lightly. If small, elongated insects fall and move, they are probably thrips. A magnifying glass helps you take a closer look. However, wiping the underside of the leaf with a tissue, as with spider mites, is of less use because thrips do not form webs.

## Thrips or something else?

Silvery spots can also have other causes. Spider mites also cause brightly mottled leaves, but in heavier infestations they form fine webs and do not leave black dots of excrement. Sunburn produces light, dry patches, but without traces of feces and usually only on the side facing the sun. You can find out more about spider mites in the guide [Fighting spider mites] (/ratgeber/spinnmilben-bekaempfen/).

## The life cycle: Why thrips are so stubborn

If you want to combat thrips, you need to know their life cycle because it explains why a single treatment is almost never enough.

1. **Egg:** The females lay their eggs in the leaf tissue using an ovipositor. There they are largely protected from sprays and washing off.
2. **Larvae:** After a few days the larvae hatch and suck on the leaves. They cause a lot of the damage.
3. **Rest stages:** The larvae transform into adults via the prepupa and pupa. Depending on the species, this happens in the soil, in leaf axils or on the leaves.
4. **Adult Thrips:** They also suckle, lay new eggs and can fly to other plants.

At room temperature, a complete cycle only takes a few weeks and the generations overlap. So at any given time there are eggs in the leaf, larvae on the plant and pupae in the soil. A treatment always only affects part of it. Therefore, each measure must be repeated several times at intervals of a few days until the later hatched animals are recorded.

## Immediate measures

1. **Isolate:** Immediately place the infected plant separately from other plants, preferably in another room.
2. **Check neighbors:** Examine all nearby plants, especially the undersides of leaves and young shoots.
3. **Remove severely infested parts:** Cut off severely damaged leaves and flowers and dispose of them in a sealed bag with household waste.
4. **Shower:** Rinse the plant thoroughly with lukewarm water, including the top and bottom of the leaves. Cover the soil with foil to prevent it from getting wet.

Showering alone will not eliminate an infestation, but it will immediately reduce the number of larvae and adults and make all subsequent treatments more effective.

## Control methods

### Preparations based on potassium soap or oil

Plant protection products based on potassium soap or vegetable oils such as rapeseed oil act as contact agents. You have to wet the thrips directly, so you spray the entire plant until dripping wet, especially the undersides of the leaves, leaf axils and shoot tips. Because the eggs are protected in the tissue, repeat the treatment every five to seven days for at least three to four weeks. Test each product on a single leaf first, as sensitive plants can react with spots.

### Neem preparations

Approved plant protection products containing the active ingredient azadirachtin from the neem tree are partially absorbed by the plant and disrupt the development of sucking larvae. Follow the instructions for use carefully, because in Germany only products that are approved for this purpose can be used in home and allotment gardens and only as stated on the label.

### Blue boards

Thrips are particularly attracted to blue. Blue sticky panels catch adult flying animals and show you whether the infestation is decreasing. They do not help against larvae and eggs, so they are primarily a control instrument and a supplement.

### Beneficial insects

For the apartment there are predatory mites that eat thrips larvae. They are usually delivered in small bags that you hang on the plants and from which new predatory mites migrate over several weeks. Predatory mites are particularly suitable for multiple infested plants and for people who do not want to use sprays. They need a certain level of humidity, and during their use you must not use any products that would also affect the beneficial insects.

Nematodes, microscopic roundworms that are spread with the irrigation water, can be used to combat the pupae in the soil. They are the same ones that help against fungus gnat larvae.

### Renew earth

Because many thrips pupate in the soil, it can help to remove the top layer of soil and replace it with fresh soil or repot the plant entirely. That alone is not enough, but it supports the other measures. The guide [repotting houseplants](/ratgeber/umtopfen-anleitung/) shows how to repot.

## A treatment plan for four to six weeks

- **Day 1:** Isolate the plant, cut off heavily affected parts, shower thoroughly, hang up the blue board.
- **Day 2:** First treatment with a potash soap or oil preparation, alternatively apply predatory mites.
- **Day 7, 14, 21 and 28:** Repeat spray treatment, rinse briefly beforehand. If necessary, reorder if there are predatory mites.
- **Week 1 to 6:** Check the undersides of leaves, shoot tips and blue board every week. Check neighboring plants too.
- **From week 6:** If there is no new damage to young leaves and no more thrips are stuck to the blue board, the plant can go back to the others. But keep checking for a few weeks.

Old, silvery spots no longer disappear, even if the thrips are long gone. It is crucial that new leaves grow back cleanly.

## Prevention

- **Quarantine for new additions:** Place new plants separately for two to three weeks and check them several times with a magnifying glass.
- **Cut flowers note:** Bouquets can bring thrips. Do not place them directly next to sensitive houseplants.
- **Check regularly:** A quick look at the undersides of leaves and new shoots when watering is often enough to detect an infestation early.
- **Humidity:** Thrips reproduce particularly well in warm, dry air. A slightly higher humidity helps preventatively. See [Humidity for Houseplants](/ratgeber/luftfeuchtigkeit-fuer-zimmerpflanzen/).
- **Shower occasionally:** A lukewarm shower every few months removes dust and many pests before they spread.
- **Summer Outdoors:** Thoroughly inspect plants that were outside in the summer before putting them away in the fall.

## Practical example: The ivy with the silver leaves

A reader noticed silvery spots on his large ivy that he initially thought were sunburn. When, after a few weeks, his Monstera and a Philodendron next to it also showed similar spots, he used a magnifying glass to discover tiny, pale yellow larvae on the undersides of the leaves and black dots in the spots.

He put all three plants in the bathroom, cut back the most affected shoots of the ivy, showered the plants and treated them with a potassium soap preparation every week for five weeks. He also hung up a blue plaque. In the first two weeks there were new thrips on the table, then fewer and fewer after that. After six weeks, all three plants continued to grow with clean new leaves. Since then, he always puts newly purchased plants in the bathroom for a few weeks.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are thrips dangerous to humans?

No. Thrips attack plants and are harmless to people and pets. In the summer they may occasionally crawl or nip at your skin outside, but this has no health consequences.

### Where do thrips come from in the apartment?

Usually with new plants, with cut flowers or in summer through open windows. They also occasionally bring plants that were outside on the balcony into the house in the fall.

### Can I still save a severely infested plant?

Often yes, if the plant still has healthy shoots and roots. Cut back severely damaged parts generously and treat them consistently over several weeks. For very large plants with infestation in all parts, it may make more sense to take healthy cuttings and dispose of the mother plant so that the thrips do not spread further.

### Do home remedies such as garlic decoction or dishwashing liquid help?

The effectiveness of garlic decoction has not been proven, and dishwashing liquid can damage the leaves. Approved preparations based on potassium soap or oil, consistently repeated, or beneficial insects are more reliable.

## Conclusion

Thrips reveal themselves through silvery spots with tiny black dots of excrement and stunted young leaves. Because their eggs are often protected in the leaf tissue and their pupae in the soil, only consistent treatment over several weeks helps: isolate, shower, remove infected parts and repeatedly use contact agents or beneficial insects every few days. With quarantine for new plants, regular checks and slightly higher humidity, you can prevent renewed infestation.
