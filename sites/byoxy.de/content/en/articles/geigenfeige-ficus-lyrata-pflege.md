The fiddle leaf fig, botanically Ficus lyrata, is one of the most impressive houseplants with its large, violin-shaped leaves. As a standard or bushy plant, it brings structure to any room. At the same time, it has a reputation for being moody: it sheds leaves, gets brown spots and reacts insultingly to moving. The good news: Your requirements are clear and easy to meet if you know them.

This guide explains what the fiddle leaf fig needs in terms of light, water, soil and nutrients, how to branch and repot it and what lies behind the most common problems.

## Light: the key to success

Most fiddleleaf fig problems have a common cause: too little light. In its homeland it grows as a tree that pushes its crown into the sun. In the apartment, even a bright place inside the room is dark for them.

- **Ideal:** directly on a large east or west window or one to two meters in front of a south window.
- **Sun:** It tolerates morning and evening sun well. She has to slowly get used to the blazing midday sun behind glass, otherwise bright, dry burn marks will appear.
- **Too dark:** The plant drops the lower leaves, new leaves remain small, the trunk becomes thin and leans towards the window.

Because the plant rotates toward the light, you should rotate the pot a quarter turn every few weeks. This way it grows evenly. More about assessing lighting conditions in [Light for Houseplants](/ratgeber/licht-fuer-zimmerpflanzen/).

## Avoid changing locations

Fiddle figs are sensitive to change. They often shed some leaves after purchasing, moving or moving to a new location. This is an adaptive reaction and usually nothing to worry about. It is important not to constantly move the plant afterwards, but to give it a good, bright place and leave it there. Cold drafts from doors or tilted windows in winter and warm air directly above the heater are also stress factors.

## Pour

1. **Finger test:** Water when the top 3 to 5 cm of soil is dry. For large containers it can also be a little deeper.
2. **Pour thoroughly** until water runs out the bottom, then remove excess after 15 minutes.
3. **Use room temperature water**. Ice-cold tap water can cause cold shock to the roots.
4. **Evenness:** The fiddle leaf fig neither likes constant wetness nor complete drying out. A consistent rhythm is more important than the exact amount.

As a rough guide: about once a week in summer, every two to three weeks in winter. But fixed days never replace the control of the earth. See [Watering houseplants properly](/ratgeber/zimmerpflanzen-richtig-giessen/).

## Soil, pot and fertilizer

- **Soil:** Loose, structurally stable houseplant soil with a proportion of around a quarter to a third of pine bark, perlite or pumice.
- **Pot:** With drain hole. Large plants are more stable in heavy ceramic or clay pots.
- **Fertilizer:** From March to September every two to three weeks with green plant fertilizer in the specified dosage. Do not fertilize in winter.

The large leaves consume a lot of nutrients. Light green, smaller new leaves in summer can indicate nutrient deficiencies, especially if the plant has been in the same soil for years. More basics in [Fertilizing houseplants](/ratgeber/zimmerpflanzen-duengen/).

## Maintain leaves

A lot of dust collects on the large leaves and absorbs light. Wipe it every few weeks with a soft, damp cloth, supporting the leaf with your other hand. In summer, a lukewarm shower is also possible if the pot drains well afterwards. Leaf shiners are unnecessary and can clog pores.

## Branching and pruning

Many fiddle leaf figs are sold as a single stem. If you want a bushier or tree-like plant, you can help:

1. **Cut the tip:** In spring or early summer, cut off the shoot tip above a leaf. One to three new side shoots often sprout from the nodes below.
2. **Scoring:** Above a sleeping eye, lightly score the bark with a clean knife. This can stimulate this node to sprout.
3. **Stop milky juice:** White milky juice comes out when cutting. Dab with a damp cloth; it stops after a short time. Wear gloves and protect the floor.

The cut tips can be rooted as top cuttings in water or moist soil. This takes a few weeks for violin figs and works best when it is warm. More in [Cuttings in a water glass](/ratgeber/stecklinge-im-wasserglas/).

## Repotting

Young plants are repotted every one to two years in spring, large specimens every three to four years. For very large pots, it is often enough to replace the top centimeters of soil with fresh soil every year. The new pot should only be a few centimeters larger. [Repotting step by step] (/ratgeber/umtopfen-anleitung/) provides detailed instructions.

## Typical problems

| Symptom | Possible cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Lower leaves fall off | Too little light, change of location, drafts | Make it brighter, don't change it anymore |
| Dark brown spots from the edge of the leaf or in the middle | Too much water, root problems | Reduce watering, check roots |
| Light, dry, papery spots | Sunburn | Get used to the sun slowly, filter midday sun |
| Small red dots on young leaves | Often water fluctuations during leaf growth | Mostly harmless, water evenly |
| Leaves hang limply | Lack of water or root rot | Check the soil, water or pot up depending on the findings |
| Fine webs, speckled leaves | Spider mites | See [Fighting spider mites](/ratgeber/spinnmilben-bekaempfen/) |
| Brown, hard bumps on the trunk | Scale insects | Scrape off and treat with oil preparation |

When it comes to spots, it's worth differentiating: brown, spreading spots with a dark edge indicate problems in the root area, while light, dry spots on the sunny side are more likely to indicate sunburn. More information in [Yellow Leaves: Causes](/ratgeber/gelbe-blaetter-ursachen/) and [Detect and Save Root Rot](/ratgeber/wurzelfaeule-erkennen-und-retten/).

## Fiddle fig throughout the year

| time | Care |
|---|---|
| March to May | Start repotting, pruning, fertilizing, slowly get used to more sun |
| June to August | Water and fertilize regularly, wipe off leaves, and watch out for pests |
| September to October | Stop fertilizing, reduce watering, possibly move closer to the window |
| November to February | Brightest place, water sparingly, distance from heating and drafts |

## Practical example: The new fiddle leaf fig loses leaves

A fiddle leaf fig was bought in November, transported in a cold car and placed at home in a bright corner of the room two meters from a west window. Five lower leaves fell off in the first three weeks. The owner then watered more frequently, which made the situation worse: the soil remained wet and new brown spots appeared. It wasn't until she put the plant right next to the window, switched the watering to the finger sample and left it there that the fiddle leaf fig stabilized. New, large leaves appeared in the spring, and two years later it was taller than before.

## Red spots on young leaves

Many owners are frightened when small reddish or brown dots appear on fresh, still light green leaves. There is usually no disease behind it, but rather a so-called edema. It occurs when the roots absorb more water than the leaves can evaporate, for example after a large amount of watering in very dry soil or in low light. Individual cells then burst and form the small dots.

The spots do not spread like fungal spots and often fade as the leaf ages. You can prevent this by watering evenly: it is better to use regular, moderate amounts when the top centimeters of soil are dry, rather than long dry phases and then a flood.

## What you should pay attention to when buying

Buy a fiddle leaf fig in spring or summer if possible. In winter, it easily suffers cold shock on the way home from the store and then sheds leaves. Look for firm, shiny leaves without brown spots, clean undersides without pests, and a root ball that doesn't smell rotten. Whether you choose a tree with one trunk or a bushy plant with several shoots is a matter of taste. Small trees appear elegant, bushy plants are visually more forgiving of the loss of individual leaves.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the fiddle leaf poisonous?

Their milky sap can irritate skin and mucous membranes and cause vomiting in pets. Wear gloves when cutting. See [Plant Safety](/pflanzensicherheit/).

### Can the fiddle leaf fig go outside in summer?

Yes, in a protected, partially shaded place as soon as the nights stay above 15 °C. Get used to the sun slowly and protect from wind, as the large leaves tear easily.

### How big does a fiddle leaf fig grow in an apartment?

With good care, two to three meters. It can be limited to the desired height by pruning.

### Why are my fiddle leaf fig leaves drooping?

Drooping leaves usually indicate thirst, especially if the soil is dry and the leaves appear soft. However, if the soil is wet, the roots may be damaged by waterlogging. Even after a move or a cold shock, the fiddle leaf fig leaves its leaves temporarily drooping.

In any case, first check the moisture a few centimeters deep with your finger before watering, because water on already wet soil worsens root damage. After a move, the leaves usually recover on their own within one to two weeks.

## Conclusion

The fiddle leaf fig is not a diva, but a light lover with clear habits. Place it directly in a bright window, water evenly according to the finger test, fertilize during the growing season, keep its leaves dust-free and leave it in place. Then it rewards you with large, shiny leaves and strong, stately growth.
