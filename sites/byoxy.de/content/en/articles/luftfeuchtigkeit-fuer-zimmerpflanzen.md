Many popular houseplants come from tropical rainforests, where humidity is often 70 to 90 percent. In heated apartments, however, it often drops to 30 percent or less in winter. For some plants this is not a problem, others react with brown leaf edges, curled leaves or spider mite infestation.

This guide explains which plants need high humidity, how to measure it, which measures really help and which are more of a myth.

## What does relative humidity mean?

Relative humidity indicates how much water vapor the air contains in relation to the maximum possible amount. Warm air can hold more water than cold air. That's why the relative humidity drops when cold winter air comes in during ventilation and is warmed up by the heater. It may be foggy outside, but the air inside is still dry.

| Location and season | Typical relative humidity |
|---|---|
| Tropical rainforest | 70 to 90 percent |
| Living spaces in summer | 50 to 65 percent |
| Heated living spaces in winter | 25 to 45 percent |
| Bathroom after showering | 70 percent and more, for a short time |
| Kitchen while cooking | Increased for a short time |

For humans, 40 to 60 percent is considered pleasant. Below that, mucous membranes dry out, and above that the risk of mold on cold walls increases.

## Which plants need high humidity?

| Need | Plants |
|---|---|
| High (over 60 percent) | Ferns such as Maidenhair Fern, Calathea orbifolia, Alocasia, Anthurium, Fittonia, many orchids except Phalaenopsis |
| Medium (40 to 60 percent) | Monstera, Philodendron, Calathea lancifolia, Peace Lily, Phalaenopsis |
| Low (less than 40 percent not a problem) | Cacti, succulents, arched hemp, lucky feather, ivy, rubber tree |

Robust plants cope well with dry indoor air. If you want little effort, choose plants from the lower group.

## Signs of too dry air

- **Brown, dry leaf tips and edges**, especially on calatheas and ferns.
- **Rolled up leaves.**
- **New sheets stick together** when unfolded or tear.
- **Buds fall off** or dry up before they open.
- **Spider mite infestation:** Spider mites love warm, dry air. See [Fighting spider mites](/ratgeber/spinnmilben-bekaempfen/).

However, brown tips can also have other causes, such as watering errors or fertilizer salts. A hygrometer creates clarity.

## Measure humidity

A digital hygrometer costs little and displays the relative humidity. Place it at plant height next to the plants, not directly next to the heater or window. Observe the values ​​over several days, morning and evening, in summer and winter. Many people are surprised at how dry the air is in winter.

## Effective measures

### humidifier

The most effective method. There are different types:

| Type | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| evaporator | Hygienic, no lime dust, self-regulating | A little louder, needs regular filter care |
| Ultrasonic nebulizer | Quiet, cheap | If the water is hard, there will be white lime dust, regular cleaning is necessary |
| evaporator | Germ-free by heating | Higher power consumption |

Important for all devices: clean regularly and change the water daily to prevent germs from forming. A device with a built-in hygrostat keeps the humidity at a set value.

### Group plants

Plants release water through their leaves. If many people stand together, a wetter microclimate develops between them. A group of several plants is easy to implement and also has a decorative effect.

### Bowl with water and gravel

A shallow bowl is filled with gravel or expanded clay and water is poured just below the surface of the stones. The pot is on the stones, not in the water. The water evaporates around the plant. The effect is limited, but noticeable when combined with grouping.

### Choice of location

- **Bathroom with window:** Ideal for ferns, calatheas and orchids if there is enough light.
- **Kitchen:** When cooking, moisture is created, but also fat vapor.
- **Distance from the heater:** The air is driest directly above or next to the radiator.

### Terrarium, showcase and glass house

For very demanding plants such as jewel orchids or small ferns, closed or semi-open glass containers and plant display cases are a good solution. Humidities of 70 percent and more can be achieved there. Light then often has to be supplemented with a plant lamp.

## Spraying: myth or help?

Misting the leaves with water is often recommended. In fact, it only increases the humidity for a few minutes until the water evaporates. Disadvantages:

- **limescale spots** on the leaves when using hard water.
- **Fungal diseases** and spots when water sits on the leaves for a long time, especially on hairy leaves.
- **Rot** due to water in leaf axils and rosettes, such as orchids and bromeliads.

Spraying can be useful for tillandsias, ferns or to wash away dust. It is not very effective as a measure against dry indoor air.

## Avoid excessive humidity

More is not always better. Consistently over 65 percent in normal living spaces can lead to problems:

- **Mold on walls**, especially on cold exterior walls and behind furniture.
- **Condensation on windows.**
- **Fungal diseases on plants** with poor air circulation.

Therefore, the following applies: ventilate regularly, i.e. open the windows wide for a few minutes several times a day instead of leaving them permanently tilted. A hygrometer helps to maintain balance.

## Practical example: The fern corner

In the living room of an old apartment there were a maidenhair fern, a calathea and an alocasia in a corner near the radiator. In winter the fern fronds dried up, the Calathea got brown edges, and spider mites appeared on the Alocasia. The hygrometer showed 32 percent. The plants were placed one meter away from the heater, grouped closely and placed on a large bowl of expanded clay and water. A small evaporator with a hygrostat kept the humidity in the corner at 50 to 55 percent. The spider mites were treated. The new leaves of all three plants remained healthy, and thanks to regular ventilation, no condensation formed on the windows.

## Costs and effort in comparison

| Measure | Acquisition | Ongoing effort | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hygrometer | Low | None | Just measurement, but essential |
| Group plants | None | None | Low to medium |
| Bowl with expanded clay | Low | Refill water | Low, local |
| evaporator | Means | Water daily, clean filter regularly | High |
| Ultrasonic nebulizer | Low to medium | Water daily, cleaning, possibly decalcified water | High |
| Plant display case | High | Ventilation, lighting | Very high, but only in the vessel |

For most households, the combination of a hygrometer, grouping and a small evaporator in the plant corner is sufficient. It is important not to point the device directly at leaves or furniture and to clean it regularly. Bacteria and mold will otherwise multiply in stagnant water and enter the room air with the water vapor. Many manufacturers recommend weekly cleaning with vinegar or citric acid. If you often have your windows open in the summer, you can usually leave the device switched off from May to September because the humidity will then be in a comfortable range.

## Humidity over the course of the year

The biggest challenge is winter. Cold outside air contains little water, and when it comes in and is heated during ventilation, its relative humidity drops sharply. In heated rooms it is often well below fifty percent, and even lower directly above radiators. This is precisely when sensitive plants such as calatheas, ferns and marantas show brown leaf edges.

In summer the air in most homes is more humid and many plants visibly recover. The transition periods in spring and autumn are usually unproblematic. Therefore, plan measures such as humidifiers or grouping plants, especially for the heating season, and use a hygrometer to observe how the values ​​change over the course of the year.

## Plants as natural humidifiers

Plants release a large portion of the irrigation water back into the air through their leaves. However, a single plant hardly changes the humidity of a room in a measurable way. Only many plants in a small space, especially large-leaved species with high water consumption such as Cyprus grass, create a slightly wetter microclimate around them. This effect is the reason why grouped plants thrive better than solitary ones.

## A good indoor climate for people and plants

For humans, a relative humidity of between around forty and sixty percent is considered pleasant. Higher values ​​over longer periods of time increase the risk of mold, especially on cold exterior walls and behind furniture. If you keep a lot of plants or use a humidifier, you should ventilate regularly and keep an eye on the values ​​with a hygrometer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a bowl of water on the heater help?

It does evaporate water, but the amount is small for an entire room. It can have a small effect in the immediate vicinity.

### Is a bathroom without a window suitable?

The humidity would be ideal, but without windows there is no light. Only useful with a plant lamp.

### Do succulents need moist air?

No. They prefer dry air. Excessive humidity can even promote rot in them.

### Does an indoor fountain help?

A small indoor fountain only increases the humidity slightly, but can contribute to a slightly humid microclimate in a group of plants. Change the water regularly and clean the fountain to prevent algae and germs from forming.

### How accurate are cheap hygrometers?

Simple digital hygrometers can be off by a few percentage points. You can check it with a simple test: Place the device together with a small bowl of moist saline slurry that still contains undissolved salt in a tightly closed container for a few hours. The air in it settles down to about seventy-five percent, and you can note the deviation in your device.

### Where is the best place to measure humidity?

At the height of the plants, not directly above the heater, near the window or next to a humidifier. After setting up the device, give it about half an hour before reading the value.

## Conclusion

Tropical plants such as ferns and calatheas benefit from humidity above 50 percent, while robust plants can survive in dry air. Measure the humidity with a hygrometer, group plants, keep away from the heater and use a humidifier for demanding species. Spraying, on the other hand, is not very effective. And don't forget to ventilate regularly to keep the plants and the apartment healthy.
