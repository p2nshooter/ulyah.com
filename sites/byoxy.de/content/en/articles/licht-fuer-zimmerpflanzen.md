Light is food for plants. With the help of light, they produce sugar in their leaves from which they grow. Nevertheless, light is often underestimated in houseplants because our eyes adapt and perceive even a dark room as bright. For a plant, however, the difference between a place right next to the window and a shelf three meters away is enormous.

This guide explains how much light plants need, how to correctly assess locations, how to recognize a lack of light and sunburn and when a plant lamp is worthwhile.

## How dark is it really?

Light intensity is measured in lux. Some comparative values show how big the differences are:

| situation | Approximate light intensity |
|---|---|
| Sunny summer day outdoors | 50,000 to 100,000 lux |
| Overcast day outdoors | 5,000 to 20,000 lux |
| Directly on a south window with sun | 10,000 to 50,000 lux |
| Directly in a bright window without sun | 2,000 to 5,000 lux |
| Two meters from the window | 500 to 1,500 lux |
| Middle of the room, hallway | 100 to 500 lux |
| Normal room lighting in the evening | 100 to 300 lux |

The values are rough guidelines, but they show the most important thing: **The amount of light decreases very quickly with the distance to the window.** Just one meter often makes up half of it. A free light meter app on your smartphone provides approximate values ​​and helps compare different places.

## The light categories

The same terms appear again and again in descriptions of plants:

| term | Meaning | Typical plants |
|---|---|---|
| Sunny | Several hours of direct sun | Cacti, lots of succulents, herbs, citrus |
| Bright, without direct sun | Directly at the window without sun or close to a sunny window with a curtain | Monstera, fiddle leaf fig, orchids |
| Partial shade | One to two meters from the window | Ivy plant, Philodendron, Calathea |
| Shady | Far from the window, never sun | Lucky feather, arched hemp, cobbler palm (survive, but grow slowly) |

Important: “Shade tolerant” means that a plant survives there, not that it grows best there. Almost every houseplant grows stronger in a brighter place.

## Window directions

- **South window:** Lots of direct sun, especially at midday. Ideal for cacti and succulents, for tropical leafy plants only with a curtain or distance.
- **West window:** Afternoon and evening sun, which is quite strong in summer. Very good for many plants.
- **East Window:** Gentle morning sun, bright the rest of the day. Ideal for most houseplants.
- **North window:** Never direct sun, even but weaker. Good for ferns, calatheas and shade-tolerant species, often too dark in winter.

In addition to the direction, other factors play a role: trees in front of the window, buildings opposite, balconies above the window, window size and floor height. A south-facing window on the ground floor of a narrow courtyard may be darker than a north-facing window on the fifth floor with a clear view.

## Seasons

In winter the days are shorter, the sun is lower and the light intensity drops significantly. A place that is ideal in summer may be too dark in winter. Conversely, the sun can suddenly be too strong on a west window in summer. Many plant lovers therefore move their plants closer to the window in autumn and back a little in spring.

## Detect lack of light

- **Growing:** Long, thin shoots with large spaces between leaves.
- **Small new leaves** that are smaller than the old ones.
- **Loss of patterns:** Variegated leaves become greener.
- **Tilt towards the window:** The plant grows at an angle to the light.
- **Shedding of lower leaves:** Especially on fiddlehead figs and birch figs.
- **No bloom** on flowering plants.
- **Soil stays wet for a long time** because the plant uses little water. This promotes rot.

## Recognize sunburn

- **Bright, whitish or yellow spots** on the side facing the sun.
- **Later brown, papery, dry spots.**
- **Reddish or brownish discoloration** in succulents as a stress reaction (not always harmful).

Sunburn often occurs when a plant is suddenly placed in stronger sun, for example after purchasing it or when moving to the balcony. Plants can adjust to more light, but only gradually over one to two weeks.

## Plant lamps

If a room is too dark, a plant lamp can help.

- **LED lamps** are standard today: economical, cool and long-lasting.
- **Full spectrum light** in neutral white looks pleasant in the living room and nourishes the plants well. Purple-pink light also works, but is often distracting in the living room.
- **Distance:** Depending on the output, 20 to 60 cm above the plant. The light intensity decreases significantly with distance.
- **Duration:** 10 to 14 hours per day, easiest with a timer.
- **Normal room lighting** is not sufficient as a replacement.

A plant lamp is particularly useful for growing on the windowsill in spring, because otherwise seedlings will quickly rot. More about this in [Sowing on the windowsill](/ratgeber/aussaat-auf-der-fensterbank/).

## Light and watering are related

A plant in a bright spot uses more water and nutrients than the same plant in the shade. If you place a plant in a darker place, you will have to water it less often and fertilize it less. Many rot problems arise when a plant is in the dark but is watered like it is at the window. See [Watering houseplants properly](/ratgeber/zimmerpflanzen-richtig-giessen/).

## Finding the right place: step by step

1. **Read the plant's requirements:** Sunny, bright, partial shade or shade tolerant.
2. **Compare locations:** Measure at multiple possible locations at lunchtime using a light meter app.
3. **Observe hours of sunshine:** When does direct sun shine on the site and for how long?
4. **Try and observe:** After a few weeks the plant will show whether it is satisfied: compact new leaves are a good sign.
5. **Adjust in fall and spring.**

## Practical example: The shelf plants

A bookshelf on the wall opposite a west window, about four meters away, was decorated with ivy, philodendron and a monstera. After a year, all of them had long, bare shoots; the Monstera only produced small, unslit leaves. A measurement showed around 200 lux on the shelf and 3,000 lux directly at the window. The monstera moved to the window, the ivy and the philodendron remained on the shelf, but were given an LED strip with a timer that burned for twelve hours a day. Within one summer, the new shoots of the shelf plants became dense and compact.

## Buying a plant lamp: what should you pay attention to?

| criterion | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Light bulbs | LED, energy saving and cool |
| light color | Neutral to daylight white (around 4,000 to 6,500 Kelvin) for living rooms |
| Performance | For a windowsill or shelf, usually 10 to 30 watt LEDs |
| Design | Strips for shelves, spotlights for individual plants, panels for larger areas |
| Control | With timer or built-in timer |
| Assembly | Height adjustable to adjust distance |

A common mistake is too much distance between the lamp and the plant. Even at twice the distance, only about a quarter of the light arrives. A lamp that hangs on the ceiling two meters above the plant is therefore of little use. Strips that are attached directly above the shelf or clamp spotlights that are 30 to 50 cm above the plant are better. The electricity costs for LEDs are low: a 20-watt strip that burns for twelve hours a day uses around seven kilowatt hours per month.

## Measuring light with your smartphone

Our eyes get used to different levels of brightness so well that we almost always overestimate the light in a room. A light meter app on your smartphone provides a rough guide in lux. The values ​​are not precise because cell phone sensors are not built for measurements, but comparisons between places in the same apartment work well: you quickly see that the light drops to a fraction just one or two meters away from the window.

It's best to measure at midday on a bright day with the sensor where the leaves of the plant are and compare several places. Shade-tolerant plants such as lucky feathers or bow hemp can cope with places where the app shows significantly lower values ​​than at the window. Flowering plants, cacti and herbs, on the other hand, belong in the brightest places.

## More light without moving

Sometimes you can improve the light in a spot without moving the plant. Clean window panes let in noticeably more light than dirty ones, and dusty leaves also capture less light; Therefore, wipe large leaves regularly. Light-colored walls and furniture reflect light into the room, and a mirror opposite the window can noticeably brighten a dark corner. In winter, when the sun is low, you move light-hungry plants closer to the window.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there enough light through a curtain?

A thin curtain filters direct sun and is ideal for many tropical plants on south and west windows. Dense curtains, on the other hand, absorb a lot of light.

### Do plants grow just as well in artificial light?

With a suitable plant lamp, yes. Many plants grow excellently in artificial light shelves.

### Can I put a plant in a windowless spot?

Only with a plant lamp that burns for many hours every day. Without light, every plant dies after a while, even the most robust ones.

### How long should a plant lamp be lit every day?

Ten to fourteen hours a day is enough for most houseplants. A timer ensures a consistent rhythm. Don't leave the lamp on around the clock, because plants also need a dark period.

### Do my plants need a lamp in winter?

Many green plants can cope with the winter light if they are placed close to the window and are watered less. Light-hungry plants such as herbs, cacti, succulents and citrus, on the other hand, benefit significantly from an additional LED plant lamp that does not hang too far above the plants.

## Conclusion

Light is the most important growth factor for houseplants and is most often underestimated. Place plants as close to the window as their type allows, observe the hours of sunshine and seasons, slowly get them used to more sun and supplement dark places with a plant lamp. If you assess light correctly, you have already done half the care.
