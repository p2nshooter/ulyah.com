The arched hemp, botanically now mostly classified as Dracaena, but commercially known as Sansevieria, is considered the perfect plant for beginners and for those who are often on the go. It tolerates drought, little light and forgotten watering dates. Nevertheless, many bow hemp plants die, and almost always for the same reason: too much water.

This guide explains what bow hemp really needs, how to water it correctly, which soil and which pot is suitable, when and how to repot, how to propagate it and what problems can arise.

## Profile

| feature | Bow hemp |
|---|---|
| Origin | Dry regions of Africa and South Asia |
| growth | Upright, leathery leaves from a rootstock (rhizome) |
| Light | Light to partially shaded, also tolerates sun |
| Temperature | 18 to 27°C, not below 12°C |
| Pouring | Only when the earth is completely dry |
| Difficulty | Very light |
| Toxicity | Slightly toxic to pets (saponins) |

## Location

The bow hemp is adaptable:

- **Bright to sunny:** This is where it grows fastest and produces the brightest colors.
- **Partial shade:** It grows slower but remains healthy.
- **Dark Corners:** It survives there but barely grows. Then water particularly little.

What is important is a **constantly warm environment.** The fleshy leaves do not tolerate cold window sills in winter and drafts well.

## Watering: less is more

Bow hemp stores water in its thick leaves and rhizome. He can go without water for weeks.

1. **Allow the soil to dry out completely,** even in depth. Check with a wooden stick: If it comes out clean and dry, you can water.
2. **Water thoroughly** until water runs out the bottom.
3. **Remove excess immediately.**
4. **Do not water into the heart of the leaf rosette:** Water in the center of the leaf can cause rot.

| season | Rough orientation |
|---|---|
| Spring and summer | Every 2 to 4 weeks |
| Autumn and winter | Every 4 to 8 weeks, even less frequently in cool locations |

## Soil and pot

- **Soil:** Cactus or succulent soil, preferably with additional sand, pumice or lava granules. Regular potting soil holds too much water.
- **Pot:** With drain hole. A heavy clay or ceramic pot will prevent tall plants from toppling over, and clay will allow excess moisture to evaporate.
- **Drainage:** A layer of expanded clay at the bottom of the pot is helpful, but does not replace the drainage hole.

## Fertilize

Bow hemp needs few nutrients. From spring to late summer, once a month with cactus or green plant fertilizer in half the dosage is sufficient. Do not fertilize in winter.

## Repotting

Bow hemp likes it tight. Repotting is necessary if:

- Roots grow out of the drain hole.
- The rootstock visibly deforms or cracks the pot (this happens with plastic pots).
- The earth is consumed and compacted after years.

Usually every two to three years in spring is enough. Choose the new pot just a little larger. After repotting, do not water for a week to allow injured roots to heal. See [Repotting Step by Step](/ratgeber/umtopfen-anleitung/).

## Multiply

Bow hemp can be propagated in two ways:

- **Division:** When repotting, divide the rhizome with a sharp knife so that each part has leaves and roots. Plant separately. This is the fastest method. See [Dividing Plants](/ratgeber/pflanzen-teilen/).
- **Leaf cuttings:** Cut a leaf into 5 to 10 cm long pieces, let the cut surfaces dry for a few days and stick the lower side (note the direction!) into sandy soil. It often takes months for new shoots to appear. With variegated varieties, the new plants usually lose their yellow edges.

## Typical problems

| Symptom | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Leaves become soft, mushy, fall over | Too much water, rot | Remove from the pot, remove rotten parts, replant dry |
| Brown, soft spots on the base of the leaves | Water in the leaf rosette or cold | Never pour in the middle, keep it warmer |
| Leaves curl or become wrinkled | Long lack of water | Water thoroughly |
| Leaves tip apart | Too little light, too much water | Place it brighter, water less, possibly tie the leaves together |
| Brown tips | Irregular watering, cold, injury | Cut the tips off at an angle |
| No new leaves | Too dark or hibernation | Lighten up, be patient |

### Save rot

1. Remove the plant from the pot and remove the soil.
2. Cut off mushy, brown roots and leaf parts with a clean knife down to the healthy tissue.
3. Allow cut surfaces to dry for a few days.
4. Place in fresh, dry succulent soil and only water carefully after a week.

More in [Detect and Save Root Rot](/ratgeber/wurzelfaeule-erkennen-und-retten/).

## blossom

Older plants sometimes produce an inflorescence in spring with fragrant, creamy white flowers that are particularly fragrant in the evening. Sticky nectar can escape. After flowering, the rosette in question no longer produces new leaves, but the rhizome sprouts new rosettes.

## Practical example: bowed hemp in the office

In an office there was a large bow hemp in a planter without a vent. Several colleagues watered it regularly “to be on the safe side.” After a few months the outer leaves fell soft. When I took it out there was water in the planter at the bottom and some of the roots were rotten. After cutting back, drying and planting in cactus soil with an expanded clay layer, the plant received a note: “Watering only on the first Monday of the month and only when the soil is dry.” Since then it has been growing new leaves again.

| variety | Appearance | Special feature |
|---|---|---|
| Laurentii | Tall leaves with yellow edges | Classic, very robust |
| Hahnii | Low, funnel-shaped rosettes | Fits on small window sills |
| Moonshine | Silvery-green, broad leaves | Needs a little more light for the bright color |
| Cylindrica | Round, rod-shaped leaves | Often sold braided |
| Kirkii and Masoniana | Very broad, single leaves | Grows slowly, looks sculptural |

Variegated varieties with light edges or silvery markings retain their color best in a bright location. In dark corners, new leaves often become darker and narrower.

## Arched hemp in the bedroom and bathroom

Bow hemp is often recommended for bedrooms. There's nothing wrong with a bright spot in the bedroom, as long as it doesn't stay below 15 °C for a long time. Many bedrooms are not very heated in winter; The following applies in particular: hardly water and do not place the plant directly in the cold window. Bow hemp feels at home in the bathroom if there is a window. He doesn't need the higher humidity, but it doesn't do any harm either. In windowless bathrooms, he is constantly missing light. He only stays there with a plant lamp that burns for several hours every day.

## Common care mistakes at a glance

1. **Watering according to a fixed weekly schedule** instead of when the soil is dry.
2. **Normal potting soil** without mineral additives that hold water for too long.
3. **Planter without control,** in which water stands unnoticed.
4. **Too big pot** when repotting, leaving a lot of wet soil with no roots.
5. **Cold location in winter,** for example directly at an unheated window or on the tiled floor.
6. **Spray into the rosette,** leaving water in the center of the leaf.

If you avoid these six points, you can hardly go wrong with bow hemp.

## Arched hemp throughout the year

Spring is the best time for repotting and dividing, and with new growth begin again with light fertilization. In summer, bowed hemp can also be placed on the balcony or terrace if you slowly get it used to the stronger light and protect it from constant rain. The first few days should be in a semi-shady place, otherwise the leaves will get sunburnt.

In autumn you reduce watering significantly, and in winter bow hemp at room temperature often only needs a little water every four to six weeks. It shouldn't be kept cool: it hardly grows below about fifteen degrees, and cold combined with wet soil is the most common cause of rot. Cold window sills above an unheated room or directly on a drafty balcony door are therefore unsuitable in winter.

## A new botanical name

If you look up a plant dictionary, you will often find arched hemp under the name Dracaena trifasciata. After genetic studies, botanists have assigned the genus Sansevieria to the dragon trees, to which it is closely related. However, the old name is still used in commerce and in everyday life, and nothing has changed in terms of care.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does bow hemp clean the air?

Plants absorb pollutants to a small extent. In normal living spaces, however, the effect on air quality is small. Regular ventilation is significantly more effective.

### How often do I have to water bow hemp?

So rare that the soil dries out completely between waterings. Usually every two to eight weeks, depending on the season.

### Is bow hemp toxic to cats?

It contains saponins, which can cause nausea in cats and dogs. See [Plant Safety](/pflanzensicherheit/).

### Can I grow bow hemp hydroponically?

Yes, this works well if the water level is kept low.

### Why are my bow hemp leaves falling over?

Most of the time the leaves at the base have become soft and rotten due to too much water. Check the base and roots. More rarely, the reason is too little light: Then the leaves grow long, thin and soft and can no longer support their weight. A pot that is too large with a lot of wet soil also makes it more likely to tip over.

### Why is my bow hemp not growing?

Bow hemp grows naturally slowly, and it often takes a break in winter. If it is permanently dark, it hardly grows even in summer. A bright place, warmth and, from spring, light fertilization stimulate growth. A slightly narrow pot doesn't hurt, because the plant invests in roots in a lot of soil first.

Patience is worth it: In a good year, a healthy bow hemp often only produces a few new leaves, but they last for many years.

## Conclusion

Bow hemp needs one thing above all: restraint when watering. Allow the soil to dry out completely, never water the leaf rosette, use well-drained succulent soil in a pot with a drainage hole and keep the plant warm. Then it will grow reliably for years, even in locations where other plants give up.
