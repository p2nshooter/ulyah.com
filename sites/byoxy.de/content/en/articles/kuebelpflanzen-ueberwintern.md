Oleander, lemon trees, olives, geraniums and fuchsias transform the balcony and terrace into a Mediterranean garden in summer. But when the nights get cold in autumn, the question arises: what to do with the plants? Many potted plants come from warmer regions and can tolerate little or no frost. However, with the right winter quarters and appropriate care, they survive the winter well and start the new season strong in spring.

This guide explains which plants are frost hardy, which winter quarters are suitable, how to adjust light, temperature and water and how to clear out again in spring.

## Frost hardiness: the three groups

| group | Examples | Overwintering |
|---|---|---|
| Not frost hardy (sensitive) | Citrus, angel trumpet, bougainvillea, hibiscus, geraniums, fuchsias, lantana | Frost-free, light or dark depending on the type, store in time before the first frost |
| Conditionally frost hardy | Oleander, olive, laurel, fig, rosemary, pomegranate, African lily | Light frosts down to around minus 5 °C, depending on the species; put away in case of prolonged frost |
| Hardy in the pot | Boxwood, cherry laurel, grasses, lavender, bamboo, many perennials | Outside with protection for pots and roots |

Important: Plants in pots are more sensitive than planted specimens because the roots in the pot are exposed to frost from all sides.

## When to concede?

- **Sensitive plants** before the first night frost, usually early to mid-October.
- **Leave frost-hardy plants** such as oleander and olive outside for as long as possible. A slight cold hardens them, and the light is better outside. Only put away when temperatures remain below minus 3 to minus 5 °C.
- **Keep an eye on the weather forecast** and act in good time if frost is forecast.

## Before putting away

1. **Check for pests:** Check the undersides of leaves, shoots and leaf axils for scale insects, mealybugs, spider mites and aphids. Treat affected plants beforehand, otherwise the pests will spread to their winter quarters.
2. **Remove wilted and diseased parts.**
3. **Pruning:** Only if space is limited. A strong pruning is better done in spring. Geraniums and fuchsias can be shortened before putting away.
4. **Reduce watering:** A few weeks in advance so that the plants can adjust to the rest phase.
5. **Stop fertilizing:** From August, September at the latest.

## The winter quarters

### Bright and cool (5 to 10 °C)

For **evergreen plants** that also bear leaves in winter: citrus, oleander, olive, laurel, bougainvillea (partly), rosemary.

Suitable places: unheated winter garden, bright staircase, greenhouse with frost guard, cool guest room with large window.

### Dark and cool (3 to 8 °C)

For **deciduous plants** and plants that are heavily pruned: fig, pomegranate, angel's trumpet (after pruning), fuchsias, geraniums (after pruning), African lily (limited).

Suitable locations: frost-free basement, garage with frost protection, cool storage room.

Rule of thumb: **The darker the area, the cooler it has to be.** In warm, dark rooms, plants grow long, pale shoots and become vulnerable.

### Warm and bright (above 15°C)

Only for tropical plants such as hibiscus or bougainvillea that grow all year round. However, in normal living spaces, light is scarce in winter and the air is dry, which encourages pests.

## Care in winter quarters

- **Watering:** Significantly less than in summer, but the root ball of evergreen plants must never dry out completely. Check with a finger test, usually every one to three weeks. Only occasionally lightly moisten deciduous plants in dark areas.
- **Do not fertilize.**
- **Airing:** Ventilate the area on frost-free days to prevent fungal diseases.
- **Check:** Check every two weeks for pests, mold and fallen leaves. Remove fallen leaves.
- **Avoid temperature fluctuations:** A thermometer with a minimum-maximum display helps.

Typical pests in winter quarters are scale insects, mealybugs and spider mites. See [Combat spider mites](/ratgeber/spinnmilben-bekaempfen/) and [Mealybugs and mealybugs](/ratgeber/wollaeuse-und-schmierlaeuse/).

## Winter outside with shelter

Hardy potted plants and partially frost-hardy species in mild regions can be left outside if the pot and roots are protected:

1. **Insulate the bucket:** Wrap with jute, coconut mats, bubble wrap or fleece.
2. **Raise from the ground:** Place on wooden slats, Styrofoam plates or feet to avoid floor frost and prevent waterlogging.
3. **Sheltered place:** Close to the wall of the house, under an eaves, protected from easterly winds.
4. **Protect the crown:** For evergreen plants, cover with breathable fleece in heavy frost, never with plastic film.
5. **Watering on frost-free days:** Evergreen plants evaporate water even in winter. Drying out is more common for them than freezing to death.

## Clear out in spring

1. **Wake up slowly:** From February or March, place the plants a little brighter and warmer and slowly increase the watering.
2. **Pruning:** Now is the best time to prune.
3. **Repotting:** If necessary, into fresh container plant soil before clearing out. See [Repotting Step by Step](/ratgeber/umtopfen-anleitung/).
4. **Hardening off:** From April on mild days, place outside during the day, initially in the shade, and bring back in at night.
5. **Finally outside:** Sensitive plants after the ice saints in mid-May, robust ones like oleander and olive often from April.

Direct sun after winter can burn leaves. So get used to it slowly.

## Practical example: The oleander in the living room

A large oleander was brought into the warm living room at the beginning of October every year. Every winter it got scale insects, dropped leaves and barely bloomed in the summer. The following year he stayed outside until mid-November and was then placed in an unheated, bright stairwell at around 8°C. Before putting away the leaves were checked and some scale insects were removed. Watering was done approximately every two weeks. In the spring it came out again in April, initially in partial shade. In the summer it bloomed more profusely than it had in years.

## Checklist before putting away

1. Follow the weather forecast and don't wait for the first night frost if the plant is sensitive.
2. Check each plant individually for pests, especially the undersides of leaves and shoot tips.
3. Remove wilted leaves, flowers and fruits.
4. Clean the outside of the bucket so that no snails or woodlice wander into the area.
5. Have coasters or rolling boards ready so that heavy buckets can be moved more easily later.
6. Hang a label on the pot with the plant name, watering requirements and storage date.
7. Hang a thermometer in your winter quarters.

If you work through these points, you will avoid unpleasant surprises in February such as scale insect infestation or dried out root balls.

## Pests in winter quarters

Warm, dry winter quarters are a paradise for spider mites, scale insects, mealybugs and whiteflies. Because plants grow more slowly in winter and produce fewer defenses, pests often spread there unnoticed. Therefore, check the undersides of the leaves, the shoot tips and the leaf axils once a week, especially on citrus plants, oleanders and angel trumpets.

If possible, keep infected plants separate from the others. Ventilate the winter quarters on frost-free days, because moving, slightly cooler air inhibits many pests. Yellow signs indicate early on whether whiteflies or fungus gnats are on the move. The sooner you intervene, the sooner gentle remedies such as showering, wiping or potassium soap-based preparations will be sufficient.

## Winter quarters without a basement

Not everyone has a bright, cool basement or winter garden. Good alternatives are an unheated guest room, a bright staircase or a garage with a window. In the stairwell, ask the property management first, because escape routes must remain clear. In garages and garden sheds, a minimum-maximum thermometer helps so that you can notice when it gets too cold at night; a small frost guard can then bridge the worst nights.

Many garden centers also offer a wintering service. For a fee, they pick up large container plants in the fall, overwinter them in the greenhouse and bring them back in the spring. This is often the best solution for an old, large oleander or a valuable lemon tree.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I overwinter potted plants in the apartment?

For many species this is difficult because it is too warm and too dark. If there is no other option, choose the brightest and coolest room and possibly add a plant lamp.

### Why does my lemon tree lose leaves in winter?

Common causes are too little light with too much heat, cold roots with a warm crown or watering errors. A bright, evenly cool place helps.

### Do I have to buy new geraniums every year?

No. Geraniums can be overwintered well, either bright and cool or, after heavy pruning, dark and cool.

### When do I cut back potted plants?

It is best to do heavy pruning in late winter or early spring, shortly before the plants sprout again. Before putting it away in the fall, only cut as much as is necessary for transport and space in the winter quarters. Fresh cuts heal more quickly in the spring when growth begins.

### How do I get plants used to the sun again in spring?

After clearing out, place it in a partially shaded, wind-protected place for one to two weeks or clear it out on an overcast day. Even sun-hungry plants such as oleander or citrus will otherwise get sunburned because their leaves have become accustomed to weak light in their winter quarters.

## Conclusion

Correct overwintering depends on the plant's frost hardiness. Evergreen plants need a bright, cool place, deciduous plants can be kept dark and cool, while winter-hardy plants stay outside with container protection. Check for pests before putting away, water sparingly, do not fertilize and slowly get the plants used to sun and open air again in spring.
