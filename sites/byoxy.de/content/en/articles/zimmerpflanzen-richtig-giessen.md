Incorrect watering is the most common cause of houseplants dying. And this is much more often caused by too much water than by too little water. The problem is that both defects can present themselves similarly: drooping leaves, yellow leaves, and brown tips can indicate both thirst and drowned roots. If you understand what is happening in the pot, you will water more safely.

This guide explains why roots need air, how you can tell when a plant needs water, what watering methods are available, which water is suitable and how to avoid mistakes.

## Why roots need air

Roots not only absorb water and nutrients, they also breathe. They need oxygen, which is found in the air pores between the soil particles. If the soil remains permanently wet, these pores are filled with water. The roots suffocate, die and begin to rot. A plant with rotten roots can no longer absorb water and wilts even though the soil is wet. Many people then water even more, and the vicious circle closes.

The goal is therefore a rhythm of **thorough watering and drying.** When watering, the entire soil is moistened; when it dries, the roots draw water and fresh air flows into the pores.

## When to water? The methods at a glance

### Finger test

The simplest and most reliable method: stick a finger two to five centimeters deep into the soil. How dry the soil should be depends on the plant:

| Plant group | Watering when... |
|---|---|
| Cacti, succulents, arched hemp, lucky feather | the earth is completely dry |
| Monstera, ivy, philodendron, rubber tree | the top 3 to 5 cm are dry |
| Ferns, calathea, arrowroot | the top 1 to 2 cm is dry |
| Orchids in bark | the roots are silvery-gray |

### Weight of the pot

Immediately after watering, lift the pot and note the weight. A dry pot is significantly lighter. With a little practice, you can recognize how much water you need by weight without touching the soil. This works particularly well with smaller and medium-sized pots.

### wooden sticks

For large pots, a shish kebab skewer or chopsticks help. Stick it deep into the ground and pull it out after a few minutes: if there is moist soil stuck to it or if the wood is dark, it is still damp underneath.

### Moisture meter

Simple commercial moisture meters provide guidance, but are not always accurate, especially in very mineral substrates. They do not replace looking at the plant.

### Plant signals

Some plants clearly show their thirst: ivy plants and peace lilies let their leaves droop, calatheas curl up, succulents become wrinkled. Waiting for this is fine for hardy species, but not ideal for sensitive ones.

## How to water?

### Pour from above

Pour slowly and evenly over the entire surface of the soil until water runs out of the drain hole. This moistens the entire root ball. After 10 to 15 minutes, remove the water from the saucer or planter.

### Pour from below

Place the pot in a bowl of water and let it soak for 15 to 30 minutes until the surface becomes moist. Then drain well. This method is suitable for plants with delicate leaves, such as African violets, or for completely dry soil. You should still water from above every now and then to rinse out salts.

### Diving

For very dry root balls and orchids: Submerge the pot completely until no more air bubbles appear, then drain thoroughly.

### Completely dried out earth

Soil containing peat, once completely dried out, absorbs water poorly. The water runs past the edge and straight out below. Diving or repeated, slow watering at short intervals helps here.

## How much water?

The question “How many milliliters per week?” cannot be answered in general. Better: **rarely, but thoroughly.** Each time, enough so that the entire bale is moistened and some water runs out from the bottom. The frequency depends on:

| factor | More water requirements | Less water required |
|---|---|---|
| Light | Bright, sunny location | Shady location |
| Temperature | Warm | Cool |
| season | Spring and summer | Autumn and winter |
| pot | Sound, small | Plastic or ceramic, large |
| Substrate | Coarse, mineral | Fine, peaty soil |
| Plant size | Lots of foliage in proportion to the pot | Little leaves, big pot |

## Which water?

- **Tap water** is suitable for most houseplants. In areas with very hard water, limescale builds up and becomes visible as a white border on the soil or on the pot.
- **Stale Water:** Leave overnight to allow room temperature and chlorine to escape.
- **Rainwater** is soft and ideal for lime-sensitive plants such as calatheas, ferns, orchids and azaleas. Do not collect from gutters with heavy contamination.
- **Filtered water** from a table filter lowers the hardness.
- **Avoid water that is too cold**. Ice-cold water can cause cold shock in tropical plants.

## Avoid waterlogging

- **Pots with a drain hole:** Without a drain hole, water collects at the bottom of the pot, invisible to you.
- **Check planters:** Check after watering and drain off any standing water.
- **Drainage layer:** A layer of expanded clay in the planter keeps the inner pot above any residual water. But it never replaces the trigger hole.
- **Suitable pot size:** A pot that is much too large will contain a lot of soil without roots that will stay wet for a long time.

## Watering in winter

In winter, most houseplants have less light and grow slower. You use less water, even if the heating air is dry. Watering intervals become longer, often twice as long as in summer. Exception: Plants directly above or next to the heater dry out quickly. More frequent checks are necessary here, but a different location is better.

## Signs of too much or too little water

| Signs | Too much water | Too little water |
|---|---|---|
| leaves | Yellow, soft, hanging | Limp, dry, curled up |
| Leaf tips | Brown with yellow edge | Brown, crispy |
| Earth | Wet for a long time, musty smell, mold | Dust dry, detaches from the edge of the pot |
| Stem | Soft, dark at the base | Wilted but firm |
| Pests | fungus gnats | Spider mites |

If small black mosquitoes fly above the ground, read [Get Rid of Fungus Gnats](/ratgeber/trauermuecken-loswerden/). Yellow leaves have many causes, which are described in [Yellow Leaves: Causes](/ratgeber/gelbe-blaetter-ursachen/).

## Practical example: The fixed watering day

In a shared apartment, all plants were watered every Sunday, regardless of which one. After one winter, the cacti and hemp were rotten, but the ferns dried up. The roommates sorted the plants into three groups: “Leave almost dry,” “Top layer dry,” and “Evenly moist,” and stuck colored dots on the pots. Watering continued on Sunday, but only after a finger test. The following year no more plants died.

## Watering according to pot and substrate

How quickly a plant needs water depends largely on the pot and the soil. In clay pots, water also evaporates over the pot wall, so the soil dries much faster than in plastic or glazed pots. Mineral substrates with a lot of pumice, lava or expanded clay dry out more quickly than pure potting soil, and coconut substrate holds water for a long time, but sometimes appears dry on the surface.

Peat-free soil is particularly often deceptive: the top layer dries quickly and becomes light, while underneath it is still moist. Therefore, always check a few centimeters deep with your finger or a wooden stick before watering and do not rely on the color of the surface.

## Watering during hot periods

On hot summer days, houseplants on sunny windows use significantly more water than usual. Then check them daily and move sensitive plants a little away from the pane behind which the heat accumulates. The widespread concern that water droplets on the leaves act like magnifying glasses in the sun is unfounded for most plants. It is still better to water directly onto the soil so that the water reaches the roots.

## Watering rules by plant group

- **Cacti and succulents:** Water thoroughly, then allow the soil to dry completely.
- **Green plants such as monstera, ivy or rubber tree:** water when the top two to three centimeters are dry.
- **Ferns, calatheas and marantas:** keep evenly slightly moist, without waterlogging.
- **Phalaenopsis Orchids:** Dive when the roots look silvery gray.

## Frequently asked questions

### Water in the morning or evening?

The time of day hardly matters for houseplants. It is important that the water is at room temperature and does not remain in the leaf axils.

### Should I spray houseplants?

Spraying only increases the humidity briefly. It can be useful for ferns or tillandsias, but not for hairy leaves and succulents. More in [Humidity for Houseplants](/ratgeber/luftfeuchtigkeit-fuer-zimmerpflanzen/).

### What to do if I travel?

For short absences, thorough watering and a shady location are sufficient. There are irrigation systems for longer trips. See [Caring for Plants on Vacation](/ratgeber/pflanzen-im-urlaub-versorgen/).

### Is boiled water better?

When boiling, some of the lime falls out, making the water a little softer. When cooled, it can help lime-sensitive plants, but it takes a lot of effort. Rainwater or filtered water are easier.

### What to do if I watered too much?

Dump excess water from the planter and saucer, tilt the pot to allow water to drain from the drainage holes, and place the plant in a bright, warm spot. If the soil remains wet for several days or smells unpleasant, repot the plant in fresh, dry soil and check the roots.

### Are there plants that hardly need to be watered?

Cacti, succulents, arched hemp and lucky feathers get by with infrequent watering. But they can't go without care either: they need light and occasional thorough water.

## Conclusion

Watering well doesn't mean sticking to a fixed schedule, but rather observing the plant and its soil. Water thoroughly when the soil has dried sufficiently depending on the species, remove excess water, use pots with a drainage hole and adjust the spacing according to light, temperature and season. This will help you avoid the most common mistake when caring for houseplants.
