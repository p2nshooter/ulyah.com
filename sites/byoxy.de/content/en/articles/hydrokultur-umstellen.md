Watering as you feel, fungus gnats in the soil, mold on the surface: many problems with houseplants have to do with soil and water. Hydroponics solves some of these in an elegant way. The plants do not grow in soil, but in a mineral substrate such as expanded clay, and a small water level indicator shows you exactly when they need water.

Hydroponics is not a new invention, but has been tried and tested in offices, practices and homes for decades. Nevertheless, there are many misunderstandings: Hydroponic plants are not permanently in water and they need a special fertilizer. This guide explains how the system works, how to move plants from soil and how to properly care for them afterwards.

## How hydroponics works

In classic hydroponics, the plant sits in a culture pot with slots that is filled with expanded clay. This culture pot stands in a waterproof planter. At the bottom of the planter there is a nutrient solution made of water and fertilizer, which is sucked up by the clay balls. The roots grow through the slits into the lower area and get water and nutrients there, while they get oxygen in the upper, airy part of the expanded clay.

A water level indicator, a narrow tube with a float, is inserted into the substrate and shows the filling level. It usually has three markings: minimum, optimum and maximum. This allows you to see at a glance how much water is left without having to feel in the soil.

The interaction of water and air is important. Roots need oxygen. If they were constantly completely submerged in water, they would suffocate and rot. That's why hydroponics involves a rhythm of filling and drying, in which the water level regularly drops to the minimum.

## Hydroponics and semi-hydro: the difference

In addition to classic hydroponics, there is semi-hydroponics. Here the plants grow in mineral granules, such as clay granules, pumice, lava or zeolite, in a pot with a water reservoir. The water rises through capillary action. Semi-hydro substrates are finer than expanded clay and hold more moisture in the root area, so the plant does not root in an open layer of water.

Both systems do not use soil and work according to the same principle of filling and drying. For beginners, classic hydroponics with expanded clay and a water level indicator are the clearest. Semi-Hydro is popular with lovers of arum plants such as Monstera, Philodendron and Anthuriums.

## Advantages and disadvantages

**Advantages:**

- **Easy Watering:** The water level indicator shows exactly when water is needed. The risk of watering too much or too little is significantly reduced.
- **Water less frequently:** Depending on the plant and the season, watering every one to three weeks is often sufficient.
- **No fungus gnats:** Without organic material, the larvae cannot find food. More about this in [Get rid of fungus gnats](/ratgeber/trauermuecken-loswerden/).
- **No mold on earth:** Expanded clay provides little food for mold.
- **Repotting less frequently:** Expanded clay does not decompose, repotting is only necessary if the plant becomes too large.
- **Clean:** No smell of earth, no crumbling earth, pleasant in offices and for people who don't want earth in their home.

**Disadvantages:**

- **Purchase:** Culture pots, planters, water level indicators, expanded clay and special fertilizers initially cost more than soil.
- **Transition Stress:** Plants that are moved from soil take weeks to adjust and some lose leaves in the process.
- **Special fertilizer:** Normal liquid fertilizer is dosed too high or is unsuitable.
- **Lime and salts:** With hard tap water, white crusts form on the expanded clay.
- **Not every plant is suitable:** Many cacti and some sensitive species find it difficult.

## Which plants are suitable?

Plants that like consistent moisture or are very adaptable thrive particularly well in hydroponics:

- [Ivy plant](/ratgeber/efeutute-pflege/) and Philodendron
- [Monstera](/ratgeber/monstera-pflege/)
- [Peace Lily](/ratgeber/friedenslilie-pflege/)
- [Glücksfeder](/ratgeber/gluecksfeder-zamioculcas-pflege/) and [Bogenhanf](/ratgeber/bogenhanf-sansevieria-pflege/), with a very economical water level
- Spider plant, dieffenbachia, carpenter aralia and bulbous thread
- Anthuriums and many other arum plants
- Dragon trees and yucca

Plants that want to be very dry, such as many cacti, and plants with very fine roots are less suitable. There are special water culture methods for orchids that differ from classic hydroponics.

## Switching from soil to hydroponics

The easiest way to make the change is with young, healthy plants or with freshly rooted cuttings. Older plants often manage it too, but need more time. The best time is spring when the plant is growing.

1. **Prepare:** Rinse expanded clay thoroughly in a sieve to remove dust and soak for a few hours. Prepare the culture pot, planter and water level indicator.
2. **Unpotting:** Remove the plant from the pot and shake off as much of the soil as possible.
3. **Wash the roots:** Carefully wash out the remaining soil in a bowl with lukewarm water until the roots are clean. Remains of earth would later rot.
4. **Check:** Cut off brown, mushy or dead roots with clean scissors.
5. **Planting:** Add some expanded clay to the culture pot, place the plant in it and fill the gaps with expanded clay. Insert the water level indicator at the same time.
6. **Low water:** In the first few weeks, only add enough water so that the water level indicator is just above the minimum. The roots of the earth have to adapt first.
7. **Protected place:** Place the plant in a bright place, but out of direct sun, and do not fertilize in the first few weeks.

During the transition period, the plant forms new roots that are adapted to life in the substrate. Some older leaves may turn yellow and fall off. This is normal as long as new leaves appear. After about six to eight weeks you can switch to the normal watering rhythm and start fertilizing.

Starting with cuttings is easier than moving large plants. If you root them in a glass of water, their roots are already used to water. More about this in [Rooting cuttings in a glass of water](/ratgeber/stecklinge-im-wasserglas/).

## Watering correctly in hydroponics

The most common mistake in hydroponics is a water level that is constantly too high. The most important rule is therefore: regularly allow the water level indicator to drop to the minimum.

- **Normal rhythm:** Only add water to about halfway between minimum and optimum.
- **Take a break:** If the meter is at minimum, wait another two to three days before refilling. During this time the roots get a lot of air.
- **Optimum only occasionally:** You only fill up to optimum for large plants with high consumption.
- **Maximum only before vacation:** The maximum marking is intended for absences, not for everyday life.
- **Room temperature water:** Cold water stresses the roots.

How quickly the water level drops depends on the plant, light, temperature and season. In the summer, a large Monstera may need water weekly, while a Lucky Feather may only need water every few weeks in the winter. You keep cactus-like and succulent plants such as arched hemp and lucky feathers in short supply anyway and leave them at a minimum for longer.

## Fertilizing in hydroponics

Expanded clay contains no nutrients, so the plant is completely dependent on the fertilizer in the water. Normal liquid fertilizer for soil plants is not intended for this. Use a special hydroponic fertilizer and stick to the dosage exactly.

Two types are widespread:

- **Liquid hydroponic fertilizer:** It is added in small doses to the irrigation water every time it is filled or at fixed intervals.
- **Ion exchange fertilizer:** A granule or tablet is added to the culture pot or placed in the water. The ion exchanger releases nutrients and in return absorbs salts from the tap water. This means the plant is supplied evenly over several months, and the lime from the water causes fewer problems. He will then be replaced.

Only fertilize fully during the growing season from spring to autumn and significantly less in winter. You can find the basics about nutrients and over-fertilization in the guide [fertilizing houseplants] (/ratgeber/zimmerpflanzen-duengen/).

## Care throughout the year

- **Rinsing:** Approximately every four to six months, pour the entire container through with clean water and then tip the solution away. This is how you remove accumulated salts.
- **Calcite crusts:** White crusts on the top expanded clay are lime and salt deposits. You can replace the top layer or soak the balls in diluted vinegar water, rinse thoroughly, and reuse.
- **Algae:** Green deposits appear when light falls into the water area. Opaque planters prevent this.
- **Repotting:** Only necessary when roots completely fill the culture pot or the plant threatens to tip over. Then you put them in a larger culture pot with fresh, washed expanded clay.

## Typical problems

- **Rotten roots, unpleasant smell:** The water level was too high for too long. Rinse roots and expanded clay, cut off brown roots and water more sparingly in the future.
- **Yellow leaves after switching:** Normal for the first few weeks. If they stop, there is often too much water in the container.
- **Brown leaf tips:** too much fertilizer or accumulated salts. Rinse thoroughly once.
- **Hanging leaves despite water:** Root damage, usually due to waterlogging. Check roots.
- **No growth:** too little light or no fertilizer. Hydroponics does not change the fact that plants need enough light. See [Houseplant Light](/ratgeber/licht-fuer-zimmerpflanzen/).

## Practical example: The office with the thirsty plants

In a small office there were twelve plants in soil that were watered by different colleagues. Some plants received water three times a week, others without water for weeks, and several pots were full of fungus gnats. Gradually, the sturdiest plants, including ivy plants, a peace lily and two lucky feathers, were converted to hydroponics in the spring.

A simple note on the coffee machine regulated the pouring: “Fill only when the indicator is at minimum, then wait two days.” After the transition period, in which some old leaves turned yellow, the plants grew more evenly than before, the fungus gnats disappeared, and watering only took a few minutes a week.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use regular flower fertilizer?

Better not. Normal liquid fertilizers are intended for soil in which some of the nutrients are bound. In hydroponics they act directly on the roots and easily lead to over-fertilization. Use a designated hydroponic fertilizer.

### Can I take expanded clay from the hardware store?

Basically yes, if it is clean, unfertilized expanded clay. Rinse thoroughly before use. Make sure the grain is consistent and appropriate for the size of the pot: smaller grain for small pots and cuttings, coarser for large plants.

### Do hydroponic plants need less light?

No. Hydroponics changes the water and nutrient supply, not the light requirements. A plant that needs a bright spot in soil also needs it in hydroponics.

### Can I put the plant back in soil later?

This is possible, but it means another change. The plant must then grow roots again that are adapted to soil. Keep the soil evenly slightly moist for the first few weeks so that the change is not too abrupt.

## Conclusion

Hydroponics is a clean and low-maintenance alternative to soil, especially for people who are unsure about watering and for rooms where fungus gnats or the smell of soil bother you. The key to success lies in the water level: let it drop regularly to a minimum, then wait a few days and only fill it up moderately. With special hydroponic fertilizer, occasional rinsing and a bright spot, many popular houseplants thrive for years without a crumb of soil. If you're new, it's best to start with young plants or cuttings and robust species such as ivy, peace lily or lucky feather.
