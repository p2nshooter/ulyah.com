Drilling a hole in the wall sounds easy, and yet it often goes wrong: the drill slips, the hole becomes too big, the anchor spins or you hit something hard and can't get any further. This is usually not due to skill, but rather to the wrong combination of wall building material, drill and machine.

This guide explains how to identify the building material of your wall, which drill and machine are suitable, how to drill a clean hole step by step and what you can do if problems arise.

## Step 1: Recognize the wall building material

Before drilling, you need to know what you are dealing with. These tests help:

| test | Concrete | solid brick | Perforated bricks | Aerated concrete | Plasterboard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Knock | Hard, dull | Hard | Partly hollow | Dull, soft | Hollow, drumming |
| drilling dust | Gray, fine | Red or yellowish | Red, suddenly cavity | White to gray, lots of dust | White, paper fibers |
| Resistance | High, even | Means | Changing | Low | Very slight, then cavity |
| Typical | New buildings, ceilings | Old buildings | Houses from 1970 | New buildings, interior walls | Drywall walls, sloping roofs |

If in doubt, make a test hole with a small diameter in an inconspicuous place.

## Step 2: Exclude pipes

Before you drill, check whether there are electricity, water or gas pipes running through the wall. Use a tracking device and stick to the installation zones. You can find the details under [Locating pipes before drilling](/anleitungen/leitungen-orten-vor-dem-bohren/).

## Step 3: Choose the right drill

| building material | Drill | License plate |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete, reinforced concrete | SDS-plus drill bit for hammer drill | Carbide plate, insertion end with grooves |
| Brick, sand-lime brick | Stone drill | Carbide tip, round or hexagonal shaft |
| Aerated concrete, plasterboard | Stone drill or wood drill, without impact | Pointed grind |
| Wood | wood drill | center point |
| Tiles | Tile or glass drill, diamond drill bit | Pointed lance shape or diamond coating |
| metal | HSS drill bits | Silvery or gold, 118° tip |

## Step 4: The right machine and setting

| building material | machine | Blow | speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concrete | Hammer drill | Yes | Means |
| Solid brick, sand-lime brick | Impact drill or hammer drill | Yes | Means |
| Perforated bricks | Drilling machine | **No** | Means |
| Aerated concrete | Drill or cordless screwdriver | **No** | Low to medium |
| Plasterboard | Cordless screwdriver | **No** | Means |
| Tiles | Drilling machine | **No** | Low |

**Important:** For perforated bricks and aerated concrete, drill without impact. Otherwise the webs between the hollow chambers will break, the hole will be too big and the dowel will not hold.

## Step 5: Drill cleanly

1. **Mark:** Mark the drilling point with a pencil and, if necessary, align with a spirit level. See [Measuring and Marking](/anleitungen/messen-und-anreissen-wasserwaage-laser/).
2. **Determine drilling depth:** Dowel length plus about 10 mm. Adjust the machine's depth stop or place a piece of tape on the drill.
3. **Mark:** On smooth or hard surfaces, make a small notch with a nail or center punch to prevent the drill from running away.
4. **Drill slowly:** Start at low speed until the drill takes hold, then increase the speed.
5. **Keep it straight:** Hold the machine perpendicular to the wall. Some machines have a level; otherwise a look from the side will help.
6. **Don't press too hard:** Let the drill work. Too much pressure overheats the tip.
7. **Remove drill dust:** Blow or vacuum the hole. Drilling dust in the hole significantly reduces the hold of the dowel.

### Drill dust-free

A folded piece of paper under the drilling site, secured with adhesive tape, catches the dust. It gets even cleaner if a second person holds the vacuum cleaner right next to the drilling site, or with a drill dust collector.

## Special case of tiles

1. Stick **adhesive tape** on the drilling site to prevent the drill from slipping.
2. **Use tile drill without impact** and at low speed.
3. **Cooling:** Use a little water for diamond core bits.
4. **Only after the tile** switch to a stone drill and, if necessary, impact.
5. **Drill into the joint if possible,** if the picture or holder allows it: joints are easier to repair.

## Special case of plasterboard

You can drill plasterboard with a cordless screwdriver without impact. Special cavity dowels are needed for loads. Heavy loads such as wall cupboards must be secured to the supports of the substructure or with reinforcements. More about this under [Select and place dowels correctly] (/anleitungen/duebel-richtig-waehlen-und-setzen/).

## Problems and solutions

| problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Drill doesn't get any further | Hit on steel reinforcement in concrete | Don't drill any further, move the spot a few centimeters |
| Hole too big | Drilled into perforated bricks with impact, drill tilted | Use larger dowels or injection mortar |
| Drill runs | Not marked, speed too high | Center punch, drill slowly |
| Dowel spins | Drilling dust in the hole, hole too big, wrong dowel | Clean the hole, choose the right dowel |
| Plaster is chipping off | Too fast and drilled with impact | First drill through the plaster without hitting it |
| Drill becomes hot and blue | Too much pressure, blunt drill | Reduce pressure, replace drill |

## Security

- **Wear safety glasses:** Stone chips and dust fly when drilling.
- **Hearing protection** for longer work with the hammer drill.
- **Dust mask** if there is a lot of drilling dust, especially in old buildings.
- **Note slip clutch:** Rotary hammers have a safety clutch that prevents the machine from swinging around if the drill gets stuck. Nevertheless, always hold the machine with both hands.
- **Respect quiet times:** Quiet times apply in apartment buildings, often at lunchtime and in the evening. Check the house rules.

## Match the drill and dowel to each other

For most plastic dowels, the drill diameter corresponds to the dowel size: for a 6 mm dowel you drill with 6 millimeters, for an 8 mm dowel you drill with 8 millimeters. The dowel package also states which screw diameters are suitable and how long the screw must be at least: dowel length plus the thickness of the component you are attaching. A screw that is too thin will not spread the dowel enough, and one that is too thick can break it. For special dowels for aerated concrete, plasterboard or perforated bricks, follow the manufacturer's instructions, as the drilling diameters sometimes differ.

## Large holes for cans and pipes

There are hole saws with a standard diameter of 68 millimeters for flush-mounted boxes in plasterboard, and box countersinks that work with a hammer drill are available for masonry. Electrical installations themselves belong in the hands of a qualified electrician, but many do-it-yourselfers take care of preparing the holes in consultation with them. Larger core drillings for pipes or ventilation ducts are professional work: they require special machines, and in load-bearing walls or ceilings, any larger opening can affect the statics.

## Drill into the ceiling overhead

Drilling overhead is strenuous and dusty. Safety glasses are mandatory here, and a dust collector that you put on the drill keeps drilling dust out of your eyes. Stand securely on a sturdy ladder or scaffolding, not a chair. Be careful with prefabricated ceilings: Tensioned steel cables run through prestressed concrete ceilings and can be damaged when drilling. If you don't know what kind of ceiling you have, ask the property manager or a specialist company before drilling deeper than a small dowel.

## Example: A hole for a mirror in an old bathroom

A typical case shows how the steps work together. A mirror with a frame should hang above the sink in the bathroom of an old building. The wall is tiled, behind it there is solid brick.

1. **Check pipes:** Pipes to the mirror light and water pipes often run above the sink. Search the entire area with the locator and avoid the installation zone vertically above the fitting.
2. **Mark drilling points:** Mark on adhesive tape with a pencil and spirit level. If possible, move it so that the hole lies in a joint.
3. **Drill through the tile:** Tile drill, low speed, no impact, no pressure. When the tip is through, you will notice the change in resistance.
4. **Drilling into the brick:** Switch to a stone drill of the same diameter and only now turn on the blow.
5. **Clean the hole, place the dowel:** The dowel must sit so deep that it does not break the tile from the inside. Many manufacturers therefore recommend placing it a few millimeters behind the tile surface.
6. **Sealing:** In damp rooms, close the gap around the screw with some sanitary silicone to prevent water from getting behind the tile.

## Care for drills and store them properly

Good drills last a long time if you look after them:

- **Allow to cool after drilling,** do not immerse in water. Carbide tips can crack due to abrupt temperature changes.
- **Store separately:** In a drill cassette with fixed locations, the cutting edges do not collide and you can find the right diameter immediately.
- **Sort out blunt drill bits:** A blunt masonry drill bit requires twice as much pressure and creates unclean, overly large holes.
- **Lightly grease the SDS insertion end** if the manufacturer recommends it. This reduces wear on the drill and drill chuck.

## Checklist before every drill hole

- Wall building material known, test drilling made if necessary.
- Cables located, installation zones avoided.
- Drill suitable for building material and dowels.
- Only hit concrete, solid brick and sand-lime brick.
- Depth stop set.
- Safety glasses put on, dust is collected.
- Quiet times in the house are observed.

## Time requirement

A clean drill hole including locating, marking, drilling, cleaning and doweling takes around five to ten minutes for beginners. If you have a drill, dowels and a locating device to hand and know the wall building material, you will quickly become faster. Most of the time is spent not by the drilling itself, but by incorrect drilling, which can be avoided with good preparation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Impact drill or hammer drill?

The hammer drill is clearly superior for concrete: it hits pneumatically with much more energy and is gentle on the wrist. For occasional holes in bricks, a hammer drill is sufficient.

### How deep do I have to drill?

As a rule, the dowel length plus about 10 mm so that there is room for drilling dust and the dowel can be completely recessed.

### Can I drill in rental apartments?

Usual drilling for pictures, shelves and lamps is part of the contractual use. When moving out, dowel holes usually have to be closed to the usual extent. If you have tiles and facades, speak to your landlord beforehand.

### How do I drill into tiles without cracks?

With adhesive tape, tile drill, low speed, no impact and no pressure. Only after the tile do you continue drilling with the stone drill.

## Conclusion

Clean drilling holes start with the right diagnosis: identifying the building material, excluding pipes, choosing the right drill and machine. Drill perforated bricks and aerated concrete without impact, concrete with a hammer drill, tiles slowly and with adhesive tape. Clean the drill hole thoroughly before inserting the dowel. This way, what you hang will last.
