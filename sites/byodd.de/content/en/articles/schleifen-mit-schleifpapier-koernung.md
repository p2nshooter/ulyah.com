Sanding is the job that no one likes to do and that determines the outcome of almost all surface projects. A varnish on poorly sanded wood shows every groove, a patch of filler without a clean transition stands out under the paint, and a piece of furniture that was painted without intermediate sanding feels rough. On the other hand, if you use the right grit size in the right order, you will get smooth surfaces more quickly and save material.

These instructions explain what grit means, which grit you need for what, when hand sanding and when a machine makes sense, how to proceed step by step and how to protect yourself from dust.

## What the grain means

The number on the sandpaper, for example P80 or P180, indicates how fine the sanding grain is. The P stands for the European standard. **The smaller the number, the coarser the paper.**

| Grain | Designation | Typical application |
|---|---|---|
| P40 to P60 | Very rough | Remove old layers of paint, heavily weathered wood, edges break |
| P80 to P100 | Rough | First sanding of raw wood, eliminating unevenness |
| P120 to P150 | Means | Smooth after rough sanding, sand in filler areas |
| P180 to P240 | Fine | Final touch before oil, wax or varnish |
| P280 to P400 | Very fine | Intermediate sanding between coats of varnish |
| P600 and finer | Extra fine | Polishing, wet sanding for high-gloss paints |

## The most important rule: grain size in steps

Each grain leaves scratches of its size. The next finer grit must remove these scratches. If you jump too far, for example from P60 directly to P240, the deep scratches will remain and only become visible under paint or oil.

Rule of thumb: **Skip a maximum of one step.** Proven sequences:

- Raw softwood for oil: P80 → P120 → P180
- Raw hardwood for varnish: P100 → P150 → P220
- Old painted piece of furniture to be repainted: P180 or P240 for sanding
- Filler area on the wall: P120 → P180

## Types of sandpaper

- **Corundum (aluminum oxide):** the all-rounder for wood and metal.
- **Silicon carbide:** very sharp, for paints, fillers, stone, can also be used wet.
- **With stearate coating:** less clogging of varnish and paint.
- **Sanding grid or mesh abrasive:** dust is very well sucked out through the open grid; ideal for fillers and varnishes.
- **Sanding sponges:** adapt to curves and profiles, good for edges, strips and intermediate sanding.

## Hand sanding or machine?

| situation | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Small areas, edges, profiles | Hand sanding with sanding block or sponge |
| Intermediate sanding of varnish | Hand sanding with fine paper or sponge |
| Table top, doors, large surfaces | Orbital sander |
| Corners and narrow strips | Delta sander or multi-sander |
| Sand the surface completely flat with little removal | Orbital sander |
| A lot of removal, old layers of paint | Belt sander, only with experience |

### Orbital sander

The eccentric sander combines rotation and vibration. It removes quickly and leaves an even grinding pattern without any obvious circles. It is the most versatile sander for most DIY enthusiasts. A 125 mm diameter plate and a suction opening for the vacuum cleaner are standard.

### Orbital sander

It swings in small movements with a rectangular plate and is suitable for corners and flat surfaces with little removal.

### Delta sander

The triangular plate goes into corners and narrow places such as window frames and rungs.

## Step by step: sanding a wooden board

1. **Check the surface:** Fill holes, cracks or dents with wood putty beforehand and allow to dry.
2. **Hatching with pencil:** When sanding, a light pencil hatching on the surface shows where sanding has already taken place. When the line disappears, the stage is completed.
3. **Coarse sanding:** With P80 or P100 evenly over the entire surface. When sanding by hand, **always sand in the direction of the grain**. Scratches appear across the grain and become visible later.
4. **Remove dust:** With a brush, vacuum cleaner or a slightly damp cloth.
5. **Medium cut:** P120 to P150, again with pencil hatching.
6. **Water (optional, for water-based paints and stains):** Lightly moisten the surface and allow to dry. Any raised wood fibers are removed during the next sanding; This means the surface remains smooth after painting.
7. **Fine polish:** P180 to P240.
8. **Thoroughly remove dust:** Vacuum and wipe with a tack cloth.
9. **Break edges slightly:** Slightly round off sharp edges with fine paper. Paint and oil adhere better there and the edges are less sensitive to impact.

## Step by step: Intermediate sanding of paint

1. **Allow the paint to dry completely,** according to the manufacturer's instructions.
2. **Walk lightly over the surface with P280 to P400** or a fine sanding sponge. The only aim is to smooth out dust inclusions and rough spots and to roughen the surface.
3. **Do not sand through,** especially on edges.
4. **Dedust** and apply the next coat.

## Sanding filler areas on the wall

1. **Let the filler dry completely.**
2. **Sand with sanding grid P120** on a sanding block or sanding board in circular movements until the area is flush with the wall.
3. **Refinish with P180**.
4. **Use grazing light:** A flashlight held flat over the wall shows every unevenness.

## Dust: health and cleanliness

Grinding dust is not only annoying, it can be harmful to your health. Wood dust, especially from oak and beech, is considered carcinogenic; old paint can contain harmful substances such as lead.

- **Use extraction:** Connect the machine to a vacuum cleaner, if possible with a fine dust filter.
- **Wear respiratory protection:** at least FFP2, better FFP3 for hardwood and old paint.
- **Work outside** if possible, or ventilate the room well and seal doors.
- **Wet mop** instead of sweeping to avoid stirring up dust.
- **Be careful with very old paintwork:** Lead-based paint can be found in old buildings. If you suspect something, do not sand dry, but instead strip it or ask a specialist company.

## Common mistakes

| Error | Episode | Better |
|---|---|---|
| Skip grain levels | Deep scratches visible under paint | Skip at most one step |
| Sand by hand across the grain | Visible cross scratches | Always in the direction of the grain |
| Too much pressure on the machine | Dents, burnt paper, slower | Let the machine work |
| Reuse added paper | Lubricate instead of grinding | Change paper |
| Don't dust off | Dust in the paint | Vacuum and tack cloth |
| Without respiratory protection | Health risk | FFP2 or FFP3 |

## Grind metal and rust

Metal is also sanded before painting. Remove loose paint and rust with a wire brush or P80 to P120 corundum paper until bare metal is visible. Then smooth with P180 to P240 so that no deep grooves show through the paint. It is important to prime immediately: bare sheet steel begins to rust again after a short time, especially in humid air. Wipe off dust and sanding residue beforehand with thinner or rubbing alcohol. Grinding metal produces sharp burrs and sparks on high-speed machines; Gloves and safety glasses are included.

## Buy suitable sandpaper and use it for longer

Grinding wheels for machines are usually attached with Velcro. Make sure that the hole pattern on the disc matches the sanding plate, otherwise the suction will hardly work. There are discs with eight holes, with six plus one hole, and discs with many small holes that fit multiple plates. A clogged paper does not have to be thrown away immediately: a cleaning pen made of natural rubber, held against the running window, loosens resin and paint residues. Store sandpaper flat and dry because wet paper curls and the grit comes off more easily.

## Wet grinding

Wet sanding is used for very smooth painted surfaces. There is waterproof silicon carbide paper in fine grain sizes starting at around P400. The paper is dipped in water with a drop of detergent and applied with light pressure over the completely hardened layer of paint. The water binds the dust and prevents the paper from clogging. Wet sanding is unsuitable on raw wood because the water causes the fibers to swell.

## Sanding wooden floors

Parquet and floorboards are processed with large roller or disc sanders, which can be rented from a tool rental company. You start with very coarse grit sizes from around P24 to P40 and work your way up to P100 or P120 in several passes, using an edge sander on the edges. Caution is necessary with finished parquet with a thin wear layer: it can only withstand a few sanding operations, and sanding too deeply exposes the carrier layer. If you are unsure, have the first floor sanded by a specialist company.

## Practical example: Preparing an old wooden door

A room door with old paint that has chipped off in some places needs to be repainted.

1. Door unhinged and placed on stands, fittings unscrewed.
2. Loose areas of paint removed with a spatula.
3. Edges of the chips sanded with P120 so that no step remains.
4. Entire surface matted with P180 and the eccentric sander, profiles with sanding sponge.
5. Vacuumed, wiped down with a tack cloth.
6. Defects primed, then two coats of varnish with intermediate sanding P320.

Thanks to clean transitions, the former spalling can no longer be seen.

## Frequently asked questions

### What grit size do I need before oiling?

For softwood usually up to P180, for hardwood up to P220 or P240. Wood that is sanded too finely absorbs less oil.

### Do I have to completely remove old paint?

No, if it sticks firmly. For overpainting, sanding with P180 to P240 is sufficient. Only peeling paint has to come off.

### How often do I have to change the sandpaper?

As soon as it becomes clogged or barely wears away. More often when sanding varnish than when sanding raw wood.

### Can I sand walls with an orbital sander?

For small filler areas, yes, with fine grain and suction. There are special long-neck sanders for entire walls.

## Checklist

- Grits provided for all levels.
- Machine connected to the suction.
- Wear respiratory protection, safety glasses and hearing protection.
- Pencil hatching for each stage.
- Sanded in the direction of the grain.
- Thoroughly dusted between steps and at the end.

## Conclusion

Good sanding follows a clear logic: start roughly, go finer in stages, don't skip any grit too far and work in the direction of the grain. Choose an eccentric sander for large areas, hand sander or sponges for edges and profiles, connect the extraction system and protect yourself from dust. Then the oil, varnish and paint adhere evenly and the surface feels the way it should look.
