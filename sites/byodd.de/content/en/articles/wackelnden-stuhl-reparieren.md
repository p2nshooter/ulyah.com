A chair that creaks and gives way with every movement is not only uncomfortable, but can also be dangerous if a connection suddenly gives way. Most wooden chairs wobble because the glue in the joints has dissolved over time. The wood works through changing humidity, and the constant strain of sitting and rocking loosens the connections. The good news: In most cases, a wobbly chair can be permanently repaired if you do it right.

This guide explains how to find the cause, loosen connections, clean and re-glue, fix screwed chairs and correct wobbly chairs with uneven legs.

## Step 1: Find the cause

Place the chair on a flat floor and check:

| Observation | Probable cause |
|---|---|
| Chair rocks but all connections are tight | One leg is shorter or the floor is uneven |
| Legs or frames can be moved against each other | Glue connection dissolved |
| Screws or metal angles wobbling | Screws loose or torn out |
| Backrest wobbles | Connection between backrest and seat or rear legs loosened |
| Crack in the wood | Breakage, may require major repairs |

Gently press the individual parts together and listen for any creaking. Mark loose connections with masking tape.

## How chairs are connected

- **Tenon connection:** A tenon at the end of a frame is inserted into a hole (tenon hole) in the leg and is glued. Typical of high-quality solid wood chairs.
- **Wooden dowel connection:** Round wooden dowels connect the parts. Common in series furniture.
- **Screw connection:** Screws, threaded bolts or metal brackets, often found on modern or kit chairs.
- **Corner blocks:** Small wooden blocks in the corners under the seat, screwed and glued, stabilize the frames.

## Step 2: Repair Loose Glue Joint (The Right Method)

Simply squirting glue into the gap usually doesn't last long because old glue hardly sticks to old glue. For a permanent repair, the connection must be disassembled and cleaned.

### Material and tools

- Wood glue (white glue, waterproof for chairs in kitchens or outside).
- Rubber mallet.
- Screw clamps or tension belts (belt tensioners).
- Chisel or cutter knife, sandpaper.
- Warm water and vinegar (to loosen old glue), cloths.
- Masking tape, pencil.

### Procedure

1. **Mark parts:** Number each part and connection with masking tape so everything falls into place when reassembled.
2. **Remove corner blocks and screws,** if present.
3. **Remove the connection:** Carefully knock apart with a rubber mallet and a wooden block as an intermediate layer. Do not use force: Do not break connections that are still strong.
4. **Remove old glue:** Scrape from tenons, dowels and out of the holes with a chisel, knife or sandpaper. Dissolve stubborn residues with warm water and a little vinegar. The new glue must adhere to bare wood.
5. **Let dry** if working with water.
6. **Check fit:** If the tenon sits loosely in the hole, it can be doubled: glue on a thin piece of veneer or wood shavings, let it dry, sand it to size. For wooden dowels, insert new, slightly thicker dowels or drill out the hole.
7. **Apply glue:** Spread thinly onto tenons and into the holes using a brush or wood chip.
8. **Assemble** in the order marked, tapping together with a rubber mallet.
9. **Pressing:** Pull the chair together using screw clamps or a tension belt around the legs. Check that it is straight and not distorted (measure diagonals).
10. **Wipe off any glue residue** immediately with a damp cloth; dried glue prevents oil or varnish from being absorbed evenly into these areas.
11. **Let it dry,** at least the pressing time stated on the glue, preferably 24 hours, before putting any weight on the chair.

## Quick solution if disassembly is not possible

If one connection cannot be disassembled without damaging others:

1. Use a thin drill to drill a small hole diagonally into the joint in an inconspicuous place.
2. Squeeze in wood glue with a syringe.
3. Press the connection and let it dry.
4. Seal the hole with wood filler.

This usually lasts for a while, but not as long-lasting as a cleanly re-glued connection.

## Bolted chairs

- **Tighten screws:** This is often enough. Don't over-twist.
- **Torn out screw holes:** Fill with wood glue and toothpicks or a wooden dowel, let dry, pre-drill again. See [Use Wood Screws Correctly](/anleitungen/holzschrauben-vorbohren-und-senken/).
- **Threaded bolts with drive-in nuts:** Tighten; If loosening frequently, use medium-strength screw locking material.
- **Metal angles** in the corners under the seat can provide additional stability.

## Corner blocks

If corner blocks are missing or are loose, cut new ones out of hardwood, glue them to the contact surfaces and screw them to the frames. They are a very effective reinforcement.

## Tilting chairs: unequal legs

If all connections are tight but the chair rocks:

1. **Check on a safely flat surface** (e.g. a large table top).
2. **Shorter leg:** Place self-adhesive or fold-in felt gliders or furniture gliders of appropriate thickness under the short leg.
3. **Short the longer leg:** Only if necessary and with care: Place the chair on the surface so that it does not tip over (place the short legs underneath), then use a flat pencil on a block to mark a line at the same height on all legs. Shorten along the line with a fine saw, breaking edges.

## Care and prevention

- **Don't tip over:** This puts a lot of strain on the rear connections.
- **Felt gliders** protect floor and chair legs.
- **Even room climate:** Strong fluctuations in humidity make wood work.
- **Check screws regularly.**

## Why glue connections give way

Wood absorbs moisture from the air and releases it again. It swells and shrinks, significantly more across the grain than in the longitudinal direction. In a mortise and tenon connection, these two directions meet: the tenon works differently than the leg in which it is inserted. Over the years, with dry heating air in winter and humid air in summer, the glue joint is repeatedly stretched and compressed until it tears. There is also the leverage effect when sitting and rocking. A chair is therefore one of the most heavily loaded pieces of furniture in the household.

## Old chairs with bone glue

Older and antique chairs are often glued with glutin glue, also called bone or hide glue. It is processed warm and can be removed with heat and moisture, which restorers appreciate. What this means for you is that modern white glue does not adhere well to leftover glutin glue. Therefore, remove the remains thoroughly, preferably with warm water, or for valuable pieces, use glutin glue, which is also available in specialist shops as ready-to-use liquid glue. This means the connection can be repaired later.

## Insert the tension belt correctly

A ratchet strap is often better than a screw clamp for chairs because it pulls all four legs together at the same time. Place it around the legs at the height of the frames and pad the corners with cardboard or felt so that there are no pressure points. Tighten the strap evenly until the joints are closed, but not so tight that glue oozes out in large quantities. Then measure the diagonals of the seat: if they are the same length, the chair is at an angle. If they differ, move the belt a little until both dimensions are correct.

## Weave and seat

With chairs with Viennese weave, it is often not the frame that is the problem, but the seat. Machine-made braid, which is inserted into a groove with a wedge cord, can be renewed yourself with a little skill: lever out the old cord, clean the groove, insert new, moistened braid and fasten it with glue and new cord. Hand-woven seats, in which the tube runs through individual holes in the frame, are a job for chair weavers.

## Practical example: Four kitchen chairs made of beech

Four beech chairs from the eighties all wobble, two of them severely. The frames are glued into the legs with wooden dowels, and there are screwed corner blocks in the corners.

1. **Mark:** Number each piece with masking tape.
2. **Dismantling:** Corner blocks unscrewed, frames carefully knocked out of the legs with a rubber mallet and wooden block.
3. **Remove old glue:** Clean the dowels and holes with a knife, chisel and sandpaper. Two dowels were torn and were replaced.
4. **Re-glue:** Apply a thin layer of D3 wood glue, put everything together, stretch it around the four legs with a tension belt and check the diagonals.
5. **Corner blocks:** Re-glued and secured with new screws, torn-out holes filled with toothpicks and glue.
6. **Dry:** 24 hours.

After the repair, all four chairs are secure. Effort: one weekend for four chairs.

## Checklist

- Cause of shaking determined.
- Parts marked before disassembly.
- Old glue completely removed.
- Loose tenons doubled or dowels replaced.
- Dried under pressure, diagonals checked.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which glue is the right one?

White glue (PVAc wood glue) for interiors. For chairs in damp rooms or outdoors, waterproof wood glue (stress group D3 or D4).

### Can I use superglue?

Not for wood connections. It is brittle and does not fill joints.

### What do I do if I have a broken chair leg?

Clean breaks can be glued together and, if necessary, reinforced with a wooden dowel. For splintering fractures, a specialist company or a new leg is the better choice.

### How long does the glue have to dry before I use the chair?

The pressing time for white glue is usually 15 to 30 minutes at room temperature, but the connection is only fully resilient after around 24 hours. Do not work in a cold room: white glue does not set properly below around ten degrees.

### Can I repair a high chair in the same way?

Tighten loose screws: yes. Broken or newly glued supporting parts on a high chair, on the other hand, are delicate because children climb, swing and push themselves off in them. If in doubt, it is better to order spare parts from the manufacturer or replace the chair, because safety comes before economy.

## Conclusion

A wobbling chair usually has loose glue connections. The only long-term solution is to dismantle the connection, thoroughly remove old glue, double up loose tenons, re-glue and let it dry under pressure. You can strengthen screwed chairs by tightening them and repairing torn holes, and rocking chairs with gliders or carefully shortening them.
