Screws seem to be the simplest connection in wood construction: attach, screw in, done. In practice, boards split at the edge, screw heads stick out or sink too deep, screws tear out of chipboard or don't hold because they are too short. With a little basic knowledge of screw types, pre-drilling and countersinking, wood connections can be made cleanly and permanently.

This guide explains the most important wood screws, when and how to pre-drill, how countersinking works, what length you need and how to solve typical problems.

## Types of screws for wood

| screw | Features | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Chipboard screw (universal screw) | Continuous or partial thread, countersunk head, often with drill point | Furniture construction, chipboard, softwood |
| Partially threaded screw | Thread only in the front part | Pulls two components together tightly |
| Fully threaded screw | Thread over the entire length | Reinforcements, chipboard if no tensile effect is necessary |
| Button head or pan head screw | Head is up | Fittings, metal sheets, hinges with unrecessed holes |
| Construction screw | Large diameter, often disc head | Timber construction, carports, terraces |
| Stainless steel screw | Rustproof | Outdoor areas, damp rooms, woods containing tannic acid such as oak |

**Drive:** Torx (TX) transmits the power best. Pozidriv (PZ) is widely used. See [Buy and use cordless screwdriver](/anleitungen/akkuschrauber-kaufen-und-benutzen/).

## The right length

**Rule of thumb:** The screw should be at least twice as deep in the lower component as the thickness of the upper component, slightly more for softwood.

**Example:** Screwing an 18 mm board onto a squared timber: 18 mm + 2 × 18 mm = 54 mm, i.e. a 4 × 50 or 4 × 60 mm screw.

Make sure that the screw **does not come out on the other side.** Be sure to measure for shelves and furniture fronts.

## The right diameter

| Application | Typical diameter |
|---|---|
| Hinges, fittings, thin back panels | 3 to 3.5mm |
| Furniture construction, shelves | 4mm |
| Squared timbers, frames | 5mm |
| Constructions, pergolas | 6mm and more |

## When do I have to pre-drill?

Pre-drilling prevents the wood from splitting and ensures that the screw sits straight. It is necessary:

- **In hardwood** such as oak, beech, ash.
- **Near edges and board ends** (less than about 5 screw diameters apart).
- **For thin strips.**
- **For screws with a diameter of around 5 mm or more** in solid wood.
- **In the upper component,** if it should be pulled tightly to the lower one.

In softwood with sufficient edge clearance and with chipboard screws with a drill point, you can often work without pre-drilling.

## Arrange screws correctly

How many screws a connection has and where they are located determines whether it holds:

- **At least two screws per connection.** A single screw acts as a pivot point around which the component can rotate.
- **Staggered rather than in a line.** Multiple screws on the same grain line act like a wedge, splitting the board along the grain.
- **There is more distance to the end of the board than to the long edge.** This is where the fibers end and the wood splits particularly easily.
- **Do not screw wide solid wood boards rigidly across the entire width,** because wood works across the grain. More about this in [Types of wood for DIY enthusiasts](/anleitungen/holzarten-fuer-heimwerker/).
- **Tighten in turn:** For the frame, first just put all the screws in place, align the component and then tighten them one after the other so that nothing is distorted.

## This is how you drill properly

There are two holes:

| bore | Where | diameter |
|---|---|---|
| **Through hole (head/shaft hole)** | In the upper component | For example, the outside diameter of the screw so that the screw does not grab there and pull the components together |
| **Core drilling (pre-drilling)** | In the lower component | About 60 to 70% of the screw diameter in hardwood, 50% in softwood |

**Example for a 4 mm screw in beech:** 4 mm through hole in the top board, 2.5 to 3 mm core hole in the bottom board.

### Procedure

1. Align components and fix them with screw clamps.
2. Mark the drilling point.
3. Through hole through the upper component.
4. Core drilling into the lower component to the required depth (adhesive tape on the drill as a mark).
5. Lower (see below).
6. Screw in the screw.

## Lowering: So that the head sits flush

Countersunk screws have a cone-shaped head. In order for it to be flush with the surface, the hole needs a cone-shaped extension, the **countersink**.

- **Countersink** (90°) on the drill, with high speed and little pressure.
- **Combined wood drills with countersinks** drill and countersink in one operation.
- **Self Countersunk Chipboard Screws** have milling ribs under the head and self-sink into softwood and chipboard.

Select the depth so that the head is exactly flush or is slightly lower.

### Cover screws

For visible furniture surfaces:

- **Sink deeper** and close the hole with a **wooden plug** (tenon) made of the same wood, glue it and sand it flush.
- **Wood putty** in matching color.
- **Cover caps** made of plastic for chipboard furniture.

## Connect concealed: from below, at an angle or with angles

Not every screw has to go through the visible surface. Three ways to take them out of sight completely:

- **Screw from below or inside:** Table tops, shelves and tops of chests of drawers are attached from the bottom using a frame or strip. The length is particularly critical here: Measure the thickness of the plate and allow for a few millimeters of safety so that no point comes through at the top.
- **Screwing at an angle with a pocket hole:** A drilling template guides the drill flat and at an angle into the back of a component. The screw then pulls it tightly to the neighboring part. This creates stable frames and bodies without visible heads. The template specifies the angle and drilling depth; use the appropriate screws.
- **Metal angles and connectors:** Placed in corners on the inside, they are hardly visible and can be removed again. For table tops made of solid wood, choose angles with an elongated hole so that the top can work.

## Combine screws and wood glue

Glue and screws complement each other: the glue connects longitudinal wood to longitudinal wood very firmly, the screws press the parts together as long as it dries, thus replacing the screw clamps. To achieve this:

1. **Apply glue thinly and evenly** to one of the two surfaces. Glue does not stick well to end grain because the open fibers absorb it; the screws are there.
2. **Tighten the screws** until some glue comes out evenly along the joint.
3. **Do not smear any glue that comes out,** but remove it immediately with a damp cloth or, when dried, remove it with a chisel. Glue residue will not absorb oil or stain later.

If you don't want screws in the finished furniture, unscrew them after they have dried and close the holes with plugs.

## Screws in end grain and chipboard

- **End grain** (board end) holds screws poorly. Use longer screws or insert a wooden dowel crosswise into which the screw fits.
- **Chipboard:** Use special chipboard screws, do not screw too close to the edge, do not overtighten. Screws hold poorly in the edges of chipboard; It is better to use connecting fittings or dowels there.

## Repair stripped screw holes

| Method | Procedure |
|---|---|
| Toothpicks or matches | Coat with wood glue, insert into the hole, cut off flush, let dry, screw again |
| Bigger screw | Use a slightly thicker or longer screw if there is space |
| wooden dowel | Drill out the hole, glue in wooden dowels, saw off flush, pre-drill again |
| Move | Move the fitting minimally if possible |

When it comes to hinges on cabinet doors, the toothpick method is often the quickest solution. Refer to [Adjust Cabinet Door and Drawer](/anleitungen/schranktuer-und-schublade-einstellen/).

## Common mistakes

| Error | Episode |
|---|---|
| Not pre-drilled into hardwood or edges | Wood splits |
| Core hole too large | Screw doesn't hold |
| No through hole | Components are not pulled together, gaps remain |
| Screw too long | Tip comes out of view |
| Not lowered | Head over |
| Galvanized screws in oak exterior | Black discoloration due to tannic acid |

## Practical example: A bench made of oak

For a bench, 30 mm thick oak boards are screwed onto a frame made of squared oak wood. Oak is hard and tends to split at the ends of the boards. In addition, their tannic acid reacts with steel.

1. **Screws:** Stainless steel countersunk screws 5 × 70mm so there are no black spots.
2. **Through Hole:** 5mm in 30mm seat board.
3. **Core hole:** 3.5 mm in the squared timber, about 45 mm deep, marked with adhesive tape on the drill.
4. **Countersink:** Using a countersink, deep enough to accommodate a 10mm wooden plug.
5. Screw in **screws**, clutch to medium level.
6. **Stoppers** made of oak tapped in with wood glue, sawn off flush and sanded after drying.

The screws are invisible, nothing is split, and the bench is a continuous wooden surface after oiling.

## Checklist

- Type of screw and material suitable for the wood and location.
- Length calculated according to rule of thumb.
- Through and core drilling prepared.
- Lowering to match the head.
- Visible screws covered with plugs or putty.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I reuse wood screws?

Yes, if the thread and drive are undamaged. But it doesn't hold as well in the same hole.

### Screws or nails?

Screws hold tension better and can be loosened. Nails are faster and are often sufficient under shear stress.

### Do I have to pre-drill chipboard screws with a drill bit?

Usually not in softwood and chipboard. In hardwood and on edges anyway.

### Why does my connection squeak or creak?

The components often rub against each other because the screw does not pull them together tightly. A through hole in the upper part helps.

### What does the statement 4 × 50 mean on the package?

The first number is the thread diameter, the second is the length, both in millimeters. For countersunk head screws, the head counts towards the length, but for screws with a resting head such as button head screws, it does not count. This is important if you have to make tight calculations, for example when screwing through a frame from below.

### What to do if the head is pulled through a thin plate?

With thin back walls or soft panels, there is often not enough material under a countersunk head. Use screws with flat, wide heads or place a washer under them and set the torque coupling lower so that the head just rests. If the hole is already frayed, replace the screw a little next to it.

### Further questions from practice

**What do I do with a loose screw?** If the head is damaged: place a rubber band between the bit and the screw, this sometimes provides enough hold. Otherwise, use a screw extractor that reaches into a small pre-drilled hole.

**How ​​long do screws last outdoors?** Galvanized screws rust over time, especially on edges. Stainless steel (A2, A4 on the coast) lasts virtually indefinitely.

**Should I lubricate screws?** In hardwood, a little soap or wax on the threads will help screw in the screw more easily and put less strain on the wood.

## Conclusion

Select the screw according to material, length and load, pre-drill into hardwood, on edges and for thick screws, with a through hole in the upper component and a core hole in the lower component. Clean countersunk screws, cover visible heads with plugs or putty, and repair ripped holes with wood glue and toothpicks or a dowel. This way your wood connections will hold tight and look neat.
