The top of the radiator remains cold even though the thermostat is turned up, there is a gurgling and hissing noise in the pipes, or individual rooms no longer get really warm: these are typical signs of air in the heating system. Air collects at the highest points of the radiators and prevents warm water from reaching the entire area. This costs warmth, comfort and energy.

Bleeding radiators only takes a few minutes and is one of the easiest household maintenance tasks. These instructions explain when it is necessary, how to do it step by step, how you can then check the pressure of the heating system and what you should pay attention to in apartment buildings.

## How you can recognize air in the radiator

- **Cold at the top, warm at the bottom:** The upper part of the radiator stays cool.
- **Grumbling and hissing** in radiators or pipes.
- **Uneven heat** despite the thermostat valve being fully turned on.
- **After working on the heating** or after refilling water.
- **At the beginning of the heating season.**

If the radiator is cold everywhere, the cause could also be a stuck thermostat valve or a problem with the heating system.

## Why air gets into the heater at all

Hardly any heating system remains completely air-free. Freshly filled tap water contains dissolved gases that bubble out when heated and rise to the top. This is why a particularly large amount of air appears after refilling or after working on the system. There are also tiny leaks in screw connections and valves. If the system pressure is too low, air from outside can enter from high-mounted radiators instead of leaving. This leads to a simple rule of thumb: venting once in the fall is normal. However, if you have to do it again every few weeks, the air is just the symptom and a specialist company should look for the actual cause.

## What you need

- **Ventilation key** (square key, costs a few euros at the hardware store). Some valves can be opened with a flathead screwdriver.
- **Container** (cup, small bowl) and a **cloth** to catch any escaping water.
- Possibly gloves as the water can be hot.

## Where the vent valve is located on your radiator

Not every radiator looks the same and the valve cannot always be found at first glance:

- **Panel radiator** (smooth or profiled front, most common today): at the top of the side, usually opposite the thermostatic valve. On models with a grille, it is sometimes located under a small cap.
- **Bathroom and towel radiators:** at the top of one of the two vertical pipes, often as a small screw on the front or on the side under a cover cap.
- **Sectional radiators and tube radiators** in old buildings: at the upper corner of the outer section, often with a slot instead of a square.
- **Convectors in floor ducts or cladding:** difficult to access. A look at the documents or asking the heating engineer can help here.

Many valves have a small outlet opening on the side. Pay attention to where it is pointing and hold the cup and cloth right there; On some models, the valve head can be turned so that the opening points downwards. Take a metal bleeder key: Plastic keys easily deform and slip on tight valves.

## Step 1: Preparation

1. **Turn all thermostatic valves** on the radiators all the way up (level 5).
2. **Let the heater run** until the radiators are warm. This causes the air to collect at the top.
3. **Turn off the circulation pump,** if possible, about an hour before venting to allow the air to settle. With floor heating, this is done on the device; With central heating in apartment buildings, this is usually not possible and not necessary.

## Step 2: Vent

1. **Find the vent valve:** It is located at the top end of the radiator, usually opposite the thermostatic valve, as a small square or slot.
2. **Hold the cloth and container** under the valve.
3. **Open the valve slowly:** Turn the key approximately half a turn counterclockwise. Don't turn it out completely!
4. **Let air escape:** It hisses. Wait until there is no more air coming in and water comes out evenly.
5. **Close the valve** as soon as only water comes out, clockwise, hand tight.
6. **Next radiator:** In houses with multiple floors, usually start at the bottom and work your way up, or start with the radiator furthest from the heater. Manufacturer recommendations or instructions from the heating engineer take precedence.

## Step 3: Check pressure and refill water

When venting, some water escapes and the pressure in the system drops.

### Read pressure

There is a **manometer on the heating system (heater, boiler or system in the basement). For single and two-family houses, the usual operating pressure is usually between around 1.0 and 2.0 bar when cold, depending on the height of the building. Many pressure gauges have a green area or marking.

### Interpret the target value correctly

The appropriate pressure depends primarily on how high the top radiator is above the heat generator. There is therefore no one value that is correct for every system. In addition to the display pointer, many pressure gauges have a second, usually red, marker pointer that the heating engineer has set to the target pressure; Sometimes the value is also written on a sticker on the thermal bath or in the maintenance log. If possible, read the pressure when the system is cold, because warm water expands and causes the pointer to rise. A slight increase when heating up is therefore normal. Write down the cold value after bleeding and compare it in the following weeks: This way you can see early on whether the pressure is gradually falling.

### Refill water

If the pressure is too low:

1. **Observe the heater's instructions.** Many devices have a built-in filling tap.
2. Connect **filling hose** to the filling and draining tap (KFE tap) and to a water pipe, first fill with water so that no air gets into the system.
3. **Open taps slowly** until the target pressure is reached, then close.
4. **Bleed again** if air has entered.

**Note:** In many heaters, only treated water can be refilled or only in limited quantities to avoid limescale and corrosion. If in doubt, ask the heating engineer.

## Step 4: Control

- **Turn the heater back on** (also the pump).
- **After some time** check whether all radiators heat up evenly.
- Set **thermostats** back to the desired level. See heating tips in [Preventing mold on the wall](/anleitungen/schimmel-an-der-wand-entfernen-und-vorbeugen/).

## If the problem remains

| problem | Possible cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Radiator stays completely cold | Thermostat valve is stuck | Unscrew the thermostat head and move the valve pin slightly |
| Constantly new air | Leaks, pressure problems, defective breathers | Heating engineer |
| Some rooms never get warm | Hydraulic balancing is missing | Heating engineer: hydraulic balancing |
| Water drips from the valve | Vent valve seal | Tighten the valve slightly or have it replaced |
| Pressure keeps falling | Leak or defective expansion tank | Specialist company |

### Thermostat valve is stuck

After summer, the valve pin under the thermostat head sometimes gets stuck:

1. Turn the thermostat head to level 5 and unscrew (cap nut or clip).
2. The small metal pin should push in easily and come out on its own.
3. If it is stuck, gently move it with pliers until it moves freely.
4. Put the thermostat head back on.

## Underfloor heating

Underfloor heating systems are usually vented at the heating circuit distributor. This is more complex and should be done according to instructions or by a specialist company.

## Rental apartment and apartment building

- Tenants are generally allowed to vent radiators in their own apartment themselves.
- **The landlord or property management is usually responsible for the central heating** in the basement (pressure, refilling). Report any problems.
- **Floor heating systems** (gas boilers in the apartment) often have a maintenance contract. Pressure testing and refilling according to instructions are usually permitted; If you are unsure, ask the landlord.

## Practical example: A flat with a gas boiler

In an old building with gas floor heating, two radiators upstairs remain cold at the beginning of the heating season and there is a gurgling noise in the bathroom. This is how the problem was solved:

1. Turn all thermostat valves to level 5 and let the heating run for half an hour.
2. According to the instructions, the heater was switched to standby mode so that the pump stopped and waited for an hour.
3. Use the bleed key to first bleed the radiator in the bathroom, then in the living room and finally in the bedroom. At the living room there was a breath of air for almost ten seconds.
4. Pressure read on the thermal bath: 0.8 bar, according to the sticker the required value is 1.3 bar.
5. Water was slowly added using the built-in filling tap until 1.3 bar was reached.
6. Switched the heater back on, checked all radiators after an hour: evenly warm.

Because the pressure remained stable in the following weeks, no specialist company was necessary.

## When would you rather call the specialist company?

| situation | Why not carry on yourself |
|---|---|
| Vent valve cannot be turned | If you force it, it can break off and the heating water will leak |
| Water leaks from screw connections even though everything is closed | Seal or valve defective |
| When heating up, pressure rises into the red zone | Have the expansion vessel and safety valve checked |
| Radiator should be removed to paint the wall | Shutting off, emptying and refilling require experience |
| Several radiators suddenly no longer heat up at the same time | The cause is more likely to be with the pump, control or heat generator |

If the valve comes out too far and water is running: Hold the cloth firmly over the opening with your glove, immediately put the valve back on and turn it clockwise. If this doesn't work, turn off the heating and call the heating engineer's emergency service or the building management.

## Common mistakes

| Error | Episode |
|---|---|
| Valve turned completely out | Water splashes, valve difficult to put back in |
| Pressure not checked after bleeding | Heating switches off or heats poorly |
| Refilled too much water | Safety valve opens |
| Thermostats not turned up | Air does not collect at the top |
| No container under the valve | Water stains on floor and wall |

## Checklist

- Bleed key, cloth and cup ready.
- Thermostat turned up, heating warm.
- Pump switched off if possible.
- Valve only opened half a turn.
- Pressure checked and refilled if necessary according to instructions.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should I bleed?

At the beginning of the heating season and when there are signs of air. For some systems it is rarely necessary.

### Is the water in the radiator dangerous?

It can be hot and dirty. Wear gloves and do not drink the water.

### Do I have to turn off the heating?

The circulation pump ideally yes, but this is usually not possible with central heating in apartment buildings; then simply vent carefully.

### Further questions from practice

**Are there automatic vents?** Yes, quick vents or automatic radiator vents allow air to escape automatically. They are screwed in instead of the vent valve. In systems with frequent air problems, a specialist company can retrofit them.

**Why does brown water come out?** Heating water is often discolored due to rust and deposits. This is mostly normal. If there is mud or a strong smell, contact a specialist garage.

**Is a cold radiator a problem in summer?** No, as long as the heating is not running. But at the beginning of the heating season, check whether the thermostat valve is working.

**What does it mean if nothing comes out when you open it?** If neither air nor water comes out, the system pressure is often too low, especially for radiators on the top floor. Close the valve again, check the pressure in the system, refill if necessary and only then vent again. If it remains dry, the valve bore may be dirty; The specialist company will clarify this.

## Conclusion

If the radiators at the top remain cold or gurgle, venting helps: turn up the thermostat, leave the heating running, switch off the pump if possible, slowly open the vent valve with the key until water comes out, and close it again. Then check the system pressure and refill if necessary according to the instructions. If the problem persists, it could be due to a stuck valve or a lack of hydraulic balancing.
