Tiles often last for decades, but their joints do not. They turn gray or black, crumble, become saturated with moisture or crack. A bathroom with dark, washed-out grout lines appears unkempt, even if the tiles themselves are still in perfect condition. The good news: Renewing joints is a manageable job that makes the bathroom or kitchen look almost new without having to knock off a single tile.

These instructions explain when joints should be renewed, how mortar and silicone joints work together, which material is suitable and how to remove and re-point old joints.

## Mortar joints and silicone joints: the difference

| Type of joint | Where | material | property |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mortar joint (rigid) | Between the tiles in the area | Cementitious grout or epoxy resin | Hard, not elastic |
| Silicone joint (elastic) | In corners, at transitions to tubs, showers, sinks, floor-wall connections | Sanitary silicone | Elastic, absorbs movements |

There should be **no mortar** in corners and connections, but silicone because the components there move against each other. Mortar would crack there. How to renew silicone joints can be found in [Renew silicone joints](/anleitungen/silikonfugen-erneuern/).

## When do joints need to be renewed?

- **Joints crumble** or can be scratched out with a fingernail.
- **Cracks** in the joints.
- **Permanent discoloration** that cannot be removed even with thorough cleaning.
- **Mold** that has grown into the mortar.
- **Damp spots** behind the tiles caused by leaky joints.

If the joints are only superficially dirty, thorough cleaning first helps (see below).

## The right material

| Grout | Properties | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Cementitious joint mortar (standard) | Cheap, easy to process | Walls and floors in living rooms, kitchens |
| Flex joint mortar | With plastic additives, easily deformable | Floors with underfloor heating, large format tiles, wooden surfaces |
| Water-repellent grout | Less absorbent | Bathrooms, showers |
| Epoxy resin joint | Very hard, chemical and dirt resistant | Showers, kitchen splashbacks, heavily used floors; more difficult to process |

**Note the width of the joint:** For narrow joints (less than 2 to 3 mm) use fine-grained mortar, for wide joints use one with a coarser grain. The package states the appropriate area.

**Color:** Grout comes in many colors. Gray or beige are more forgiving of dirt than white.

## tool

- Joint scraper (carbide) or an oscillating multi-tool with a joint blade.
- Vacuum cleaner.
- Rubber pusher or joint board with rubber pad.
- Joint sponge (coarse-pored sponge) and bucket with clear water.
- Mixing bucket and whisk.
- Joint iron or joint wood for smoothing (optional).
- Safety glasses, respiratory protection (when scraping), gloves.
- Painter's tape to protect adjacent surfaces.

## Step 1: Remove old joints

1. **Protect tiles:** Cover the bathtub, shower tray and fittings.
2. **Scrape out the joints:** Use the joint scraper to scrape out the old joint at least 2 to 3 mm deep, preferably two thirds of the tile thickness. Work evenly and with little pressure to prevent the tile edges from chipping.
3. **It's quicker with the multitool**. Guide the joint sheet in the middle and do not touch the tiles.
4. **Also remove silicone joints** in corners and connections; they will be redrawn later.
5. **Vacuuming:** Thoroughly vacuum dust and mortar residue from the joints.
6. **Check tile edges:** Now re-glue loose tiles.

**Caution with very old tiles:** In buildings built up to the early 1990s, tile adhesives and fillers may contain asbestos. If there is suspicion and before large-scale milling, have a material sample examined.

## Step 2: Preparation

- **Lightly moisten the joints** with a damp sponge, especially with highly absorbent tiles and on hot days. This prevents the mortar from drying out too quickly.
- **Unglazed or porous tiles** (natural stone, cotto) should be treated with a protective impregnation beforehand so that the grout does not discolor them.

## Step 3: Mix the grout

1. Pour water into the bucket, then sprinkle in the powder (mixing ratio according to package instructions).
2. Mix with the mixer on low speed until there are no lumps.
3. Wait for the ripening time, usually a few minutes, then stir briefly again.
4. **Only mix as much** as you can manage in the processing time.

## Step 4: Grouting

1. **Apply mortar:** Use the rubber squeegee to apply a portion of mortar to the tiles.
2. **Work in diagonally:** Pull the slider across the surface at an angle of approximately 45 degrees to the joints and press the mortar firmly into the joints. Working diagonally prevents the slider from pulling the mortar out of the joints.
3. **Remove excess:** Use the vertically held slider to remove excess mortar diagonally from the tiles.
4. **Work in sections:** Grout approximately 1 to 2 m² at a time, then wash.

## Step 5: Wash off

1. **Let it set:** Wait until the mortar “sets”, i.e. becomes matt and no longer gives in to finger pressure. Depending on the product and temperature, this takes 10 to 30 minutes.
2. **Wipe the tiles diagonally to the joint with the grout sponge** damp, wrung out well. Wash the sponge frequently in clean water.
3. **Not too wet:** Too much water washes out the joints and makes them lighter and softer.
4. **Smooth the joints:** If necessary, tighten evenly with jointing wood or a round wooden stick.

## Step 6: Remove cement film

After drying, a gray haze usually remains on the tiles.

- After a few hours, rub with a dry, soft cloth.
- Treat stubborn haze with a **cement haze remover** after complete curing. Attention: Do not use acidic cleaners on acid-sensitive natural stones such as marble.

## Step 7: Silicone joints and curing

- **Note curing time:** Usually 24 hours until it can be walked on, longer until it is fully exposed to water. Pay attention to package instructions.
- **Re-draw silicone joints** in corners and connections when the grout has hardened.
- **Impregnation:** Treat cement joints in showers with a joint impregnation. They become less able to absorb dirt and water.

## Clean joints instead of replacing them

Not every gray joint has to go:

- **Clean regularly** with a pH-neutral cleaner and a joint brush.
- **Treat discoloration** with a special grout cleaner or a paste made from baking soda and water.
- **Treat mold on the surface** with a mold remover. If it has grown deeply, the only solution is to replace it.
- **Joint pen or joint paint** as a cosmetic solution for intact, discolored joints.
- **After showering** dry tiles with a squeegee and ventilate well. This prevents limescale and mold.

## How much grout do I need?

Consumption depends on the tile format, the joint width and the joint depth. Many manufacturers offer calculators, but the calculation can also be done by hand: length plus width of the tile divided by length times width, multiplied by joint width, joint depth and the density of the mortar of roughly 1.6 to 1.8 kilograms per liter. For tiles measuring 20 × 20 centimeters with 3 millimeter wide and 7 millimeter deep joints, this works out to around 0.35 kilograms per square meter. Small format tiles and wide joints need significantly more. Buy some reserve from the same batch to keep the color consistent.

## Epoxy resin joints

Epoxy resin joints are almost waterproof, very hard and resistant to grease and many cleaning agents. But they are more demanding: resin and hardener are mixed and the processing time is short, often just half an hour to three quarters of an hour. The excess must be washed off thoroughly and in a timely manner with water and a special pad or sponge, because hardened residue can hardly be removed from the tiles. Work in small sections, wear gloves, and practice on a small area before grouting an entire shower.

## Temperature and underfloor heating

Grout likes it neither too cold nor too hot. Process it at room and surface temperatures of around 5 to 25 degrees, not in blazing sun and not on warm surfaces. Underfloor heating should be switched off or heavily reduced while grouting is being carried out and only slowly turned back on again a few days later. Otherwise the mortar will dry too quickly, become brittle and crack. The manufacturer gives the exact times.

## Joints in the kitchen

Grease and cooking fumes get stuck in the joints on kitchen back walls. Epoxy resin joints or at least impregnation of the cement joints are worthwhile here. Wipe up spills as soon as they are fresh and occasionally clean the grout lines with a degreasing but mild cleaner and a soft brush.

## Common mistakes

| Error | Episode |
|---|---|
| Old joint scratched out too flat | New joint doesn't stick and is crumbling |
| Mortar in corners instead of silicone | Cracks due to movement |
| Washed off too wet | Bright, soft, sandy joints |
| Washed off too soon | Mortar is pulled out of the joints |
| Washed up too late | Mortar sticks, strong haze |
| Different mixtures | Color differences |

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I put new grout over old?

A thin layer on old joints does not last. The old joint must be removed sufficiently deep.

### How long does a new joint last?

With good care they last for many years, epoxy joints last even longer.

### Which color is easiest to care for?

Medium gray tones or tones that match the tiles. Pure white shows any contamination.

### What do I do with a cracked tile?

Individual tiles can be replaced: scrape out the joints around the tile, carefully smash and remove the tile, remove the adhesive, insert a new tile and grout.

### Further questions from practice

**How long should I not shower after grouting?** Usually at least 24 hours, with some mortars longer. Silicone joints also need 24 hours afterwards. Plan the bath for two to three days.

**Can I only renew individual grout lines?** Yes, but new grout lines often look lighter than old ones. For a uniform image, it is better to renew an entire wall or area.

**Why do new joints become stained?** Uneven wet washing or different mixtures were often used. Work evenly and wring out the sponge well.

**Do I need to clean the tiles before grouting?** Yes. Dust from scraping, soap residue and lime prevent the mortar from sticking to the sides of the joint and make washing up more difficult. Vacuum tiles and joints thoroughly and wipe with a damp cloth, then allow to dry briefly.

## Conclusion

Discolored or crumbling joints can be replaced without changing tiles. Scrape out the old joints sufficiently deeply, vacuum thoroughly, choose the right grout, work it in diagonally with the rubber squeegee and wash it off with a well-wrung out sponge as soon as it has set. Corners and connections get silicone. With impregnation and regular care, the new joints stay bright for a long time.
