Raw wood is beautiful, but sensitive: it absorbs stains, turns gray, swells when exposed to moisture and quickly becomes scratched. A surface treatment protects the wood and at the same time determines how it looks and feels. An oiled dining table looks warm and natural, a lacquered children's table is particularly robust, and a waxed shelf has a silky shine.

These instructions explain the differences between oil, hard wax oil, wax, glaze and varnish, help you choose your piece of furniture and show step by step how to apply and care for each surface.

## The surfaces at a glance

| Treatment | Effect | Look and feel | Protection | Repair |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oil (linseed oil, tung oil, furniture oil) | Penetrates the wood and hardens there | Natural, open-pored, “lighting up” the grain | Medium against water and stains, low against scratches | Very simple, re-oil in places |
| Hard wax oil | Oil penetrates, wax content forms a thin protective layer | Satin matt, of course | Good in everyday life | Simple, can be improved in places |
| Wax (beeswax, carnauba wax) | Lies on the surface | Silky shine, pleasant | Low, sensitive to heat and water | Simple, requires frequent care |
| Glaze | Colored, thin layer, wood structure remains visible | Colored, grain visible | Medium, especially outdoors (UV) | Means |
| Varnish (water-based varnish, synthetic resin varnish) | Closed layer on the surface | Matt to high-gloss, wood appears “sealed” | Highly resistant to water, stains, abrasion | Elaborate, mostly the entire area is new |

## Which treatment for which piece of furniture?

| piece of furniture | Recommendation | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| Solid wood dining table | Hard wax oil | Beautiful, suitable for everyday use, scratches can be repaired locally |
| Coffee table, sideboard | Oil or hard wax oil | Natural look |
| Children's room furniture, toys | Saliva and sweat-proof varnish or oil according to toy standards | Robust and harmless |
| Wooden kitchen worktop | Oil, regularly re-oiled | Food contact, easy to maintain |
| cutting board | Food grade oil | No paint that chips off |
| Wooden bathroom furniture | Varnish or oil specifically suitable for damp rooms | Protection against moisture |
| Wooden floor | Hard wax oil or parquet varnish | Stress from running |
| Garden furniture | Special wood oil for outside or glaze | UV and weather protection |
| Shelves, decorative objects | Wax, oil or varnish to taste | Little strain |

## Pay attention to the type of wood: Not all wood reacts the same way

- **Pine and spruce:** Oil and solvent-based varnishes increase the yellowish tone, and softwood darkens over time anyway. If you like it bright, use a white pigmented oil or a colorless water-based varnish. Remove any resin escaping from branches before coating according to the manufacturer's instructions, otherwise it can penetrate the surface.
- **Oak:** The tannic acid contained forms dark spots with iron. For oak, do not use steel wool, but rather abrasive fleece made of plastic.
- **Teak and other oil-rich tropical woods:** Their own ingredients can interfere with the drying of oil and varnish. Degrease the surface shortly beforehand with a solvent specified by the manufacturer.
- **Beech:** dense and even, absorbs oil easily, but swells significantly when wet. It needs a seamless surface, especially in the kitchen and bathroom.
- **Veneer and multiplex:** The top layer is thin. Only sand finely and without pressure, especially on edges, otherwise you will sand through.

More about the individual woods can be found under [Types of wood for do-it-yourselfers](/anleitungen/holzarten-fuer-heimwerker/).

## Preparation: Sanding is mandatory

Every surface is only as good as the preparation:

1. **Remove old coatings** if they are flaking or if you want to change from varnish to oil. Oil does not penetrate through paint.
2. **Grinding** in steps, for example P80 → P120 → P180. P180 is enough for oil and P220 to P240 for paint. See [Sanding with Sandpaper](/anleitungen/schleifen-mit-schleifpapier-koernung/).
3. **Water** for water-based paints and stains: moisten the wood, let it dry, sand lightly with the last grit.
4. **Thoroughly dust off.**

## Sample, end grain, edges and underside

**Sample:** The color on the can says little about your wood. Sand a leftover piece or a hidden area with the same grit as the surface and apply the agent in the same way. Don't judge until it's dry, as every surface appears darker and shinier when wet. If you combine stain with oil or varnish, the test piece will also show whether the two go together.

**End grain:** The front sides of a panel absorb much more than the surface and therefore become darker. Sand the end grain a level finer and plan an additional thin application instead of working thickly there.

**Edges:** On sharp edges, paint retreats and remains thin. Break edges slightly with sandpaper before painting; this also prevents chipping.

**Bottom:** Treat table tops and shelves on both sides, one coat is usually sufficient on the bottom. If only the top is closed, the wood absorbs moisture unevenly and can warp.

## Step by step: oiling

1. **Apply oil thinly** with a brush, cloth or pad, in the direction of the grain.
2. **Let it absorb:** 15 to 30 minutes, depending on the product. Re-oil areas that look dry quickly.
3. **Thoroughly remove excess:** Use a lint-free cotton cloth to wipe off any oil that has not been absorbed. This is the most important step: excess oil becomes sticky and hardens unevenly.
4. **Let dry:** Depending on the oil, 12 to 24 hours.
5. **Second application:** Repeat after light intermediate sanding with P240 or a fine pad. Two to three applications provide good protection.
6. **Curing:** Oils harden completely over days to weeks. Treat gently during this time.

### Warning: spontaneous combustion

**Cloths soaked in drying oils (linseed oil and many furniture oils) can self-ignite.** After use, let rags lay flat outside to dry or store in a sealed glass or metal container filled with water. Never throw it balled up in the trash can.

## Step by step: Hard wax oil

1. **Apply very thinly** with a brush or spatula, spreading in the direction of the grain.
2. **Let it take effect briefly** according to the manufacturer's instructions.
3. **Remove excess** or massage in with a pad, depending on the product.
4. **Dry,** then second thin coat.

## Step by step: Grow

1. **Pre-oil or seal wood** if it needs more protection, otherwise directly on raw, sanded wood.
2. **Apply wax thinly with a cloth** in circular movements.
3. **Let it dry,** usually 15 to 30 minutes.
4. **Polishing** with a soft cloth or polishing brush to desired shine.

## Step by step: painting

1. **Stir the paint well,** do not shake (air bubbles).
2. **Apply the first coat thinly** with a fine paint roller or a good paint brush. First spread it crosswise, then spread it out in the direction of the grain.
3. **Let dry** according to manufacturer's instructions.
4. **Intermediate sanding** with P280 to P400, remove dust.
5. **Apply second and if necessary third coat**.
6. **Dust-free environment:** Do not paint in a room where sanding has just occurred. Open windows but avoid drafts.

Water-based paints have little odor and dry quickly; Synthetic resin paints are very robust, but smell stronger and take longer.

## Clean tools and dispose of leftovers

Wash brushes and rollers for water-based paints with water immediately after work; for oils and synthetic resin paints, use the cleaning agent that is on the can. For short breaks, wrap your brush or roller airtight in foil. Liquid paint and oil residues do not belong in the drain, but rather in the pollutant collection; Your municipality regulates how dried cans are disposed of. Treat oily brushes and pads like rags, because they can also catch fire.

## Typical error patterns and how to fix them

| Error image | Cause | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Surface still sticks after days | Too much oil, excess not removed | Loosen with a pad and a little fresh oil, wipe everything thoroughly and let it dry again |
| Light, patchy areas after oiling | Glue residue or unevenly sanded areas | Re-sand, completely remove any glue residue, re-oil at certain points |
| Runners and drips in the paint | Applied too thickly, especially on vertical surfaces | Allow to dry, sand flat and apply a thin coat of paint |
| Rough surface after the first water-based paint | Wood fibers have stood up | intermediate sanding; Water beforehand for the next project |
| Grains of dust in the paint | Dust in the air or on the workpiece | After drying, sand finely, next coat in a clean room |
| Milky veil | Too cold or too wet when painting | Work warmer and drier; It remains, sanded and repainted |
| Wax smears and dulls | Applied too thickly | Remove excess with a clean cloth and polish thoroughly |

Work at room temperature, not in direct sunlight or in a cold basement; The minimum temperature for processing is stated on the can.

## Care

| surface | Care |
|---|---|
| oil | Clean with a slightly damp cloth and re-oil every few months to once a year depending on use |
| Hard wax oil | Clean with the manufacturer's care products and refresh stressed areas |
| wax | Dusting, occasional waxing and polishing |
| Lacquer | Wipe with a damp cloth, do not use abrasive cleaners, use a coaster |

**General:** Do not place hot pots directly on wood, wipe up spilled liquids immediately, use felt pads under decoration.

## Practical example: A beech dining table is oiled

An untreated beech table made of glued wood should have a natural surface that is suitable for everyday use.

1. **Sanding:** P100, P150, P180 with the eccentric sander, edges broken by hand.
2. **Water:** Moisten the plate with a damp cloth, dry it and briefly sand it with P180.
3. **First application:** Hard wax oil applied thinly with a spatula, massaged in with a pad after 20 minutes, excess removed completely.
4. **Dry:** 24 hours at room temperature.
5. **Second application:** After light intermediate sanding with a fine pad.
6. **Curing:** Gently used for one week, with coasters and without moisture.

The rags were spread out on the clothesline to dry and then disposed of in the trash.

## Time requirement

| surface | Working hours | Drying until use |
|---|---|---|
| Oil, two to three orders | 2 to 3 hours | 2 to 3 days, full hardness after weeks |
| Hard wax oil, two coats | 2 hours | 1 to 2 days |
| wax | 1 hour | a few hours |
| Varnish, three coats | 3 to 4 hours | 2 to 3 days, full resilience later |

## Checklist

- Treatment chosen to match the furniture.
- Sanded and dusted in stages.
- Applied thinly, excess removed.
- Oily rags dried safely.
- Curing time adhered to.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I apply oil to painted wood?

No. Oil does not penetrate through paint. For an oiled surface, the paint must be completely sanded off.

### Does oil darken wood?

Yes, oil “fires up” the grain and deepens the color. Try it in an inconspicuous area.

### Is linseed oil food safe?

Pure, untreated linseed oil, yes. When using furniture oils, pay attention to the food safety information.

### How many coats of varnish do I need?

Usually two to three for furniture, three for heavily used surfaces such as table tops.

### Further questions from practice

**Can I varnish oiled wood later?** Only after the oil has completely hardened and sanded thoroughly, preferably after consulting the varnish manufacturer. Many varnishes adhere poorly to oily surfaces.

**How ​​does oiled wood become fresh again?** Clean, sand lightly if necessary and apply a thin layer of oil. This is the big advantage of oiled surfaces.

**How ​​do I know that an oil treatment has hardened?** The surface hardly smells of oil and feels dry and not sticky everywhere. The information on the can is a guide; it takes longer in cool rooms and for thick jobs. Until then, use coasters and don't leave anything wet.

## Conclusion

Oil and hard wax oil are ideal for natural, easily repairable surfaces, wax for a silky shine on lightly used furniture, varnish for maximum protection on children's furniture and in damp rooms. Sand carefully, apply all agents thinly, remove any excess when oiling and store oil-soaked rags so that they cannot catch fire. With regular care, the wood will remain beautiful for decades.
