Ciabatta, or *ciabatta* in Italian, is a young bread in the history of baking: it was popularized in Italy in the 1980s as an answer to the French baguette. Its name means "slipper", due to its flat and elongated shape. What makes it special is its very open, moist and shiny crumb, with large, irregular alveoli, and a thin, crispy crust, perfect for sandwiches and for dipping in oil.

The secret is in two elements: a very hydrated dough and a preferment that gives it flavor and strength. This recipe uses a **poolish**, a liquid preferment of Polish origin that was adopted by French and Viennese bakers.

## What is a poolish

A poolish is a mixture of equal parts flour and water, with a minimal pinch of yeast, which is left to ferment for 12 to 16 hours before preparing the final dough. During that time:

- The yeast multiplies slowly.
- Enzymes in flour break down some of the starch into sugars.
- Acids and aromas develop that enrich the flavor.
- Gluten begins to organize.

The result is a bread with more flavor, better structure and that lasts longer than one made with direct yeast.

## Ingredients for two ciabattas

### Poolish (the night before)

| Ingredient | Quantity |
|---|---|
| Breadmaking or strong flour | 200g |
| Water at room temperature | 200g |
| Dry bakery yeast | 0.5 g, a very small pinch |

If you use fresh yeast, 1.5 g, the size of a small chickpea, is enough.

### Final dough

| Ingredient | Quantity |
|---|---|
| All the poolish | 400g |
| Breadmaking or strong flour | 300g |
| water | 175g |
| salt | 10g |
| Dry bakery yeast | 2g |
| Extra virgin olive oil, optional | 10g |

In total, the recipe has 500 g of flour and 375 g of water: 75% hydration.

## Step 1: prepare the poolish

The night before, dissolve the pinch of yeast in the water, add the flour and mix until you obtain a homogeneous paste, like a thick crepe batter. Cover the container and leave it at room temperature for 12 to 16 hours.

The poolish is ready when it has risen, its surface is full of bubbles, it begins to sink slightly in the center and it smells like ferment, with an alcoholic touch. If it has sunk a lot and smells very strong, it has gone a little too far, but it is usually still usable.

## Step 2: mix the dough

In a large bowl, mix the poolish with 150 g of the final dough water and the yeast. Add the flour and mix until no dry flour remains. Cover and let it rest for 20 minutes.

Add the salt and the remaining 25 g of water, little by little, pinching the dough with your fingers to integrate them. If you use oil, add it last.

The dough will be very sticky. It's normal. Do not add flour.

## Step 3: Develop Gluten

Doughs this wet are difficult to knead on the table. The best technique at home is to combine short kneading in the bowl with folds.

1. With your wet hand, grab the dough underneath, stretch it up, and let it fall on top of itself. Repeat for 5 minutes, rotating the bowl.
2. Transfer the dough to a rectangular container greased with a little oil.
3. Every 30 minutes for the first two hours, make a series of folds: With wet hands, lift the dough in the center, let the ends fold under, turn the bowl, and repeat.

With each series, the dough will be more elastic, smooth and full of bubbles.

## Step 4: block fermentation

After folding, let the dough rest, covered, until it has doubled in volume and is very airy, with large bubbles on the surface. At about 24°C, the total time from the end of mixing is usually three hours.

## Step 5: Split Without Degassing

This is the key difference with other breads. The ciabatta **is not formed**: it is divided and manipulated as little as possible so as not to lose the accumulated gas, which will give those large alveoli.

1. Generously flour the table.
2. Sprinkle flour over the dough and carefully tip the bowl over, letting the dough fall under its own weight.
3. Using the floured scraper, gently stretch the dough into a rectangle and cut it into two oblong pieces.
4. Fold each piece in three, like a letter, very gently.
5. Place each piece, seam side down, on a heavily floured cloth or baking paper.

## Step 6: final rest

Let the covered pieces rest for 30 to 45 minutes. Meanwhile, preheat the oven to 240°C with a stone or thick tray in the center and an empty tray at the bottom.

## Step 7: Steam Bake

1. Carefully turn the pieces over, so that the floured side is on top, and stretch them slightly lengthwise to give them their slipper shape.
2. Put them in the oven on the paper.
3. Pour a glass of hot water into the bottom tray and close the oven quickly.
4. Bake for 10 minutes with steam.
5. Remove the pan from the water and bake for another 12 to 15 minutes, until the crust is golden brown.

The steam keeps the surface moist for the first few minutes, allowing the bread to expand as much as possible before the crust forms.

## Step 8: Cool

Let the ciabatta cool on a rack for at least 45 minutes before cutting.

## How to handle very wet doughs

High hydration masses scare many people. These tricks help:

- **Wet hands, not floured**, during folds. Water prevents the dough from sticking without adding flour.
- **Always scraper by hand**, to remove the dough from the table.
- **Lots of flour when dividing**, but only on the outside surface.
- **Definite and fast movements.** The longer you touch the dough, the more it will stick.
- **Patience with the folds.** Gluten develops with time, not force.

## Common problems

**The ciabatta is flat and without alveoli.** The dough has degassed too much when dividing it or the fermentation has been short. Handle as little as possible and wait until the dough is very airy.

**The dough does not hold up and spreads.** There is a lack of gluten development. Make one more set of folds or use a flour with more protein.

**The crust is thick and hard.** There is a lack of steam in the first minutes or baking is too long.

**The crumb is sticky.** The bread needed a few more minutes in the oven or it was cut hot.

## Variants

- **Whole-grain ciabatta:** replace 100 g of the flour in the final dough with whole-wheat flour and add 15 g more water.
- **Cabatta with olives:** add 100 g of pitted black olives in the last fold.
- **Cabatta with rosemary:** add a tablespoon of chopped fresh rosemary to the dough.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I do it without poolish?

Yes, but it will have less flavor and the crumb will be somewhat less open. You can replace the poolish with the same amount of flour and water in the final dough and extend the block fermentation in the refrigerator overnight.

### Can it be made with sourdough?

Yes. Replace the poolish with 150 g of 100% active sourdough, adjust the water to maintain hydration and prolong fermentation.

### How much is preserved?

Ciabatta is at its best the day it is baked. The next day, the crust softens, but recovers with five minutes of baking at 200 °C. To store it longer, cut it in half lengthwise and freeze it wrapped in a bag; It defrosts directly in the oven.

### Can I make ciabatta rolls?

Yes. Divide the dough into eight rectangles instead of two pieces and bake for 15 to 18 minutes. They are perfect for individual snacks.

## How to enjoy ciabatta

Ciabatta is a bread designed to accompany. Some ideas:

- **Bread with tomato:** Toast open slices, rub them with garlic and ripe tomato, and add extra virgin olive oil and salt.
- **Snacks:** Its open crumb and thin crust make it ideal for juicy fillings, such as roasted vegetables, fresh cheese or tortilla.
- **Bruschetta:** toasted slices with chopped tomato, basil and oil.
- **To dip:** in olive oil with a little flake salt, or in the broth of a stew.
- **Breadcrumbs or croutons:** Ciabatta from the previous day, cut into cubes and toasted with oil, makes excellent croutons for soups and creams.

## How to know if hydration is adequate for your flour

Not all flours accept 75% water. If your dough turns into an unmanageable soup even after folding, your flour absorbs less water. Next time, start with 68 or 70% and increase by two points at a time. A flour with more protein, strength, usually accepts more water than an all-purpose flour.

## In summary

A poolish the night before, a very wet dough worked with folds, a delicate division without degassing and a hot oven with steam. Ciabatta does not forgive haste, but it rewards patience with an open, shiny crumb that cannot be achieved any other way.
