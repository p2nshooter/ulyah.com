Pickles are vegetables preserved in an acidic medium, usually vinegar. In Spain they have a long tradition: the banderillas and gildas of the bars, the olives with gherkins and chives, the Ibarra chillies, the pickled garlic or the Almagro eggplants. Made at home, they allow you to control salt, sugar and aromas, and take advantage of seasonal vegetables.

This guide explains how to safely pickle in vinegar, the difference from fermented pickles, and two ways to do it: quick pickles for the refrigerator and canned pickles for the pantry.

## Vinegar Pickles vs. Fermented

Although sometimes the same words are used, they are different processes:

- **Vinegar pickles** are acidified directly with vinegar. The acetic acid in vinegar is what preserves the vegetable from the first moment.
- **Those fermented in brine**, such as sauerkraut or Central European-style pickles, are acidified thanks to lactic acid bacteria, which produce lactic acid for days or weeks. We explain them in our guide to [fermented vegetables in brine](/recetas-y-guias/verduras-fermentadas-en-salmuera/).

Pickles in vinegar are faster, have a more aggressive and pungent flavor, and their result is more predictable.

## Vinegar: acidity is not negotiable

Vinegar is the safety ingredient in this recipe. For a pickle to be safe, the final acidity must be sufficient to prevent the growth of dangerous microorganisms, including botulism.

- **Use vinegar with at least 5% acidity.** Most commercial wine and apple cider vinegars list their acidity on the label. Avoid homemade vinegars, whose acidity is unknown.
- **Do not reduce the ratio of vinegar** to water in a proven recipe. You can change spices, sugar or salt, but not the amount of vinegar.
- As a general rule for homemade pickles, use **at least the same amount of vinegar as water**, i.e. a 1:1 ratio. Many traditional recipes use more vinegar than water.

### What vinegar to choose

| Vinegar | Flavor | Recommended use |
|---|---|---|
| white wine vinegar | Clean, fruity | All kinds of pickles |
| apple cider vinegar | Smooth and slightly sweet | Pickles, onion, sweet pickles |
| Sherry vinegar | Intense, with wood notes | Character Chives and Pickles |
| alcohol vinegar | Neutral and strong | Pickles where you don't want wine flavor |
| red wine vinegar | Intense and colorful | Red onion, beet |

## The Basic Pickling Brine

For approximately one liter of liquid:

- 500 ml of white wine or apple vinegar with 5% acidity.
- 500 ml of water.
- 20 g of salt.
- From 0 to 50 g of sugar, depending on whether you want it more acidic or more balanced.

Bring everything to a boil until the salt and sugar dissolve. Use this hot brine on vegetables.

## Quick refrigerator pickles

They are the easiest and do not require water bath treatment. Instead, they should be stored in the refrigerator and consumed within three or four weeks.

### Pickled red onion

1. Cut two red onions into fine julienne strips.
2. Place them in a clean jar.
3. Pour the hot brine over them to cover them.
4. Let cool, close and store in the refrigerator.

In two hours they will be pink and crispy. They are perfect for tacos, salads, sandwiches or legumes.

### Carrot and cucumber in sticks

Cut carrot and cucumber into sticks, place them in a jar with a clove of garlic, some mustard seeds and some dill sprigs, and cover with the hot brine. In one day they will be ready.

## Canned Pickles for the Pantry

If you want your pickles to last for months at room temperature, you need a heat treatment in a bain-marie, as we explain in our guide to [safely canning in a bain-marie](/recetas-y-guias/conservas-al-bano-maria-con-seguridad/).

### Pickled gherkins

**Ingredients for three 500 ml jars:**

- 1 kg of small, firm pickles.
- 750 ml of 5% white wine vinegar.
- 500 ml of water.
- 30 g of salt.
- 30 g of sugar, optional.
- Per jar: a clove of garlic, a sprig of dill, a teaspoon of mustard seeds and a few grains of pepper.

**Preparation:**

1. **Salt the gherkins.** Wash the gherkins well, cut a millimeter from the end of the flower and place them in a bowl covered with water with 50 g of salt per liter for 12 hours. This step extracts water and makes them crispier. Then rinse them well.
2. **Prepare the brine.** Bring the vinegar, water, salt and sugar to a boil.
3. **Fill the jars.** Place the garlic, dill and spices in the hot, clean jars. Place the pickles tightly.
4. **Cover.** Pour the hot brine to cover the pickles, leaving one centimeter free.
5. **Eliminate air**, clean the edges and close.
6. **Treat in a water bath** 10 to 15 minutes, depending on the size of the jar.
7. **Wait.** Let sit at least two weeks before opening so the flavors can settle.

### Pickled chives

Peel 500 g of small chives. To easily peel them, blanch them for a minute in boiling water and rinse them in cold water. Place them in jars with a bay leaf and a few peppercorns, cover with the hot brine and process in a water bath for 10 minutes.

### Pickled chillies

Long green chillies, like those from Ibarra, are traditionally pickled in white wine vinegar with a little salt. Wash them, dry them, stand them up in tall jars and cover with hot brine with a higher proportion of vinegar, for example two parts vinegar to one part water. Treat in a water bath for 10 minutes. They are the essential ingredient of gilda, along with olives and anchovies.

## Traditional pickles from Spain

The Spanish pickling tradition is rich and very regional. Some of the best known:

- **Ibarra chillies:** small green chillies from the Basque Country, mild and barely spicy, which are picked young and pickled in wine vinegar. They are the basis of the gilda.
- **Aubergines from Almagro:** small aubergines from Ciudad Real, cooked and seasoned with vinegar, oil, paprika, cumin, garlic and fennel, and held with a fennel stick. They have Protected Geographical Indication and their production includes prior fermentation.
- **Capers and capers:** The flower buds and fruits of the caper, highly appreciated in the Balearic Islands and Andalusia, are preserved in vinegar or salt.
- **Pickled garlic:** peeled garlic cloves in vinegar, sometimes with a touch of chilli.
- **Banderillas:** skewers that combine chives, pickles, chillies, carrots and pickled olives, present in almost any tapas bar.

## La gilda: the pincho that was born from a pickle

The gilda is probably the most famous pincho of Spanish pickles. She was born in San Sebastián in the 1940s, and her name is attributed to the film *Gilda*, starring Rita Hayworth: like the protagonist, the pincho was "green, salty and a little spicy." To assemble it at home you only need three quality ingredients:

1. A green olive, traditionally manzanilla, pitted.
2. An anchovy fillet in oil, of good quality.
3. Two or three pickled chillies, preferably from your own jars.

Prick the olive on a toothpick, fold the anchovy in a zigzag and thread it, add the chillies and finish with another olive if you wish. Drizzle with a drizzle of extra virgin olive oil just before serving.

## More ways to use pickles

- **In Russian salad:** some chopped pickles give it freshness and contrast.
- **In sauces:** Chopped pickles and capers with mayonnaise form the base of a homemade tartar sauce.
- **With legumes:** some pickled chillies are a wonderful accompaniment to a bean dish or a stew.
- **In sandwiches and hamburgers:** pickled red onion provides color and acidity.
- **In dressings:** a tablespoon of the liquid from refrigerator pickles livens up a vinaigrette.

## How to Keep Pickles Crispy

- Use very fresh and firm vegetables, freshly picked or purchased.
- Remove the flower end on the gherkins.
- Pre-salt cold.
- Add leaves rich in tannins, such as vine or bay leaves.
- Do not make the water bath longer than necessary.

## Common problems

**The pickles are soft.** Vegetables are not very fresh, excessive heat treatment or lack of prior salting.

**Liquid is cloudy.** This may be due to salt with anti-caking agents, ground spices, or very hard water. If there is also a bad smell, bubbles or mold, throw away the jar.

**The garlic has turned blue or green.** It is a natural reaction between compounds in garlic and acid. It's not dangerous.

**The lid is bulging.** Discard the jar without tasting it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I reuse the pickling liquid?

For quick refrigerator pickles, yes, once. But do not use it for new pantry preserves: its acidity has decreased when mixed with the water of the vegetables.

### Can I use less salt?

Yes, the salt in pickles is mostly a matter of flavor. What you can't reduce is the vinegar.

### Can I pickle cooked vegetables?

Yes, some vegetables like beets or green beans are blanched or boiled before pickling. Always follow a proven recipe that indicates the bain-marie times.

## In summary

Vinegar with at least 5% acidity, in a proportion that should never be reduced, fresh, firm vegetables, and a hot brine. For the refrigerator, just cover and wait; For the pantry, treat the jars in a bain-marie. This way you will have crispy and safe pickles ready for the aperitif.
