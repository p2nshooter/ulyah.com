Salt is the smallest ingredient in most of the workshop's recipes, and one of the most important. In bread it accounts for only 2% of the weight of flour, but if you forget it, the bread comes out tasteless, spreads like a cake and ferments at full speed. With a cheese, you decide how long it will keep and what its rind will be like. In sauerkraut, it's the difference between clean fermentation and rotten cabbage.

This guide explains what salt does in each preparation, how much to use, how to calculate it, and what type of salt to choose.

## Salt beyond flavor

Salt, sodium chloride, has several functions in cooking:

- **Enhances flavor:** reduces the perception of bitterness and enhances aromas.
- **Removes water:** by osmosis, extracts water from vegetable cells, curds or microorganisms.
- **Controls microorganisms:** at moderate concentrations, it stops many bacteria and molds; at high concentrations, it stops almost all growth.
- **Modifies proteins:** strengthens the gluten network in doughs and affects the texture of milk proteins.

## salt in bread

### How much salt

The usual proportion in baking is **1.8 to 2.2% of the weight of the flour**. That is, about 9 to 11 g of salt per 500 g of flour. 2%, 10 g per 500 g, is an excellent reference.

Some traditions use slightly less, such as Tuscan bread, which is made without salt. In Spain, limits have been established on the salt content of common bread to reduce the population's salt consumption, so many commercial breads are now around 1.5 to 1.7% of flour.

### What does salt do in dough?

- **Strengthens gluten.** Salt helps gluten proteins organize in a more orderly way. Dough with salt is more elastic, tenacious and easier to form. Without salt, it is sticky and spreads.
- **Slows down fermentation.** Salt slows down the yeast and bacteria in the sourdough. That is why bread without salt ferments very quickly and loses structure.
- **Improves the crust.** By controlling fermentation, you leave more sugars available to brown the crust.
- **Improves conservation.** Retains moisture and stops mold growth.

### When to add salt

Many recipes add salt after a first rest of flour and water, autolysis, because this way the dough develops more easily. If you use fresh yeast, avoid direct, prolonged contact with the concentrated salt before mixing.

## Salt in cheeses

In the cheese factory, salt is essential:

- **It gives flavor:** a cheese without salt is flat and bland.
- **Helps whey:** extracts whey from the curd and contributes to the final texture.
- **Controls ripening:** stops undesirable bacteria and molds and regulates the activity of cultures and enzymes.
- **Forms the crust:** In aged cheeses, the salt on the surface helps dry and form the crust.

### How much salt in fresh cheeses

In [homemade fresh cheese](/recetas-y-guias/queso-fresco-casero/), salt is usually 1 to 1.5% of the weight of the drained cheese, that is, 10 to 15 g per kilo of cheese. It is added directly to the curd or sprinkled on the surface.

### Dry and brine salting

- **Dry Salting:** Salt is sprinkled on the surface of the cheese or mixed with the curds. It is the most common method at home.
- **Brine Salting:** The cheese is soaked in a concentrated brine, with about 18 to 23% salt, near the saturation point, for a time that depends on its size. It is the method of many aged cheeses, such as Manchego, and also mozzarella, which is briefly immersed in brine.

## Salt in fermented foods

In fermented vegetables, salt plays a **selection** role: it creates an environment in which salt-tolerant lactic acid bacteria can thrive, while many spoilage bacteria are held back. Plus, it draws water out of the vegetables to form the brine and helps maintain a crispy texture.

| Fermented | Recommended salt |
|---|---|
| Sauerkraut, dry salted | 2% of the weight of the cabbage |
| Vegetables in brine | 2 to 5% of the weight of water |
| Fermented pickles | 3.5 to 5% of the weight of water |
| Olives in brine | 8 to 10% of the weight of water |

With less salt than recommended, the vegetables become soft and the risk of contamination increases. With more, fermentation becomes very slow.

### How to calculate the salt of a fermented product

For dry salting, such as sauerkraut, calculate the salt based on the **weight of the vegetable**: 1 kg of 2% cabbage requires 20 g of salt.

In the brine, calculate the salt over the **weight of water**: 1 liter of 3% water needs 30 g of salt. Some fermenters calculate salt based on the total weight of water and vegetables, which gives slightly more precise results, but calculating on water is simpler and reliable enough to start with.

## Calculation examples

Once the rule is understood, the calculation is always a simple multiplication:

| Preparation | Basis of calculation | Percentage | salt |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bread with 1 kg of flour | 1000 g flour | 2 % | 20g |
| 600 g drained fresh cheese | 600g cheese | 1,2 % | about 7g |
| Sauerkraut with 2.5 kg of cabbage | 2500 g cabbage | 2 % | 50g |
| Pickle brine, 2 liters | 2000 g of water | 3,5 % | 70g |
| Brine for olives, 1.5 liters | 1500 g of water | 8 % | 120g |

A liter of water weighs a kilo, so in brines it is enough to weigh the water or measure it with a reliable jug. Always write down the percentage next to the recipe: if a fermentation turns out well, you can repeat it exactly.

## Prepare a brine step by step

1. **Weigh the water and salt** according to the chosen percentage.
2. **Dissolve the salt** in part of the warm water, stirring until there are no crystals left at the bottom.
3. **Add the rest of the cold water** and let the brine come to room temperature before pouring it over the vegetables. A hot brine softens vegetables and can harm lactic acid bacteria.
4. **Use good quality water.** If your tap water tastes a lot like chlorine, you can use filtered or bottled water.
5. **Prepare a little more than necessary** and store it in a closed jar in the refrigerator: it will be used to fill the fermented product if the level drops and some vegetables are exposed.

Never fill a fermented product with water alone, because you would lower the salt concentration. Always use brine of the same percentage.

## Types of salt

| Type | Features | Use in the workshop |
|---|---|---|
| fine table salt | Fine grain, often with iodine and anti-caking agents | Bread, pastries, general cuisine |
| fine sea salt | Fine grain, not over-refined | Bread, cheese, fermented |
| Coarse or coarse salt | large grain | Pickles, salted cheeses, olives |
| Salt flakes | crunchy flakes | To finish dishes, not for recipes |
| Salt without iodine for canning | Pure, without additives | Fermented and pickled |

### Does iodine matter?

Iodized salt is an important public health measure to prevent iodine deficiency, and is perfectly suitable for bread and everyday cooking. In fermented and pickled foods, some people prefer to avoid it because the anti-caking agents that usually accompany it can cloud the brine. Iodine, in the amounts present in salt, does not prevent fermentation.

### Does grain size matter?

Yes, when you measure by volume. A tablespoon of fine salt weighs much more than a tablespoon of coarse or flake salt, because the grains leave less air between each other. For this reason, in the workshop **salt is always weighed**, it is never measured in tablespoons.

## Salt and health

The World Health Organization recommends that adults consume less than 5 g of salt per day. Breads, cheeses, pickles and sausages are some of the main sources of salt in the diet. Making them at home allows you to control the quantity, but without going below the minimum that guarantees the safety of the fermented and preserved products.

If you want to reduce the salt in bread, you can go down to 1.5% without major technical problems. In fermented foods, however, it is better to maintain the recommended salt and, if necessary, rinse the vegetables before eating them.

## Frequently asked questions

### What happens if I forget the salt in the bread?

The dough will be sticky, it will ferment very quickly and the bread will be tasteless. If you notice during the first fermentation, you can dissolve the salt in a tablespoon of water and fold it in.

### Can you use Himalayan salt or colored salts?

Yes, although they do not provide technical advantages. Make sure you weigh it and make sure it doesn't contain additives.

### Why is my fresh cheese too salty?

Probably because it was salted without regret or because it was left in brine for too long. Weigh the salt based on the weight of the cheese.

## In summary

In bread, 2% of the flour strengthens the dough and controls fermentation; in cheese, salt gives flavor, helps drain and preserves; in fermented ones, it selects the good bacteria. Always weigh it, choose a salt without additives for fermentation and do not go below the minimums that guarantee safety.
