Homemade jam is one of the oldest and most grateful ways to preserve seasonal fruit. With ripe strawberries in spring, apricots in summer or plums at the end of August, an afternoon of cooking is transformed into several jars that brighten breakfasts for months. All you need is fruit, sugar, a little lemon and some technique.

This guide explains what makes jam set, how much sugar it needs to preserve, and how to can it safely. If you are interested in the general principles of home canning, also consult our guide to [canning in a bain-marie](/recetas-y-guias/conservas-al-bano-maria-con-seguridad/).

## The three pillars of a jam

For a jam to gel, that is, for it to go from being a liquid compote to a spreadable cream that holds, three elements are needed in balance:

1. **Pectin:** a natural fiber present in fruit, especially in the skin, seeds and unripe fruit. It is what forms the gel.
2. **Sugar:** removes water from the pectin and allows its molecules to bond with each other.
3. **Acid:** reduces the electrical charges of the pectin, which repel each other, and makes it easier for them to form the network.

If any of the three are missing, the jam will be runny.

## Fruits with a lot and little pectin

| Lots of pectin | medium pectin | Little pectin |
|---|---|---|
| Sour apple, quince, currant, citrus (peel and seeds), sour plum | Apricot, blackberry, blueberry, raspberry | Strawberry, peach, cherry, pear, fig, mango |

Fruits with little pectin can be mixed with others that have more, or you can add a grated apple, which provides pectin without changing the flavor too much. Lemon juice provides the acid that is usually lacking in sweet fruits.

## The role of sugar in preservation

Sugar doesn't just sweeten. In a traditional jam, it represents about 60 to 65% of the final product. At this concentration, the sugar retains water so that microorganisms cannot use it, and the jam is preserved for months once opened.

Therefore, when sugar is greatly reduced:

- Jam sets worse and usually requires added pectin or longer cooking.
- It keeps for less time once opened and should be stored in the refrigerator.
- It is more prone to mold.

The most common proportions are:

| Type | Sugar per kilo of clean fruit | Conservation once opened |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional | 750g to 1kg | Several weeks in the refrigerator |
| Balanced | 500 to 600g | Two or three weeks in the refrigerator |
| Low in sugar | 300g or less | A week in the refrigerator |

## Recipe: strawberry jam

### Ingredients for about 4 jars of 250 g

- 1 kg of clean strawberries, without the stem.
- 600 g of sugar.
- The juice of one lemon, about 40 ml.
- Optional: a grated green apple with the skin, wrapped in cheesecloth.

### Preparation

1. **Macerate.** Cut the strawberries into pieces, mix them with the sugar and lemon juice in a bowl and let them macerate for at least 2 hours, or overnight in the refrigerator. The sugar extracts the juice from the fruit and the cooking time will be shorter.
2. **Prepare the jars.** Wash the jars and lids with hot soapy water, rinse them and keep them warm until ready to fill, for example in hot water or in the oven at 100 °C.
3. **Cook.** Put the macerated fruit with its juice in a wide, thick-bottomed casserole. Wide pots allow water to evaporate sooner and the fruit retains its color and flavor better. Bring to a boil over medium-high heat, stirring frequently.
4. **Remove the foam.** During cooking, foam forms on the surface. Remove it with a slotted spoon.
5. **Watch the point.** Cook over high heat, stirring so it doesn't stick, between 20 and 30 minutes, until reaching the gelling point.

## How to know when it's on point

### With thermometer

The jam gels when it reaches about **104–105 °C** at sea level. At that temperature, enough water has evaporated for the sugar to reach the necessary concentration. If you live at altitude, the point is reached a few degrees lower; A practical reference is to add 4 or 5 °C to the boiling temperature of the water in your kitchen.

### With the cold plate

Before you start, put two or three small plates in the freezer. When you think the jam is ready, remove the pan from the heat, put a teaspoon on a cold plate and wait a minute. Push the jam with your finger: if it forms wrinkles on the surface and does not come back together, it is ready. If it is still liquid, cook for a few more minutes and repeat.

### with the spoon

Dip a wooden spoon into the jam and lift it. If the drops fall slowly and coalesce into a sheet hanging from the edge, you've reached the point.

Remember that the jam thickens a lot when it cools. It is better to remove it a little sooner than to overcook it: an overcooked jam turns dark, loses its aroma and can turn out like candy.

## Packaging

1. Fill the hot jars with the hot jam, using a wide-mouth funnel, leaving **one centimeter free** at the top.
2. Clean the edges with a clean, damp cloth: any residue prevents a good seal.
3. Closes with lids in good condition.

### Water bath treatment

The safest way to preserve jam at room temperature for months is heat treatment in a water bath:

1. Place the closed jars in a large pot on a cloth or rack, without them touching.
2. Cover them with hot water until they are at least 2 or 3 cm above the lids.
3. Bring to a boil and keep **10 minutes** from when it starts to boil.
4. Remove the jars and let them cool without moving them for 12 to 24 hours.

Check the closure: the lid should be slightly recessed in the center and should not "click" when pressed. If a jar has not closed properly, store it in the refrigerator and consume it soon.

The old method of turning hot jars upside down often produces a seal, but is less reliable than a double boiler and is not recommended by food safety guides.

## Conservation

- Well-closed jars treated in a water bath can be stored for up to a year in a cool, dry and dark place.
- Once opened, store them in the refrigerator and always use a clean spoon.
- If mold appears on the surface, discard the entire jar. It is not enough to remove the moldy part.

## A fruit calendar for the year

The best jam comes from fruit at its best and at a good price. As a guide for Spain, although the dates change depending on the area and the year:

| Era | Fruits |
|---|---|
| winter | bitter orange, orange, tangerine, lemon, kiwi |
| Spring | strawberry, loquat, first cherries |
| beginning of summer | apricot, cherry, raspberry |
| Midsummer | peach, nectarine, plum, wild blackberry |
| End of summer and autumn | fig, grape, pear, apple, quince |

The bitter orange, from the trees that adorn the streets of Seville, is hardly eaten raw, but it produces one of the most aromatic jams that exist. And quince deserves its own recipe: [homemade quince candy](/recetas-y-guias/dulce-de-membrillo-casero/).

## Small batches, better results

It may seem practical to cook five kilos of fruit at once, but the result is usually worse. The more jam there is in the casserole, the longer it takes for the water to evaporate: the fruit cooks for longer, darkens, loses aroma and the pectin degrades. The ideal is to work with batches of one or two kilos of fruit in a wide pot. If you have a lot of fruit, macerate several batches at a time and cook them one after the other.

## Label and check the pantry

Write down the fruit, the proportion of sugar and the date of preparation on each jar. Place the new jars behind the old ones so you can consume the oldest ones first. Before opening one, check it: if the lid is bulging, if the jar has leaked, if gas comes out when you open it or it smells fermented or alcoholic, throw it away without tasting it. A well-made and well-sealed jam simply smells like fruit.

## Common problems

**It has been liquid.** There was a lack of cooking, pectin or acid. You can cook it again for a few minutes with a little more lemon juice.

**It has crystallized.** Excess sugar or cooking too long. Adding lemon juice helps prevent it.

**It has darkened.** Cooking too long or at too low a heat. Use a wide saucepan and high heat.

**It has mold.** Defective closure, dirty edges or little sugar. Discard the jar.

## In summary

Ripe fruit, enough sugar to preserve it, lemon juice for the acid, a wide saucepan and high heat until 104–105 °C or until the cold plate shows wrinkles. Pack hot, leave one centimeter free, treat the jars for 10 minutes in a water bath and check the closure. This way you will have seasonal fruit all year round.
