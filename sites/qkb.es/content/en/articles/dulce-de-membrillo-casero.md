Quince is one of those fruits that almost no one eats raw. It is hard, acidic, rough and somewhat astringent, but it has a perfumed aroma that fills the kitchen in autumn. Cooked with sugar for long enough, it is transformed into **meat or quince paste**: a firm paste, reddish in color and jewel-like shine, which is cut into slices and accompanies the cheese in one of the most classic pairings of Spanish cuisine.

Making quince at home is simple and very economical. Above all, it requires patience and a good stirring arm. This guide explains each step.

## Quince, a fruit full of pectin

Quince is one of the fruits richest in **pectin**, the natural fiber that makes jams and sweets gel. That's why it doesn't need any added gelling agent: with fruit, sugar and a little lemon, the paste sets on its own when it cools.

Its color is also special. Raw, quince is pale yellow; During long cooking, certain compounds in the fruit are transformed and it acquires that characteristic orange-red tone. The longer the cooking, the more intense the color.

## Choose the quinces

- Choose **ripe** quinces, with intense yellow skin, with little or no green tone.
- They must be firm and without bumps or soft areas.
- Its aroma is a good indicator: a ripe quince perfumes the room.
- The fluff that covers the skin is normal. It is removed by rubbing them with a cloth.

## Ingredients

- 1 kg of quinces.
- Sugar: between 700 g and 1 kg for each kilo of cooked and crushed pulp.
- The juice of a lemon.
- Water for the first cooking.

### How much sugar to use

The traditional recipe uses the same weight of sugar as pulp. It is the proportion that guarantees the best conservation and a firm texture. Many people prefer to reduce it to 70 or 80% of the weight of the pulp: the quince is less sweet and with more fruit flavor, although it preserves somewhat less and should be stored in the refrigerator.

## Step 1: clean

Rub the quinces with a dry cloth to remove the fluff and wash them well with water.

## Step 2: Peel and cut, or cook whole

There are two ways to get started:

### with skin

Cut the quinces into quarters, remove the core with the seeds and cut them into pieces. The skin contains a lot of pectin and aroma, and is then crushed. It is the simplest method.

### without skin

Peel the quinces, remove the core and cut them into pieces. The quince will be finer and somewhat lighter.

If you prefer a very fine texture, you can cook the skins and hearts wrapped in cheesecloth along with the pieces, to take advantage of their pectin, and remove them before grinding.

Quince rusts quickly when cut. As you cut them, put them in a bowl with water and lemon juice.

## Step 3: first cooking

Put the pieces in a pot and cover them just with water. Cook them over medium heat for 20 to 30 minutes, until they are very tender and fall apart when pierced.

Drain them well. You can reserve the cooking water: it is rich in pectin and is used to make quince jelly.

## Step 4: Crush and weigh

Puree the cooked quinces with a blender or pass them through a food mill until you obtain a fine puree. Weigh the puree to calculate the sugar.

## Step 5: cook with sugar

1. Put the puree in a wide, thick-bottomed saucepan.
2. Add the sugar and lemon juice.
3. Cook over medium-low heat, **stirring constantly** with a long-handled wooden spoon.

This is the long step: it can take between 40 minutes and an hour and a half, depending on the quantity and width of the casserole. The puree will thicken, darken and acquire an increasingly intense reddish color.

### Be careful with splashes

The quince puree with sugar bubbles like a volcano and splashes very hot drops that stick to the skin. Use a long-handled spoon, protect your hand with a glove or cloth, and reduce the heat if the bubbles are too large. Don't let children near the pot.

## Step 6: the point

The quince is ready when:

- It has acquired a **red-orange or maroon color**.
- It is so thick that, when you pass the spoon along the bottom of the pan, it **leaves a groove** that takes a few seconds to close.
- It comes off the walls and bottom of the pot when stirring.

If you remove it too soon, it will be soft and cannot be sliced. If you overdo it, it will be very hard and dark, although it will still be edible.

## Step 7: mold

Pour the hot quince into rectangular glass or silicone molds, or into wide-mouth jars. You can line the molds with baking paper to remove them easily. Smooth the surface with a spatula dipped in water.

## Step 8: Rest and Dry

Let the quince cool at room temperature, covered with a clean cloth, for at least 24 hours, and preferably two days. During this time it will finish gelling and a light crust will form on the surface.

If you want it firmer, you can unmold it and let it dry for another day or two on a rack, turning it from time to time.

## Conservation

- **In the pantry:** Wrapped in baking paper or film and stored in an airtight container, in a cool, dry place, the quince with the traditional proportion of sugar is preserved for several months.
- **In the refrigerator:** The quince with less sugar should be stored in the refrigerator and will keep for a few weeks.
- **In jars:** you can hot pack it in sterilized jars and treat them in a water bath like a jam to preserve it for a year.

If mold appears on the surface, discard the piece.

## How to serve it

- **With cheese:** the classic pairing is with cured Manchego cheese, but it also works with sheep's cheese, fresh cheese or cottage cheese. In Argentina and Uruguay, cheese with quince or sweet potato sweets is known as "dessert vigilante" or "Martín Fierro."
- **On toast**, with butter or cheese spread.
- **In baking**, as a filling for sweet dumplings, puff pastries or pastas.
- **With meat**, melted with a little wine as a sauce for pork or game.
- **On cheese boards**, cut into cubes.

## quince jelly

With the water from the first cooking you can make a transparent jelly:

1. Strain the water through a fine cloth, without pressing.
2. Measure it and add 750 g of sugar per liter.
3. Cook over high heat until reaching 104–105°C or until a drop on a cold plate forms wrinkles.
4. Pour into hot jars.

## A fall tradition

The quinces ripen between September and November, and in many Spanish homes their arrival marks the day of making the sweet of the year. In Andalusia, the town of Puente Genil in Cordoba is famous for its quince industry, and in many towns there are still quince growers in the patios whose fruit is distributed among neighbors. If someone gives you quinces, don't wait too long: they ripen quickly at room temperature and, once they are very soft or have dark spots, they are no longer suitable for a good sweet.

## Flavor variants

- **With vanilla:** add half an open pod during cooking with the sugar and remove it before molding.
- **With spices:** a cinnamon stick and two cloves, removed at the end, give it a Christmas air.
- **With apple:** replace a quarter of the quince with sour apple; The sweet is softer and somewhat clearer.
- **With nuts:** Add a handful of chopped walnuts just before pouring it into the mold.
- **With a touch of wine:** a few tablespoons of sweet wine at the end of cooking add aroma; The alcohol almost completely evaporates with heat.

## Cut and present

To obtain clean slices, use a long, sharp knife that you will dip in hot water and dry before each cut. A thickness of half a centimeter to one centimeter is the most comfortable to accompany cheese. On a board, alternate slices of quince and cheese, add some toasted walnuts or almonds and serve at room temperature: the cold of the refrigerator dulls its aroma.

## Common problems

**Does not set.** Lack of cooking. You can reheat it and cook it for a few more minutes.

**It has become very clear.** Lack of cooking or underripe quinces. Next time, cook longer over low heat.

**It is grainy.** The puree was not well mashed. Pass it through a fine food mill before adding the sugar.

**It has crystallized.** Excess sugar or cooking too long. Lemon juice helps avoid this.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I do it in the microwave?

You can cook the puree with sugar in the microwave, in a large container and in short intervals, stirring between them, but it is difficult to control the point and it splashes a lot. The traditional casserole method gives better results.

### Can I use panela or brown sugar?

Yes. The quince will be darker and with a deeper, caramel flavor. Keep in mind that the characteristic reddish color will be less noticeable.

### Why does quince release liquid into the jar?

If the quince is not quite ready or has little sugar, it may release some syrup over time. Store it in the refrigerator and consume it before.

## In summary

Ripe and aromatic quinces, first cooked in water, a fine puree, sugar in almost equal parts, lemon juice and patiently cooked, stirring constantly, until the color turns reddish and the spoon leaves a groove. Mold, let it rest and serve with a good cheese. Few preserves are so grateful.
