Flour is the main ingredient in bread and almost all pastries, and also the one that generates the most doubts in the supermarket. Wheat, bread-making, strength, pastry, wholemeal, spelled, rye, 00, T65 flour... Each one behaves in a different way, and choosing the wrong one can turn a spongy brioche into a sticky dough that doesn't rise, or a tender muffin into a hard muffin.

In this guide we explain what differentiates some flours from others, how to read the label and what flour to use for each recipe in the home bakery.

## What is inside a grain of wheat

A grain of wheat has three parts:

- **Endosperm:** the central and largest part, made up mainly of starch and proteins. This is where the white flour comes from.
- **Bran:** the outer layers, rich in fiber and minerals.
- **Germ:** the embryo of the future plant, rich in fats, vitamins and enzymes.

White flour comes almost exclusively from the endosperm. Whole wheat flour includes all three parts. Between the two there are semi-whole wheat flours, with a part of the bran.

## Gluten: the network that traps gas

Two wheat proteins, **glutenin** and **gliadin**, come together when they come into contact with water and work together, forming gluten. Gluten is an elastic and extensible network that traps the gas produced by yeast, allowing the dough to rise and the bread to have a honeycomb crumb.

- Glutenin provides elasticity, the tendency of the dough to recover its shape.
- Gliadin provides extensibility, the ability to stretch without breaking.

A flour with more quality protein forms more gluten and has more "strength." That's why not all flours are suitable for everything.

## What does force or W mean?

In Spain, many professional flours indicate their **W**, a value that measures the strength of the flour with a device called an alveograph. This device inflates a sheet of dough like a balloon and measures how much energy it takes to break it. The higher the W, the stronger the flour.

| Type of flour | W indicative | Approximate protein | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loose or pastry | Less than 150 | 8 to 10% | Cakes, muffins, cookies |
| Breadable or medium strength | 150 to 250 | 10 to 12% | Common bread, loaves, pizza |
| Of strength | 250 to 350 | 12 to 14% | Pastries, brioche, roscón, enriched breads |
| Of great strength | More than 350 | 13 to 15% | Croissants, panettone, very long fermentations |

Supermarket flours don't usually list the W. For reference, look at the protein on the nutrition label: it's a useful, but not perfect, indicator of strength.

## Spanish supermarket flours

### All-purpose wheat flour

It is the most common flour. It usually has between 9 and 11% protein. It works well for batters, béchamel, sponge cakes and some simple breads, although for breads with a lot of water or long fermentations it falls a bit short.

### Bread flour

It has a little more protein and is the most balanced for everyday bread. If you can only have one flour for bread, let it be this.

### strength flour

With more than 12% protein. It is essential for pastries with a lot of butter, sugar or eggs, such as brioche, roscón or croissants, because these ingredients weaken the gluten and a flour capable of supporting them is needed.

### Pastry flour

Very weak, with little protein. It produces soft biscuits and cookies that do not shrink. It doesn't work for bread.

## Whole wheat flours

Whole wheat flour contains the bran and germ. This has several consequences for the baker:

- **Absorbs more water.** Bran is very absorbent, so whole wheat doughs need more hydration.
- **Develops less gluten.** Bran particles cut the gluten network, and the bread rises less.
- **Ferments faster.** Provides more nutrients to the yeasts.
- **It goes rancid sooner.** The fats in the germ oxidize, so it is best to buy it in small quantities and store it in a cool place, or even in the refrigerator.

A good starting point is to start with 20 or 30% whole wheat in your breads and increase.

## Rye

Rye flour behaves very differently than wheat. Its gluten barely forms a network, and its structure depends on compounds called pentosans, which retain a lot of water. That's why rye breads are dense, moist and compact, and are almost always made with sourdough, whose acidity helps control the rye enzymes and prevents the crumb from being rubbery.

A little rye, between 10 and 20%, adds flavor and color to a wheat bread without changing its structure too much.

## Spelled

Spelled is an ancient species of wheat. It has gluten, but it is more extensible and fragile than that of common wheat, so spelled doughs are worked more delicately and tolerate long kneading less. It has a mild and somewhat sweet flavor. It is not suitable for people with celiac disease.

## Other flours

- **Corn flour:** gluten free; It is not suitable for leavened bread without mixing it with wheat.
- **Rice flour:** gluten-free; Useful in gluten-free batters and doughs.
- **Legume flours:** such as chickpea flour, for tortillas or batters.
- **Duro wheat semolina:** durum wheat, yellow, for pasta and Mediterranean-style breads.

## International equivalences

Many recipes use foreign nomenclatures. These equivalences are indicative:

| Nomenclature | Approximate equivalence in Spain |
|---|---|
| French T45 | Loose or pastry flour, very white |
| French T55 | All-purpose or bread flour |
| French T65 | Breadable with a little more extraction |
| French T80 and T110 | Semi-integral |
| French T150 | Comprehensive |
| Italian 00 | Highly refined flour; exists in different forces |
| Italian 0 | Refined flour, slightly less than 00 |

Please note that in France and Italy the numbers indicate the degree of refining, that is, the amount of minerals, and not the strength. A 00 flour can be weak, for fresh pasta, or very strong, for Neapolitan pizza or panettone.

## What flour to use in each recipe

| Recipe | Recommended flour |
|---|---|
| Sourdough loaf | Bread-able, with 10 to 20% whole wheat |
| sliced bread | Breadable or force |
| ciabatta | Bread-able or strong, due to its high hydration |
| Croissants | Of strength or great strength |
| Roscón de Reyes and brioche | Of strength |
| Ensaimada | Of strength |
| Muffins and biscuits | Loose or all-purpose |
| Churros | Of all use |
| Cookies | lazy |

## How to know how much water your flour admits

Two flours with the same label can absorb very different amounts of water, depending on the wheat, the crop, and the mill. Therefore, when you change flour, it is advisable to do a small test before launching into a high hydration recipe:

1. Weigh 100g of flour into a bowl.
2. Add 60 g of water and mix by hand until a dough forms.
3. Add water 5 g at a time, kneading for one minute at a time.
4. For when the dough starts to stick to your fingers and loses shape.

The total amount of water you have added is a good reference for the comfortable hydration of that flour. If it became sticky with 62 g, it is a flour that absorbs little; If it holds 72 g without problems, it accepts wet masses. Write down the result: it will save you many surprises.

Also remember that freshly milled flour, especially whole wheat, behaves somewhat differently than flour that has been sitting for a few weeks. Mills usually let the flour mature before selling it precisely so that its behavior is more stable.

## Mix flours at home

You don't need to have six different packages in the pantry. With two or three flours you can obtain almost all the others:

- **Homemade "baking" flour:** For every 100 g of all-purpose flour, replace about 15 g with corn starch. The cake is more tender and lighter.
- **More strength for a bread:** mix bread-making flour with strength flour in equal parts; It is a good base for breads with a lot of water or long fermentations.
- **A Whole Grain Twist:** Add 10 to 30% whole wheat or rye flour to any bread dough for flavor, adjusting the water upwards.

Powdered wheat gluten, sold in some stores, allows you to strengthen a loose flour, but in small quantities and with caution: too much gives gummy crumbs.

## Mill flours

In Spain there are mills that stone-ground grain and sell their flour directly, often made from traditional varieties of wheat, spelled or rye. They are flours with a lot of flavor, but with more variable behavior than industrial flours: ask the miller for the protein content or approximate strength, buy small quantities if they are whole grains, and do the water absorption test before using them in a demanding recipe.

## A note on celiac disease

All wheat, spelled, rye and barley flours contain gluten and are not suitable for people with celiac disease. If you cook for someone with celiac disease, use certified gluten-free flours, your own utensils and surfaces, and keep in mind that flour suspended in the kitchen air is enough to contaminate other foods.

## How to preserve flour

- Store it in an airtight container in a cool, dry, dark place.
- White flours keep for several months; the integral ones, less so.
- If you live in a hot place, store whole grains in the refrigerator.
- If you see small bugs, throw it away and clean the pantry.

### A warning about raw flour

Flour is a raw product. Although it may seem harmless, it can contain bacteria, so you should not eat raw dough or try uncooked mixtures.

## In summary

For cakes and cookies, loose flour; for bread, bread-making; for pastries with butter and egg, strong flour. Look at the protein on the label, remember that whole grains absorb more water and ferment faster, and that rye and spelled have their own rules. With the right flour, half the job is done.
