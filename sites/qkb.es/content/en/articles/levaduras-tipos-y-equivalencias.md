In Spain, the word "yeast" is used for very different things, and this confusion is responsible for many failures in the kitchen. Whoever puts chemical yeast in bread obtains a dense cake; who puts baker's yeast in a sponge cake, a bread-flavored sponge cake that does not rise as it should. This guide clarifies the differences, explains how each type works, and offers equivalences so you can adapt any recipe.

## Two very different families

Everything we call yeast is divided into two large families:

1. **Biological yeasts:** are living organisms, microscopic fungi, that ferment the sugars in the dough and produce carbon dioxide, alcohol and aromas. They need time, adequate temperature and food. They are used in bread, pizza and fermented pastries: croissants, roscón, ensaimada, brioche.
2. **Chemical gasifiers:** are mixtures of substances that, when in contact with humidity and heat, react and release carbon dioxide. They act immediately, do not need fermentation time and do not add flavor. They are used in cakes, muffins, cookies and pancakes.

## Baker's yeast

All commercial bakery yeasts are of the same species, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, the same one used to make beer and wine. What changes is the format.

### Fresh or pressed yeast

It is sold in small cubes or tablets in the refrigerated area of the supermarket. It is moist, beige in color, and has a texture that can be crumbled with your fingers, with a pleasant smell of ferment.

- **Advantages:** It is the favorite of many bakers, it dissolves easily and has a very vigorous activity.
- **Disadvantages:** It is perishable. It lasts about two or three weeks in the refrigerator and must be protected from air.
- **How ​​to use it:** crumbled directly into the flour or dissolved in a little water or warm milk.

If it is dry, cracked, dark or smells bad, it is no longer useful.

### active dry yeast

It comes in small granules. It has an outer layer of inactive cells that protects the living ones.

- **How ​​to use it:** It is traditionally hydrated first in warm water, about 35–38 °C, with a pinch of sugar, for 5 to 10 minutes, until it foams. Many modern active dry yeasts can be mixed directly with flour, but it is best to follow the manufacturer's instructions.

### Instant or baker's dry yeast in sachets

It is the most common dry yeast in Spanish supermarkets, in sachets of about 5 to 11 g. It comes in a fine powder.

- **How ​​to use it:** directly mixed with the flour, without the need to hydrate it.
- **Conservation:** closed, lasts many months at room temperature. Once opened, store it tightly closed in the refrigerator or freezer.

### Osmotolerant yeast

There is a variant of dry yeast designed for doughs with a lot of sugar, such as brioche or panettone. Sugar removes water from yeast cells and can slow down their activity; This yeast is adapted to those conditions. It is not essential at home, but it improves the results in very sweet doughs.

## Equivalences between bakery yeasts

| fresh yeast | active dry yeast | Instant dry yeast |
|---|---|---|
| 5g | 2g | 1.5 to 2g |
| 10g | 4 to 5g | 3 to 4g |
| 20g | 8 to 9g | 6 to 7g |
| 25g | 10g | 7 to 8g |
| 50g | 20g | 15g |

An easy rule of thumb to remember: **divide the amount of fresh yeast by three** to get the amount of instant dry yeast. The equivalences are approximate, because the activity varies depending on the brand and freshness.

## How much yeast to use

A bread dough with fermentation of about two or three hours usually contains between 1 and 2% of fresh yeast by weight of the flour, that is, between 5 and 10 g for every 500 g of flour. Doughs enriched with sugar and butter need something more, because these ingredients slow down fermentation.

Less yeast and more time almost always give breads with more flavor. Many home bakers use only 1 to 3 g of fresh yeast per 500 g of flour and leave the dough to ferment overnight in the refrigerator.

## Temperature and yeast

Yeast is a living being and is very sensitive to temperature:

| Temperature | Effect on yeast |
|---|---|
| Less than 4°C | Activity almost stopped, like in the refrigerator |
| 10 to 20°C | Slow fermentation, more complex flavor |
| 24 to 28°C | Optimal fermentation for most doughs |
| 35 to 40°C | Very active, but produces less fine flavors |
| More than 50°C | It starts to get damaged |
| About 55 to 60 °C | die |

That is why the water or milk to dissolve the yeast must be **warm, never hot**. If when you insert your finger you notice that the liquid is hot, wait.

## Is my baker's yeast still alive?

If you have concerns about a long-opened package, test it before risking an entire dough. Dissolve a teaspoon of sugar in 100 ml of water at about 35°C, add the yeast you are going to use, or some of it, and wait ten minutes. If a clear foam forms on the surface and smells fermenty, it is active. If the glass remains the same, the yeast has lost strength and it is better to buy another one.

## Adjust the yeast to your schedule

The amount of yeast is a tool to organize the day. As a guide, for 500 g of flour in a bread dough at about 24 °C:

| fresh yeast | Approximate time to double the volume |
|---|---|
| 10g | about 2 hours |
| 4g | about 4 hours |
| 1 to 2g | overnight in the refrigerator, between 12 and 16 hours |

If you want to bake after work, reduce the yeast and let the dough rise slowly; If you're in a hurry, increase it within reason. Slow fermentation bread usually gains in flavor and preservation.

## Chemical yeast or baking powder

In Spain it is known as "chemical yeast" or, by a well-known brand, "Royal yeast". It is a mixture of:

- **Sodium bicarbonate**, which is a base.
- **One or more acids** in powder form, such as pyrophosphate or tartaric acid.
- **Starch**, which absorbs moisture and prevents them from reacting prematurely.

When mixed with the moisture of the dough and, above all, with the heat of the oven, the acid and base react and release carbon dioxide. Many chemical yeasts are "double action": they react partly cold and partly in the oven.

- **Usual amount:** one 16g sachet for every 500g of flour, although many homemade recipes use less.
- **Excess:** produces a metallic or soapy taste, a crumb with large holes, and biscuits that rise very high and then sink.
- **Conservation:** in a dry and well closed place. It loses strength with humidity and time.

To check if your baking powder is still active, drop a teaspoon into a glass of hot water: if it bubbles vigorously, it's good.

## Sodium bicarbonate

The baking soda is just the base, without the acid. For it to release gas, you need an acidic ingredient in the recipe: yogurt, buttermilk, lemon juice, honey, natural cocoa, or molasses. It is much more potent than baking powder, so it is used in small quantities: about a quarter of a teaspoon per 125g of flour.

If used without enough acid, it leaves a bitter, soapy taste and a yellowish color in the dough.

## Raising agents in double sachets

In many Spanish supermarkets, so-called "carbonating agents" or "gaseosilla sachets" are sold in two colored sachets: one contains an acid and the other bicarbonate. They are mainly used in batters, buñuelos, churros and porras, and in some traditional donut recipes. They work similar to baking powder.

## sourdough

Sourdough is a live culture of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria, fed with flour and water. It makes the bread rise more slowly than commercial yeast and provides acidity, aroma and better preservation. We explain it in detail in our guides for [creating sourdough](/recetas-y-guias/crear-masa-madre-desde-cero/) and for [caring for it](/recetas-y-guias/cuidar-y-alimentar-la-masa-madre/).

As a guide, to replace fresh yeast with active sourdough in a bread recipe, you can use between 15 and 25% sourdough by weight of the flour and eliminate the yeast, greatly lengthening the fermentation times.

## What yeast to use in each recipe

| Recipe | Recommended yeast |
|---|---|
| Bread, pizza, ciabatta | Baker's yeast or sourdough |
| Roscón, ensaimada, croissants, brioche | Baker's yeast |
| Cakes, muffins | chemical yeast |
| Cookies | Chemical yeast, baking soda or none |
| Pancakes, fluffy crepes | chemical yeast |
| Donuts, batons | Raising agent or bicarbonate |
| Classic churros dough | None |

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I substitute baking yeast for baking yeast in a cake?

It is not recommended. The cake would need time to ferment, it would have a bready flavor and a different texture.

### Can I freeze fresh yeast?

Yes, although it loses part of its activity. Cut it into portions, wrap them well and use them directly frozen, increasing the quantity a little.

### Does salt kill yeast?

In direct and prolonged contact, salt in high concentration can damage fresh yeast. In the dough, where the salt is diluted, it only slows down its activity. It is enough not to dissolve the yeast and salt together in a little liquid.

## In summary

Baker's yeast is a living organism that needs time and warm temperature; Chemistry is an instant reaction that needs moisture and heat. For fermented bread and pastries, baker's yeast or sourdough; for cakes and muffins, chemical yeast. And to convert fresh yeast to instant dry, divide by three.
