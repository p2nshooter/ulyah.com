Yogurt is probably the easiest fermented product to make at home and, at the same time, one of the most appreciated for precision. No machine is needed: with a pot, a thermometer, some jars and a warm place, you can get a creamy natural yogurt, without additives and for a fraction of what it costs in the supermarket. The key is two temperatures and an incubation time that you control.

In this guide we explain what happens inside the jar, why the milk is heated before inoculating, four ways to incubate without a yogurt maker and how to get thicker or smoother yogurt depending on your taste.

## What is yogurt and what differentiates it from other fermented foods?

Yogurt is milk fermented by two specific bacteria: *Streptococcus thermophilus* and *Lactobacillus delbrueckii* subspecies *bulgaricus*. They both work as a team. The first starts quickly and acidifies the milk at first; The second takes over, continuing to lower the pH and providing a good part of the characteristic aroma. When acidified, milk proteins, especially casein, clump together and form a gel: liquid milk becomes yogurt.

Unlike [milk kefir](/recetas-y-guias/kefir-de-leche/), which ferments at room temperature with a community of bacteria and yeast, yogurt requires heat. Its bacteria are **thermophilic**: they work best between 40 and 45 °C. That's why the challenge of making yogurt without a yogurt maker is only to keep the milk warm for several hours.

## Ingredients and material

For a liter of yogurt you need:

- 1 liter of whole milk, pasteurized or UHT.
- 2 tablespoons of natural yogurt with live ferments (about 30 g), or an envelope of freeze-dried yogurt ferment.
- Optional: 2 or 3 tablespoons of powdered milk, for a firmer yogurt.

Regarding the material:

- A thick-bottomed pot.
- A kitchen thermometer, probe or instant read.
- Glass jars with lids, clean and scalded.
- A system to keep warm: oven, thermos, portable refrigerator or blankets.

The yogurt you use as a starter should be natural, without sugar or flavors, and with live ferments. Yogurts pasteurized after fermentation, which are often labeled as "dairy dessert" or "heat-treated after fermentation," are useless because their bacteria are no longer alive.

## What milk to use

Whole milk makes the creamiest, most stable yogurt. Semi-skimmed works, but the result is more liquid; the skimmed, even more so. If you want to reduce fat without losing body, add skimmed milk powder: it increases the proteins and the yogurt sets more firmly.

UHT milk is very convenient because it has already been treated at high temperature and some people skip the step of heating it too much. Even so, heating it to 85 °C improves the texture, as we explain below. Fresh pasteurized milk works perfectly. We do not recommend raw milk for beginners: in addition to the health risk, its own bacteria compete with the yoghurt ferments.

Vegetable drinks, such as soy drinks, can be fermented, but they behave differently. Soy is the one that sets the best due to its protein content; oat or almond ones usually need thickeners. This guide focuses on cow's milk.

## Step 1: heat the milk to 85°C

Heat the milk over medium heat, stirring occasionally so it doesn't stick to the bottom, until it reaches between 85 and 90°C. Maintain that temperature for five to ten minutes.

This step has two objectives. The first is to eliminate microorganisms that could compete with the ferments. The second, more important for texture, is to modify the whey proteins. When heated, these proteins denature and bind to casein, allowing the gel to retain more water. The result is a thicker, smoother yogurt with less whey on the surface.

If you add milk powder, dissolve it in the cold milk before starting to heat.

## Step 2: Cool to 43–45°C

Remove the pot from the heat and let the milk cool to 43–45°C. You can speed up the process by placing the pot in a bowl of cold water, stirring occasionally. Do not inoculate above 50 °C: at that temperature the bacteria in the yogurt begin to be damaged. Don't wait for it to cool too much either, because fermentation will be slow.

## Step 3: Inoculate

Put the two tablespoons of yogurt in a small bowl and add a little of the warm milk. Mix until you have a smooth cream without lumps, and then add it to the rest of the milk, stirring gently.

More starter is not better. If you put too much yogurt, the bacteria accumulate, acidify very quickly and the texture tends to be grainy and with more whey. Two tablespoons per liter is a balanced proportion.

Distribute the milk into the jars and close them.

## Step 4: Incubate without yogurt maker

Now it's time to keep the jars between 40 and 45 °C for 6 to 10 hours without moving them. These are four ways to do it with what you have at home.

### in the oven

Preheat the oven to 50°C for a few minutes and turn it off. Put the jars in and leave the light on: in many ovens, the bulb maintains a temperature of 35 to 45 °C. Check the temperature with a thermometer the first time, because each oven is different. If your oven has a proofing or "dough" function, use it.

### in a thermos

A large, wide-mouth thermos, the kind used for food, keeps heat for hours. Scald it with boiling water, empty it, fill it with the inoculated milk at 45 °C and close it. It is the simplest method for small quantities.

### In a portable refrigerator

Place the jars inside a beach cooler along with one or two bottles of hot water, at about 50°C. Close the lid. The refrigerator insulates both cold and heat as well. If the incubation is going to be long, change the water halfway through the process.

### Wrapped in blankets

Place the jars in a pot, surround them with 45°C water, cover the pot and wrap it in blankets or thick towels. It is the oldest method and works best in temperate times.

## How long to incubate

At 43 °C, a yogurt with active ferment usually sets in 5 or 6 hours. From there, every hour makes it more acidic and firmer.

| Incubation time | Usual result |
|---|---|
| 5 to 6 hours | Smooth, low acidity, delicate texture |
| 7 to 8 hours | Balanced, firm, slightly acidic |
| 10 to 12 hours | Very firm and acidic, more serum |

You'll know it's ready when, when you tilt a jar, the yogurt separates from the wall like a block instead of moving like a liquid. If some yellowish serum remains on the surface, it is normal.

## Step 5: Cool and Rest

Take out the jars and put them in the refrigerator for at least four hours before eating. The cold almost completely stops the fermentation and finishes setting the gel. Yogurt fresh from incubation always seems softer than after a night in the refrigerator.

## How to make Greek or strained yogurt

Greek yogurt is nothing more than yogurt with some of the whey removed. Place a strainer over a bowl, cover it with a thin cotton cloth or a large coffee filter, and pour in the yogurt. Leave it in the refrigerator:

- 2 hours for thick, creamy yogurt.
- 4 to 6 hours for a Greek type texture.
- 12 hours or more for something like a cheese spread, same principle as [labneh](/recetas-y-guias/labneh-queso-de-yogur/).

The whey that drains is not thrown away: it is used to knead bread, to marinate meats or to add to smoothies.

## Use your yogurt as a starter

You can save two tablespoons of each batch to start the next. Do this with freshly made yogurt, before opening it to eat, and use a clean spoon. This method works for several generations, but over time the balance between the two bacteria is altered and the yogurt curdles more slowly or changes flavor. When you notice it, start again with purchased yogurt or a sachet of ferment.

## Troubleshooting

**It has not curdled.** The most common cause is temperature: either the milk was too hot when inoculating and killed the ferments, or it became too cold during incubation. It may also be that the yogurt starter did not have live bacteria. If the milk is still runny after eight hours, you can try reheating it to 43°C, adding new starter and incubating again.

**It has been liquid or like custard.** It usually lacks protein or time. Try whole milk, add powdered milk, or extend the incubation by an hour or two.

**It has a lot of whey.** This is a sign of too long incubation, too high a temperature, or that the jars were moved while curdling. Heating the milk to 85°C at first greatly reduces this problem.

**Grainy texture.** Normally due to excess initiator or having raised the temperature too much during incubation. Reduce the amount of yogurt and watch the heat.

**Very acidic flavor.** Shorten the incubation time and cool the jars as soon as they set.

**Pink, orange, green or hairy spots.** These are contaminations. Discard the yogurt without tasting it and wash the jars well.

## Conservation

Homemade yogurt keeps in the refrigerator, tightly closed, for seven to ten days. As the days go by it becomes somewhat more acidic, which is normal. Always use a clean spoon to serve.

## How to take it

Homemade natural yogurt goes with almost everything: fresh fruit, honey, nuts, homemade jam or granola. In savory cuisine, it is used to make cold sauces with cucumber and garlic, dress salads, soften spicy stews or marinate chicken before roasting it. To sweeten it, always do it after fermenting: sugar added before does not prevent fermentation, but it changes the flavor and texture in a less predictable way.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I make yogurt with lactose-free milk?

Yes. Lactose-free milk contains lactose already broken down into glucose and galactose, which bacteria can use. It usually ferments well, although the final flavor is somewhat sweeter.

### Why does my yogurt taste like cheese?

Too long incubation or at too low a temperature can favor strange flavors. Make sure to keep the temperature between 40 and 45 ° C and do not exceed ten or twelve hours.

### Can it be frozen?

You can, but when you defrost it the texture becomes grainy and releases whey. It is good for smoothies or pastries, not so much for eating alone.

## In summary

Heat the milk to 85°C, cool it to 43–45°C, add two tablespoons of live yogurt per liter and keep it warm for six to eight hours without stirring. Chill in the refrigerator and enjoy. With these four rules, any kitchen becomes a small yogurt shop, without having to buy any appliance.
