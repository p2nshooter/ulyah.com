Sourdough is a culture of wild yeast and lactic bacteria that feeds on flour and water. Bread has been made with it for thousands of years, long before commercial yeast existed. Creating it at home doesn't require anything special: flour, water, a jar, a scale and about ten days of perseverance.

This guide accompanies you day by day, explains what happens in each phase and helps you not to give up at the moment when almost everyone gives up: when, after a spectacular rise, the jar seems to die.

## What exactly is sourdough?

Flour naturally contains yeasts and bacteria that come from the grain, the mill and the environment. When you mix flour with water and leave it in a warm place, these microorganisms wake up and begin to feed on the sugars that are released from the starch.

At first many different species grow. With each feeding, the medium becomes more acidic and only the most adapted survive: yeasts that produce gas and aroma, and lactic acid bacteria that produce lactic and acetic acid. After a week or two, a stable community is established that will make your bread rise and give it its characteristic flavor.

## Material and ingredients

- A glass jar of about 500 ml, with a lid that does not close tightly.
- A kitchen scale.
- A spoon or spatula.
- Whole wheat or rye flour, to start.
- White or bread-making wheat flour, for subsequent feedings.
- Chlorine-free water, at about 26°C.

### Why start with whole wheat flour

Whole wheat flour contains the bran and germ of the grain, where microorganisms and nutrients are concentrated. That's why sourdoughs start faster and stronger with whole wheat flour, especially rye. Once the crop is established, you can feed it with white flour or a mixture.

## Temperature changes everything

The factor that most influences the speed of the process is temperature. The ideal is between 24 and 27 °C. At 20°C, sourdough can take twice as long to stabilize. If your house is cold, look for a warm place: near a radiator without direct heat, inside the oven turned off with the light on, or on top of the refrigerator.

## Day by day calendar

We use a simple proportion: the same amount of flour as water, by weight. This is what is called a 100% hydration sourdough.

### Day 1: the initial mix

Mix 50 g of whole wheat or rye flour and 50 g of warm water in the jar. Stir well until no dry flour remains. Scrape the walls, place the lid open and mark the level with a rubber band or marker. Leave the jar in a warm place for 24 hours.

### Day 2: first signs

You may see some bubbles, or you may not see anything. Both things are normal. Add 50 g of flour and 50 g of water, without removing anything, stir and leave for another 24 hours.

### Day 3: the false rise

This is the day when many people believe their sourdough is ready. It is common to find the jar full of bubbles, with doubled mass and a strong, sometimes unpleasant, smell of cheese, vomit or socks.

It's not your sourdough yet. This activity is produced by bacteria that grow quickly at first and disappear when the medium becomes more acidic. This is what is known as the "false rise."

From today the discards begin. Remove and discard all but 50g of the mixture. Add 50 g of flour and 50 g of water, stir and leave for 24 hours.

### Days 4 and 5: the eerie calm

Most likely, activity will plummet. The dough barely rises, smells sour or vinegary, and a grayish liquid may appear on the surface. Many people throw away their sourdough at this time, believing it to have died.

He hasn't died. The bacteria of the first phase have disappeared due to the acidity, and the yeasts and lactic bacteria that form a true sourdough are still few. Continue feeding the same: save 50 g, add 50 g of flour and 50 g of water, every 24 hours.

From day 4 you can start using white wheat flour, or a mixture of white and whole wheat.

### Days 6 to 10: awakening

Little by little, the dough rises again after each feeding, this time more regularly and with a more pleasant smell: sour, yogurt, fruit or beer. When you see that it increases clearly in the hours after feeding, start feeding it twice a day, every 12 hours, with the same proportion.

### Days 10 to 14: the sourdough is ready

Your sourdough is ready to bake when, for two or three days in a row, it meets these conditions:

- It doubles or almost triples its volume between 4 and 8 hours after feeding, at about 24°C.
- It has a structure full of bubbles, like a sponge.
- It smells acidic and pleasant, without putrid odors.
- If you drop a teaspoon into water, it floats. This test is not infallible, but it guides.

## Keep a small log

A sheet of paper stuck on the refrigerator or some notes on your cell phone help a lot during these two weeks. For each feeding, write down the time, the kitchen temperature, the amounts of flour and water and, a few hours later, how much it has risen compared to the mark on the rubber band. After a few days you will see a pattern: what time it reaches its highest point, how it responds to the cold of the night or a change of flour. That knowledge is just what you will need later to plan your breads without depending on luck.

## The first bread

When the sourdough meets the conditions of a ready dough for several days in a row, choose a simple bread to use it for the first time. A moderately hydrated loaf, around 65 or 70%, is much easier to handle than a very wet dough, and allows you to clearly see how your culture is fermenting. Use the sourdough at its highest point, a few hours after feeding, when it is full of bubbles. We explain the complete process in the [sourdough loaf step by step](/recetas-y-guias/hogaza-de-masa-madre-paso-a-paso/) guide. Don't worry if the first loaf comes out a little dense: young sourdoughs gain strength during the first weeks of use, and each baking will teach you something new.

## Common problems

**Nothing happens after three days.** It will most likely be cold. Find a warmer place and use warm water. Also check that the water does not have too much chlorine.

**A dark liquid appears on the surface.** It is alcohol and water that separate when the sourdough is hungry. It's not dangerous. You can remove it or mix it in, and feed sooner next time.

**It smells like acetone or nail polish remover.** It's a sign of hunger. Feed more frequently or with more flour, for example in a 1:2:2 ratio.

**There is mold.** Fuzzy green, black, orange, or pink spots are mold. For a sourdough that is just starting out, the safest thing to do is to throw it away and start again, cleaning the jar well.

**It has risen so much that it has come out of the jar.** Good sign. Use a larger jar or reduce the amount.

## What to do with discards

During sourdough creation, discards from the first few days are not yet stable sourdough and are best discarded. From the second week onwards, however, the discards can be used in pancakes, crepes, crackers or biscuits, where they are mixed with baking powder or simply add flavour.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I make sourdough with white flour from scratch?

Yes, but it usually takes longer to start and is easier for it to stall. If you only have white flour, add a tablespoon of whole wheat or rye flour in the first feedings.

### Should we add fruit, honey or yogurt?

No. They are unnecessary additions that can introduce foreign microorganisms and confuse the process. Flour and water are enough.

### When can I make bread?

When it consistently meets the conditions of a ready sourdough, usually between days 10 and 14. If you use it before, the bread will rise little and will be dense. With a little more patience, the result will be much better. The next step is to learn how to [maintain your sourdough](/recetas-y-guias/cuidar-y-alimentar-la-masa-madre/) long term.

## In summary

Wholemeal flour, warm water, a place at about 25°C and regular feedings. Be wary of the big rise on the third day, don't give up on the calm of the fourth and fifth days, and wait for the dough to double its volume constantly in a few hours. When it does, you will have a crop that, well cared for, can accompany you for years.
