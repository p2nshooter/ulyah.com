Homemade preserves in glass jars are part of the tradition of many Spanish families: tomato in summer, roasted peppers, jams, peaches in syrup. Well made, they allow you to enjoy seasonal products all year round. Done poorly, they can be dangerous. The main threat has its own name: **botulism**, a rare but very serious poisoning.

This guide is not intended to scare you, but rather to clearly explain which preserves can be made safely in a home kitchen, with a pot and boiling water, and which require other methods. If you follow these rules, you can fill your pantry with peace of mind.

## Botulism: why canning requires rigor

Dietary botulism is caused by a toxin produced by the bacteria *Clostridium botulinum*. This bacteria lives in the soil and is naturally present in many vegetables and other foods in the form of **spores**, very resistant structures.

The spores are harmless under normal conditions. The problem appears when they find the ideal environment to germinate and produce the toxin:

- **Absence of oxygen**, like inside a closed jar.
- **Low acidity**, with a pH above 4.6.
- **Humidity.**
- **Ambient temperature.**

A closed jar of low-acid vegetables stored in the pantry meets exactly those conditions. And most importantly: **spores resist boiling water**. At 100°C, which is the maximum temperature of the water bath, they are not reliably destroyed.

The toxin, on the other hand, has no smell or taste in many cases, so a contaminated canned product may appear normal. That's why the safety of canning doesn't depend on how the jar looks, but on following a proper method from the beginning.

## The golden rule: acidity

What allows you to make safe preserves in a bain-marie is **acidity**. Below a pH of 4.6, *Clostridium botulinum* spores cannot germinate or produce toxin. So the big division is this:

| Acidic foods, pH 4.6 or less | Low acid foods, pH greater than 4.6 |
|---|---|
| Most fruits: apple, plum, peach, apricot, cherry, berries | Vegetables: green beans, asparagus, peppers, artichokes, mushrooms, corn |
| Jams and fruit preserves | Legumes |
| Pickles in vinegar prepared with proven recipes | Meats, fish and broths |
| Lactic fermented foods like sauerkraut | Vegetable mixes, such as ratatouille or sauces with onion |
| Tomato, **only if acidified** | Unacidified tomato mixed with other vegetables |

- **Acidic foods** can be preserved with a water bath treatment, in boiling water, for the appropriate time.
- **Low acid foods** require pressure treatment, at around 116–121°C, which can only be achieved with a sterilizer or autoclave designed for canning. A conventional kitchen pressure cooker is not a reliable substitute, because it does not guarantee the necessary temperature or time and is not validated for this use. The domestic alternative for these foods is to freeze them or acidify them with a proven recipe, such as a vinegar pickle.

## The case of the tomato

The tomato is on the limit. Many varieties have a pH close to 4.6 or even higher, especially very ripe tomatoes or low-acid varieties. That is why food safety guides recommend **always acidify the tomato** before packaging it in a bain-marie:

- Add **one tablespoon of bottled lemon juice per 500 ml jar**, or two per liter jar.
- Or add a quarter teaspoon of citric acid per 500 ml.

Bottled lemon juice is used because its acidity is constant; that of fresh lemons varies. The lemon is barely noticeable in the flavor, and if you want, you can offset it with a pinch of sugar.

If you add onion, garlic, pepper or other vegetables to the tomato, the mixture becomes less acidic and is no longer safe for a double boiler with the usual times.

## Garlic and herbs in oil

A warning that deserves its own section: **garlic, fresh herbs, mushrooms or vegetables submerged in oil** are an oxygen-free and low-acid environment, ideal for botulism. Never store garlic in oil at room temperature. If you make homemade flavored oils, keep them in the refrigerator and consume them within a few days, or use dry ingredients.

## Necessary material

- Glass jars designed for canning, without cracks or damaged edges.
- New covers or in perfect condition. Screw caps with inner gasket can be reused if they are intact, but it is recommended to use new caps when possible.
- A tall pot, which allows the jars to be covered with 2 or 3 cm of water.
- A rack or cloth for the bottom of the pot, so that the jars do not touch the metal directly.
- Jar tongs, a wide-mouth funnel, and a clean cloth.

## Step by step of the bain-marie

1. **Check the jars.** Discard any that have cracks or chips on the rim.
2. **Wash** jars and lids with hot soapy water and rinse well.
3. **Keep the jars warm** until filling, in hot water or in the oven at 100°C. This way you will prevent them from breaking due to the temperature contrast.
4. **Fill the jars** with the hot food, leaving the free space indicated in the recipe, usually one centimeter.
5. **Remove air bubbles** by running a plastic spatula inside.
6. **Clean the edges** with a clean, damp cloth.
7. **Close** the lids firmly, without forcing.
8. **Place the jars** in the pot on the rack, without them touching each other.
9. **Cover with hot water** up to 2-3 cm above the lids.
10. **Bring to a boil** and start counting the time **from when the water boils**.
11. **Maintain a constant boil** for the entire time indicated.
12. **Remove the jars** with tongs and let them cool on a cloth, without moving them, between 12 and 24 hours.

## Indicative times

The times depend on the food, the size of the jar and the altitude. Always follow a proven recipe from a reliable source. As a guide, for jars up to 500 ml at sea level:

| Food | Time in the water bath |
|---|---|
| Jams and preserves | 10 minutes |
| Fruits in syrup | 20 to 25 minutes |
| Pickles in vinegar | 10 to 15 minutes |
| Acidified crushed tomato | 35 to 40 minutes |
| Acidified whole tomato | 40 to 45 minutes |

If you live above 300 meters above sea level, water boils at less than 100°C and the times must be increased. A common rule is to add 5 minutes between 300 and 900 meters and 10 minutes between 900 and 1,800 meters.

## Check vacuum

After cooling, check each jar:

- The lid should be slightly **sunken in the center**.
- When you press it, **it should not move or "click"**.
- If you lift the jar holding it only by the lid, carefully and on a soft surface, it should hold.

If a jar has not been emptied, store it in the refrigerator and consume it within a few days, or repeat the treatment in the first 24 hours with a new lid.

## Label and store

Label each jar with the contents and date. Store them without the screw ring, if possible, or with it loose, in a cool, dry and dark place. Consume canned food preferably within a year.

## How to detect spoiled preserves

**Discard without testing** any jar that contains:

- Lid that is bulging or has lost its vacuum.
- Liquid that comes out under pressure or bubbles when opened.
- Strange smell.
- Mold, even if it is on the lid.
- Cloudy liquid in preserves that should be clear, or an obvious color change.

To dispose of it, do not open it indoors if the lid is bulging. Wrap it in a closed bag and throw it in the trash. Wash any surface that may have come into contact with the contents with diluted bleach.

If someone presents symptoms such as double vision, drooping eyelids, difficulty swallowing or speaking, or muscle weakness after consuming a canned food, **go to the emergency room immediately**: botulism is a medical emergency.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do jars have to be sterilized before filling them?

If the water bath treatment lasts 10 minutes or more, it is enough for the jars to be very clean and hot: the treatment itself sanitizes them along with their contents. What is essential is to wash them with hot water and soap, check them one by one and keep them warm until it is time to fill them.

### Can I reuse jars and lids from purchased products?

Glass jars in good condition are used to store food in the refrigerator or to ferment. For pantry preserves, on the other hand, it is advisable to use jars designed for this use and, above all, new lids: the lids of industrial products are designed for a single closure, and a worn gasket is the most common cause of a jar not vacuuming.

### Is it enough to turn the jars upside down?

No. Turning hot jars upside down sometimes produces a seal, but does not subject the contents to the necessary heat for long enough and is uneven. To preserve at room temperature, the reliable method is a water bath with the times of a proven recipe.

## In summary

Only acidic foods are treated in a bain-marie: fruits, jams, pickles in vinegar and acidified tomatoes. Vegetables, legumes, meats and fish need a pressure treatment that cannot be achieved in a normal pot. Use jars in good condition, follow proven recipes, respect the times, check the vacuum and discard without tasting any suspicious jars. With these rules, the home pantry is safe and delicious.
