Kefir, yogurt, kombucha, sauerkraut, kimchi or fermented pickles: in recent years, fermented foods have gone from being a domestic tradition to starring on entire shelves in supermarkets, often accompanied by eye-catching health promises. Some of these promises are scientifically based, others are exaggerations, and many simply have not been studied.

At QKB we like fermented ones for their flavor, their history and the pleasure of making them at home. Precisely for this reason we want to honestly explain what is known and what is not. This article is informative and does not replace the advice of a health professional.

## Fermented doesn't always mean probiotic

It is the most common confusion. A **fermented food** is any food transformed by the action of microorganisms. A **probiotic**, according to the definition proposed in 2001 by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations and the World Health Organization, is a live microorganism that, administered in adequate quantities, provides a benefit to the health of those who consume it.

For something to be considered a probiotic, therefore, three conditions are necessary: ​​that the microorganisms are alive, that they are in sufficient quantity and that a specific benefit has been demonstrated with that specific strain. Many fermented foods do not meet the first condition:

- **Bread**, including sourdough bread, is baked, and the heat kills yeast and bacteria.
- **Beer** and **Wine** are usually filtered or pasteurized.
- Many industrial pickles and sauerkrauts are pasteurized to extend their shelf life.
- **Vinegar pickles** are not fermented: they are directly acidified with vinegar.

Other fermented foods, such as yogurt, kefir, unpasteurized homemade sauerkraut, or raw kombucha, do contain live microorganisms. But the fact that they are alive does not in itself prove that they provide a concrete benefit.

## What does have solid support

### Yogurt and lactose digestion

It is the best established example. The European Food Safety Authority accepted in 2010 that live yoghurt cultures improve the digestion of lactose in people who have difficulty digesting it. The reason is that the bacteria in yogurt produce the enzyme that breaks down lactose and remain active for a time in the intestine. That's why many people with mild lactose intolerance tolerate yogurt better than milk.

### Fermented foods as nutritious foods

Beyond the microorganisms, fermented foods retain a good part of the nutrients of the original food. Yogurt and kefir provide protein, calcium and B vitamins. Fermented vegetables provide fiber. Fermentation can also reduce some compounds that hinder the absorption of minerals, such as phytates from grains, an effect well documented in long-fermented breads.

### A study that sparked interest

In 2021, a team from Stanford University published a study with 36 healthy adults over ten weeks in the journal *Cell*. Those who significantly increased their consumption of fermented foods showed a greater diversity of their intestinal microbiota and a reduction in several markers of inflammation in the blood, compared to those who increased their fiber consumption.

It is an interesting result, but it should be read with caution: it was a small, short-term study with healthy people. It suggests a promising line of research, it does not prove that fermented foods prevent or cure diseases.

## What is not yet proven

Claims circulate on the Internet such as that kombucha "detoxifies the liver", that kefir "cures" serious digestive problems or that fermented foods "strengthen the immune system" in general. As of today, there is not enough evidence in humans to support these claims.

In the European Union, in fact, the word "probiotic" cannot be used in food advertising as a health claim without specific authorization, and authorities have rejected most applications submitted for lack of evidence. That doesn't mean that fermented foods are useless, but that science is still working to understand what effects they have, on whom, and in what amounts.

## Aspects to take into account

Fermented foods are safe and healthy foods for most people, but there are nuances that should be known.

### salt

Fermented vegetables, such as sauerkraut, kimchi, or pickles, contain a lot of salt. The World Health Organization recommends not exceeding 5 g of salt per day for adults. If you follow a low-salt diet, take into account the salt content of these foods, or rinse them lightly before eating them.

### Histamine

Biogenic amines, such as histamine, can be formed during fermentation. Most people metabolize them without problems, but those with histamine intolerance may notice symptoms such as headache, flushing, or digestive discomfort with fermented foods.

### sugar and alcohol

Kombucha and water kefir contain some residual sugar and small amounts of alcohol. They are not calorie-free or completely alcohol-free drinks.

### Acidity and teeth

Acidic drinks, like kombucha, can affect tooth enamel if sipped throughout the day. It is better to take them at a specific time and rinse your mouth with water.

### Discomfort when starting

Some people notice gas or bloating when they start taking fermented foods in quantity. The reasonable thing is to introduce them little by little.

## How to read the label of a commercial fermented product

If you're looking for live microorganisms, the label gives useful clues:

- **Yogurt**: in Spain, the quality standard for yogurt requires that it contain live ferments in sufficient quantities until the best-before date. When the product has been heat treated after fermenting, it should be called “post-fermentation pasteurized yogurt,” and no longer contains live ferments.
- **Sauerkraut and fermented vegetables**: Jars sold at room temperature are usually pasteurized. Those that contain live ferments are kept in the refrigerated shelf and expressly indicate so.
- **Kombucha and water kefir**: raw versions are usually sold refrigerated; Those kept on regular shelves have often been pasteurized or filtered.
- **Pickles**: If vinegar appears in the ingredients list, they have most likely been acidified without fermenting.

None of these options are "bad": a pasteurized sauerkraut still provides fiber and flavor. It is simply a good idea to know what you are buying and not pay for promises that the product cannot fulfill.

## Fermenting at home safely: the essentials

Most home ferments are safe when a few rules are followed: use the right amount of salt, always keep the vegetables covered by the brine, work with clean jars and hands, and ferment at a moderate temperature. The acidity produced by lactic bacteria is what protects the food, and that is why a fermented product that does not acidify, that smells rotten or that has colored mold on the vegetables should be discarded. We explain it in detail in the guide for [fermented vegetables in brine](/recetas-y-guias/verduras-fermentadas-en-salmuera/) and, for dairy products, in the guide for [milk kefir](/recetas-y-guias/kefir-de-leche/).

## Who should be especially careful

Homemade fermented foods, which do not go through industry controls, require more caution in some groups:

- **Immunocompromised people**, undergoing cancer treatment, transplants or immunosuppressive medication. You should consult your doctor before taking homemade fermented foods with live microorganisms.
- **Pregnant**, especially with products made with raw milk or with fermentations of uncertain results, such as homemade kombucha.
- **Babies and small children**, for whom controlled commercial products should be preferred.

For everyone, the basic rule is the same: a fermented product with mold, a bad smell or a strange appearance is discarded without tasting it. On our [food safety](/seguridad-alimentaria/) page we explain the criteria we follow.

## How to incorporate fermented foods with common sense

If you like them, fermented foods can be part of a varied and balanced diet. Some reasonable ideas:

- A natural yogurt or a glass of kefir for breakfast or snack.
- A tablespoon of sauerkraut or fermented vegetables as a garnish.
- A small glass of kombucha or water kefir as an occasional alternative to soda.

It is not necessary to take them all, neither daily nor in large quantities. What the general evidence says about healthy eating remains valid: plenty of vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains and nuts, with fermented fruits as another complement.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is sourdough bread probiotic?

No, because baking kills microorganisms. It may have other advantages, such as easier digestion for some people or better use of minerals, but it does not contain live microorganisms.

### Are homemade fermented products better than commercial ones?

Homemade ones may contain a greater variety of live microorganisms, especially if commercial ones are pasteurized. In exchange, commercial companies have quality control that homeowners do not have. Both can be part of a healthy diet.

### Can I eat fermented foods if I take antibiotics?

In general yes, although it is advisable to ask the doctor or pharmacist, especially in long-term treatments or if you have other illnesses.

## In summary

Fermented foods are nutritious, tasty and have a long history. Some benefits, such as better lactose digestion with yogurt, are well demonstrated; others are being investigated and many of those advertised do not have sufficient evidence. Enjoy them for what they are, prepare them with hygiene and consult with a professional if you belong to a risk group.
