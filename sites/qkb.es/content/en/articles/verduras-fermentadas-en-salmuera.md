If sauerkraut is the fermentation of dry-cut and salted cabbage, brine fermentation is its versatile cousin: whole or chopped vegetables, covered in salted water, that ferment for a few days or weeks until they become acidic and crisp. With this method you can ferment carrots, cauliflower, cucumbers, radishes, peppers, chives, turnips, asparagus and many other vegetables.

The advantage is that you don't need to massage or press anything: you cut the vegetables, place them in a jar, cover them with brine and let nature work. This guide explains the principles that make it work and how to apply them to different vegetables.

## The four principles of brine fermentation

Before the recipes, it is worth understanding why it works. There are four principles that do not change, no matter what you ferment.

### 1. Salt in the right proportion

Salt selects microorganisms. Lactic acid bacteria tolerate moderate brine well, while many spoilage bacteria and molds are inhibited. The most common concentration ranges from 2 to 5%, that is, from 20 to 50 g of salt per liter of water.

### 2. Without oxygen

Lactic acid bacteria work without oxygen; molds need it. That is why vegetables should always be below the brine. Anything that sticks out of the liquid is an open door to mold.

### 3. Increasing acidity

As lactic acid bacteria ferment the sugars in vegetables, they produce lactic acid and the pH drops. When the medium is sufficiently acidic, below a pH of 4.6 and usually around 3.5, the vegetable is protected against most harmful microorganisms, including botulism bacteria.

### 4. Moderate temperature

Between 18 and 22 °C, fermentation is stable and produces good aromas. With more heat, it goes faster but the vegetables soften faster; When cold, it moves very slowly.

## How to prepare the brine

For a 3% brine, dissolve 30 g of non-iodine salt in one liter of dechlorinated water. If your tap water has a lot of chlorine, boil it and let it cool, or use filtered or mineral water.

A practical way to do this is to heat part of the water, dissolve the salt and top up with cold water. The brine should be at room temperature before pouring it over the vegetables.

### What concentration to use depending on the vegetable

| Vegetable | Recommended brine | Indicative time at 20 °C |
|---|---|---|
| Carrot sticks | 2.5 to 3% | 7 to 14 days |
| Cauliflower in florets | 3 % | 7 to 14 days |
| Whole or halved radishes | 2.5 to 3% | 5 to 10 days |
| Whole pickles | 3.5 to 5% | 7 to 21 days |
| Peppers in strips | 3 % | 7 to 14 days |
| Chives or shallots | 3 % | 10 to 21 days |
| peeled garlic | 3 to 5% | 3 to 6 weeks |

Pickles and other water-heavy vegetables need more concentrated brines to stay crisp. Dense vegetables, such as carrots, accept lower concentrations.

## Base recipe: fermented carrots with dill and garlic

It is a perfect recipe to start with, because the carrot is firm, sweet and ferments very reliably.

### Ingredients for a one liter jar

- 500 g of carrots.
- 2 cloves of garlic peeled and crushed.
- 2 sprigs of fresh dill, or a teaspoon of dill seeds.
- 1 teaspoon black peppercorns.
- 500 ml of 3% brine (15 g of salt in 500 ml of water).

### Preparation

1. Peel the carrots and cut them into sticks the height of the jar, leaving about 3 cm free at the top.
2. Place the garlic, dill and pepper in the bottom of the jar.
3. Place the carrot sticks upright, tightly, so they do not float.
4. Pour the brine until the carrots are completely covered.
5. Place a weight on top to keep them submerged.
6. Close loosely or with a valved lid, and leave the jar on a plate at room temperature.

Starting on the third or fourth day, the brine will become cloudy and bubbles will appear. Test from the first week; When they are sour and you like them, store them in the refrigerator.

## Fermented Pickles: How to Keep Them Crispy

Pickles fermented in brine, in the Central European style, are very different from pickles in vinegar: more complex, less aggressive and with a mild acid. Their great challenge is that they do not soften.

- Use small, firm, very fresh cucumbers, the gherkin type. Large salad cucumbers soften easily.
- Cut a millimeter from the end where the flower was: it contains enzymes that soften the cucumber.
- Add some leaves rich in tannins to the jar, such as vine leaves, oak leaves, bay leaves or a little black tea. Tannins help maintain texture.
- Use 3.5–5% brine and ferment in a cool place.

## Aromatics that you can add

Spices and herbs are not mandatory, but they add character. Some classic combinations:

- **Dill, garlic and black pepper**, for pickles and carrots.
- **Cumin, coriander grains and chilli**, for cauliflower.
- **Ginger and fresh turmeric**, for carrots and cauliflower.
- **Oregano, garlic and bay leaf**, for peppers.
- **Fennel and orange peel**, for radishes and turnips.

Whole spices work better than ground spices, which cloud the brine.

## How to keep vegetables submerged

It is the practical part that generates the most doubts. Some options:

- **Glass fermentation weights**, which fit into wide mouth jars.
- **A small jar filled with water**, placed inside the mouth of the large jar.
- **A freezer bag with brine**, which conforms to the shape of the jar and, if broken, does not dilute the fermentation.
- **A large cabbage leaf** folded over the vegetables, with a weight on top.
- **Place the vegetables very tightly**, so that they hold together.

## Normal signals and alarm signals

During fermentation it is normal:

- Let the brine become cloudy and milky.
- Let bubbles and some foam appear.
- That a white sediment forms at the bottom, composed of lactic acid bacteria.
- May the colors of the vegetables change and fade a little.
- A smooth white film appears on the surface, usually yeast, which is removed with a spoon.

These are warning signs:

- Fluffy, hairy, colorful mold on vegetables.
- Slimy or mushy vegetables like porridge.
- Rotten, garbage or very unpleasant smell, different from the normal sour smell.

If you see any of these signs, throw away the entire jar without tasting it.

## What to expect day by day

A fermentation in brine almost always follows the same script, although the rhythm changes with the temperature:

| Moment at about 20 °C | What you will see |
|---|---|
| Days 1 and 2 | hardly any changes; the brine is still clear |
| Days 2 to 4 | first bubbles, somewhat cloudy brine, slightly acidic smell |
| Days 5 to 7 | a lot of activity, evident acid taste when tasting a piece |
| Second week | the activity calms down, the flavor is rounded |
| After | to the refrigerator when you like the acidity point |

To test, remove a piece with a clean fork, never with your fingers, and check again that the rest is well submerged.

## Check acidity

If you want objective confirmation, food pH strips or a simple pH meter solve the doubt. Dip the strip in some of the removed brine with a clean spoon: a finished ferment is usually around a pH of 3.5, and in any case below 4.6. If after two weeks the brine has not acidified, something has gone wrong, for example a temperature that is too low or too little activity, and it is prudent to discard it and start again.

## From the jar to the table

Fermented vegetables go very well with simple dishes: carrots with a plate of lentils, fermented cauliflower with cured cheese, radishes in a potato salad or peppers in a tortilla sandwich. Serve them in small quantities, as a condiment, and add a little of their brine to dressings.

## Conservation

Once fermented, brined vegetables are kept in the refrigerator for several months, as long as they are still covered by the liquid. Its flavor will continue to evolve slowly, becoming more acidic.

It is not advisable to close hermetically and store fermented vegetables at room temperature believing that they are sterilized preserves: they are still active and generate gas. Its place is the refrigerator.

## The brine is also used

The brine from a good fermentation is acidic, salty and aromatic. You can use it to dress salads, to give a touch to cold soups, to marinate meats or as a starter for the next batch: adding a splash of brine from a previous fermentation speeds up the start of the new one.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I reduce salt?

Below 2% increases the risk of vegetables becoming soft or spoiled. If you want a less salty result, desalt the vegetables with water just before serving.

### Why has garlic turned blue or green?

It is a natural chemical reaction of some compounds in garlic with acids. It is not dangerous and does not mean that it is contaminated.

### Can I mix several vegetables in the same jar?

Yes. Mixtures of cauliflower, carrot and pepper, for example, are very good. Choose a brine suitable for the most delicate vegetables.

### Can it be fermented with tap water?

If it has little chlorine, yes. If you have too much, chlorine can stop bacteria. Boiling and cooling it eliminates much of the free chlorine.

## In summary

2 to 5% brine, always submerged vegetables, a cool place and patience. These four elements are enough to transform almost any vegetable into an acidic and crunchy fermented product. Start with carrots, learn to read the signs on the jar, and when you feel confident, experiment with blends and aromatics.
