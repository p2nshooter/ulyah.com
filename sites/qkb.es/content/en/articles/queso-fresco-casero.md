Fresh cheese is the best gateway to the world of homemade cheese making. It does not need a maturation cave or months of waiting: in one afternoon a liter of milk is transformed into a tender, white and soft cheese, similar to the Burgos cheese found in any Spanish market. All you need is milk, rennet, a little salt, a thermometer and some patience.

Making cheese at home is also something of a small miracle. Watching liquid milk turn into a firm curd that you can cut with a knife helps you understand what cheese really is: concentrated milk with much of the water removed. In this guide we explain the complete process, why each step works and how to solve the most common problems.

## The ingredients

For a cheese of about 250 to 300 grams:

- **2 liters of pasteurized whole milk**, cow's, goat's or mixture. Avoid UHT milk, which curdles poorly, and raw milk, for safety.
- **Liquid rennet**, the amount indicated by the manufacturer for 2 liters. Many home rennets use between 1 and 2 ml for that amount, but the concentration varies, so always follow the label.
- **A level teaspoon of calcium chloride** (optional, in solution), which helps pasteurized milk form a firmer curd.
- **Fine salt**, to taste: about 6 to 10 grams.
- **Water without chlorine** to dilute the rennet: a little mineral water or boiled and cooled.

## The utensils

- A large stainless steel pot.
- A kitchen thermometer.
- A long knife or spatula to cut the curd.
- A skimmer.
- One or two molds for fresh cheese, with holes. If you don't have one, a strainer lined with clean cheesecloth will do.
- A tray or rack to drain the mold.

Before starting, wash everything with very hot water. In cheesemaking, cleanliness is half of the success.

## Milk: why pasteurized and not UHT

Raw milk can contain dangerous bacteria that pasteurization kills. Cheese factories that work with raw milk follow strict health controls and, in many cases, long maturation processes. At home, for fresh cheeses that are consumed within a few days, the safe option is **pasteurized milk**.

UHT milk, on the other hand, is heated to such high temperatures that it modifies the proteins responsible for forming curd. With it, the rennet barely acts or forms a soft curd that falls apart. Read the label carefully: look for "pasteurized milk" or "fresh milk," which is usually in the refrigerated area of ​​the supermarket.

## Step 1: heat the milk

Pour the milk into the pot and heat it over low heat, stirring occasionally, until it reaches **between 35 and 37 °C**. It is the temperature at which the rennet works best. Do not exceed 40 °C; If you overdo it, let it cool before continuing.

If you use calcium chloride, add it now, diluted in a couple of tablespoons of non-chlorinated water, and stir well.

## Step 2: add the rennet

Dilute the dose of rennet in about four tablespoons of dechlorinated water. Chlorine in tap water can weaken rennet.

Remove the pot from the heat, add the diluted rennet and stir gently for about thirty seconds with up and down movements. Then, stop the movement of the milk by passing the spoon backwards a couple of times. From here on, **do not move the pot**: the milk needs stillness to curdle.

Cover the pot and let it sit in a warm place for **between 40 and 60 minutes**. If your kitchen is cold, wrap the pot in a cloth or leave it in the turned-off oven.

## Step 3: Check the clean cut

The milk is ready when it forms a firm curd, like a flan. To check, insert a clean knife at an angle, raise it a little and observe: if the curd opens with a clean crack and a transparent greenish-yellow liquid appears in the gap, the whey, it is ready. Cheesemakers call this "clean cut."

If the curd is still soft or falling apart, wait another ten or fifteen minutes.

## Step 4: Cut the Curd

Cut the curd into cubes to release the whey. For a soft fresh cheese, the cubes should be large, about 2 or 3 centimeters:

1. Make parallel vertical cuts along the length of the pot.
2. Make another series of cuts perpendicular to the first, forming a grid.
3. With the knife at an angle, make diagonal cuts to divide the blocks deeply.

Let it rest for five minutes. You will see how the cubes begin to separate and the serum surrounds them.

## Step 5: Stir gently

Stir very gently for five to ten minutes, with a slotted spoon, so that the cubes release a little more whey without breaking. For a very soft fresh cheese, stir little; for a slightly firmer one, a little more.

## Step 6: mold

Using the slotted spoon, transfer the curd to the molds, placed on a rack or a deep plate. Fill them to the top: the curd will go down as it drains.

You can reserve some of the whey to make cottage cheese; We explain how in another guide.

## Step 7: Drain and Flip

Let the cheese drain at room temperature for about 30 minutes. Then, carefully turn it inside the mold: this way the cheese will have a uniform shape on both sides. Repeat the flipping one or two more times over the next hour.

## Step 8: salt

There are two ways to salt fresh cheese:

- **Dry salt:** Sprinkle fine salt on the top side, let it sit for half an hour, turn the cheese over and salt the other side.
- **Brine:** Submerge the firm cheese in salted water (about 150 to 180 grams of salt per liter of water) for 20 to 40 minutes, depending on size and taste.

You can also add the salt directly to the curd before molding it, mixing it gently.

## Step 9: Refrigerate

Store the cheese in the refrigerator, inside the mold and on a rack to continue draining, for a few hours. Then, transfer it to a closed container. Consume it in **three or four days**.

## Homemade molds

There is no need to buy cheese molds to get started. A food-grade plastic container, clean and unscratched, with several small holes in the base and sides, fulfills the same function; A small colander lined with a fine cloth also works. Choose a container of a size that the curd fills almost to the edge: in a mold that is too wide, the cheese sits low and dries out sooner. Wash the molds with hot soapy water and blanch them before each use.

## A fried cheese for the weekend

If you prefer a somewhat firmer texture, leave the salted cheese for a few days in the refrigerator, uncovered, on a rack and in a large container, turning it every day and drying up any water it releases. After two or three days a thin skin forms and the pasta gains body and flavor. It is still a fresh cheese: consume it within a week of making it and discard any moldy or bad-smelling pieces.

## Why it works: science in two minutes

Milk is a mixture of water, fat, sugar (lactose), minerals and proteins. Among proteins, **caseins** form small structures called micelles, which are in suspension. Rennet contains an enzyme, chymosin, which cuts a part of those caseins. Without that part, the micelles join together and form a network that traps the fat and water: the curd.

As the curd is cut and stirred, the network contracts and expels the whey. The smaller the cubes and the more you stir, the drier and firmer the cheese will be.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | probable cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Milk does not curdle | UHT milk, expired rennet or chlorinated water | Use fresh pasteurized milk and undamaged rennet |
| Very soft curd | Low temperature, little rennet or highly treated milk | Maintain 35–37 °C and add calcium chloride |
| Very dry cheese | Small cubes or too much stirring | Cut bigger and stir less |
| bitter taste | Excess rennet | Adjust dosage according to label |
| Flavorless cheese | little salt | Salt a little more or serve with flaked salt |

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use semi-skimmed milk?

Yes, but the cheese will be less creamy and the yield somewhat lower. Whole milk gives better results.

### Can I make it without rennet?

You can coagulate milk with acid, such as lemon juice or vinegar, but the result is another type of cheese, more similar to cottage cheese or paneer. For a fresh Burgos-type cheese, rennet is necessary.

### How much cheese comes from a liter of milk?

With cow's milk, between 120 and 180 grams of fresh cheese per liter, approximately, depending on how drained you leave it. Goat and sheep milk, richer in solids, give something more.

### Can I freeze it?

It is not recommended: fresh cheese loses its texture when thawed.

## In summary

Pasteurized milk at 35–37 °C, diluted rennet, one hour of rest, clean cutting, large cubes, shaping, turning and salt. With these steps, in one afternoon you will have a tender and delicious homemade fresh cheese. From here on, each new batch will teach you something: the temperature of your kitchen, the behavior of your milk and the exact point of curd that you like best.
