Those who start making cheese at home usually focus on rennet, temperatures and molds. However, the most important decision is made before, in the supermarket aisle: **which milk to buy**. The same recipe can give a perfect cheese or a total failure depending on the milk chosen.

In this guide we explain how heat treatment, animal species, fat content and other factors influence, and what is the best option for each homemade preparation.

## Heat treatment: what matters most

In Spain, the milk sold may have received different treatments:

### pasteurized milk

It is heated to a moderate temperature, usually around 72°C for a few seconds, and cooled quickly. This treatment eliminates pathogenic microorganisms without altering the proteins too much. It is sold refrigerated, with a short shelf life, and is often labeled as "fresh milk" or "pasteurized milk."

**It is the best option for making cheese at home.** It sets well and is safe.

### UHT milk

It is heated to very high temperatures, above 135°C, for a few seconds. It can be stored outside the refrigerator for months. It is the most common carton milk in Spanish supermarkets.

The problem for the cheesemaker is that this intense heat modifies the whey proteins, which stick to the caseins and prevent the rennet from forming a firm curd. **UHT milk is not suitable for rennet cheeses**, although it works for yogurt, kefir, acidic cottage cheese and some dairy desserts.

### Ultra-pasteurized or refrigerated "long-life" milk

Some refrigerated milk receives intermediate treatments to last longer. Sometimes they come together well and sometimes they don't; It depends on the brand. If one milk gives you problems, try another.

### raw milk

Raw milk has not received any heat treatment. The cheese factories that use it follow strict health controls and, in many cheeses, a prolonged maturation that reduces the risks. At home, raw milk may contain bacteria such as *Listeria monocytogenes*, *Brucella*, *Campylobacter* or pathogenic strains of *E. coli*. In fresh cheeses, which are consumed in a few days without ripening, this risk is not reduced.

**For safety, we do not recommend using raw milk to make fresh cheeses at home**, and in no case for pregnant, children, elderly or immunosuppressed people.

## How to identify suitable milk in the supermarket

- Look for it in the **refrigerated area**.
- The label should say "pasteurized" and not "UHT."
- Check that the expiration date is a few days or weeks, not months.
- If possible, choose **non-homogenized** or mildly homogenized milk, which forms somewhat better curds, although homogenized also works.

## Cow, goat or sheep

### cow's milk

It is the easiest to find and the cheapest. It has a mild flavor and produces fresh, tender and dairy cheeses. Its solids content is lower than that of sheep, so the yield is moderate.

### goat milk

It has a more intense, slightly acidic and characteristic flavor. Its fat globules are smaller and its curd is more delicate, so it should be cut and handled with more care. It produces fresh white and very aromatic cheeses, highly appreciated in many Spanish regions.

### sheep's milk

It is the richest in fat and protein, which translates into a much higher yield: much more cheese is obtained from the same amount of milk. Its flavor is intense and unctuous. It is the milk of such emblematic cheeses as Manchego, Idiazábal or Torta del Casar. It is more difficult to find pasteurized in general stores, although it is becoming more common.

### Mixtures

Many traditional Spanish cheeses are made with a mixture of milks. At home, mixing cow and goat, for example, gives cheeses with a very pleasant intermediate flavor.

## The fat

- **Whole milk:** gives creamier, more flavorful cheeses with better performance. It is the recommended one.
- **Semi-skimmed:** firmer and less tasty cheeses.
- **Skimmed:** dry and rubbery cheeses. Not recommended for fresh cheeses.

For especially creamy cheeses, some recipes add a little cream to the milk.

## Calcium and calcium chloride

Calcium is essential for rennet to form a good curd. Pasteurization and prolonged refrigeration slightly reduce available calcium. That is why many domestic cheesemakers add a small amount of **calcium chloride** dissolved in water before rennet. It does not change the flavor and improves the firmness of the curd, especially with pasteurized cow's milk.

## How to use calcium chloride

Calcium chloride for cheesemaking is sold as a liquid solution, usually 30 or 33%. Always follow the dosage indicated by the manufacturer; As a guide, use about a quarter teaspoon of solution for every 4 liters of milk. Dilute it in a little chlorine-free water, add it to the warm milk and stir well a few minutes **before** adding the rennet, never at the same time, because both could react in the glass. More is not better: too much gives a bitter taste and a curd that is too hard.

## A quick test before a big batch

If you are going to try a new milk, don't risk four liters. Heat a 250 ml glass to 32 °C, add a very small amount of diluted rennet, proportional to the recipe, stir for a few seconds and let it rest. After ten or fifteen minutes, tilt the glass: if the milk has turned into a gel that separates cleanly from the wall, the milk curdles well. If it's still runny or forms soft clumps, look for another brand before making the full batch.

## Save the milk until cheese day

Buy milk as close to the day you plan to use it as possible and store it in the coldest part of the refrigerator, not in the door. Do not freeze it: freezing alters the fat and proteins, and the curd comes out worse. If the milk has been out of the cold for several hours, do not use it for cheese.

## Lactose-free milk

Lactose-free milk is obtained by adding an enzyme that breaks down lactose into glucose and galactose. Many of these milks are UHT, which already rules them out for rennet cheese. Those that are pasteurized can curdle, although the flavor is somewhat sweeter. Keep in mind that many fresh cheeses have little lactose because some of it goes with the whey.

## milk powder

Some recipes use reconstituted powdered milk. For rennet cheeses it gives irregular results, because the drying process also affects the proteins. It can be used to enrich homemade yogurt.

## Farm and local milk

It is increasingly easier to find milk from small farms, pasteurized and packaged in glass bottles, in markets and neighborhood stores. It is often an excellent choice for home cheesemaking: it is often gently pasteurized and, in some cases, not homogenized, which promotes the formation of a firm curd. Its flavor can vary throughout the year depending on the animals' diet, something that is noticeable in the cheese and is part of its charm. Ask the producer what treatment the milk receives before using it.

## Frequent errors

1. **Buying UHT milk to make fresh cheese.** It is the most common mistake.
2. **Use milk close to its expiration date**, which may have altered acidity.
3. **Thinking that raw milk is always better.** At home, safety must come first.
4. **Do not adjust the recipe to the type of milk**: goat milk requires more delicacy; the sheep yields more.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I boil raw milk to make cheese?

Boiling milk eliminates pathogens, but reaching high temperatures modifies the proteins and the milk will curdle worse. Some home cheesemakers pasteurize raw milk by heating it to about 63°C for 30 minutes and cooling it rapidly, a slow method that requires a thermometer and monitoring. If you don't have experience, buy already pasteurized milk.

### Why does the same recipe work with one brand and not another?

Because each brand applies somewhat different thermal and homogenization treatments. When you find milk that curdles well, write it down.

### Can you make cheese with vegetable drinks?

Not in the traditional sense: soy, almond or oat drinks do not contain caseins. There are fermented vegetable preparations, but they are another type of product.

### Does organic milk work?

Yes, as long as it meets the same conditions: pasteurized at low temperature and not UHT. Whether milk is organic refers to how the animals are raised and the feed is produced, not the heat treatment. Read the label too.

### How much cheese comes from a liter of milk?

It depends on the type of cheese and milk. As a guide, one liter of cow's milk gives about 150 to 200 g of fresh cheese, about 100 to 130 g of mozzarella and about 100 g of mature cheese after ripening. Sheep's milk, richer in fat and protein, yields much more.

## In summary

To make cheese at home, choose pasteurized, refrigerated, non-UHT whole milk. The cow one is the easiest to get; the goat provides aroma; that of sheep, yield and unctuousness. Add calcium chloride if the curd comes out soft and avoid raw milk for safety. With the right milk, the rest of the process is much easier.
