When someone starts making bread, cheese or fermented foods at home, it is easy to get carried away by specialized stores and fill the cupboards with utensils that are then rarely used. The reality is that most QKB recipes can be made with simple materials, much of which you already have in your kitchen. Some tools, however, make a huge difference and should be purchased from the beginning.

This guide organizes the material for the home workshop into three levels: what is essential, what is useful when you advance and what can wait. For each utensil we explain what it is for and what to keep in mind when choosing it.

## Level 1: the essentials

### digital scale

It is the most important utensil in the workshop. In baking, cheesemaking and fermentation, quantities are expressed by weight because volumes are unreliable: a cup of flour can weigh 120 g or 160 g depending on how it is filled.

- **What to look for:** Accuracy of 1 g, capacity of at least 5 kg and tare function to zero with the container on top.
- **Extra useful:** a small jewelry scale with an accuracy of 0.1 g for weighing minute amounts of yeast, rennet or cultures.

### probe thermometer

We explain it in detail in our guide on [the temperature in the workshop](/recetas-y-guias/la-temperatura-en-el-obrador/). It is used for the milk of yogurt and cheese, the water of dough, the inside of bread, jams and frying oil.

- **What to look for:** Fast reading, range from 0 to 200 °C and possibility to calibrate it.

### glass jars

For fermented foods, yogurts, kefir, sourdough and preserves. The glass does not retain odors, does not react with acids and allows you to see what is happening inside.

- **What to look for:** Wide-mouth jars, which make it easy to fill, clean, and place weights. For canning in a bain-marie, jars designed for that use, with lids in good condition.

### large bowls

For kneading, fermenting dough and mixing vegetables with salt. Better made of glass, stainless steel or resistant food plastic.

### Thick Bottom Casserole

To heat milk without sticking, cook jams and quince. The thick bottom distributes heat and prevents burns. For jams, better wide than tall.

### baker's scraper

A sheet of flexible plastic or metal. It is used to handle sticky dough, divide it, scrape the bowl and clean the table. It's cheap and once you use it, you don't understand how you worked without it.

### cotton cloths

To cover fermentations, flour as a lining for bread baskets, drain curds and cover kombucha jars. Better from dense cotton, without fabric softener or perfume.

## Level 2: what is useful when you advance

### Cheesecloth or cheesecloth

A finely woven cotton cloth for draining fresh cheeses, cottage cheese, labneh or Greek yogurt. It can be replaced with double gauze or a very fine, clean cotton cloth.

### Cheese molds

Perforated containers that shape the cheese and allow the whey to drain. At first you can use a strainer or a plastic food container with holes cut into it.

### Banneton or fermentation basket

A basket, usually made of wicker or natural fiber, in which bread ferments after forming. It keeps its shape and leaves a very pretty spiral mark. A bowl lined with a floured cloth serves the same function.

### Cast iron pot with lid

To bake loaves. It retains the steam released by the dough and creates an environment similar to that of a bakery oven. It must be suitable for 250 °C, including the lid knob.

### Grabbing blade

A razor blade mounted on a handle, for making clean cuts on the surface of bread. A very sharp knife or a new cutter can work.

### Fermentation weights

Glass or ceramic discs that keep the vegetables submerged in the brine. Alternatives: a small jar filled with water, a freezer bag with brine, or a folded cabbage leaf.

### Lids with valve for fermentation

They let the gas out without letting the air in. They are not essential, but they reduce the risk of surface yeast and avoid having to open the jar.

### Mixer or kitchen robot

It makes kneading enriched doughs such as brioche or roscón, which require a lot of time by hand, much more comfortable. For medium hydration breads, hands and folds are sufficient.

### roller

Essential for croissants, puff pastry, ensaimadas and palm trees. A smooth wooden roller without handles offers a lot of control.

### Pastry bag and nozzles

To fill, decorate and, with a star tip, make churros. Better made of resistant fabric or reusable silicone.

### Strips or pH meter

To check the acidity of fermented, kombucha or sourdough. pH strips are cheap and enough to get your bearings.

## Level 3: What to Expect

- **Cheese ripening chamber:** only necessary for long-ripened cured cheeses. Many people use an old refrigerator with a temperature controller.
- **Stone or baking steel:** useful for pizzas and breads without a pot, but not essential.
- **Fermentation chamber:** There are temperature-controlled fermentation boxes, but the oven with the light on or a portable refrigerator serve the same function.
- **Sterilizer or autoclave for preserves:** It is only necessary if you want to preserve low-acid vegetables, legumes, meats or fish, which cannot be treated in a water bath.
- **Cereal mill:** for those who want to grind their own flour. It is an exciting world, but not to begin with.

## Materials: what to avoid

- **Reactive metals**, such as untreated aluminum or untinned copper, in prolonged contact with acidic foods such as fermented foods, kombucha or jams. They can release metals to the food and alter the flavor. Stainless steel is safe.
- **Decorative ceramics of unknown origin**, whose glazes may contain lead that dissolves with acids.
- **Scratched or old plastics**, which accumulate bacteria in the cracks and retain odors.
- **Cracked wooden utensils**, difficult to clean thoroughly.

## Maintenance

- Wash utensils immediately after use: dried dough and curdled milk are much more difficult to clean.
- Dry the iron pot well and lightly oil it to prevent rust.
- Do not wash the banneton with water: let it air dry, brush it and, from time to time, put it in the sun.
- Check jars and lids before each use and discard any damaged ones.
- Boil cheesecloths after use and dry them well.

## Organize the workshop in a normal kitchen

You don't need a special room to have a home workshop, but you do need a little order:

- **A shelf or a box just for the workshop**, with the scale, thermometer, fabrics and molds. Knowing where everything is saves time and avoids using utensils that are not clean.
- **A corner for fermentations**, away from direct sun, the oven and radiators, where the jars can remain for several days without getting in the way. A tray underneath collects the brine that overflows.
- **Separate tables** for bread and fruit, on the one hand, and for raw meat or fish, on the other.
- **Labels and a marker** always at hand: each jar, with its contents and date.
- **Cultures with their own space**: many fans keep kombucha, kefir and sourdough at a certain distance from each other and with their own utensils, so that yeasts and bacteria from each other do not mix.

## Buying second-hand: what's yes and what's not

Flea markets and second-hand stores are a good source of material for the workshop, with some precautions:

- **Yes**: cast iron pots (surface rust is removed and the pot is seasoned again), stainless steel casseroles, glass jars without cracks or dents in the mouth, wooden rolling pins in good condition, working mixers and scales.
- **With care**: old preserving jars, which can be used for fermentation, but whose rubbers and lids should be renewed before using them for canning in a bain-marie.
- **Don't**: Scratched plastics, fermentation baskets with mold stains, used cheesecloths, decorative ceramics of unknown origin, and non-stick pans with damaged coatings.

Wash everything thoroughly before first use, as if it were new.

## A reasonable budget to start

With a digital scale, a probe thermometer, some wide-mouth jars and a scraper, you have everything you need to make yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, fresh cheese, sourdough and good bread. From there, buy each utensil when a specific recipe needs it and you know that you are going to repeat it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a mixer to make bread?

No. Most homemade breads are made very well by hand and with folds. The mixer is useful for enriched doughs and for large quantities.

### Can I use plastic containers to ferment?

If they are made of food-grade plastic, they are in good condition and not scratched, yes. Still, glass is preferable because it does not retain odors or scratch.

### What do I do if I don't have an iron pot?

Bake the bread on a preheated tray and generate steam with a tray of hot water in the lower part of the oven, as we explain in our bread recipes.

## In summary

Scale, thermometer, jars, bowls, a good saucepan, a scraper and some cloths: almost everything is done with that. Add the rest of the utensils little by little, as your recipes call for it, and take special care of the materials that come into contact with acidic foods. A home workshop is not measured by its devices, but by what comes out of it.
