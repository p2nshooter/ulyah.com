Spain is one of the countries in Europe with the greatest diversity of cheeses. The variety of climates, livestock breeds and pastoral traditions has given rise to hundreds of elaborations, many of them protected with a designation of origin. Knowing them helps to enjoy them more, to choose well in the market and, for those who make cheese at home, to understand where the techniques they practice in their kitchen come from.

This guide is not intended to be an exhaustive catalogue, but rather a way to orient yourself: how Spanish cheeses are classified, which are some of the most representative of each area and how to serve them.

## How they are classified

Cheeses can be grouped according to several criteria:

### for the milk

- **Cow**, predominant in the Cantabrian coast and Galicia.
- **Sheep**, typical of the plateau and transhumant grazing areas.
- **Goat**, very present in the Canary Islands, Andalusia, Extremadura and Mediterranean mountain areas.
- **Mixture**, common in Asturias, Cantabria and other areas.

### Due to maturation

- **Fresh:** without ripening or with very few days. White, moist and soft paste.
- **Tender:** short maturation, a few weeks.
- **Semi-cured:** maturation of approximately two to four months.
- **Cured:** longer maturation, several months.
- **Old or aged:** prolonged maturation, intense flavor.

### For the pasta and the crust

- Soft, semi-hard or hard pasta.
- Natural, moldy, washed or smoked rind.
- Blue cheeses, with internal molds.

## fresh cheeses

- **Burgos cheese:** the fresh Spanish cheese par excellence. From cow or sheep's milk, white, moist and soft. It is the model of homemade fresh cheese.
- **Mató:** Catalan fresh cheese, very tender, traditionally served with honey.
- **Fresh goat cheese:** made in many regions, with a more intense flavor.
- **Cottage cheese:** technically different, but similar in use, very present in Spanish tradition.

## The Meseta and the center

- **Manchego:** the most internationally known Spanish cheese. Made from La Mancha sheep's milk, with its characteristic crust marked with the drawing of ancient esparto grasses. It can be semi-cured, cured or old. Its flavor becomes more intense and spicy with maturation.
- **Castilian sheep cheeses:** made in Castilla y León, in a similar style, with flavors that vary depending on the area and maturation.

## Estremadura

- **Torta del Casar:** sheep's cheese curdled with thistle, with a paste so creamy that it is served by cutting the top lid and removing the inside with a spoon or bread. Its flavor is intense, with a slight bitterness characteristic of vegetable rennet.
- **Torta de la Serena:** a close relative, also made from sheep and thistle rennet.
- **Ibores:** goat cheese, often with the rind spread with paprika.

## Cantabrian coast and Galicia

- **Tetilla:** Galician cow cheese, with a very characteristic conical shape, soft paste and mild, milky flavor.
- **Arzúa-Ulloa:** also Galician, cow, creamy and smooth.
- **San Simón da Costa:** Galician, smoked with birch wood, with a shiny toasted crust.
- **Cabrales:** Asturian blue cheese, with a very intense and spicy flavor, matured in natural caves. It can be made with cow's milk or with a mixture of cow, goat and sheep.
- **Cantabrian cheese and Liébana cheeses:** cow's cheese, in Cantabria; those from Liébana can be made from cow, sheep or goat, and some are smoked.
- **Picón Bejes-Tresviso:** blue cheese from Cantabria, with an intense flavor.

## Basque Country and Navarra

- **Idiazábal:** latxa or carranzana sheep cheese, which may or may not be smoked. It has an intense and slightly spicy flavor.
- **Roncal:** Navarrese sheep cheese, with a hard paste and a powerful flavor, with a long pastoral tradition.

## Catalonia and Aragon

- **Garrotxa:** Catalan goat cheese, with a grayish moldy rind and a soft white paste.
- **Tupí:** a traditional Catalan production of cheese fermented in a vessel with brandy.
- **Tronchon Cheese:** from Aragon and Castellón, with a characteristic shape with a depression at the top.

## Balearic Islands

- **Mahón-Menorca:** cow's cheese, square in shape with rounded corners, rind sometimes spread with oil and paprika. It can be tender, semi-cured or cured, with a flavor that ranges from dairy to intense and salty.

## Andalusia and Murcia

- **Murcia cheese with wine:** Murcia-Granada goat cheese, with the rind washed in red wine, which gives it a characteristic maroon color.
- **Payoyo:** goat and sheep cheese from the Sierra de Grazalema, made with native breeds.
- **Goat cheeses from Malaga and other Andalusian mountains.**

## Canary Islands

The Canary Islands have a very rich cheese tradition, especially goat cheese:

- **Majorero:** from Fuerteventura, made from Majorera goat milk, often with the rind spread with paprika or gofio.
- **Palmero:** from La Palma, goat, often smoked.
- **Guide Flower:** from Gran Canaria, made in part with thistle flower vegetable rennet.

## How to serve a Spanish cheese board

1. **Choose between three and five cheeses** that vary in milk, maturation and style: for example, a fresh one, a tender cow cheese, a cured Manchego cheese, a blue cheese and a creamy cake.
2. **Take them out of the refrigerator** half an hour or an hour before serving: the cold dulls the aromas.
3. **Order them from mild to intense** and try in that order.
4. **Cut according to shape**: wedges for round cheeses, thin sheets for cured cheeses, spoonfuls for cakes.
5. **Accompany with simplicity**: bread, nuts, quince, grapes, figs, honey or olives.

## Buy cheese wisely

- **Read the label:** it indicates the type of milk, whether it is pasteurized or raw, and the maturation time. When a cheese is made with raw milk, European regulations require it to be expressly indicated.
- **Look for the designation of origin seal** if you want authentic cheese from an area: it guarantees that it has been made according to its specifications.
- **Buy by the cut** at cheese shops and markets when you can. Ask to try it beforehand and have them cut just the right amount: freshly cut cheese retains its aroma better than packaged cheese.
- **Calculate the quantities:** for an appetizer board, about 50 to 80 g per person is enough; if cheese is the main course of an informal dinner, about 120 to 150 g.

## Is the bark eaten?

It depends on the cheese. Natural moldy rinds, such as that of Garrotxa, and those spread with paprika or gofio, such as those of some Extremaduran and Canarian goat cheeses, are edible, although not everyone likes them. The hard, dry rinds of cured sheep are often shunned for their texture, and many are treated with waxes or coatings that are not eaten. When in doubt, ask at the cheese shop or remove the rind: it does not provide anything to justify the risk of eating a coating that is not food.

## How to preserve cheeses at home

- **Fresh:** in the refrigerator, in a closed container, and consume them within a few days.
- **Cured and semi-cured:** wrapped in cheese paper or waxed paper, inside a container or in the vegetable drawer. Plastic wrap directly on the cheese makes it sweat.
- **Blue:** well wrapped and separated from other foods, so that they do not transmit their aroma.
- **Molds on the surface:** in hard cured cheeses, they can be removed by cutting at least one centimeter around the affected area. In fresh or soft cheeses, any unforeseen mold forces the cheese to be discarded.

## What homemade cheese learns from tradition

Each of these cheeses applies in a different way the same principles that are used at home: choosing the milk, curdling it, cutting the curd, draining it, salting it and, where appropriate, maturing it. Knowing the tradition helps to understand why a finer cut gives a drier cheese, why thistle rennet gives creaminess or why salt in brine forms a crust.

## Pairings with Spanish wines and preserves

Cheese is enjoyed more when it is served well. Some classic combinations:

- **Cured Manchego cheese** with a red wine from La Mancha or Ribera del Duero, and with quince paste.
- **Torta del Casar** with toasted bread and a full-bodied white wine or an Oloroso.
- **Idiazabal** with a txakoli or a natural cider, and with walnuts.
- **Cabrales and other Asturian blues** with cider, with a sweet wine like a Pedro Ximénez or with honey and pear.
- **Mahón-Menorca** with a Mediterranean white wine and sobrasada.
- **Tetilla and San Simón da Costa** with an albariño, quince or pepper jam.
- **Murcia cheese with wine** with a red wine from Jumilla, the same family of wine with which its rind is washed.
- **Fresh cheeses** with honey, nuts or a homemade fig or tomato jam.

There are no strict rules. A good guideline is to combine cheeses and wines from the same region, which have grown together for centuries, and contrast very salty or spicy cheeses with something sweet.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does a protected designation of origin mean?

That the cheese is made in a delimited geographical area, with milk, production and maturation conditions defined in a document and controlled by a regulatory council.

### Can I make manchego at home?

Similar style sheep cheeses can be made, but they require a press, a temperature- and humidity-controlled ripening chamber, and months of patience. They cannot be called Manchego, because it is a protected denomination.

### Can pregnant people eat cheese?

Health recommendations usually advise avoiding cheeses made with raw milk and soft cheeses or cheeses with moldy rinds, unless they are cooked. Always consult with your doctor or midwife.

## In summary

From the fresh ones of Burgos to the creamy Torta del Casar, from Manchego to Cabrales, from Tetilla to Majorero, Spanish cheeses reflect the diversity of the country. Knowing them by their milk, their maturation and their region allows you to enjoy them better and understand the techniques that are also applied in the small home workshop.
