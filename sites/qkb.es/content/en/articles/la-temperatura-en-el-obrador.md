If you had to choose a single factor that explains why a recipe turns out well one day and poorly the next, that factor would be temperature. Yogurt curdles or doesn't curdle depending on a few degrees; the sourdough rises in four or twelve hours; fresh cheese is soft or rubbery; a jam gels or remains liquid; The frying oil seals a torrija or soaks it. In the home bakery, temperature is the invisible ingredient.

This guide explains what thermometer you need, how to check that it measures correctly, what the key figures are for each preparation and some tricks to control the heat in a home kitchen.

## Why temperature matters so much

Behind almost everything we do in the workshop there are two types of processes that are very sensitive to heat:

- **Biological processes:** Yeasts, lactic acid bacteria and enzymes are living beings or proteins that work within a range of temperatures. Below, they slow down; Above, they are damaged or die.
- **Physical and chemical processes:** the sugar that is concentrated in a jam, the proteins that curdle in a cheese or an egg, the starch that gelatinizes in bread, the fat that melts in puff pastry. Each one occurs at a specific temperature.

An error of five degrees can completely change the result.

## What thermometer do you need

### Digital probe thermometer

It is the most useful and versatile. It has a metal rod that is inserted into the food and a digital display. The instant reading ones give the result in two or three seconds. It is used to measure milk, water, dough, bread, oil and syrups.

Look for one that measures at least 0 to 200°C, with an accuracy of one degree.

### oven thermometer

Many domestic ovens heat ten, twenty or even thirty degrees more or less than the control indicates. An oven thermometer, which is placed on the rack, tells you the actual temperature. It's cheap and solves many mysteries of pale breads or burnt biscuits.

### Sugar or frying thermometer

It has a clip to attach it to the edge of the pan and a high range, up to 200 °C or more. It is useful for jams, candies and fried foods, although a good probe thermometer can do the same function.

### ambient thermometer

A small room thermometer tells you at what temperature your sourdough, kefir or sauerkraut ferment. It helps to understand why times change from season to season.

## How to check that your thermometer measures correctly

An out-of-calibration thermometer is worse than no thermometer at all. Check yours from time to time with these two methods:

### ice water

Fill a glass with crushed ice and cold water, stir and wait a couple of minutes. Insert the probe without touching the bottom or the walls. It should read **0°C**.

### boiling water

Introduce the probe into boiling water in spurts, without touching the bottom. At sea level, it should read **100°C**. Keep in mind that the boiling point drops approximately one degree for every 300 meters of altitude: in Madrid, at about 650 meters, water boils at about 98 °C.

If your thermometer drifts, many digital models allow you to recalibrate it. If not, write down the difference and take it into account.

## The danger zone

In food safety, we speak of the "danger zone" to refer to the temperature range in which the bacteria that cause poisoning multiply rapidly: approximately **between 5 and 60 °C**. Therefore:

- Cooked foods that you will not eat immediately should be cooled quickly and stored in the refrigerator, at 4°C or lower.
- Do not leave creams, creams, egg or dairy fillings at room temperature for hours.
- When reheating, do so until the food is very hot throughout.

Fermented foods are a controlled exception: salt, acidity and beneficial microorganisms protect them. But that only works if the recipes are followed well.

## The temperature of the bread dough

Professional bakers not only control the temperature of the oven, but also that of the dough after kneading. A dough at 24–26°C ferments predictably; one at 20°C will take much longer, and one at 30°C will pass prematurely.

The way to control it is to adjust the water temperature. A simple method is the **factor three** formula:

**Water temperature = (desired temperature × 3) − flour temperature − room temperature − heating by kneading**

The kneading heating, or friction factor, depends on how you knead: by hand it is usually about 2 to 4 degrees; with a powerful mixer, 8 to 12 degrees.

**Example:** you want a dough at 25 °C. The flour and the kitchen are at 20 °C and you knead by hand, with a factor of 3 degrees.

25 × 3 = 75. 75 − 20 − 20 − 3 = **32 °C** for water.

In summer, with the kitchen at 28°C, the calculation would give very cold water or even ice. If you use sourdough or pre-ferment, its temperature is added as a fourth factor and multiplied by four.

## Tricks to control heat at home

### to keep warm

- **Oven off with light on** is usually kept between 30 and 40°C. Measure it first with a thermometer.
- **A cooler** with hot water bottles is an excellent incubator.
- **Microwave off** with a glass of boiling water inside maintains a warm and humid environment for a while.
- **On top of the refrigerator** or near a radiator it is usually a few degrees warmer.

### To maintain freshness

- **Ferment in the coolest part of the house** in summer, such as an interior closet or the floor of the pantry.
- **Use cold water or ice** in the doughs.
- **The refrigerator** allows slow and controlled fermentations, both for bread and pastries.
- **A basin of water** around a fermentation jar buffers temperature changes.

## The seasons inside the house

In much of Spain, the temperature of a kitchen can go from 17 or 18 °C on a winter morning to 28 or 30 °C on a July afternoon. For the workshop, those are two different worlds. In winter, sourdough takes twice as long to rise, yogurt needs a warm incubator, and sauerkraut ferments slowly. In summer, the doughs are prepared in a few hours, the kefir is ready ahead of schedule and the butter in the puff pastry softens as soon as it comes out of the refrigerator. That is why recipes indicate ranges and signals, not just times: the same bread may need four hours of fermentation in August and nine in January.

## A temperature notebook

Writing down a few figures for each preparation greatly speeds up learning: the temperature of the kitchen, the temperature of the water, the temperature of the dough at the end of kneading, the hours of fermentation and the result. After a few weeks you will have your own table, adjusted to your house, your oven and your ingredients, much more useful than any generic recipe.

## Take care of the thermometer

Wash the tube with hot water and soap after each use, especially if it has touched milk, meat or eggs, and dry it well. Do not submerge the electronic part or leave the thermometer inside the oven if it is not designed for this. Change the battery as soon as the screen dims, because some readings become erratic before turning off completely.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I cook without a thermometer?

Yes, and for centuries it was done. Visual cues, touch, and experience work, but a thermometer shortens learning a lot and eliminates most failures. It is the most profitable investment in the workshop.

### How do I measure oil temperature without a thermometer?

Throw in a piece of bread or dough: at about 170–180°C, it bubbles vigorously and browns in about 30 to 60 seconds. If it darkens immediately, it is too hot; If it barely bubbles, it is cold.

### Where is the probe placed in a bread?

In the center of the piece, introduced from the side or the base, so that the tip is in the thickest part.

## In summary

A well-calibrated probe thermometer and oven thermometer solve most of the mysteries of home cooking. Learn the key figures for each preparation, remember the danger zone between 5 and 60 °C and adjust the water temperature so that your doughs ferment predictably. Controlling the heat is controlling the result.
