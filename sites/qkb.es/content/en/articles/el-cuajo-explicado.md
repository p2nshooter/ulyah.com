Without rennet there would not be most of the cheeses we know. That small amount of liquid, sometimes just a few drops per liter of milk, is responsible for transforming a liquid food into a firm curd that can be cut, molded and, over time, turned into a manchego, an idiazábal or a humble fresh cheese.

Even so, rennet is a great unknown for those who start making cheese at home: where does it come from? What types are there? How much should you use? Why does it sometimes not work? This guide answers those questions and explains how to get the most out of it.

## What is rennet

Rennet is a preparation that contains **enzymes capable of coagulating milk**. The main one is **chymosin**, an enzyme that is produced naturally in the fourth stomach, the curdle, of lactating ruminants, such as calves, lambs and kids. Its natural function is to help babies digest breast milk: by coagulating it, the milk remains in the stomach longer and is digested better.

Shepherds discovered thousands of years ago that milk stored in containers made from animal stomachs curdled, and from there the cheese factory as we know it was born.

## How it acts on milk

Milk contains proteins called caseins, grouped into microscopic structures, micelles. These micelles have on their surface a layer of a special casein, kappa-casein, which acts as a kind of "fluff" that keeps them separated and in suspension.

Chymosin cuts that protective layer. Without it, the micelles lose their repulsion, join together with the help of the calcium present in the milk and form a three-dimensional network that traps fat and water. That network is the **curd**.

This enzymatic coagulation is different from that caused by an acid, such as lemon or vinegar. With acid, caseins clump together because the electrical charge of the micelles changes; The resulting curd is more fragile and grainy. That is why Burgos-type fresh cheese needs rennet, while cottage cheese or paneer are made with acid.

## Types of rennet

### animal rennet

It is obtained from the fruit set of young ruminants. It is the traditional one and the one used in many cheeses with designation of origin. It contains mainly chymosin and some pepsin. It gives clean tasting cheeses and is very effective.

### Microbial rennet

It is produced from certain fungi that generate coagulant enzymes. It is suitable for vegetarians. It works well in fresh and short-ripened cheeses, although in long-ripened cheeses some versions may generate a slight bitterness.

### Chymosin obtained by fermentation

It is chymosin identical to animal chymosin, produced by microorganisms to which the corresponding gene has been incorporated. It is very pure and is widely used in the cheese industry. It is usually suitable for vegetarians.

### Vegetable rennet: thistle

In Spain and Portugal there is a unique tradition: some cheeses are curdled with the dried flowers of the **thistle** (*Cynara cardunculus*). This is the case of Torta del Casar, Torta de la Serena or Flor de Guía cheese in the Canary Islands, which uses part of vegetable rennet. Thistle enzymes, called cardosins, produce cheeses with a very creamy paste and a characteristic, slightly bitter flavor. Making cheese with thistle at home is possible, but it requires experience: the dosage and temperature are more delicate.

## Presentations

- **Liquid:** the most common at home. Easy to dose with a syringe or dropper.
- **In tablets:** they dissolve in water. They last a long time and are practical for small batches.
- **Powder:** for more professional use.

## How to dose it

The concentration of rennet varies greatly between manufacturers, so the golden rule is: **always follow the dosage indicated on the label** for the volume of milk you are going to use. As a guide, many domestic liquid rennets recommend between 0.5 and 1 ml per liter of milk, but it can be quite a bit more or less.

Some tips:

- **Measure accurately**, with a syringe without a needle or a measuring spoon.
- **Dilute it in non-chlorinated water** just before using it: about four tablespoons of mineral or boiled and cooled water. Chlorine can inactivate enzymes.
- **Do not mix it with citric acid or calcium chloride** in the same container before adding it to the milk.
- **Stir briefly** when adding it and leave the milk still.

### What happens if you go too far or fall short?

- **Little rennet:** the milk takes a long time to curdle or forms a soft curd.
- **Too much curd:** Curd forms too quickly, can be rubbery, and the cheese can develop a bitter flavor.

## Calculate the dose for any amount of milk

The labels indicate the dosage for a specific volume, and it almost never matches that of your pot. The account is a simple rule of three. If the manufacturer recommends 1 ml for every 4 liters and you are going to curdle 2.5 liters, you will need 2.5 × 1 ÷ 4 = 0.6 ml, approximately. To measure such small quantities accurately, dilute first: mix 1 ml of rennet with 9 ml of dechlorinated water, so that each milliliter of the mixture contains 0.1 ml of rennet, and take 6 ml of that dilution into a syringe. Prepare the dilution just before using it and discard any leftovers.

## Homemade thistle rennet

The dried flowers of thistle, sold in some herbalists and rural stores, can be used to curdle milk, especially sheep's milk. They are left to soak in a little water for several hours, crushed in a mortar and the liquid is filtered, which is what is added to the milk. Its strength varies greatly from one batch to another, so it is a good idea to first do a test with a small amount of milk and note the proportion that works.

## Factors that influence coagulation

- **Temperature:** Rennet works best between 30 and 40 °C. Underneath, it acts very slowly; above about 50 °C, it is inactivated.
- **Acidity:** A slightly more acidic milk curdles faster and forms a firmer curd.
- **Calcium:** is essential for the micelles to unite. Pasteurization somewhat reduces the availability of calcium, which is why many home cheesemakers add **calcium chloride**.
- **Type of milk:** UHT milk or milk treated at very high temperatures curdles poorly because heat alters the whey proteins, which adhere to the caseins and prevent the formation of the network.

## The clean cut test

To know if the curd is ready:

1. Insert a clean knife or slanted clean finger into the curd.
2. Gently lift it up.
3. If the curd opens with a clean edge and a clear, greenish-yellow whey appears, it is ready.
4. If it falls apart or the whey is milky, wait more.

## Conservation of rennet

- Store the liquid rennet **in the refrigerator**, tightly closed and protected from light.
- Respect the expiration date: it loses strength over time.
- The tablets are stored well in a cool, dry place, or in the freezer.

## Where to buy it

In Spain it can be found in pharmacies, drugstores, home-made product stores, some agricultural stores and online stores specialized in domestic cheesemaking.

## Alternatives without rennet

If you don't have rennet, you can make acid coagulation cheeses:

- **Cottage cheese:** with whole milk, heat and lemon or vinegar.
- **Paneer:** pressed Indian cheese, made with milk and acid.
- **Labneh:** drained yogurt, without added heat or acid.

None of them replace a fresh rennet cheese, but they are excellent on their own.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is rennet suitable for vegetarians?

Animal rennet does not. Microbial, chymosin obtained by fermentation and thistle usually are; check the label.

### Can I reuse whey as rennet?

No. Whey does not contain enough active enzyme to reliably curdle a new batch of milk.

### Why doesn't my milk curdle even though the curd is new?

The most common thing is that the milk is UHT or ultra-pasteurized, or that the dilution water has chlorine. Too low a temperature also plays a role.

### Can expired rennet be used?

Rennet loses strength over time, especially if it has not been stored in the refrigerator. An expired curd can still work, but with less power, and the curd will take longer to form or will be soft. If in doubt, test a small amount of milk before making a full cheese.

### How long do you have to wait before cutting the curd?

It depends on the temperature, the dose and the milk, but for fresh cheeses it is usually between 30 and 60 minutes. Don't be guided only by the watch: always do the clean cut test.

## a little history

The use of rennet is almost as old as grazing. It is believed that the first cheeses may have arisen accidentally, by transporting milk in containers made from animal stomachs, which contained coagulating enzymes. In the Iberian Peninsula, the use of thistle as a vegetable rennet has been documented for centuries and is still alive in cheeses such as Torta del Casar or Serena, which owe their creamy texture and slight bitterness to the enzymes in the thistle flower. For a long time, each shepherd prepared his own rennet by drying and salting the stomach of suckling lambs or kids. Today, most cheeses are made with standardized rennets, which allow for much more precise dosing.

## In summary

Rennet is an enzyme that cuts the protective layer of caseins and allows the milk to form a network: curd. It can be animal, microbial, obtained by fermentation or vegetable, like the thistle in our Extremaduran cakes. To use it correctly at home: dosage on the label, chlorine-free water, milk pasteurized at 32–37 °C, calcium if necessary and rest until the clean cut.
