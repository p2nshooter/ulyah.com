In the home workshop we work with microorganisms on purpose. We want lactic acid bacteria to ferment cabbage, yeast to make bread rise, and yogurt cultures to curdle milk. But other microorganisms that we do not want can also be present in the same space: molds, putrefactive bacteria or, in rare cases, bacteria capable of causing diseases. Hygiene consists of tipping the balance in favor of the former and against the latter.

There is no need to turn the kitchen into an operating room. Most traditional fermentations have been made for centuries in ordinary kitchens. But it is advisable to follow some simple guidelines and understand why each one matters. This guide brings them together.

## Cleaning is not the same as disinfecting

They are two different and complementary steps:

- **Cleaning** is removing visible dirt: food remains, grease, dry dough, milk. It is done with hot water and soap or detergent, rubbing.
- **To disinfect** is to reduce the number of microorganisms that remain on an already clean surface. It is done with heat, such as boiling water, or with a disinfectant product suitable for food contact surfaces.

A disinfectant does not work well on a dirty surface, because the dirt protects microorganisms. That is why **first it is cleaned and then, if necessary, it is disinfected**.

## What each preparation needs

Not all preparations require the same level of hygiene. It depends on whether the microorganisms we are looking for come from the food itself or we add them, and how protected the environment is.

| Preparation | Recommended hygiene level | why |
|---|---|---|
| Sauerkraut and fermented vegetables | Careful cleaning | The bacteria come from the vegetable; salt and acidity protect |
| sourdough and bread | Careful cleaning | The acid culture is very resistant; the oven bakes everything |
| Yogurt and kefir | Cleaning and blanching jars | Milk is a very favorable environment for other microorganisms |
| fresh cheeses | Cleaning and disinfection of utensils | Rich medium, little acid and without subsequent cooking |
| Kombucha | Careful cleaning and initial acidity | The acidity of the starter protects from the beginning |
| Preserves in a bain-marie | Cleaning and heat treatment | Heat treatment is the safety step |

## hands and clothes

- Wash your hands with soap and water for at least 20 seconds before starting, after touching raw foods such as meat, eggs or vegetable soil, and after going to the bathroom, touching pets or your cell phone.
- Tie back long hair and remove rings and bracelets if you are kneading or handling curds.
- Wear a clean apron.
- If you have a wound on your hands, cover it with a waterproof dressing or wear gloves.
- Do not prepare food for other people if you are vomiting or diarrhea.

## Jars and containers

For most fermentations, simply wash the jars with hot water and soap, rinse them very well and let them dry upside down on a clean cloth or in the drainer.

For yogurt, kefir and cheeses, it is also advisable to **scald** the jars and utensils: pour boiling water over them or immerse them in boiling water for a few minutes just before using them.

For canning in a water bath, the jars must be clean and hot at the time of filling. The subsequent heat treatment is what guarantees safety, as we explain in our guide to [canning in a bain-marie safely](/recetas-y-guias/conservas-al-bano-maria-con-seguridad/).

### Soap residue

Rinse well. Leftover detergent can affect the flavor and slow down fermentation cultures.

## Fabrics and cloths

- Use different cloths to dry hands, to dry utensils and to cover fermentations.
- Boil cheesecloths after each use, dry them well and store them in a dry place.
- Wash kitchen towels frequently at high temperatures. A damp, dirty cloth is a breeding ground for bacteria.

## Surfaces and tables

- Clean the counter before kneading or handling food.
- Use different boards, or wash them very well between uses, for raw foods of animal origin and for foods that will not be cooked, such as vegetables to ferment or cheeses.
- Wooden boards are suitable if they are in good condition. Discard any that have deep cracks.

## Cross contamination between crops

In the home bakery, several live cultures coexist: sourdough, kefir, kombucha, yogurt. Each one has its community of microorganisms, and it is advisable that they do not mix:

- Keep jars a few meters apart, especially kombucha, whose acetic bacteria can colonize other fermented foods.
- Use clean spoons and utensils for each crop.
- Do not use the same cloth to cover different fermented foods.

## Raw milk: an important warning

Raw, unpasteurized milk may contain bacteria such as *Listeria monocytogenes*, *Brucella*, *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter* or pathogenic strains of *Escherichia coli*. Professional cheese factories that work with raw milk do so under strict veterinary and health controls, and with maturation times that reduce risks.

At home, and especially for fresh cheeses, which do not ripen, **we always recommend using pasteurized milk**. We explain it in detail in our guide on [what milk to use to make cheese](/recetas-y-guias/que-leche-usar-para-hacer-queso/).

## People at risk

Some people are more vulnerable to food poisoning and should be especially careful with homemade cheeses and fermented foods:

- Pregnant women.
- Babies and toddlers.
- Older people.
- People immunosuppressed due to illness or treatment.

For them, it is advisable to avoid products made with raw milk and consult with a health professional before consuming homemade fermented foods.

## Storage

- Store perishable foods in the refrigerator at 4°C or lower.
- Do not leave dairy products, creams or egg fillings at room temperature for hours.
- Label the jars with the contents and the date of preparation.
- Always use clean utensils to remove food from a jar.

## When to throw away food

There is a rule that applies to the entire workshop: **when in doubt, throw away**. Discard without trying any food that contains:

- Hairy, colored mold, on the surface or inside.
- Rotten, garbage or very unpleasant smell, different from the normal acidic smell of a fermented product.
- Slimy texture in foods that should not have it.
- Domed lids or liquid that comes out under pressure in preserves.
- Strange colors, such as pink or orange spots in yogurt or sourdough.

It is not enough to remove the moldy part in soft or liquid foods: mold spreads invisible filaments and some produce toxins.

## The water

Tap water in Spain is suitable for consumption and is used for washing and most preparations. For fermented products, keep in mind that the chlorine added to tap water to disinfect it can stop lactic acid bacteria and kefir or kombucha cultures. If your water smells strongly of chlorine, boil it for a few minutes and let it cool, let it sit uncovered for a few hours, or use filtered water. If you use well or spring water, make sure it is sanitary controlled.

## Pets, plants and other visitors

The kitchen of a home is not a professional workshop, but it is advisable to take some precautions:

- Keep pets off the counter and away from fermentation jars.
- Avoid fermenting next to houseplants with damp soil, which can be a source of mold spores.
- Protect open jars from fruit flies and gnats with tight cloths or valved lids. Vinegar flies are especially attracted to kombucha and water kefir.
- Do not ferment near the garbage can or overripe fruit.

## Cleanliness also has limits

More cleaning is not always better. Some examples:

- **Do not overwash vegetables to ferment.** Simply remove the soil and damaged leaves: lactic acid bacteria live on their surface.
- **Do not use bleach or strong disinfectants on fermentation jars** if you are not rinsing them thoroughly: the residue can kill the culture.
- **Do not wash the banneton with water**: it deforms and may become moldy. Simply brush it and let it dry.

## A simple routine

Before starting any preparation:

1. Clear and clean the countertop.
2. Wash your hands.
3. Prepare clean utensils and, if the recipe calls for it, blanch them.
4. Have a clean, dry cloth handy.
5. When finished, wash everything immediately and let it air dry.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it necessary to use bleach in the workshop kitchen?

For most preparations, hot water, soap and, when the recipe calls for it, boiling water are enough. If you want to disinfect a surface, choose a bleach whose label indicates that it is suitable for surfaces in contact with food, dilute it as indicated by the manufacturer and then rinse with drinking water. Never mix it with other cleaning products.

### Is the dishwasher suitable for jars?

Yes. A high temperature program leaves jars and utensils clean and very sanitized. For yogurt, kefir and cheeses, use them fresh and still hot, or blanch them just before starting.

### How often should you change sponges and scouring pads?

Often because moisture and food debris make them a good home for bacteria. Let them dry between uses, do not clean a raw meat board and cheese utensils with the same sponge, and replace them as soon as they smell bad or fall apart.

## In summary

Clean first and disinfect later when necessary; clean hands; well washed and scalded dairy jars; boiled fabrics; crops separated from each other; pasteurized milk for fresh cheeses, and the golden rule: when in doubt, throw it away. With these guidelines, the homemade bakery is as safe as it is tasty. Please also see our [food safety](/seguridad-alimentaria/) page.
