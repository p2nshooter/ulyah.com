There are hundreds of types of cat food available in supermarkets, drugstores and pet stores. Juicy pieces of meat are depicted on the packaging and there is talk of “finest ingredients”, “pure nature” and “premium”. But what is actually in the lining is not written on the front, but in small print on the back. Anyone who can read this information will make better decisions and be less impressed by advertising terms.

This guide explains step by step what the individual information means and how you compare different types of food.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - For daily nutrition, the food must be labeled as **complete feed**.
> - The composition lists the ingredients in descending order of weight.
> - “With chicken” does not mean that the food consists predominantly of chicken.
> - Wet and dry food can only be fairly compared based on the dry matter.
> - Terms like “premium” or “natural” are not protected by law.

## Complete feed or supplementary feed?

The most important information on the package is the type of food:

- **Complete food** contains all the nutrients a cat needs in sufficient quantities. A cat can receive a long-term healthy diet if it gets the recommended amount.
- **Supplementary feed** alone does not cover the need. This includes most treats, pastes and snacks, but also some types of wet food that only consist of meat or fish in jelly, for example. They may only be fed additionally.

This information is often written in small letters on the back. Many wet foods that look like a complete meal are actually just supplements. Anyone who feeds them permanently as their main food runs the risk of deficiency symptoms.

There are different complete foods for kittens, adult cats and older cats. Kitten food contains more energy and certain nutrients for growth.

## The composition

All ingredients are listed under “Composition” in descending order of weight. Whatever comes first is included most.

There are two types of declaration:

**Open declaration:** Each ingredient is stated individually by name and often with a percentage, for example “chicken meat 30%, chicken hearts 15%, beef liver 10%, broth 40%, minerals”. That is transparent.

**Closed declaration:** Ingredients are grouped together, for example “meat and animal by-products (including 4% chicken), grains, vegetable by-products, minerals”. This shape is allowed and doesn't say anything bad about the quality, but you don't know exactly which animals and which parts are included.

### What are animal by-products?

The term sounds unsavory, but it refers to parts of slaughtered animals that are not used for human consumption, although they would often be fit for human consumption: heart, liver, kidneys, lungs, stomachs and others. Offal is nutrient-rich for cats and is also part of nature's prey. However, the term can also include less valuable components. This cannot be distinguished in a closed declaration.

## What “with chicken” really means

The designation on the front follows fixed rules in the European feed industry, which are based on the proportion of an ingredient. Roughly:

| Designation | Minimum proportion of the mentioned ingredient |
|---|---|
| “Chicken” as the sole product name | almost exclusively chicken |
| “Chicken Menu”, “Chicken Recipe” | at least 26 percent |
| “rich in chicken”, “extra chicken” | at least 14 percent |
| “with chicken” | at least 4 percent |
| “Taste of chicken” | less than 4 percent, but noticeable |

A food “with chicken” can therefore consist of ninety-six percent other ingredients. This is not a scam, it is a regulated designation, but it is worth reading the composition.

## Analytical components

Under “Analytical components” are the results of a chemical analysis of the feed. The word “raw” refers to the analysis method, not the quality:

- **Crude protein:** the protein content
- **Crude fat:** the fat content
- **Crude fiber:** indigestible plant components
- **Crude ash:** the mineral content, i.e. what would be left over if the food was burned
- **Moisture:** the water content

Sometimes calcium, phosphorus, sodium or magnesium are also specified.

## Compare wet and dry food fairly

The percentages refer to the food as it is in the can or bag, i.e. including water. Wet food with 80 percent moisture therefore appears to have little protein, while dry food with 8 percent moisture appears to have a lot. To compare them fairly, convert the values ​​to the **dry matter**, i.e. what would be left if you removed the water.

The formula is simple: value divided by (100 minus moisture), times 100.

**Example:**

| | Wet food | Dry food |
|---|---|---|
| Crude protein according to label | 10 % | 32 % |
| Moisture | 80 % | 8 % |
| Crude protein in the dry matter | 10 ÷ 20 × 100 = **50 %** | 32 ÷ 92 × 100 = **about 35%** |

In this example, the wet food contains significantly more protein in relation to the dry matter than the dry food, even though the number on the label is smaller.

## Estimate carbohydrates

Carbohydrates do not have to be listed on cat food. But you can roughly estimate it: 100 minus crude protein, crude fat, crude fiber, crude ash and moisture roughly equals the proportion of carbohydrates. Cats do not need carbohydrates and are designed to eat a diet rich in protein. A moderate proportion is not a problem, but a very high proportion can cause obesity and diabetes in some cats.

## Additives

“Additives” include vitamins, trace elements and amino acids that are specifically added to the feed, such as vitamin A, vitamin D3, vitamin E, taurine, zinc, iron or iodine. They are important for the food to be complete. Taurine is essential for cats; a deficiency can damage the heart and eyes. It is therefore always included in complete feed.

It may also contain technological additives such as binders, preservatives or antioxidants. They are approved and harmless in the permitted quantities.

## Advertising terms that say little

- **“Premium”, “Gourmet”, “Super-Premium”:** not defined by law.
- **“Natural”, “close to nature”:** often just means that no artificial preservatives, colors or flavors have been added.
- **“Grain-free”:** Grains are not a problem for most cats. Grain-free foods often contain other carbohydrate sources such as potatoes or peas.
- **“No added sugar”:** makes sense, because sugar does not belong in cat food. Cats cannot taste sweets.
- **“Human Grade”:** This designation is not bindingly defined in feed law.

## The feeding recommendation

There is a feeding recommendation on every package, usually in grams per kilogram of body weight or as a table. It is a starting point, not a hard and fast law. Neutered indoor cats often need less than stated, young, active outdoor cats sometimes need more. Monitor your cat's weight and adjust the amount.

## Shelf life and storage

Even the best food loses quality if it is stored incorrectly. You will find a best-before date on each package. Until this date, the manufacturer guarantees that the unopened food will retain its properties.

- **Wet food:** Opened cans and bowls should be kept covered in the refrigerator and should be used within one to two days. Take out the portion in good time or warm it slightly, as many cats don't like cold food.
- **Dry food:** Store in the original packaging or in a tightly sealing container, cool, dry and dark. Fat in dry food can go rancid over time when exposed to air and heat. Don't buy giant bags that will sit open for months if you only have one cat.
- **Clean the container:** If you transfer dry food, clean the container occasionally before refilling it. Old crumbs and fat residue on the ground spoil the fresh food.
- **Retain batch number:** If you are decanting, cut out the portion of the package with the best-before date and batch number and keep it. In the event of a recall, you can check whether your food is affected.

## Calculate the energy content yourself

The energy content does not have to be stated on many food packaging, but it is important if you combine several types of food or if you want your cat to lose weight. You can estimate it from the analytical components using a simple rule of thumb. Per 100 grams of feed, around 3.5 kilocalories per gram of crude protein, 8.5 kilocalories per gram of crude fat and 3.5 kilocalories per gram of carbohydrates are calculated.

**Example of a wet food** with 10 percent crude protein, 5 percent crude fat and an estimated 1 percent carbohydrates: 10 × 3.5 + 5 × 8.5 + 1 × 3.5 results in around 81 kilocalories per 100 grams. A dry food with the same calculation is often 350 to 400 kilocalories per 100 grams. This shows why even a small handful of kibble is so significant. It's best to discuss approximately how much energy your cat needs with your veterinarian, especially if you want her to lose weight, as described in [Obesity in Cats](/katzenwissen/uebergewicht-bei-katzen/).

## Diet food for sick cats

A special group is diet foods. They are formulated for specific nutritional purposes, such as supporting kidney function, dissolving certain urinary stones or for food intolerances. Their label states the exact purpose, often the recommended duration of feeding and the advice to seek veterinary advice beforehand. These foods are not wellness products for healthy cats. For example, a kidney diet contains less phosphorus and adjusted amounts of protein, which are not ideal for a healthy young cat. Therefore, only feed diet food according to veterinary recommendations.

## Check with the manufacturer

The name and address of the manufacturer or dealer as well as the registration or approval number of the manufacturing company are on every packaging. If you are unclear about any information, such as which animal species are behind “meat and animal by-products” or what the energy content is, write to the manufacturer. Reputable providers answer such questions willingly and in detail. Evasive answers or no answers at all are also information.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is expensive food always better?

No. The price also depends on marketing, packaging and distribution. What is crucial is a complete, complete feed, a comprehensible composition and that your cat tolerates it well.

### Do I need a food with as much protein as possible?

Cats need a lot of animal protein. However, in certain illnesses, such as advanced kidney disease, an adjusted amount of protein may be necessary. The vet will determine this.

### What does the batch number mean?

It can be used to trace production. In the event of a recall, you can see from the batch whether your food is affected.
