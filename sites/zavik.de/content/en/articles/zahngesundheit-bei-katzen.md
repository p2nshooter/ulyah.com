When cats have a toothache, they still eat. This is why dental disease in cats is so often overlooked. A cat with sore gums or a loose tooth will not whine or refuse the bowl. She may eat a little more slowly, only chew on one side, or swallow the kibble whole. And their humans don't notice.

Dental diseases are very common in adult cats. Research shows that a large proportion of cats over the age of three show signs of gum disease or other diseases in their mouths. This guide explains the most important problems and what you can do.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Adult cats have 30 teeth, kittens have 26 milk teeth, which are changed at around three to six months.
> - Common diseases include tartar, gingivitis and FORL, a painful dissolution of the teeth.
> - Typical signs include bad breath, drooling, one-sided chewing and food falling out of the mouth.
> - Brushing your teeth is the most effective prevention if the cat learns to do it.
> - Thorough dental treatments require anesthesia and x-rays.

## The cat's teeth

Adult cats have 30 teeth: small incisors, four long canines, premolars and molars. They specialize in holding and cutting prey, not grinding it up. Cats therefore hardly chew their food; they cut it into pieces and swallow it.

Kittens initially develop 26 milk teeth. Between the third and sixth months of life they fall out and are replaced by permanent teeth. Sometimes a baby tooth remains even though the permanent tooth is already there, usually a canine. The vet should then decide whether he needs to be pulled.

## The most common dental diseases

### Dental plaque and tartar

Like humans, a plaque made of bacteria and food particles forms on cats' teeth. If it is not removed, minerals from saliva harden it into tartar, a yellowish-brown crust that usually first becomes visible on the back molars. Tartar itself is not painful, but it provides a place for bacteria to settle and attack the gums.

### Gingivitis and periodontitis

Gingivitis can be recognized by a red band at the edge of the gums, sometimes with swelling and bleeding. If left untreated, it can spread to the periodontium. Pockets then form between the tooth and gum, the bone is broken down, teeth become loose and eventually fall out. This periodontitis is painful and a permanent source of inflammation in the body.

### FORL: Teeth that dissolve

FORL stands for feline odontoclastic resorptive lesions, now often simply called tooth resorption. The body's own cells break down the tooth substance, often starting at the neck of the tooth directly at the gum line. The cause is still not fully understood. Tooth resorption is very common in cats, especially as they age.

The tricky thing: From the outside you can often only see a little redness on the gums or a place where the gums seem to be growing into the tooth. Most of the damage is beneath the gums and is only visible on x-ray. When the pulp is exposed, it is very painful. Affected teeth cannot be repaired and are usually removed or, depending on the type of resorption, worn down to the root.

### Gingivostomatitis

Some cats develop severe, chronic inflammation of the entire mucous membrane of the mouth, especially in the back of the throat. The cause is an overreaction of the immune system to plaque. The cats are in severe pain, hardly eat, drool and no longer groom their fur. This condition requires intensive treatment, often removing many or all of the molars, which significantly helps most cats.

### Tooth fractures

Broken teeth, especially canines, occur from falls, accidents, or bites on hard objects. If the pulp is exposed, the tooth can become infected and an abscess develops at the root.

## How to recognize a toothache

Cats rarely show tooth pain clearly. Look out for these signs:

- **Bad breath,** which is stronger than the normal smell of food
- **Drooling,** sometimes with blood in saliva
- **chewing on one side** or holding the head crooked while eating
- **Food falls out of the mouth,** or the cat shakes its head while eating
- **Preference for soft food,** Dry food is refused or swallowed whole
- **Paws on mouth,** Rubbing face
- **Teeth chattering or jaw shaking** when eating
- **shaggy fur** because the cat grooms itself less because of the pain
- **Withdrawal, irritability,** Defensiveness when head is touched
- **Weight loss**

After dental treatment, many owners report that their cat is more playful, cuddly and lively again. Only then does it become clear how long she was in pain.

## Prevention: brushing your cat's teeth

Brushing your teeth is the most effective way to reduce plaque. This sounds adventurous to many cat owners, but many cats are okay with it if you get them used to it slowly, ideally as kittens.

**What you need:** a soft cat toothbrush or a finger toothbrush and cat toothpaste. Don't use human toothpaste. It contains substances that should not be swallowed and foams.

**Step by step:**

1. Let your cat lick a little bit of cat toothpaste off his finger. Many people like the taste.
2. Gently lift the lip and touch the teeth and gums with your finger, just a few seconds, then reward.
3. After a few days, take your finger toothbrush and gently rub the outside of your molars.
4. Slowly increase the time until you reach all the outer surfaces of the teeth. The cat's tongue largely cleans the insides itself.
5. Daily cleaning is ideal. Even doing it several times a week helps.

Break off before the cat fights back and end each session with a reward. If the gums are already inflamed and bleeding, have the cat treated first, otherwise they will associate brushing with pain.

**Supplements:** Special dental care foods with a special structure, chews or additives for drinking water can make a certain contribution, but do not replace brushing. Normal dry food hardly cleans your teeth.

## Dental treatment at the vet

During the annual examination, the veterinarian will also look into your cat's mouth. If there is tartar, inflammation or suspected resorption, she recommends dental treatment.

This always takes place under **general anesthesia**. A cat would not tolerate cleaning under the gums, and without anesthesia the pockets and insides of the teeth could not be examined. A tube in the trachea also protects water and bacteria from entering the lungs. Tartar removal without anesthesia, as is sometimes offered, only removes visible plaque, not the causes, and is not recommended by professional societies.

Thorough dental treatment includes:

- **X-rays** of all teeth to detect resorption, root inflammation and bone loss
- **Removal of tartar** above and below the gums with ultrasound
- **Examining each tooth pocket** with a probe
- **Extracting diseased teeth** if necessary
- **Polishing the tooth surfaces** so that new plaque builds up less quickly

Many cat owners worry if their cat loses several teeth. However, cats cope surprisingly well with few or even no teeth and often eat better afterwards than before because the pain is gone. Most can even eat dry food again.

## Changing teeth in kittens

Kittens lose their baby teeth between three and six months of age, often without you noticing because they swallow the small teeth. Sometimes the gums are a little red during this time and the kitten chews more than usual. It is important to have them checked at around six to seven months. If there are milk teeth next to the permanent teeth, especially on the canines, they are referred to as persistent milk teeth. They can displace permanent teeth, cause misalignment and hold onto leftover food and are therefore usually extracted, often during castration, so that only anesthesia is necessary.

## Food, chews and teeth

Normal dry food hardly cleans your teeth. However, there are special dental care foods whose croquettes are designed in such a way that they brush over the tooth surface like a brush when you bite into them, or which contain additives that slow down the formation of tartar. The effectiveness of some of these products has been tested in studies, for example for a seal of approval from the American Veterinary Oral Health Council. They can supplement cleaning, but not replace it. Be careful with hard chews: they can break teeth. Cooked bones splinter and are dangerous for cats.

## After dental treatment

Most cats recover quickly after dental treatment, even if teeth have been extracted. On the day of the procedure, they are often still tired from the anesthesia. In the first few days, offer soft food, such as wet food with a little extra water, and give the painkillers provided exactly as instructed. Slightly bloody saliva on the first day is normal. If your cat is still not eating after two days, is drooling heavily, or has swollen face, call the practice. A check after one to two weeks will show whether everything has healed well.

## Frequently asked questions

### Isn't anesthesia too dangerous for older cats?

Modern anesthesia is also safe for older cats if blood tests are done beforehand and the cat is monitored during the procedure. The risk of leaving a cat with toothache and chronic inflammation untreated is usually greater.

### How often does my cat need teeth cleaning?

That is very different. Some cats never need one, others need one every year. The vet assesses this during the annual examination.

### Can I remove tartar myself?

No. Using tools can injure the gums and damage tooth enamel, and the causes under the gums remain.

### My cat has a smell coming from his mouth but is eating normally. Do I have to go to the vet?

Yes, get it checked at the next opportunity. Bad breath is often the first sign of dental disease, long before the cat starts eating worse. A noticeably sweet smell or smell reminiscent of ammonia can also indicate metabolic diseases such as diabetes or kidney disease and should be clarified quickly.
