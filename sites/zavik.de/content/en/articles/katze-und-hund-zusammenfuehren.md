“Like a dog and a cat” is what they say about two people who don’t get along. In many households, dogs and cats live peacefully together, and some even sleep together in a basket. Whether it works depends primarily on three things: the character of both animals, the dog's upbringing and how carefully the first encounters are conducted.

The biggest misunderstanding between dogs and cats lies in their language. A wagging tail often means joy and excitement for a dog, but tension and anger for a cat. For a dog, a raised paw is a request to play; for a cat, it is a threat. Both must first learn to understand the other. This guide will help.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Dogs with a strong hunting instinct are often not suitable for living with cats.
> - The dog should be able to confidently use basic commands such as “sit”, “down” and “stay”.
> - The cat needs elevated escape routes and dog-free retreats at all times.
> - First meetings only with the dog on a leash and in small steps.
> - Food and the litter box must be out of reach of the dog.

## Which couples are a good fit?

Living together is easiest when at least one of the animals has known the other since they were young. A puppy who grows up with cats comes to know them as part of the family. A cat that meets dogs as a kitten often does not run away from them later.

It is more difficult with adult animals without experience with the other species. You should be particularly careful with:

- **Dogs with a strong hunting instinct,** such as many greyhounds, terriers or hunting dog breeds. There are exceptions, but the risk is higher.
- **Dogs that have previously chased or injured cats.**
- **very anxious or very old cats** for whom a lively dog means permanent stress.
- **very young kittens** together with large dogs because even one clumsy swipe of the paw can be dangerous.

If you get a dog or cat from the shelter, ask if the animal knows the other species. Many animal shelters test dogs to see if they are compatible with cats.

## Preparation: the apartment is cat-proof and dog-friendly

Before the animals meet for the first time, arrange the apartment so that the cat can always avoid:

- **Elevated places:** Scratching post, shelves, cupboards and window sills that the dog cannot reach. The cat feels safe from above and can watch the dog.
- **Dog-free zone:** at least one room that the dog cannot enter, for example through a door gate with a cat flap or a gate through which the cat can slip.
- **Cat feeding area increased:** Many dogs are passionate about eating cat food. This is too fat for dogs in the long run and the cat loses its food.
- **Litter box out of reach:** Some dogs eat cat feces, which is unsavory and disturbs the cat when going to the toilet. Place the toilet in the dog-free zone or use a toilet with top entry.

## The dog must be able to obey

A dog that doesn't respond reliably to calls can cause a cat to panic in seconds. Before the animals meet, the dog should master these signals:

- “Sit” and “down”, even under distraction
- “Stay” so he stays in place while the cat walks by
- a termination signal such as “No” or “Off” to which he reacts immediately
- Call back if he does run after the cat

Practice these signals beforehand, with treats and praise. If your dog is young or very spirited, a dog trainer can help.

## Step by step to the first encounter

### Step 1: Get to know smells

As with bringing two cats together, it all starts with smell. First, keep the animals in separate areas and exchange blankets, pillows or toys. Let the dog sniff a cloth that you have rubbed over the cat's cheeks and reward him if he stays calm. Let the cat explore the dog blanket in peace.

### Step 2: See through a grid

Let the two see each other through a gate. The dog is on a leash or lying in its place, the cat has distance and opportunities to escape. Reward the dog generously if he looks at the cat and remains calm or looks away. Stop before he gets upset, barks, or fusses.

Pay attention to the cat: If it ducks, hisses, flattens its ears or runs away, the distance is too close. If she looks curious, sniffs or even grooms herself, that's a good sign.

### Step 3: Meet in the same room, dog on a leash

If both remain relaxed through the grid, the first meeting will take place in the same room. The dog is on a leash and lying on a blanket, ideally after a long walk when he is tired. The cat is allowed to move freely and decide how close it comes.

Let the cat set the pace. When she approaches and sniffs the dog, praise the dog quietly. If the dog jumps up, pulls on the leash, or barks, interrupt calmly and try more distance later.

### Step 4: free encounters under supervision

If meetings on a leash go smoothly over several days, you can let the leash drag loosely on the ground so that you can intervene at any time. Stay involved in every encounter. Only when the animals have met peacefully for weeks are they allowed to be together without supervision, and even then the cat should always have dog-free places to retreat.

## Take warning signs seriously

For dogs:

- rigid fixation of the cat, tense body, trembling with excitement
- Sneaking up, suddenly jumping out
- Ignoring signals when the cat is nearby
- Chasing the cat, even if it looks playful

At the cat:

- permanent hiding, no more eating, uncleanliness
- Weight loss, increased grooming and even bald spots
- constant attacks on the dog out of fear

If these signs persist, seek help from a dog trainer with experience in reunification or a veterinarian who specializes in behavioral medicine. Sometimes two animals just don't fit together. Then it is fairer to look for another solution than to let both of them live under constant stress.

## Misunderstandings in animal language

| signal | in the dog | at the cat |
|---|---|---|
| tail wagging | Excitement, often joy | Tension, anger |
| raised paw | Play request, appeasement | Defense, threat |
| Rolling onto your back | Submission, request for scratching | Trust, but often also defensiveness |
| direct view | Interest, sometimes threat | Threat |
| Growl | Warning | Warning |

These differences explain why well-intentioned approaches sometimes go awry. Over time, many dogs and cats learn to interpret each other's signals. You can help by interrupting situations before they escalate.

## Health in mixed households

Where dogs and cats live together, health care also needs attention. Many flea and tick treatments for dogs contain active ingredients such as permethrin, which are highly toxic to cats. Even close contact with a recently treated dog can cause symptoms of poisoning in a cat. Therefore, talk to the veterinary practice about products that are safe for both animal species and carefully follow the instructions on how long the animals should be kept apart after treatment. More about this can be found in [worm treatment and flea protection](/katzenwissen/wurmkur-und-flohschutz/).

Separation also applies to food. Cat food is too fatty and high in protein for dogs, and dog food does not contain all the nutrients cats need, such as not enough taurine. Anyone who constantly feeds their cat dog food runs the risk of deficiency symptoms.

## Puppy meets adult cat

A puppy is curious, stormy and does not yet understand the cat's signals. Many adult cats set boundaries with a hiss or a swipe of their paws. This is fine in moderation, but can be dangerous if the claws hit the puppy's eye. Therefore, supervise the first encounters carefully, give the puppy quiet activity such as a chewing item in his place and reward him when he leaves the cat alone. Give both of them regular breaks from each other so that the puppy doesn't get overexcited and the cat isn't permanently stressed.

## Everyday life with both of them

If the reunion is successful, both animals benefit from a reliable everyday life. Consciously plan time for each animal: walks and training for the dog, play sessions and rest periods for the cat. Many cats enjoy the time when the dog is out and about to roam around the apartment undisturbed. Maintain the cat's safe haven permanently, even if the two seem to be best friends.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does the merge take?

For well-matched animals, sometimes only a few days, more often a few weeks. For dogs with hunting instincts or very anxious cats, it can take months or even not work at all.

### Can the dog chase the cat in the game?

Better not. What is a game for the dog is often fear of death for the cat, and hunting can become more intense. Interrupt chasing consistently and offer the dog other games.

### Should dogs and cats eat together?

No. Separate feeding areas prevent arguments and ensure that each animal gets the food it needs. The cat eats best elevated or in its dog-free room.

### Can I bring a dog to a cat that has never seen a dog before?

Yes, many cats get used to a calm, cat-friendly dog. Choose a dog that has a proven track record of getting along with cats, go particularly slowly and allow several weeks for your cat to relax.

### Can a cat live with several dogs?

This is possible, but more demanding. In a group, dogs goad each other more easily, and harmless curiosity can quickly turn into a hunt. Introduce each dog individually to the cat, supervise the first shared situations with all dogs particularly closely and ensure there are plenty of high escape routes.
