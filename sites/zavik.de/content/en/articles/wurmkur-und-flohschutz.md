Parasites are not a sign of poor care. Even the most well-groomed cat can get worms or fleas, an outdoor cat anyway, but also an indoor cat if flea larvae are carried in on shoes. Most parasites can be easily treated. However, some can also be passed on to humans and can cause serious problems in young, old or sick cats.

This guide explains which parasites are common in cats in Germany, how you can recognize them and how often they should be prevented. The recommendations of the European specialist organization ESCCAP, which publishes guidelines for combating parasites in dogs and cats, serve as orientation.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - How often the feces are dewormed or the feces are examined depends on the risk of infection: outdoor cats that hunt mice need more frequent treatment than indoor cats.
> - Only a small proportion of fleas are on the cat; the rest live in the apartment as eggs, larvae and pupae.
> - Dog medications, especially those containing permethrin, can be fatal to cats.
> - Always treat all animals in the household at the same time.

## worms

### Roundworms

Roundworms are the most common intestinal worms in cats. Kittens can become infected through their mother's milk, older cats through worm eggs from the environment or through mice that carry larvae. In adult cats, an infestation often goes unnoticed. Kittens, on the other hand, may exhibit a bloated stomach, shaggy fur, diarrhea and stunted growth. Roundworms can also affect people, especially children, if they ingest worm eggs.

### Tapeworms

Cats can become infected with various tapeworms. The **cucumber seed tapeworm** is transmitted via fleas that the cat swallows while cleaning. Small, rice-grain-like limbs that can be seen at the anus or on the sleeping place are typical. Cats ingest other tapeworms through mice. This includes the **fox tapeworm**, which is mostly harmless to cats but can cause serious liver disease in humans. This is why regular tapeworm treatment is particularly important for cats that eat mice.

### More worms

Hookworms and lungworms are also found in outdoor animals. Lungworms can cause coughing and are transmitted via snails or prey that carry the larvae.

### How often to deworm?

The recommendations depend on the risk. A rough guide:

| cat | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Kittens | from around the third week of life every two weeks until two weeks after weaning, then monthly until the age of around six months |
| pure indoor cat without contact with prey animals | Treat once or twice a year or have your feces examined |
| outdoors | treat or fecal examinations at least four times a year |
| Outdoor animal that regularly catches and eats mice | more frequently, against tapeworms up to monthly, after consultation with the practice |
| Cat in a household with small children or people with weakened immune systems | treat or examine more often |

Instead of blindly deworming, the feces can also be examined regularly. For this purpose, samples are collected over several days because worm eggs are not contained in every pile of feces. If the sample is negative, no treatment is necessary. However, a fecal examination does not detect every infestation with certainty.

Worm treatments are available as tablets, pastes or spot-ons that are dripped onto the skin on the back of the neck. Spot-ons are a good alternative for cats that don't take tablets. Get advice on which remedy works against which worms, because not every preparation covers tapeworms.

## Fleas

### This is how you recognize a flea infestation

Fleas are a few millimeters long, brown and very agile. Often you don't see the fleas themselves, but rather their droppings: small black crumbs in the fur, especially on the back in front of the base of the tail. You can identify it safely with the **flea feces test**: comb the cat over a white, moistened paper towel. If the black crumbs turn reddish-brown, it is flea dirt because it consists of digested blood.

Other signs include frequent scratching and grooming, small crusts on the skin, hair loss and, in some cats, an allergic reaction to flea saliva with severe itching. In kittens, a heavy flea infestation can lead to anemia.

### Why the apartment needs to be treated

Only a few percent of a flea population live on the cat as adult fleas. The vast majority live as eggs, larvae and pupae in the environment: in carpets, upholstered furniture, cracks in the floor and in places where they sleep. Eggs fall from the cat, larvae hide in the dark and pupate. The pupae can lie dormant for weeks and hatch when they sense warmth and vibration.

That's why it's not enough to just treat the cat. This is how you do it:

1. **Treat all animals in the household,** cats and dogs, each with a product approved for the animal species.
2. **Vacuum thoroughly,** daily for several weeks, especially carpets, upholstery, cracks and sleeping areas. Then dispose of the vacuum cleaner bag.
3. **Wash blankets and pillows hot,** at least sixty degrees.
4. **Continue treatment** even if fleas are no longer visible. Since pupae can hatch over weeks, control often takes two to three months.

If the infestation is severe, an environmental spray with active ingredients against larvae can help. Make sure it is suitable for households with cats and follow the instructions carefully.

## Ticks

Outdoor animals often bring ticks home with them during the warm season. Ticks should be removed as soon as possible. You can use tick tweezers or a tick card to grab the tick close to the skin and slowly pull it out. You should not use oil, glue or alcohol.

Cats are less likely to contract tick-borne diseases than dogs. Nevertheless, tick protection can be useful for outdoor animals. There are spot-ons, tablets and collars for cats. When it comes to collars, you should choose models with a safety clasp that open if the cat gets caught on something.

## Warning: Dog medications can kill cats

Many flea and tick treatments for dogs contain permethrin or related active ingredients. They are well tolerated by dogs, but highly toxic for cats. Even a small dog dose dropped on a cat can cause tremors, convulsions and death. Close contact with a newly treated dog can also be dangerous for cats.

Therefore:

- For cats, only use products that are expressly approved for cats.
- Never split a dog pipette to use on the cat.
- If a dog in the household is being treated with a product containing permethrin, talk to the veterinarian about how long the dog and cat should stay apart, or choose a different product for the dog.

If your cat shakes, drools, staggers, or convulses after a treatment, it is an emergency. Go to the veterinary clinic immediately and take the packaging with you.

## Ear mites and other pests

Ear mites cause severe itching and dark, crumbly deposits in the ear. They are particularly common in kittens and in multi-cat households and are very contagious. Skin fungi and hair lice also occur. If your cat scratches frequently, shakes his head, or has bald patches, get him checked out.

## Giardia: persistent diarrhea pathogens

Giardia are not worms, but rather single-celled intestinal parasites. They are particularly common in kittens, cats from animal shelters and in multi-cat households. Recurring, mushy, often slimy and foul-smelling diarrhea is typical, while the cat may otherwise appear lively. Giardia is detected using a special test from a fecal sample. The vet prescribes the treatment. Hygiene is just as important as the medication, because the resistant cysts remain contagious in the area: clean the cat's litter boxes thoroughly with hot water every day during treatment, wash blankets in hot water and clean the cat's fur and rump at the end of the treatment so that it does not become infected again while cleaning.

## Collect a fecal sample correctly

Worm eggs and Giardia cysts are not excreted with every stool. Therefore, a collective sample from three days is more meaningful than a single sample. Every day, use a spoon or spatula to remove a hazelnut-sized amount of fresh feces from the toilet, without much litter, and place it in a clean, sealable container that you can get from the practice or buy at the pharmacy. Store the sample in a cool place, such as in the refrigerator in an extra bag, and bring it to the practice after the third day. Label the jar with the cat's name and dates.

## Plan parasite protection

The easiest thing is to have a fixed plan that you discuss with your veterinary practice: how often the worms will be dewormed or the feces will be examined, which flea and tick protection is useful in which months and which products your cat can tolerate. Enter the appointments in a calendar or a reminder app. Weigh your cat before each treatment, as most products are dosed according to body weight and a dose that is too low will not work reliably.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does an indoor cat even need to be dewormed?

The risk is lower, but not zero. Worm eggs and flea larvae can enter the home on shoes and clothing, and feeding raw meat can also be a risk. Treatment or fecal examination once or twice a year is often recommended.

### Can I buy wormers without a prescription?

Some remedies are freely available, others require a prescription. Nevertheless, get advice from your practice so that the remedy works against the relevant parasites and is dosed correctly.

### Do home remedies like garlic or coconut oil help against parasites?

No. Garlic is actually poisonous to cats. There is no reliable evidence of the effectiveness of such home remedies.

### Can I get worms from my cat?

This is generally possible with some parasites, such as roundworms, whose eggs can be ingested through dirty hands. The risk is low with simple hygiene: wash your hands after cleaning the litter box and before eating, clean the toilets daily and deworm the cat according to schedule. This is particularly important in households with small children, pregnant women or people with weakened immune systems.
