It's the vaccination date. The transport box is taken out of the basement, and before it even reaches the hallway, the cat has disappeared under the bed. What follows is unpleasant for everyone involved: chases through the apartment, a cat that braces itself against the box entrance with all four paws, scratches on its arms and an animal that arrives at the practice completely distraught.

Many cats are taken to the vet less often not because their humans don't want to, but because the journey there is so stressful. Much of this stress can be avoided. The key is a transport box that the cat knows not as a threat, but as a familiar place.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - The transport box does not belong in the basement, but rather as a cozy place in the apartment.
> - A box that opens from the top makes it easier to put it in and examine it.
> - Practice going in, closing the door, and carrying in small steps with rewards.
> - Partially cover the box on the way and secure it in the car.
> - Ask your practice about cat-friendly procedures.

## Why cats fear the box

Cats learn quickly. If the transport box only appears when a car ride, strange smells, other animals and an examination follow, they associate the box with exactly these unpleasant experiences. The box itself becomes a signal of danger.

The solution is to change this connection: the box should be primarily associated with pleasant things, with rest, food, play and a warm place to sleep. Then the occasional visit to the vet is just an exception.

## The right box

A good transport box makes everything else easier:

- **Stable:** Solid plastic boxes protect better than soft bags in the event of an emergency stop.
- **Open from top:** Boxes with an additional opening at the top or a removable top are ideal. The cat can be placed in from above, and at the vet's office it can often remain in the bottom half of the box while it is examined.
- **Big enough:** The cat should be able to stand, turn around and lie down in it.
- **Well ventilated,** with secure closure.
- **Easy to clean** in case the cat vomits or urinates on the way.

## Step 1: The box becomes a piece of furniture

Permanently place the box in a room where your cat likes to spend time. Remove the door or fasten it open so it doesn't slam accidentally. Place a soft blanket that smells like your cat or you.

Make the box interesting:

- Every now and then put a treat or some catnip inside.
- Feed your cat near the box, then directly in front of it, and finally inside it.
- Throw a toy into the box while playing.

After a few days or weeks, many cats begin to voluntarily use the box as a place to sleep. Then you have already achieved the most important thing.

## Step 2: Door, Lift, Carry

If your cat is lying relaxed in the box or eating, you can practice the next steps, each time only as long as he remains relaxed:

1. **Reattach the door,** but leave it open.
2. **Close the door briefly** while the cat is eating and open it again immediately. Slowly increase the duration.
3. **Lift the box:** Lift the closed box a few centimeters and put it down again, then open the door and reward.
4. **Carry it a few steps,** later through the apartment, then to the apartment door.
5. **In the car and back again,** without driving off.
6. **Short rides** around the block, and then a special treat at home.

When carrying the box, hold it horizontally in front of your body with both hands, not swinging on the handle. Many cats find swaying very unpleasant.

## When things have to happen quickly

There isn't always time for long training. If a visit to the vet is necessary at short notice and your cat is still afraid of the box, these tips will help:

- **Prepare the box in advance:** Don't put the box out just before you go, but the evening before.
- **Stay calm:** Cats sense excitement. Act as normal as possible.
- **Insert from above:** For a box with an opening at the top, place the cat in from above, hind legs first. This is easier for most cats than pushing them forward through a door.
- **Place the box upright:** For boxes without an opening at the top, you can stand the box upright, with the door facing up, and place the cat in it with its hind legs first.
- **Wrap in a towel:** For very panicked cats, it may help to wrap them loosely in a large towel.
- **Don't chase:** If the cat is hiding, don't start a chase. Close the doors to the rooms with hard-to-reach hiding places beforehand.

## The ride

- **Secure the box:** In the car, the box belongs in the footwell behind the passenger seat or is secured with the seat belt. It can fly around on the seat when braking.
- **Partially cover:** A cloth over the box calms many cats because they have less visual stimulation. Leave one side open for airflow.
- **Drive quietly,** without loud music, at a moderate temperature.
- **Speak quietly:** A calm voice can be calming.
- **Pheromone spray:** Some owners spray a spray with synthetic cat pheromones on the blanket in the box about fifteen minutes before the ride. Do not spray directly on the cat.
- **Fasting?** The practice will tell you whether the cat should not eat anything before the appointment. For cats who get sick while driving, an empty stomach can help.

## In practice

- **Place the box high:** In the waiting room, place the box on a chair or table, not on the floor where dogs can sniff.
- **Leave covered** and turn the opening towards the wall.
- **Ask about cat-friendly procedures:** Many practices have separate waiting areas for cats, set cat consultation hours or let you wait in the car until you are called.
- **Leave the cat in the box:** For boxes with a removable top, the examination can often take place in the bottom half where the cat feels safer.
- **For very anxious cats:** Ask the vet whether an anti-anxiety medication would be useful before the appointment. There are remedies that are given a few hours in advance and make the visit much easier for many cats.

## Back home

After visiting the vet, let your cat come out of the box in peace. If you have multiple cats, the cat that stayed at home may hiss at the one that returns because it smells like practice. In this case, keep the two separate for a while until the familiar smell returns and rub both with the same cloth.

Then leave the box back in the apartment with a blanket and the occasional treat. This way it remains a familiar place and the next visit will be easier.

## By train, bus and plane

A well-trained cat is also a big advantage when traveling on public transport. As small animals, cats generally travel free of charge on Deutsche Bahn trains in a closed transport box. The rules of the respective transport association apply on buses and trams, and cats in a box are usually not a problem there either. Place the box on your lap or in front of your feet, not in the hallway, and partially cover it so that the many impressions are not too much.

Air travel is stressful for cats and should remain the exception. Many airlines allow small cats in a soft carry bag in the cabin if the animal and bag combined do not exceed a certain weight. The exact conditions and number of seats per flight vary greatly, so you must register the cat in good time. When traveling abroad, the rules regarding pet passports, microchips and rabies vaccinations also apply, as described in [Vacation with or without a cat](/katzenwissen/urlaub-mit-oder-ohne-katze/).

## Start early: training with kittens

Training is easiest with young cats. Kittens are curious and quickly learn that the box is a comfortable place with treats. Because they have to go to the practice several times in their first year of life, for example for basic immunization, it is worth starting the exercises in the first few weeks after moving in. A box that grows with you or is large enough for an adult cat saves you having to buy a new one later.

## Clean the box after the vet visit

After a visit to the vet, the box often smells of practice, disinfectant and fear. Clean with warm water and a mild, unscented cleanser, rinse well, and allow to dry completely. Then put the familiar blanket back in that you washed before or that smells like home. You should avoid strongly scented cleaners and disinfectants with chlorine or phenols, as they bother your cat's sensitive nose and can sometimes be toxic to him.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does the training last?

That depends on the cat. Some people use the box as a place to sleep after a few days, but for very anxious cats it can take weeks. Every little progress counts.

### My cat vomits in the car. What helps?

Short practice rides, a well-secured box, a calm driving style and an empty stomach help many cats. For longer trips, the vet can prescribe a medicine for motion sickness.

### Can I put two cats in one box?

Better not. In stressful situations, cats who are friends can also hurt each other. Each cat should have its own box.

### What do I do if my cat urinates in the box?

This especially happens with anxious cats. Place an absorbent pad under the blanket and take a towel and a change bag with you. Then clean the box thoroughly with an enzyme cleaner so that no smell remains. If this happens every time you ride, it's a sign of great stress, which you can reduce by training more slowly.
