A fall from a balcony, a bleeding paw, a nibbled bouquet of lilies or a seizure in the middle of the night: emergencies for cats usually come without warning. In these moments, the first few minutes often decide how well a cat will survive the situation. If you are prepared, know a few steps and know when to go to the veterinary clinic immediately, you will act calmer and more effectively.

First aid is not a substitute for veterinary treatment. It bridges the time until the practice, prevents an injury from getting worse and protects you from bites and scratches. This guide explains what should be in an emergency first aid kit, how to check the most important vital signs and what to do in typical emergencies.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Save the numbers of your veterinary practice and the nearest veterinary clinic with emergency services for before you need them.
> - Injured cats bite and scratch out of fear. A large towel will protect you and calm the cat.
> - Normal values: body temperature around 38.0 to 39.2 degrees, breathing at rest around 20 to 30 breaths per minute, pink mucous membranes.
> - Never give human medication, induce vomiting, or pull on a string hanging from the mouth or anus.
> - Call the practice before the trip so the team can prepare.

## Be prepared

The most important first aid measure begins long before an emergency. Save the phone number of your veterinary practice, the number of the nearest veterinary clinic with 24-hour emergency service and the number of the veterinary emergency service in your region in your mobile phone. Also hang the numbers in a clearly visible place in the apartment, for example on the refrigerator, so that cat sitters and family members can also find them. Keep the transport box handy and include a folder with your pet's ID card and a list of your cat's medications and illnesses.

## The emergency first aid kit for cats

A small kit for cats fits in a box and should be in a fixed place:

| Content | For what |
|---|---|
| sterile compresses and gauze bandages | Cover wounds, pressure bandage |
| self-adhesive bandage | Bandage on paws and legs, does not stick to fur |
| sterile saline solution | Rinse wounds and eyes |
| Wound disinfection for animals | after consultation with the practice, without alcohol |
| Tick card or tick tweezers | Remove ticks |
| Scissors with rounded tips | Trim fur around wounds, trim bandages |
| Disposable gloves | Hygiene and personal protection |
| digital thermometer and Vaseline | Measure temperature |
| Syringe without needle | Give fluids, rinse wounds |
| large towel, emergency blanket | Secure cat, keep warm |
| small flashlight | look in the mouth or in the eyes |
| Card with emergency numbers | quickly at hand |

Medicines for humans are not included in this set. Many painkillers, especially paracetamol, but also ibuprofen and aspirin, are life-threatening for cats even in small quantities. Check the set once a year for expired products.

## Know the vital signs

In an emergency, it helps enormously if you know what your cat looks like when it's healthy. Practice checking the values ​​in quiet moments and write them down.

- **Breathing:** While the cat is lying relaxed or sleeping, count the number of breaths by watching the chest rise. About 20 to 30 breaths per minute are normal. Breathing with an open mouth is always a warning sign in cats.
- **Heartbeat:** Place your hand on the left side of your chest, directly behind your elbow. When at rest, a cat's heart beats around 120 to 180 times per minute, and faster when excited.
- **Mucous membranes:** Carefully lift the lip. The gums should be pale pink and moist. White, gray, yellow, bluish or brick red are warning signs. If you briefly press your finger on the gums, the color should return within about two seconds.
- **Temperature:** It is measured rectally with a digital thermometer and some petroleum jelly. Normal is around 38.0 to 39.2 degrees. If it is below 37.5 or above 39.5 degrees, you should call the practice.

[Recognizing signs of illness](/katzenwissen/krankheitsanzeichen-erkennen/) describes how you can recognize illnesses early through small changes.

## Keep an injured cat safe

Even the gentlest cat can bite and scratch when it is in pain or very scared. Approach calmly and speak quietly. Place a large towel over the cat and wrap it so that only the head is visible, or if there is an injury to the head, wrap it so that the injured area remains exposed. Lift with one hand under your chest and one under your pelvis to keep your back supported. A wrapped cat often feels safer and cannot injure itself further.

## What to do in typical emergencies

### Bleeding and wounds

Press a sterile compress or clean cloth firmly onto a bleeding wound and hold the pressure for a few minutes. For wounds on the paw or leg, you can apply a pressure bandage: apply a compress to the wound, then a gauze bandage and a self-adhesive bandage, tight but not so tight that the toes become cold or swollen. Don't tie off one leg. Rinse small abrasions with saline solution. Bite wounds often look harmless, but they easily become infected and form abscesses. So always have them checked out by a vet.

### Fall and accident

After a fall or an accident with a car, cats often initially appear uninjured and run away. Internal injuries, fractures, bruised lungs or broken jaws sometimes only become apparent after hours. Therefore, always take the cat to the veterinary clinic. Transport her as flat as possible, for example in a box with a solid base or on a board with a blanket, keep her warm and do not give her anything to eat or drink. More about preventing falls can be found in [Proof your balcony for cats] (/katzenwissen/balkon-katzensicher-machen/).

### poisoning

If you suspect poisoning, call the veterinary practice or animal clinic immediately. Take the packaging, plant parts or leftovers with you. Do not induce vomiting or give home remedies such as milk, oil, or salt water. If a substance gets on the fur, such as oil, paint or a cleaning product, prevent the cat from grooming itself by wrapping it in a towel and, after consultation, wash the area with lukewarm water and mild dishwashing liquid. Typical sources of danger are described in [Poisonous plants for cats](/katzenwissen/giftige-pflanzen-fuer-katzen/) and [Poisonous foods for cats](/katzenwissen/giftige-lebensmittel-fuer-katzen/).

### Heat stroke

Cats rarely pant. A cat that is breathing with its mouth open, salivating heavily, staggering, or apathetic on a hot day is in danger. Immediately move her to a cool, shady place, wet the fur with lukewarm, not ice-cold, water, provide air movement, and offer water if she can swallow. Then drive straight to the practice, as heat stroke can damage organs, even if the cat appears to be recovering.

### Hypothermia

Cats that have been outside wet, very old or sick animals and kittens cool down quickly. Dry the cat, wrap it in a warm blanket, and add a hot water bottle wrapped in a towel with warm, not hot, water. A hypothermic cat should be examined by a veterinarian.

### Burns

Cool a burn, such as from a stovetop, for a few minutes with lukewarm to cool, not ice-cold, water. Do not apply any ointments, oils or home remedies and drive to the practice. Even small burns on the paw pads are very painful.

### Foreign bodies and threads

If your cat gags, salivates heavily, or scratches its mouth, there may be something stuck in its mouth or throat. Look inside carefully with a flashlight, but only grab it if the object is clearly visible and you can safely grab it. If there is a thread, string or ribbon hanging from the mouth or anus, do not pull it under any circumstances. The other end may be caught in the intestines and pulling can seriously injure the intestines. Go to the practice immediately. Shortness of breath with blue mucous membranes is a life-threatening emergency.

### Seizure

During a seizure, the cat twitches uncontrollably, often lies on its side, salivates, sometimes leaks urine or feces, and does not respond to speech. Don't hold her or put anything in her mouth. Clear away objects that could hurt her, darken the room and keep your distance. Watch the clock and film the seizure if possible. A seizure that lasts longer than about five minutes, or multiple seizures in quick succession, is an emergency. Even after a single, short seizure, the cat should be examined by a veterinarian.

### Urinary retention

A tomcat who repeatedly goes to the toilet, strains and does not urinate is in acute danger to his life. Go to the practice or veterinary clinic immediately, even at night. More about this can be found in [Cat pees in the apartment](/katzenwissen/katze-pinkelt-in-die-wohnung/).

## Resuscitation: when the heart stops

If a cat is no longer breathing and no heartbeat can be felt, cardiopulmonary resuscitation can bridge the time until the hospital. If possible, have a second person do it while you drive or call the clinic. Lay the cat on its right side, grasp the chest with one hand just behind the front legs and compress it by about a third, about 100 to 120 times per minute. After every 30 compressions, keep your mouth closed and briefly blow into your nose twice until your chest rises slightly. Unfortunately, the chances of success are slim, but every minute counts. Many veterinary practices and animal welfare associations offer first aid courses for pet owners in which you can practice these maneuvers on models.

## The path to practice

Call before you leave, briefly describe what happened and say when you will arrive. This way the team can prepare and you will find out whether you should go straight to a clinic. Place the cat in the carrier, partially cover it, and secure it in the car. Drive calmly, even when you're in a hurry. Take your pet ID card, medication and, if necessary, packaging for toxic substances with you.

## Frequently asked questions

### When is an emergency an emergency?

Cats with shortness of breath, breathing with their mouths open, heavy bleeding, seizures, paralysis, suspected poisoning, after a fall or accident, with urinary retention or repeated vomiting should be taken to the practice or clinic immediately. If in doubt, call and get advice.

### Can I disinfect wounds with iodine or alcohol?

Better not. Alcohol burns badly, and some products are unsuitable for cats because they will lick the area. Rinse wounds with sterile saline solution and ask the practice which disinfectant is suitable for your emergency kit.

### How much does a visit to the emergency service cost?

The veterinary emergency service charges higher fees than during regular consultation hours. How much the bill will be depends on the examination and treatment. However, the question of cost should never lead to a cat not being treated in a real emergency. A reserve or insurance provides security here.
