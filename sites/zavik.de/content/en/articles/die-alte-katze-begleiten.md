Cats are living longer today than ever before. Fifteen, eighteen, sometimes over twenty years are no longer uncommon. Anyone who accompanies a cat for such a long time will experience how the wild kitten becomes a calm companion who enjoys the sun on the windowsill, sleeps longer and perhaps becomes a little more stubborn.

Age brings changes, and some of them need our support. Many problems in older cats are overlooked because they are thought to be part of normal aging. A lot can be alleviated. This guide shows you how to give your old cat a good retirement and how to prepare for the farewell as it approaches.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - From around ten to twelve years old, a cat is considered a senior. Two checkups a year make sense.
> - Osteoarthritis is very common and often overlooked because cats hardly limp and jump less.
> - Ramps, low toilets, warm places to lie down and accessible bowls make everyday life easier.
> - Nocturnal calling and disorientation can indicate illness or age-related mental changes.
> - Quality of life is the most important measure, even when making the difficult decision about leaving.

## When is a cat old?

Cats age differently, but roughly speaking, life phases can be distinguished. From around seven years of age one speaks of a mature cat, from around eleven to twelve years of age it is called a senior cat, from around fifteen years of age it is called a very old cat. The often-quoted conversion “one cat year equals seven human years” is not true: cats age very quickly in the first two years, then more slowly afterwards. A fifteen-year-old cat is roughly equivalent to a person in their seventies.

## The most common age-related complaints

Certain diseases occur more frequently as we get older. Many of these can be treated if they are detected:

- **Arthrosis:** painful changes in the joints
- **Chronic kidney disease:** increased drinking, weight loss, loss of appetite
- **Hyperthyroidism:** Cravings when losing weight, restlessness, shaggy fur
- **High blood pressure:** often as a result of other illnesses; can cause sudden blindness
- **Dental disease:** Bad breath, careful eating
- **Diabetes:** especially in overweight cats
- **Tumor diseases**
- **Decreased senses:** worse vision and hearing

That's why veterinarians recommend two checkups a year for older cats, including weight checks, blood and urine tests and blood pressure measurements.

## Osteoarthritis: the silent pain

Osteoarthritis is one of the most common and overlooked diseases in older cats. X-ray studies show that a large proportion of older cats have joint changes, even if their humans don't notice it.

Cats rarely limp noticeably. Instead, they change their behavior:

- They are less likely to jump on furniture, window sills or the scratching post or hesitate before jumping.
- You jump in several stages, for example over a chair onto the table.
- You walk stairs more slowly or avoid them.
- You no longer use the toilet reliably because the entrance is too high.
- They groom themselves less, especially on their backs and rumps.
- Their claws become too long because they scratch less.
- They are irritated when touched on their back or hips.
- You sleep more and play less.

If you notice any changes like this, talk to your vet. There are well-tolerated pain medications for cats, including newer medications that can be injected monthly. Many cats become noticeably more lively with pain treatment. But never give painkillers to people. Many of them are poisonous to cats.

## Make the apartment suitable for seniors

Small changes make everyday life much easier for an old cat:

- **Ramps and steps:** to favorite places such as sofa, bed or windowsill. A stool or a box as an intermediate step is often enough.
- **Low toilet:** with a shallow entry, at least one on each floor, not too far from the sleeping area.
- **Accessible bowls:** Food and water can be reached without jumping, preferably slightly elevated so that the cat does not have to bend down deeply.
- **Warm places to lie down:** Old cats freeze more quickly. Soft, warm blankets, a place by the radiator or a heating pad suitable for animals are very popular.
- **Non-slip pads:** Carpets or mats on smooth floors provide support.
- **Night light:** For cats who have poor eyesight, a weak light at night helps with orientation.
- **Consistency:** Do not move furniture, maintain feeding and toilet areas. Cats with failing eyesight in particular orientate themselves on familiar paths.

## care and employment

**Grooming:** Older cats groom themselves less. Regular, gentle brushing helps against tangles and is a nice time for two.

**Claws:** Check the claws regularly, especially the thumb claws. In old cats they often become too long and can grow in.

**Play:** Old cats also like to play, just quieter and for a shorter time. A spring fishing rod that slowly glides over the ground or a food game keeps the body and mind moving.

**Diet:** Many elderly cats lose weight. Easily digestible, high-energy wet food, small, frequent meals and warmed food help. Find out more in our guide to nutrition for older cats.

## If the cat calls at night or becomes forgetful

Some old cats begin to call loudly at night, appear disoriented, stare into space, forget where the toilet is, or briefly fail to recognize familiar people. This can have many causes, such as hyperthyroidism, high blood pressure, pain, vision or hearing loss.

If such causes are ruled out, an age-related change in the brain may be behind it, similar to dementia in humans. Experts speak of cognitive dysfunction syndrome. It cannot be cured, but it can often be alleviated:

- fixed daily routines with regular meals and play times
- mental stimulation through food games and gentle play
- Night light and a toilet near the sleeping area
- a place to sleep close to people so the cat doesn't feel alone at night
- on veterinary advice, special foods or supplements intended to support brain function

## Assess quality of life

At some point the question arises as to how well the cat is still doing. A simple way to help is to regularly and honestly check:

- Is she still eating and drinking enough?
- Can she reach and use her toilet?
- Is she still grooming herself?
- Does she have pain that cannot be adequately relieved?
- Does she still show interest in her surroundings, in her people, in her favorite places?
- Are there more good days than bad?

Some owners keep a simple diary in which they mark each day as good or bad. This helps to recognize changes over weeks that are lost in everyday life. Many veterinary practices also use structured quality of life questionnaires.

## The farewell

If, despite treatment, a cat's quality of life is no longer good and cannot be improved, euthanasia is a final act of care. For most people, this decision is one of the hardest decisions they will ever make about an animal. There is rarely one right day. Many veterinarians say: It's better to be a week too early than a day too late.

Speak openly with your vet. She can help you assess your cat's condition and explain how the euthanasia process works. The cat is usually given a sedative first so that it falls asleep before the actual medication is given. Many practices offer to euthanize the cat at home, in familiar surroundings. Ask about it.

Consider beforehand whether you want to be there, what should happen to the cat afterwards, whether it should be buried in your own garden, if that is allowed, or whether an animal burial is desired, and whether other animals in the household should say goodbye. Some cats search for their deceased companion for a while.

Grief for an animal is real grief. Take your time for it. It can be helpful for children to honestly and age-appropriately explain what happened and say goodbye together.

## Nutrition and drinking in old age

Many older cats eat less because their sense of smell and taste decreases or because their teeth and gums hurt. Slightly warmed wet food smells stronger and is often preferred. Offer several small meals throughout the day and monitor weight: gradual weight loss is an important warning sign in seniors and should always be clarified. Also pay attention to the amount you drink. If your cat suddenly drinks significantly more, it could be due to kidney disease, an overactive thyroid or diabetes. Several water points, a drinking fountain and wet food help to get enough fluids. ## Grooming for seniors

Old cats often groom themselves less thoroughly because their mobility decreases or because pain makes contortion uncomfortable. The fur then becomes dull, matted on the back and hind legs, and the claws grow too long because they are sharpened less often. Brush your cat regularly and gently, check its claws every few weeks and trim them if necessary. How to do this is explained in [Grooming cats](/katzenwissen/fellpflege-bei-katzen/) and [Cutting claws](/katzenwissen/krallen-schneiden/).

## Frequently asked questions

### My old cat sleeps almost all day. Is that normal?

Older cats sleep more. But if she shows little interest, eats little or withdraws even when she is awake, have her examined. There is often pain behind inertia.

### Are treatments still worth it for a very old cat?

This depends on the disease, the prognosis and the character of the cat. Many treatments, such as pain therapy or medication for hyperthyroidism, significantly improve the quality of life and are not very stressful. Talk to your vet about the benefits and burdens.

### Should I get a young cat to keep the old one company?

Mostly not. A young kitten often overwhelms an old cat with its play instinct. If you're considering a second cat, choose a quiet, older cat and be particularly careful when reuniting.

### Do nutritional supplements help your joints?

Some preparations, such as those containing omega-3 fatty acids, can relieve joint problems. However, they do not replace pain therapy if your cat suffers from osteoarthritis. Only give products that are intended for cats and speak to the vet's office beforehand to make sure they fit in with other medications and food.
