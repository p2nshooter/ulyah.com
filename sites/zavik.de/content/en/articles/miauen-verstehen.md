Cats communicate with each other primarily through body language and smells. They use sounds rather sparingly among adult conspecifics: growling, hissing and screeching in conflicts, calls during mating season, cooing between mother and young. The classic “meow,” on the other hand, is surprisingly rarely heard among adult cats.

It's different with people. Many cats meow to their humans, often and in different ways, and each cat develops its own language. Researchers suspect that cats have particularly developed their meowing ability when living with humans because we react more strongly to sounds than to subtle body signals. This guide will help you understand your cat's sounds.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Kittens meow to call their mother. Adult cats retain the meowing habit primarily for humans.
> - Every cat has its own repertoire. The best way to get to know it is through observation.
> - Cooing and trilling are friendly greetings, chirping shows hunting excitement.
> - Hissing and growling are warnings that you should respect.
> - Sudden, frequent or nocturnal calling, especially in older cats, should be checked by a veterinarian.

## Why cats meow

Kittens meow in the first days of life when they are cold, hungry or have lost their mother. The mother responds to these calls and comes. As kittens grow up, they largely lose this meowing sound towards other cats.

With humans, many cats retain and refine the meow. You learn which sounds are successful: the meow that causes the door to open, the meow that causes the bowl to be filled, the meow that causes someone to come play. That's why every cat's meow sounds different, and why people can often interpret their own cat's sounds well, but can hardly interpret those of another cat.

Research has shown that cats adapt their meowing to their humans and that humans are better at identifying their cats' sounds than those of other cats. The meaning therefore arises in the shared history.

## The most important sounds

### Meow

Meowing is versatile. A few forms can be roughly distinguished:

- **Short, bright meow:** a greeting or a short message, such as when you come home.
- **Several short meows in a row:** great joy, excitement, often as a greeting.
- **Long-drawn, demanding meow:** The cat wants something, such as food, through a door or attention.
- **Deep, plaintive meowing:** Dissatisfaction, complaint, sometimes discomfort.
- **Very loud, drawn-out calling:** in unneutered cats during mating season; in older cats sometimes a sign of disorientation or illness.
- **Silent meow:** The cat opens its mouth, but hardly a sound comes out. Many people find this particularly touching. Often the sound is simply too high or too quiet for our ears.

### Cooing and trilling

A short, cooing sound with a closed mouth that rises slightly upwards is a friendly greeting. Mother cats call their kittens this way, and many cats greet their humans this way when they come into the room or when they invite them to follow them, for example to the bowl.

### Purr

Purring is a separate chapter. It often accompanies contentment, but can also occur with stress or pain. Find out more in our guide to purring.

### Cackling and chattering

When a cat sits at the window watching a bird and quickly twitches its jaw and makes a clattering, chirping sound, it is called peeping. It occurs when the cat sees prey that it cannot reach. The meaning is not finally clear. Some experts see this as excitement and frustration, others suspect a kind of preparation for the fatal bite.

### Hiss

When hissing, the cat opens its mouth, pulls back its lips and audibly lets out air. It's a clear warning: Don't come any closer. Hissing is a defensive reaction and not a sign of malice. A hissing cat feels threatened. Give her space.

### Growl

A deep, sustained rumble is also a warning, often louder than hissing. Cats growl towards other cats, dogs, strangers, sometimes even when eating, when they are defending their prey.

### Screaming and screaming

A shrill, loud scream occurs when there is pain, great fright or during a fight. If your cat suddenly cries out, perhaps because you stepped on its tail, apologize and leave it alone. Repeated screaming for no apparent reason, especially when running or jumping, may indicate pain.

### Rolling calls

Unneutered female cats cry loudly, in a long-drawn manner and plaintively, often for hours during heat. Unneutered male cats respond with similar sounds. Castration stops this behavior.

## Talkative and quiet cats

Some cats can hardly be heard, others seem to be constantly saying something. This partly has to do with the breed: Siamese cats and related oriental breeds are considered to be particularly talkative, with loud, piercing voices. Many other cats are rather quiet.

Experience also plays a role. Cats whose people talk to them a lot and respond to their meowing often become more talkative. Many cats respond when you talk to them and real conversations develop.

## When a cat meows too much

Excessive meowing can have various causes:

- **Hunger or Expectation:** The cat has learned that meowing brings food. Fixed feeding times and an automatic feeder help.
- **Boredom:** Indoor cats without sufficient activity demand attention. More play and food toys can help.
- **Loneliness:** Some cats call when they are alone or look for their people at night.
- **Stress and Changes:** Moving, new animals, new people.
- **Heating** in uncastrated animals.
- **Illnesses,** especially in older cats.

Once meowing is learned, it helps to only respond to calm behavior. Don't feed while the cat meows demandingly, but wait for a quiet moment. Praise and notice her when she is relaxed. This requires patience, because here too the meowing often gets louder before it subsides.

## When sounds are a warning sign

Get your cat checked by a vet if:

- She suddenly meows much more or much louder than usual
- She calls, wanders around or seems disoriented, especially at night
- their voice sounds hoarse or changed
- she screams when urinating or defecating
- she screams when you pick her up or touch her in certain places
- an otherwise talkative cat suddenly falls silent

In older cats, increased nighttime calling is often due to an overactive thyroid, high blood pressure, declining eyesight or hearing, or age-related mental changes. Many of these causes can be treated.

## Talk to the cat

Cats recognize their people's voices and respond to their names, even if they don't always come. A calm, friendly voice helps build trust, especially with new or fearful cats. Many cats like it when you speak to them in a slightly higher, gentler voice.

You can also use your reactions to improve communication: greet your cat when it coos at you, respond when it tells you something, and respond reliably to sounds that you find good. This creates a common language.

## Kitten sounds

Kittens are particularly talkative. During the first few days in their new home, many people call for their mother and siblings at night because they feel alone. This is normal and usually goes away after a few days. It is helpful to have a warm, sheltered place to sleep near you, such as a cave with a blanket that smells like your previous home, and a short, quiet session of play and food before going to bed. A second kitten as a companion helps the most. [The first days with the new cat](/katzenwissen/die-ersten-tage-mit-der-neuen-katze/) describes how the first days are successful.

## When a cat doesn't hear what it's saying

Deaf cats are often particularly loud because they cannot hear their own voice and cannot adjust the volume. Congenital deafness occurs more often in white cats, especially in animals with blue eyes. Even with age, many cats' hearing deteriorates and they then call louder than before. A deaf cat is easily startled when approached from behind. Make yourself noticeable through vibrations, for example by stepping firmly or through light signals, and do not touch them suddenly while you are sleeping. Deaf cats can be approached surprisingly well with hand signals, for example for food or to come here.

## Get to know your cat's repertoire

Every cat has its own vocabulary. If you want to understand their sounds better, record them on your cell phone for a while and note the situation: before feeding, at the balcony door, when greeting, at the window. After a few weeks you will recognize patterns and can often distinguish their wishes just by the sound. Such recordings also help the vet if your cat's voice changes or it calls unusually.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does my cat meow at me when I'm on the toilet?

A closed door and a person who has disappeared are reason enough for many cats to call. Some just want to be there. This is harmless.

### My cat never meows. Is she sick?

Not necessarily. Some cats are simply quiet and prefer to communicate with their bodies. The only thing that is worrying is when an otherwise talkative cat suddenly falls silent.

### Does my cat understand what I'm saying?

Cats don't understand sentences, but they learn to associate words and tone of voice with events. Many people recognize their name and words like “food” or “play” when they often hear them in certain situations.

### Why does my cat meow at night?

Cats are particularly active at dusk and early in the morning. Nighttime meowing often has to do with hunger, boredom or a desire for company. A game and a small snack before bedtime help many cats sleep more soundly. If an older cat suddenly calls at night, it should be checked by a vet.
