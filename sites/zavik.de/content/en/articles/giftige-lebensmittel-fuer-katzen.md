Cats are generally pickier than dogs when it comes to eating. They are less likely to pounce on anything that falls off the table. Nevertheless, food poisoning occurs again and again: because a curious cat licks the leftovers in the pan, because it nibbles on the plate or because people with good intentions give it something that is harmful to it.

Many foods that are healthy or harmless to humans are processed differently by a cat's metabolism. Your liver has difficulty breaking down certain substances, and even small amounts can be enough to cause damage. This guide shows the most important dangers in the kitchen.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Onions, garlic, leeks and chives destroy red blood cells, even cooked or powdered.
> - Chocolate, coffee, tea and energy drinks contain substances that are toxic to cats.
> - Raw pork can transmit the Aujeszky virus, which is fatal to cats.
> - Many adult cats cannot tolerate milk.
> - If poisoning is suspected, call the veterinary practice immediately and do not induce vomiting yourself.

## The most dangerous foods

### Onions, garlic, leeks and chives

All plants in the Allium genus contain sulfur compounds that damage red blood cells in cats. Anemia can occur, which often only becomes apparent a few days after eating: the cat becomes weak, its mucous membranes become pale, and it breathes more quickly. Cats are more sensitive to these substances than dogs.

The danger also lies in boiled, fried and dried forms, such as onion powder, garlic granules and ready-made sauces. Baby food, broths, sausage and many ready meals often contain onion or garlic powder. Anyone who gives leftovers from their own plate often feeds them unnoticed.

### Chocolate, coffee and tea

Chocolate contains theobromine, coffee and black tea contain caffeine. Both substances belong to the group of methylxanthines and have an effect on the heart and nervous system. Signs of poisoning include restlessness, trembling, vomiting, diarrhea, rapid heartbeat and, in the worst case, seizures. The darker the chocolate, the more theobromine it contains. Although cats don't like the sweet taste because they can't taste sweets, they are sometimes interested in the fat in milk chocolate or cream. Coffee powder, tea bags and energy drinks should also be kept out of reach.

### Grapes and raisins

Grapes and raisins can cause acute kidney failure in dogs. Cases have been described much less frequently in cats, but cannot be ruled out. Since the cause and the dangerous amount are not exactly known, veterinarians advise generally not to give cats grapes and raisins.

### Alcohol

Even small amounts of alcohol are dangerous for cats. They lead to staggering, vomiting, hypoglycaemia, falling body temperature and can even lead to coma. Also be careful with raw yeast dough: the yeast produces alcohol in the warm stomach and the dough expands.

### Raw pork

Raw pork and raw wild boar meat can transmit the Aujeszky virus, the causative agent of the so-called pseudorage. The virus is harmless to humans, but infection is almost always fatal for cats and dogs. Domestic pigs in Germany are considered free of this virus, but it does occur in wild boars, and since the origin cannot always be determined with certainty, cats should only be given pork well cooked.

### Raw fish in large quantities

Many types of raw fish contain the enzyme thiaminase, which breaks down vitamin B1. If raw fish is fed regularly and in large quantities, a vitamin B1 deficiency with neurological deficits can occur. Cooked fish is not a problem. Watch out for bones.

### Cooked bones

Boiled or fried bones, especially from poultry, splinter easily and can injure the mouth, esophagus or intestines. Raw, meaty bones are fed in certain forms of feeding; However, this should only be done with sound knowledge and ideally after consultation.

## Also not edible, but in the kitchen

- **Medicines:** Paracetamol is fatal to cats even in small doses. Ibuprofen, aspirin and many other painkillers for humans are also dangerous. Never give your cat medication without a vet's advice.
- **Essential Oils:** Tea tree oil and many other essential oils are toxic to cats because their liver cannot break them down. Be careful with fragrance lamps and room sprays.
- **Cleaning products:** Cats clean their paws and pick up residue from freshly mopped floors.
- **Lilies:** The flowers on the kitchen table are one of the most common causes of poisoning. Even small amounts of pollen or the vase water can lead to kidney failure. Find out more in our guide to poisonous plants.

## What cats can eat

If you want to do something good for your cat, there are safe alternatives in small quantities:

- unseasoned, cooked chicken or turkey meat
- cooked, boneless fish
- a small piece of boiled egg
- special cat snacks that are included in the daily ration

Anything given in addition to the complete feed should only make up a small part of the daily diet.

## What to do if poisoning is suspected?

1. **Keep calm and remove the cat from the source of the poison.** Put away leftovers so that nothing more can be eaten.
2. **Call the vet's office or animal clinic immediately.** Describe what the cat ate, approximately how much and when.
3. **Take packaging or leftovers with you.** This will help determine the toxin.
4. **Do not induce vomiting yourself.** Salt water, mustard or fingers in the throat are dangerous and can do more harm than good. The vet decides whether vomiting makes sense, and she has safe remedies for this.
5. **Do not use home remedies.** Milk does not neutralize toxins, and oil can even accelerate the absorption of some substances.

The quicker a poisoning is treated, the better the chances. Don't wait for symptoms to appear. With many poisons, such as onions or lilies, the damage only becomes apparent after hours or days, when treatment becomes more difficult.

## Holidays and festivals: special caution

There are particularly many temptations within reach on holidays. At **Christmas** there are cookies, chocolate, roast with sauce and onions on the table, as well as tinsel and gift ribbons that cats like to swallow. At **Easter** there are chocolate eggs and daffodils in the vase. When **barbecuing** in the garden or on the balcony, leftover meat, bones, marinated pieces with garlic and wooden skewers attract, which can cause serious injuries to the intestines. On **New Year's Eve** there are often half-full glasses of alcohol lying around. On such days, clear up the area consistently, ask guests not to give the cat anything, and give it a quiet space to retreat away from the hustle and bustle.

## Dangers around the kitchen

Not only food itself is dangerous, but also what is attached to it. Roaster strings, sausage skins, aluminum foil with leftover fat, cling film and toothpicks smell like food and are therefore often nibbled on or swallowed. Threads and cords are particularly treacherous because they can get caught in the intestines and cause it to contract. A trash can with a tight lid that cannot be opened by a cat's paw will prevent many emergencies. Hot stoves and open pots are another danger. Get your cat used to the work surface being off-limits right from the start and don't leave him unattended in the kitchen while cooking.

## Dietary supplements for humans

Vitamin tablets and nutritional supplements from the medicine cabinet can also harm cats. High doses of vitamin D, even in comparatively small amounts, lead to a dangerous increase in calcium levels in cats, and iron supplements can cause serious damage to the stomach and intestines. Therefore, store such preparations just as safely as you would medicines and do not leave any tablets lying open on the table or bedside table.

## Clear rules at the dinner table

It's easiest if there is a simple rule in your household: There is nothing from the table. Cats that have never been successful at begging tend to stay away from the dinner table. If all family members and guests adhere to this, it not only protects against poisoning, but also against obesity. If you still want to treat your cat to something special, give it from its own bowl, with its own meal.

## Frequently asked questions

### My cat ate a piece of pizza with onions. Is that dangerous?

A single small piece does not necessarily cause poisoning, but the dangerous amount depends on the cat's weight and the amount of onion. Call your veterinary practice and describe the situation. She can assess whether treatment or control is necessary.

### Is commercial cat milk safe?

Special cat milk has reduced lactose and is usually well tolerated. However, it is not a drink, but a high-calorie snack and should only be given occasionally.

### Can my cat eat grass?

Yes. Many cats like to eat cat grass, such as wheat grass or oat grass. It is non-toxic. Be careful with ornamental grasses with sharp edges and with plants that have been treated with pesticides.

### Can my cat eat yogurt or quark?

Some cats tolerate small amounts of natural yogurt or curd because they contain less lactose than milk. But that is not necessary. Sweetened products and those with sweeteners such as xylitol do not belong in the cat bowl.

### Is tuna healthy for cats?

An occasional small piece of tuna in its own juice is not a problem for most cats. It is not suitable as a main diet because it is unbalanced, and you should avoid tuna in oil or with salt completely.
