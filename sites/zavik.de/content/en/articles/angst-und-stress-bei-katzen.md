Cats are seen as independent and calm, but in reality they are very sensitive animals. A new piece of furniture, an unfamiliar noise, a strange cat in front of the window or a loud visitor can upset them more than we realize. Because cats were originally not only hunters, but also prey of larger animals, they often only show fear and discomfort quietly. Many owners only notice their cat's stress when it becomes unclean, constantly hides or becomes ill.

This guide explains how you can recognize stress and anxiety in your cat, which triggers are particularly common and how you can design their environment so that they feel safe. It also covers special situations such as New Year's Eve, thunderstorms and visitors and the question of when professional help makes sense.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Stress often shows itself quietly: hiding, eating less, excessive cleaning, uncleanliness or irritability.
> - Common triggers include change, noise, conflict with other cats, boredom and pain.
> - Every cat needs safe retreats and separate places for food, water, toilet, scratching and rest.
> - On New Year's Eve, cats stay indoors, windows and curtains are closed, and a quiet room is provided.
> - Prolonged stress can make you sick. Have behavioral changes checked by a veterinarian.

## How you recognize stress

Acute fear is usually easy to recognize: the cat crouches, flattens its ears, dilates its pupils, bristles its fur, tucks its tail or fluffs it out, hisses or flees. It is more difficult to notice persistent, underlying stress. Watch out for these changes:

- **Withdrawal:** The cat hides more often and for longer periods of time, avoids certain rooms or people.
- **Changed eating:** less appetite, sometimes eating too quickly or only eating at night.
- **Excessive grooming:** especially on the stomach and inner thighs, including bald spots.
- **Uncleanliness and marking:** Urine outside the toilet, often on vertical surfaces or on textiles.
- **Irritability:** The cat hisses more quickly, scratches or bites, or attacks other cats.
- **Excessive vigilance:** She sleeps restlessly, jumps at small noises, and constantly watches doors and windows.
- **More shouting:** especially at night.
- **Physical signs:** Vomiting, diarrhea, frequent trips to the toilet with small amounts of urine.

## Acute and chronic stress

Short-term stress is a part of life. A visit to the vet or a loud bang puts the body on alert for a short time, then the cat calms down again. It becomes problematic when stress persists, for example because a cat feels constantly threatened in its own home. Chronic stress weakens the immune system and can promote illness. It is known, for example, that in cats with herpes viruses in their bodies, stress can trigger a new episode of cat cold. A certain form of bladder infection that is not caused by bacteria is also closely related to stress.

## Common triggers

| Trigger | Examples |
|---|---|
| Changes | Moving, renovation, new furniture, changed daily routine |
| new roommates | Baby, partner, new cat, dog |
| Conflicts with other cats | Arguments in the household, strange cats in front of the window or at the cat flap |
| noise | Fireworks, thunderstorms, construction sites, loud music, vacuum cleaners |
| strangers | Visits, craftsmen, celebrations |
| Lack of resources | too few toilets, a single feeding place for several cats |
| Boredom | Indoor cats with no activity or hunting game |
| Pain and illness | Osteoarthritis, toothache, bladder problems |

The last point in particular is often overlooked. A cat that suddenly reacts irritably or withdraws is often in pain. That's why any significant change in behavior should be preceded by a visit to the vet.

## Five pillars of a cat-friendly environment

International feline medical societies have described what cats need to feel safe in their environment. These five principles help prevent stress:

1. **A safe place:** Every cat needs places to retreat where they can hide and not be disturbed, ideally elevated places with an overview.
2. **Separate resources:** Food, water, toilets, scratching areas, play areas and sleeping areas should be in different locations and in multiple cat households so that no cat has to pass another.
3. **Opportunity to hunt and play:** Daily hunting games and food that has to be worked for reduce tension.
4. **Reliable, friendly interaction:** Cats appreciate people whose behavior is predictable and who give them a choice about whether they want contact.
5. **Respect for the sense of smell:** Cats orient themselves by smells. Strong scents, scented litter and frequent washing of all berths remove familiar signals.

How to implement these principles in an apartment is described in [Keeping an indoor cat in a species-appropriate manner](/katzenwissen/wohnungskatze-artgerecht-halten/).

## New Year's Eve and thunderstorms

Fireworks are the worst night of the year for many cats. Sudden bangs, flashes of light and the smell of black powder trigger panic. How to prepare:

- **Bring outdoor cats in in time:** Bring your cat into the house in the afternoon on New Year's Eve and leave it inside until New Year's morning. There are also bangs in many places in the days before.
- **Close windows, shutters and curtains:** This dampens noise and flashes of light.
- **Prepare a quiet room:** if possible without a window facing the street, with a hiding place, toilet, water and familiar blankets.
- **Background noise:** Quiet music or a television at normal volume will mask individual bangs.
- **Stay calm:** Stay calm around yourself. You can comfort your cat when it seeks closeness, but don't pull it out of hiding.
- **Take precautions in good time:** For very anxious cats, the vet can prescribe an anti-anxiety medication in advance, which is given before the evening. Discuss this a few weeks in advance and, if possible, try it out before New Year's Eve.

During thunderstorms, the same principle applies: offer a place to retreat, muffle noise, stay calm. Many cats hide under the bed or in the closet. Leave it there until it comes out on its own.

## Getting used to noises

With patience you can reduce your fear of certain noises. To do this, play recordings of fireworks or thunderstorms at a very low volume while your cat eats, plays, or gets a treat. The volume stays low enough to show no fear. You increase it in tiny steps over weeks. The cat associates the noises with something pleasant. Don't start this training just before New Year's Eve, but rather at a quiet time of year and stop as soon as she becomes restless.

## Visitors and strangers

For some cats, every visit is a burden. Ask your guests to ignore the cat at first, not to stare at it or to not approach it. Many cats come on their own when they realize that no one wants anything from them. A room where the cat can retreat and where guests do not enter gives it security. At large parties, it is often best to put the cat in a quiet room with a toilet, water and food and close the door. Tradesmen should know that a cat lives in the household so that no door is left open.

## Conflicts with strange cats

A strange cat that regularly sits in front of the window, comes through the cat flap or marks in the garden can greatly unsettle an indoor cat. A privacy film in the lower part of the window, a cat flap with chip recognition and the thorough removal of urine marks on doors and windows will help. Sometimes it's worth having a friendly conversation with the owner of a strange cat.

## Introduce changes carefully

Many stress triggers cannot be avoided, but you can mitigate them. Introduce changes gradually if possible. The cat can first sniff a new sofa extensively before its old blanket lands on it. If renovations are carried out, one room remains unchanged in which she can find her usual places. If your daily routine changes, for example due to a new job, keep the feeding times and evening play sessions the same, even if they change slightly. If a new addition to the family is coming, start carefully introducing children's rooms, baby noises and new smells weeks in advance, as described in [Cats and Children](/katzenwissen/katze-und-kinder/). The more predictable everyday life remains, the easier it is for a cat to cope with new things. A short stress diary in which you note changes and your cat's behavior also helps you to recognize connections that are easily lost in everyday life.

## When you should get help

If, despite all the changes, your cat continues to hide, no longer eats properly, remains unclean, grooms itself bald or becomes aggressive, you should seek help. The first step is always a thorough veterinary examination to rule out pain and illness. A veterinarian with additional training in behavioral therapy can then analyze the situation and create a plan. In severe cases, behavioral changes and environmental adjustments are combined with medication to relieve anxiety.

## Frequently asked questions

### Am I increasing anxiety when I comfort my cat?

No. Calm attention when your cat seeks it does not reward fear, but rather gives it security. It is important to remain calm and not to hold the cat or pull it out of its hiding place.

### Do Bach flowers or tranquilizers from the pet store help?

There is no scientific proof of effectiveness for Bach flowers. Diffusers with synthetic cat pheromones can calm some cats, but do not replace changes in the environment. If you are very afraid, the veterinary practice is the right place to go.

### Can a cat regain trust after a bad experience?

Yes, most cats can do this, although it takes time. Fixed routines, safe places to retreat and interaction in which the cat can decide for itself when it wants contact help it. Poorly kept cats can take months to relax.

### Are some cats naturally more anxious?

Yes. Disposition and experiences in the first weeks of life shape how calm a cat is later. Kittens that have had friendly contact with people and a variety of impressions during this time are usually more self-confident.
