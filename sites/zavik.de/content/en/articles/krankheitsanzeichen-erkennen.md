“She didn’t show anything.” Veterinarians hear this sentence very often when a cat comes into the practice with an advanced disease. Cats are programmed to hide weakness. In nature, a sick cat is easy prey for predators and an easy opponent for rivals. This is why cats often only show pain and illness when there is no other option.

For cat owners, this means: You have to look closely. It's not the dramatic symptoms that are the problem, but rather the small changes that are easy to overlook. This guide shows what you should pay attention to.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Pay attention to changes from your cat's normal behavior, not isolated observations.
> - Withdrawal, less cleaning, changes in eating and toilet changes are common early signs.
> - A cat that doesn't eat for a whole day needs to be taken to the vet.
> - Get to know your cat's normal values: weight, breathing, color of mucous membranes.
> - Shortness of breath, urinary retention and poisoning are emergencies.

## Know your cat in a healthy condition

The most important step in detecting illness early is to get to know your cat well when it is healthy. How much does she normally eat? How often does she go to the toilet and how big are the lumps? Where does she like to sleep? How does she react when you come home? How much does she groom herself?

When you know these things, you notice discrepancies. A cat that always comes to the door to greet you and then suddenly stops doing so is sending a signal, even if it otherwise appears healthy.

## Behavioral changes

Changes in behavior are often one of the first signs that something is wrong:

- **Withdrawal:** The cat hides, lies in unfamiliar places, avoids company.
- **Less activity:** She no longer jumps on the scratching post or window sill, plays less, sleeps more than usual.
- **Irritability:** She hisses when touched or is sensitive to certain parts of her body.
- **Changed grooming behavior:** Less grooming leads to shaggy, greasy fur. Excessive brushing in one area may indicate pain, itching, or stress.
- **Unusual vocalizations:** frequent meowing, especially at night, or unusual silence from an otherwise talkative cat.
- **Changed posture:** Curved back, tucked head, the cat sits in a tense, “loaf-like” position with its paws folded under its feet and its eyes half closed.

## Recognizing pain: the cat face

Researchers have developed a scale that can be used to detect pain in cats based on facial expressions, called the Feline Grimace Scale. It assesses five characteristics:

| feature | relaxed cat | Cat in pain |
|---|---|---|
| ears | upright, facing forward | twisted apart, tilted outwards |
| eyes | opened | pinched, narrow |
| Snout | plump, relaxed | tense, rather oval |
| Whiskers | loose, slightly curved | straight, facing forward |
| Head posture | above the shoulder line | below the shoulder line, lowered |

If your cat shows several of these signs, especially when at rest, he may be in pain.

## Eating and drinking

- **Loss of appetite:** A cat that does not eat at all for an entire day should be examined by a veterinarian. Prolonged fasting is dangerous for cats because it can cause fatty liver disease.
- **Changed appetite:** Sudden cravings may indicate hyperthyroidism or diabetes.
- **Picky eating:** The cat goes to the bowl, sniffs and leaves, or only eats soft food. Common causes include nausea or toothache.
- **Increased drinking:** An indication of kidney disease, diabetes or hyperthyroidism.
- **Weight loss:** Often gradual and only noticeable on the scale.

## The cat toilet as a health mirror

The litter box reveals a lot about your health. Pay attention to:

- **more or larger urine clumps:** increased drinking and urination
- **Frequent, small amounts of urine, straining, blood in the urine:** Bladder infection or urinary stones
- **Squeezing without urine:** Emergency, especially for tomcats
- **Diarrhea or very hard stools:** Digestive problems, lack of fluids
- **Urinating or defecating outside the toilet:** may indicate pain, bladder problems, joint problems, or stress

## Check the body itself

You can do some simple checks at home, preferably regularly when the cat is relaxed. This is how you get to know their normal values.

**Weight:** Weigh your cat once a month, preferably with a baby scale. Write down the values. A loss of five percent is a reason for clarification.

**Breathing:** Count how many times your cat's chest rises while he sleeps comfortably. When resting, healthy cats usually breathe around 20 to 30 times per minute. A resting breathing rate that is consistently over 40 breaths per minute while sleeping is a warning sign, for example of heart disease, and should be investigated. Breathing with an open mouth is always an emergency in cats.

**Mucous membranes:** Carefully lift the lip and look at the gums. It should be pale pink and moist. Very pale, white, yellowish, bluish or brick-red mucous membranes are an alarm sign.

**Eyes:** clear, shiny, both pupils the same size. Cloudiness, discharge, a visible third eyelid, or uneven pupils should be examined.

**Ears:** clean, without dark deposits or odor.

**Coat and Skin:** Shiny fur with no bald spots, scales, crusts or knots. Check your cat regularly. New lumps or swellings should be examined.

**Temperature:** A cat's normal body temperature is approximately between 38.0 and 39.2 degrees. Measurements are taken rectally, which many cats don't like. Warm ears alone are not a reliable sign of fever.

## Keep a health diary

A simple health diary is particularly helpful for older cats or cats with a known illness. It doesn't have to be elaborate, a notebook or a note in your cell phone is enough. Record your weight once a month and note anything you notice: vomiting, diarrhea, appetite, amount of water, changes in the toilet, coughing, sneezing, limping or unusual behavior.

Such a diary has two advantages. Firstly, you notice subtle changes that you wouldn't notice in everyday life, for example that your cat has become a little lighter every month for the last three months. Second, you can give the vet accurate information instead of relying on your memory. The question “Since when has she been drinking more?” can then be answered with a date instead of “I think for a while”.

If you notice a change, a short video can also help, such as a coughing fit, an unusual gait, or strange breathing. Many symptoms do not appear in the practice because the cat is excited there or behaves completely differently than at home.

## Emergencies that cannot be postponed

If you see any of these signs, you should call a veterinary practice or animal clinic immediately:

- Shortness of breath, breathing with an open mouth, blue mucous membranes
- Straining on the toilet without urinating
- Suspected poisoning
- Accident or fall, even if the cat initially appears uninjured
- sudden paralysis of the hind legs
- Seizures, loss of consciousness
- repeated vomiting, especially with blood
- heavy bleeding

## Prevention instead of waiting

Many diseases that cats hide are discovered during regular examinations before they cause symptoms. An annual examination, twice a year for older cats, with blood tests and blood pressure measurements from a certain age onwards, is the best prevention.

## Illnesses that quietly announce themselves

Some illnesses are so common in cats that it's worth knowing their typical early signs.

| disease | Early signs | More on this |
|---|---|---|
| Chronic kidney disease | more thirst, more urine, gradual weight loss, dull fur | [Renal Insufficiency in Cats](/katzenwissen/niereninsuffizienz-bei-katzen/) |
| Hyperthyroidism | Cravings due to weight loss, restlessness, crying at night, vomiting | [Accompanying the Old Cat](/katzenwissen/die-alte-katze-begleiten/) |
| Diabetes mellitus | a lot of thirst, large clumps of urine, weight loss despite appetite, later unsteady gait | [Obesity in Cats](/katzenwissen/uebergewicht-bei-katzen/) |
| Osteoarthritis | Jumps less often and lower, stiff movements after sleeping, less cleaning | [Accompanying the Old Cat](/katzenwissen/die-alte-katze-begleiten/) |
| Dental diseases | Bad breath, one-sided chewing, food falling out of the mouth | [Cat Dental Health](/katzenwissen/zahngesundheit-bei-katzen/) |
| Heart disease | often no signs for a long time, later rapid breathing at rest, fatigue | Count the resting breathing rate, see above |

Many of these diseases can be diagnosed with a blood and urine test before significant symptoms appear and are then often easily treatable.

## Prepare well for your visit to the vet

A visit to the vet is stressful for many cats, and they often behave very differently in the practice than at home. Your observations are therefore particularly valuable. Before the appointment, write down what you noticed and since when: eating, drinking, toilet, behavior, weight. Bring videos of any unusual behavior, such as coughing, limping, or a seizure, and a list of any medications and supplements your cat is taking. If you have problems with urine, ask in advance whether you should bring a urine sample with you and how best to collect it. Also write down your own questions, because it's easy to forget something during a conversation. This way the vet can more quickly assess what's wrong with your cat.

## Frequently asked questions

### My cat purrs. Can she still be sick?

Yes. Cats purr not only when they are happy, but also when in pain, stress or fear, presumably to calm themselves. Purring alone is not a sign of health.

### How long can I wait?

That depends on the characters. Not at all if there are emergency signs. If there are slight changes, such as a little less appetite, you can observe it for one day. If it doesn't get better or you're unsure, call.

### How do I take my cat to the vet without stress?

Get her used to the transport box so that she knows the box as a normal resting place. Find out more in our guide to transport box training.

### Is health insurance for cats worth it?

That depends on your financial situation. Insurance or surgical insurance can protect against high bills in the event of accidents or serious illnesses. Compare services, waiting times, deductibles and exclusions, especially for existing medical conditions and older cats. If you don't want insurance, it's best to set aside a fixed amount every month as a reserve for veterinary costs.
