Vaccinations are among the most effective ways to protect cats from serious infectious diseases. Diseases such as cat disease, which used to kill entire litters, have become rare thanks to vaccinations. At the same time, many owners ask themselves whether their indoor cat needs to be vaccinated at all and how often a booster is really necessary.

In Germany, the Standing Vaccination Commission for Veterinary Medicine (StIKo Vet) issues recommendations that veterinarians follow. This guide explains the basics. You will determine the specific vaccination plan for your cat with your veterinary practice, as it depends on their lifestyle and health status.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Every cat, even an indoor cat, should be vaccinated against cat flu and cat disease.
> - Outdoor animals also need protection against rabies; A vaccination against leukosis is often useful for outdoor animals.
> - Kittens receive basic immunization with several vaccinations from around the eighth week of life.
> - After that, depending on the vaccine and risk, it is refreshed every year to every three years.
> - The annual examination is also useful if no vaccination is due.

## What diseases are vaccinated against?

### Cat flu

Cat cold is not a harmless cold, but an infection with various pathogens, especially the feline herpes virus and the feline calicivirus. Sneezing, discharge from the eyes and nose, fever and sometimes painful mouth ulcers are typical. Kittens and weakened cats in particular can become seriously ill. After an infection, many cats remain carriers of the herpes virus for life and experience repeated attacks under stress.

The vaccination does not prevent infection in every case, but it significantly reduces the course of the disease. Since the pathogens can also enter the home on clothing and shoes, vaccination is also important for indoor cats.

### Cat plague

Cat disease, also known as panleukopenia, is caused by a parvovirus. It causes severe vomiting, diarrhea, high fever and a breakdown in white blood cells. Unvaccinated kittens often die from it. The virus is stable in the environment for months and can easily be carried into any home via shoes and objects. The vaccination provides very reliable protection.

### Rabies

Germany has been considered free of classic rabies in land animals for years. However, bats can carry rabies viruses, and a valid rabies vaccination is required for travel abroad and return. Vaccination is recommended for outdoor animals. Indoor cats that never travel usually don't need them. In addition, a lack of a rabies vaccination can have significant regulatory consequences if suspected.

### Leukosis (FeLV)

Feline leukemia is transmitted by a virus that is mainly passed on between cats during close contact, for example through mutual grooming, shared bowls or bites. It can weaken the immune system and cause tumors. The vaccination is recommended for cats that have contact with other cats, especially outdoor cats. Before the first vaccination, the cat should be tested for the virus, because the vaccination is of no use to cats that are already infected.

### More vaccinations

There are vaccines against some other diseases, such as against chlamydia or feline infectious peritonitis (FIP). They are only used in special situations, for example in breeding or animal shelters with a known problem, and are not part of standard protection.

## Who needs which vaccination?

| Vaccination | Indoor cat | outdoors | Traveling abroad |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cat flu | yes | yes | yes |
| Cat plague | yes | yes | yes |
| Rabies | usually no | yes | prescribed |
| Leukosis | usually not necessary | often recommended after testing | depending on the destination and contact |

Cats that go to a boarding kennel should also be fully vaccinated against cat flu and cat disease. Many pensions require proof of vaccination.

## Basic immunization for kittens

Kittens receive antibodies through their mother's milk that protect them in the first weeks of life. However, these maternal antibodies can also intercept a vaccination so that it does not work. Since it is not known exactly when they disappear in a particular kitten, kittens are vaccinated several times.

A typical process based on the recommendations looks like this:

1. First vaccination at around **8 weeks**: cat flu and cat disease
2. Second vaccination at around **12 weeks**: cat flu and cat disease, possibly rabies in outdoor animals
3. Third vaccination at around **16 weeks**: cat flu and cat disease
4. Completion of the basic immunization at around **15 months**

The leukosis vaccination also begins in kittenhood and requires two vaccinations a few weeks apart. Adult cats with unknown vaccination status receive a shortened primary vaccination course with two vaccinations.

## How often is it refreshed?

After completing the basic immunization, not every vaccination has to be repeated every year. How often the booster is given depends on the vaccine and the risk:

- **Feline plague:** in most cats about every three years
- **Cat flu:** depending on the risk, annually, for example for outdoor cats, in multi-cat households or before boarding stays, or at longer intervals for indoor cats with a low risk
- **Rabies:** every one to three years depending on the vaccine, as directed by the manufacturer
- **Leukosis:** depending on the risk at intervals of one to three years

Your vet will tailor the plan to your cat. It is important that you keep the pet ID card in which every vaccination is recorded.

## The annual examination

Even if a vaccination is not due every year, your cat should be thoroughly examined once a year. Weight, teeth, heart, lungs, eyes, ears, fur and stomach are checked. Many diseases that cats hide for a long time are discovered so early. Older cats benefit from two examinations per year.

## Side effects

Most cats tolerate vaccinations well. Mild, temporary reactions are common in the first one or two days: tiredness, slightly less appetite, a slight fever or a small, tender swelling at the injection site.

Allergic reactions with vomiting, shortness of breath or swelling of the face rarely occur, usually shortly after vaccination. Then the cat has to go back to the practice immediately. That's why it makes sense to stay near the practice for a moment after the vaccination.

A very rare but well-known complication is a malignant tumor at the injection site, called fibrosarcoma. Veterinarians therefore vaccinate in specific areas, often the limbs or the side of the chest wall, and avoid unnecessary injections. If a swelling at the injection site persists for more than three months, is larger than two centimeters, or is still growing one month after vaccination, it should be examined.

The risk of serious side effects is significantly lower than the risk of contracting one of the diseases that are being vaccinated against.

## Cats from animal welfare and with unknown vaccination status

Cats from the animal shelter are usually already vaccinated, and the vaccinations are listed on the pet ID card that you receive when you take them over. Check with your vet to see when the next booster is due. The situation is different for found cats, cats from animal shelters abroad without documents or for animals whose vaccinations occurred many years ago. For them, you usually start with a new basic immunization, which for adult cats usually consists of two vaccinations a few weeks apart. Before a leukosis vaccination, the cat is first tested, because the vaccination is pointless if a cat is already infected.

## The vaccination appointment without stress

A vaccination appointment only takes a few minutes, but stress for the cat often begins as soon as it sees the transport box. Therefore, get her used to the box, as described in [Transport box training](/katzenwissen/transportbox-training/), and ask for appointments in a quiet consultation hour. Use the appointment for the annual health check and write down questions beforehand. After the vaccination, the cat is allowed to rest. Slight tiredness in the evening is normal, and the next day it's usually back to normal.

## Measuring antibodies instead of vaccinating?

In the case of cat disease, a blood test can be used to determine whether a cat still has sufficient antibodies. If the value is good, the refresh can sometimes be postponed. However, such a test is not very meaningful for cat colds because the protection there does not depend solely on the antibodies in the blood. It is best to discuss with your veterinarian whether an antibody test makes sense for your cat, for example if it has had a previous vaccination reaction.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does an indoor cat really need to be vaccinated?

Yes, at least against cat flu and cat disease. The pathogens can enter the home through clothing, shoes and objects, and in the event of an emergency, a move or a stay at the boarding house, the cat is suddenly in contact with other animals.

### Can I postpone a vaccination if my cat is sick?

Yes. Cats should only be vaccinated if they are healthy. In the event of an acute illness, the vaccination is postponed. For chronically ill cats, the veterinarian makes individual decisions.

### My cat missed her vaccination by a few months. Does everything have to start again?

As a rule not. A refresher is usually enough. If the gap is very long, another basic immunization may be necessary. Ask your practice.

### Can a pregnant cat be vaccinated?

Usually not, at least not with live vaccines because they can harm unborn kittens. It is best to have a cat fully vaccinated before a planned pregnancy. If an unvaccinated cat is pregnant, discuss with the veterinarian how to protect mother and kittens.

### Where is vaccination taking place and why at this point?

Many veterinarians now no longer vaccinate cats between the shoulder blades, but rather in places such as the limbs or the side of the abdominal wall and note the location on the ID card. The reason is the very rare fibrosarcoma at the injection site: If a lump forms there, it is easier to detect and treat at these points. Feel the vaccination site occasionally in the weeks following. A small thickening that disappears after a few weeks is usually harmless. If a lump persists for more than about three months, becomes larger than two centimeters, or continues to grow one month after vaccination, have it examined.
