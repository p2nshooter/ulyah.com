Many adults remember a cat from their childhood: one that lay on the notebook while doing homework, that was trusted with secrets, that slept at the foot of the room at night. Cats can be wonderful companions for children. They teach consideration, patience and a sense that another being has their own needs.

In order for this to be successful, both sides need rules: the children, so that they learn how to deal with a cat, and the cat, so that it has places where it can find peace. This guide shows what is important at what age.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - The cat decides when it wants contact. Children learn to let them come.
> - Every cat needs places to retreat where children are not allowed to disturb them.
> - Never leave small children and cats together unattended.
> - Responsibility for the cat always remains with the adults.
> - Pregnant women should avoid cleaning the litter box themselves if possible.

## Which cat is right for a family?

When a cat moves into a family with children, its character is more important than its appearance. Adult, calm cats that already know children and don't run away at every loud noise are well suited. Ask specifically about it at the animal shelter.

Very shy cats, cats with bad experiences and very young kittens are less suitable for families with small children. Kittens are fragile, and children cannot yet judge when a game becomes too wild. Conversely, young cats still scratch and bite uninhibitedly during play.

## The basic rules for children

Even small children can understand these rules if they are patiently explained and demonstrated to them:

1. **The cat comes to you, not you to the cat.** If the cat wants contact, it comes. If she runs away, she won't be followed.
2. **Leave cats sleeping and eating alone.** Just like humans, cats don't like to be disturbed while sleeping or eating.
3. **Gently stroke, only on the head, cheeks and back.** Belly, paws and tail are taboo for many cats.
4. **Do not pull by the tail, do not hold it, do not pick it up.** Small children cannot carry a cat properly and will drop it if it struggles.
5. **Be quiet around the cat.** Screaming, running and slamming doors scare cats.
6. **The cat's hiding places are taboo.** When the cat withdraws, it wants to be alone.
7. **Do not play with hands or feet.** There are spring fishing rods and balls for playing. Anyone who plays with their hands gets scratched.

## Learn to understand body language

Children learn quickly when you show them what the cat is saying. Explain to them a few important signs:

| characters | What the cat says |
|---|---|
| Tail straight up | “Hello, nice to see you” |
| slow blinking | “I trust you” |
| Tail whips back and forth | “I’m annoyed, stop” |
| Ears turned back or to the side | “It makes me uncomfortable” |
| Hissing, growling | “Go away, otherwise I’ll defend myself.” |
| Cat gets up and leaves | “I’ve had enough” |

When a cat scratches a child, it has almost always been warned beforehand. Children who recognize these warnings are rarely scratched.

## Retreats for the cat

In a family with children it is often loud and lively. The cat therefore needs places where it is safe from children's hands:

- **Elevated places:** a high scratching post, a shelf, the closet. The cat can watch the action from above without being in the middle of it.
- **a quiet room:** for example the parents' bedroom, where the cat can escape at any time.
- **Feeding and toilet areas that children cannot reach** or are not allowed to enter. A cat that is disturbed on the toilet will look for another place.

A door gate with a cat flap can be useful for crawling children: the cat can get through, but the child cannot.

## What children can do at what age

Children can and should take responsibility for the cat, but in an age-appropriate manner. Overall responsibility always remains with the adults.

| Dude | What children can take on |
|---|---|
| up to 3 years | watch, gently stroke with help; never alone with the cat |
| 4 to 6 years | Put food in the bowl under supervision, fill up with water, play with the feather rod |
| 7 to 10 years | Feeding according to schedule, taking over play times, helping with brushing |
| from 11 years | Clean the litter box with gloves, keep an eye on the amount of food, and take care of the cat during the holidays |

A plan on the refrigerator helps to distribute tasks clearly. But be prepared for children to forget tasks. Check daily that the cat has food, water and a clean toilet.

## Hygiene in everyday family life

Healthy, vaccinated and regularly dewormed cats do not pose a health risk to children if a few simple rules apply:

- Hands are washed after petting and before eating.
- Children only clean the litter box when they are of a certain age and with gloves.
- The cat will be treated regularly for worms or its stools will be examined as recommended by your veterinary practice. Experts often recommend more frequent checks for outdoor animals and households with small children.
- Scratches are cleaned. If a scratch becomes red, warm or swollen, or lymph nodes swell, the child should be examined by a doctor.
- Do not wash food bowls in the sink with the family dishes if you can avoid it.

## Pregnancy and baby

Many expectant parents wonder whether the cat can stay. The answer is in almost all cases: yes.

**Toxoplasmosis:** The pathogen can be transmitted through cat feces and is dangerous for unborn children if the mother becomes infected for the first time during pregnancy. However, the eggs in the feces only become infectious after one to five days. Therefore, the following applies: clean the toilet every day, and if possible have someone else clean it. If the pregnant woman has to do it herself, do it with gloves and wash her hands thoroughly. Important to know: People are more likely to become infected through raw meat and unwashed vegetables than through their cat. Your gynecologist can determine whether you are already protected with a blood test.

**Preparing for the baby:** Get the cat used to changes before the birth: to the new nursery, to baby noises that you can play quietly, to the smell of baby cream. Don't change their feeding and toileting locations right before birth.

**After birth:** Let the cat sniff the baby while you hold him. Never leave cat and baby together unattended, and close the nursery door when the baby is sleeping. The concern that cats “steal babies’ breath” is a myth. However, a cat can lie down in a warm cot and this should be prevented.

Give the cat conscious attention even after the birth. A cat that is suddenly overlooked can react with stress, for example by becoming unclean.

## If a child is allergic

A cat allergy is common and should be checked by a doctor before bringing in a cat if allergies are already known in the family. If the child spends time with cats beforehand, for example with friends, it will often be clear whether he or she will react. Having to give up a cat from the family is painful for everyone and can often be avoided by being careful before moving in.

## Preparing children for moving in

Before the cat comes, it's worth having a family discussion. Explain to the children that a new cat is afraid at first, will hide and needs time to gain trust. Decide together who will take on which task and discuss the basic rules. Children's books about cats or a visit to the animal shelter together help to transform anticipation into understanding. On move-in day: quiet instead of a welcome party. The children sit on the floor, let the cat come and reward it with a treat when it comes near. [The first days with the new cat](/katzenwissen/die-ersten-tage-mit-der-neuen-katze/) describes how the first days go.

## When friends come to visit

Strange children don't know the rules of your family. Many people immediately want to pet the cat, pick it up or run after it. Briefly explain the most important rules to the visiting children or let your own children take over and become little cat experts. Make sure the cat can retreat to a quiet room at all times and close it if there is a loud birthday party.

## Farewell and sadness

Cats often live to be fifteen years old or older, and many children experience the death of a beloved animal for the first time with their cat. Talk about it honestly and in a child-friendly way. Phrases like “She fell asleep” can unsettle small children and cause them to fear their own sleep. Better is a simple, true explanation: the cat was very sick and old, its body stopped working, and it is no longer in pain. Allow grief, including your own. Rituals help when saying goodbye, such as painting a picture, creating a photo album or decorating a place in the garden. How to accompany an old cat in its final phase of life is described in [Accompanying the Old Cat](/katzenwissen/die-alte-katze-begleiten/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can the cat sleep in the cot?

For babies and small children, no. For older children it is a matter of hygiene and personal choice. A healthy, dewormed cat is usually no problem in the bed of a school-age child.

### Our cat scratches the children. What to do?

Pay close attention to the situations in which it happens. The cat is usually harassed, held or encouraged to play with the hands. Change these situations and give the cat more places to retreat. If you scratch her for no apparent reason, have her checked by a vet, as pain makes cats irritable.

### When can a child have their own cat?

A cat is never quite a child's cat. She is part of the family and the adults are responsible. From primary school age onwards, children can develop a close relationship with a cat and take on many tasks.
