Two cats playing together, grooming each other and sleeping snuggly together: That's the image that many people have in mind when they think about getting a second cat. It can happen like that. But it doesn't come by itself. Cats are territorial animals, and a strange cat is initially an intruder for them. Whether two cats become a couple depends very much on how the first few weeks go.

The most common mistake is impatience: the new cat is placed in the living room, you hope that “they will sort it out between themselves”, and you are met with hissing, fights and sometimes months of hostility. This guide shows how to do it better.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Choose a second cat that matches your first cat in age and temperament.
> - Start with separate rooms and let the cats get to know each other through smells first.
> - Increase contact in small steps: scent, hearing, sight, short meetings.
> - Expect weeks, not days. Hissing is normal, serious fights are a sign to take a step back.

## Will a second cat fit?

Not every cat wants company. Some have lived alone for years and don't get along well with other cats, others have had bad experiences. At the same time, many indoor cats whose people are away from home during the day suffer from boredom, and a suitable partner can significantly enrich their lives.

Signs that your cat might enjoy company:

- she is young, playful and constantly demands attention
- She used to live with other cats and got along well
- she is alone for many hours a day and seems bored

Caution is advised if your cat is very old or sick, if it reacts with great excitement to cats outside the window or if it was adopted as an only cat at an animal shelter.

## Find the right partner

Cats that are similar in age, temperament and play behavior have the best chances. A young, wild tomcat who loves to play rough games will be more likely to stress than please a calm, twelve-year-old female cat. Two playful young cats, on the other hand, usually complement each other well.

Gender plays less of a role in neutered cats than character. Ask the animal shelter specifically about cats that other cats like and tell them in detail what your first cat is like. Some animal shelters offer to take back a cat after a failed reunion.

## Preparation: two areas

Before the new cat moves in, set up its own room with a toilet, food, water, hiding place, scratching area and place to sleep. The door to this room initially remains closed. The first cat keeps the rest of the apartment and its usual places.

Plan enough resources for two throughout the apartment: several feeding places, watering holes, scratching posts and at least three toilets. Cats rarely fight out of dislike, but often fight over scarce resources. When there is enough of everything, the tension decreases.

## Step 1: Getting to know each other through smells

Cats first recognize each other by their smell. Before they see each other, they should smell each other and associate the other's smell with something pleasant.

- **Swap scent:** Gently stroke one cat's cheeks with a soft cloth and place it in the other's area. Also swap out blankets and pillows.
- **Swap rooms:** Let the new cat explore the apartment for an hour while the first cat is in the guest room, and vice versa. The smells mix throughout the area.
- **Feeding at the door:** Place both cats' food bowls on each side of the closed door, initially at a distance, later closer. The cats smell and hear each other while they eat and associate each other's presence with something good.

This step usually takes a few days to a week. You can move on when both cats are relaxed eating at the door and no longer hiss when they smell the other.

## Step 2: Visual contact at a distance

Next, the cats should see each other without being able to reach each other. A good choice is a gate like the one used for babies, a fly screen in the door frame or a secured door that is only open a crack.

Feed both cats on their side of the gate, play with both cats at the same time with a feather rod or give treats. The goal is for the cats to see each other and have positive experiences. Keep these periods short and end them before a cat becomes upset.

## Step 3: short, supervised meetings

When both cats look relaxed through the gate, eat and play, the first direct meetings can take place. Open the door at a time when you can stay there and keep the cats busy with play or food. Keep the first meeting short and end it in a relaxed moment, not just when the mood changes.

Over time you extend the shared phases. Only leave the cats together unattended after they have seen each other for several days without serious arguments.

## Hissing, growling, thrashing: which is normal

A certain amount of hissing, growling and threatening is part of it. This is how cats negotiate their boundaries. Even short scuffles, after which both relax again, are usually unproblematic.

| behavior | Assessment |
|---|---|
| occasional hissing, turning away | normal, the cats set boundaries |
| Pursuit in the game, with role reversal | mostly game when both take turns hunting |
| Staring, lurking in front of the toilet or feeding place | Warning signal, resource competition |
| serious fights with screaming, tufts of fur, injuries | Abort, take a step back |
| a cat hides permanently, doesn't eat, becomes unclean | Stress, go slower and get checked by a vet |

If a serious fight breaks out, don't use your hands to intervene. Throw a blanket between the cats, make a loud noise, or push a pillow between them. Then separate the cats again and start a few steps earlier.

## Common mistakes

- **Proceed too quickly.** If you put the cats together on the first day, you risk the first impression being a struggle. Correcting this impression takes much longer than avoiding it.
- **“They sort it out between themselves.”** Cats don’t settle conflicts like dogs do through a clear hierarchy. An inferior cat can suffer for months without being noticed.
- **Penalties.** A cat that hisses will be yelled at or sprayed with water. What she learns from this is not that she likes the other cat, but that being close to them has unpleasant consequences.
- **Neglect the first cat.** If you only care about the new addition, you will make the first cat even more insecure. Maintain her rituals, play with her and give her her usual places.
- **Not enough resources.** One toilet and one bowl for two cats is a recipe for arguments.

## How long does it take?

Some cats get along after a few days. For most it takes a few weeks, for some it takes several months. What matters is not that they become best friends. Many pairs of cats live peacefully next to each other without sleeping together. That's a good outcome.

If after two to three months there is no improvement despite patient approach, a cat is constantly under stress or injuries keep occurring, seek advice from a veterinarian who specializes in behavioral medicine. Sometimes it's best for both cats if one of them finds another home.

## Health check before moving in

Before a new cat moves in with your first one, it should be examined by a veterinarian. This includes a look at vaccination status and parasites and, depending on the origin, tests for feline leukosis and feline immunodeficiency virus. Feline leukosis is transmitted through close contact such as mutual grooming and sharing bowls. A cat that tests positive should therefore not live with a healthy, unvaccinated cat. The immunodeficiency virus, on the other hand, is primarily transmitted through deep bite wounds, which is why neutered cats that live harmoniously together have a low risk of infection. Get veterinary advice in such cases.

The separate room for the new cat has a double benefit. It is not only a safe retreat for the first few days, but also a kind of quarantine. If the new cat shows signs of cat cold, diarrhea or parasites, you can have her treated before she can infect the first cat.

## Maintain peace in everyday life

Even if the merger is successful, it is worth remaining vigilant. Feed the cats in separate places so that no one crowds the other from the bowl, and keep the number of toilets, scratching posts and raised places to lie down high. Play with both cats regularly, together or separately, depending on what they like. Small tensions often announce themselves quietly, for example when a cat suddenly takes different paths or hides more often. Anyone who notices such signs early can take countermeasures before a serious conflict arises. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can I put a kitten with an older cat?

This can work, but a single kitten will often overwhelm an older cat with its play instinct. It usually works better if the kitten has a play partner of the same age or if the older cat is still very playful itself.

### Should I use pheromone plugs?

Diffusers with synthetic cat pheromones can relax the mood of some cats. However, they do not replace the step-by-step approach.

### What if the new cat wants to go to the first one straight away?

Curious, friendly cats also benefit from slow introductions. The first cat has the right not to suddenly have to share its territory. If both of you meet in a friendly manner through the gate, you can take the steps more quickly.

### Do two cats need twice as much space?

Not necessarily twice as many square meters, but more opportunities to avoid each other. Elevated places, shelves, scratching posts, multiple hiding places and paths for one cat to get around the other are more important than the size of the apartment. Even in a small apartment, two cats can live well together if the apartment is well structured.
