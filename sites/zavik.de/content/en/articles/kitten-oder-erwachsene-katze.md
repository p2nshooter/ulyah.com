When you think of a new cat, you often picture a kitten: tiny, fluffy, with clumsy paws and big eyes. It's no wonder that kittens are usually adopted quickest in animal shelters, while adult and older cats often wait for months. A kitten is not the best choice for every household.

This guide compares the phases of life honestly: What does a kitten bring with it, what does an adult cat bring, what does a senior citizen bring to the table? How much time, money and nerves does each variant cost? And what questions will help you make a decision?

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Kittens need a lot of time, activity and, ideally, a second kitten as a playmate.
> - With adult cats, you already know the character and know what you're getting into.
> - Older cats are often calm and affectionate but need more veterinary attention.
> - Kittens should not be separated from their mother before 12 weeks of age.

## Kitten: lots of energy, open character

### What awaits you

Young cats are little bundles of energy. They run around the apartment, climb up curtains, chase flakes of dust and feet under the covers and try out everything that moves or rustles. It's exciting, but also exhausting. A kitten can get up to more mischief in one night than you think possible.

At the same time, kittenhood is a formative phase. What a kitten experiences now determines how it will later deal with people, noises and new situations. However, the most important imprinting phase occurs between the second and seventh weeks of life, long before most kittens move in with their new people. A kitten that has met friendly people during this time is likely to become trusting. One that has grown up without human contact often remains shy for a long time.

### The correct submission age

Kittens should stay with their mother and siblings for at least twelve weeks. During this time they learn things from their mother and by playing with their siblings that no one can teach them: how hard you can bite, how to communicate with cats, how to use the toilet. Kittens that are given away too early are more likely to develop behavioral problems later in life, such as rough play with their claws and teeth.

Be wary of offers that sell kittens at eight weeks of age or younger, often without vaccinations and without contact with the mother. Reputable animal shelters and breeders only hand over kittens from around twelve weeks of age, vaccinated, dewormed and examined by a vet.

### Better with two

A single kitten in an apartment whose people work during the day quickly becomes bored. Many animal shelters therefore only place kittens in pairs or with an existing, playful cat. Two kittens engage with each other, learn from each other and let off steam together instead of letting it out on your hands and furniture.

### Vet and costs in the first year

A kitten needs several visits to the vet in the first year of life. For the basic immunization against cat flu and cat disease, several vaccinations are necessary at intervals of a few weeks, usually starting at around eight weeks and up to at least the sixteenth week of life, followed by a booster. In addition, there are dewormings and castration, which is usually carried out around five to six months of age. The first year for a kitten is therefore significantly more expensive than for an adult cat from the animal shelter that has already been vaccinated and neutered.

## Adult cats: character with instructions for use

A cat between about two and ten years old has found its personality. Whether she is cuddly or rather distant, whether she likes children, whether she likes living with other cats, whether she plays a lot or prefers to lie in the sun: the animal shelter or the previous owner can usually tell you all of this quite accurately.

That's a big advantage. If you have a quiet household and are looking for a cat that will lie on your lap in the evening, you can specifically choose such a cat. With a kitten it's a matter of luck.

Adult cats are also usually house trained, know scratching posts and have the wildest phase behind them. They sometimes adapt to a new home a little slower than kittens because they already have habits. But everyday life with them is often relaxed after just a few weeks.

A common misconception: Adult cats no longer form close bonds with new people. The opposite is often the case. Many cats who have lost their homes become particularly affectionate with a new family.

## Seniors: quiet companions with special needs

Cats aged around ten to twelve years are considered seniors. Many of them live for many years: domestic cats today often live to be fifteen, some over twenty. Older cats are usually calm, sleep a lot, enjoy warmth and company and don't turn their house upside down.

They wait the longest in animal shelters because many people fear that they will soon become sick or die. The risk of chronic diseases such as kidney failure, hyperthyroidism, dental problems or osteoarthritis is actually higher. Therefore, if you have a senior, you should expect regular visits to the vet, ideally twice a year, and possible costs for medication or special food.

For people who lead a quiet life themselves, such as older people or working people without much time for wild play, a senior cat can be just the right companion. Some animal shelters also place older cats at a lower nominal fee or as foster animals with assistance with veterinary costs.

## Questions that will make your decision easier

**How much time do you really have?** A kitten needs intense playtime several times a day and can't be alone for hours without getting into mischief. If you work full-time, you are often better off with two adult cats.

**Who lives in your household?** Small children and kittens aren't always a good combination: kittens are fragile, and children don't yet understand when a game gets too wild. A calm, adult cat that knows children is often a safer choice.

**How ​​long can you plan?** A kitten can live with you for twenty years. If you're planning on moving, going abroad, or making major life changes in the near future, this is an important thought.

**What do you expect?** If you want a cat that will lie next to you in the evening, a calm adult cat is a sure way to get there. If you love watching a young animal grow up and have the time and patience for it, a pair of kittens is wonderful.

**Do you already have a cat?** An older, calm cat is often overwhelmed by a wild kitten. The right second cat usually has a similar temperament and age.

## Make the apartment kitten-proof

Kittens explore everything with their paws and mouths. Before a kitten moves in, you should look at the apartment through his eyes. Loose cables are nibbled on and small objects such as hair ties, rubber bands, sewing thread and tinsel are swallowed. Stitches are particularly dangerous because they can get caught in the intestines and require emergency surgery. Close the washer, dryer, oven, and toilet lid before using them, and always look inside before closing a door because kittens will slip into every crack. Secure windows and balconies, remove poisonous plants, and store cleaning supplies and medications safely. ## Where does your cat come from?

Whether kitten or adult cat: The origin determines how healthy and social your cat is. Animal shelters and animal welfare associations provide cats of all ages that have been examined by a veterinarian, vaccinated, chipped and often already neutered, and will give you honest advice about which cat is right for you. Reputable breeders will show you the mother cat, will not give away kittens before the twelfth week and will take time to answer your questions.

Be careful with classified ads in which very young kittens are sold cheaply and immediately, without vaccinations, without papers and without you being able to see the mother. Behind such offers there is often uncontrolled breeding or illegal trade, and the animals are often sick. ## Frequently asked questions

### Will an adult cat love me as much as a kitten I am raising?

Yes. The bond between cat and human is created through trust, reliability and time together, not through age when they move in.

### Is a kitten easier to train?

Kittens learn quickly, but they also have a lot to learn. An adult cat already has most of the basics. Neither one nor the other can be truly trained in the sense of obedience, but both learn what is permitted if they are offered better alternatives.

### Should I expect high veterinary costs for a senior citizen?

With higher levels than a young cat, yes. Ask the animal shelter about known illnesses and plan a reserve. However, many older cats are healthy and only need their preventive examinations for years.

### How much does a cat from the animal shelter cost?

Animal shelters charge a nominal fee that covers part of the costs of accommodation, vaccinations, microchipping and neutering. Their amount varies depending on the animal shelter. With a protection contract, you commit yourself to taking good care of the cat and not simply passing it on if problems arise.
