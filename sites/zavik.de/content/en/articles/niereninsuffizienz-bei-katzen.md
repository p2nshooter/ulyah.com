When veterinarians talk about older cats, one term almost always comes up: chronic kidney disease, or CKD for short. It is one of the most common diseases in cats aged around ten and over, and the proportion of animals affected increases with each additional year of life. The disease develops slowly, over months and years, and the first signs are so subtle that they are easily overlooked.

The good news: Kidney disease cannot be cured, but it can be easily treated. Many cats live for years with a good quality of life after diagnosis, especially if the disease is detected early. This guide explains what you should pay attention to and what the treatment includes.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - The kidneys can lose much of their function before symptoms appear.
> - Early signs include increased drinking and urination, later weight loss, loss of appetite and vomiting.
> - Preventive blood and urine tests can detect the disease early.
> - A kidney diet with reduced phosphorus is one of the most important components of treatment.
> - High blood pressure is common and must be treated to protect the eyes, heart and brain.

## What the kidneys do

The kidneys filter waste materials from the blood and excrete them in the urine. They regulate water and salt balance, keep blood pressure in balance and produce hormones, including one that stimulates the formation of red blood cells. Cat kidneys can concentrate urine particularly strongly, a legacy of their desert-dwelling ancestors.

In chronic kidney disease, functional kidney cells are gradually lost. The causes are often not clearly determined. Age, previous inflammation, infections, high blood pressure, hereditary diseases such as cystic kidneys in certain breeds and poisoning, for example from lilies, play a role.

## Why the disease remains undetected for so long

The kidneys have large reserves. Only when a large part of its function has been lost do the classic kidney values ​​in the blood rise and only then does the cat show clear symptoms. However, a change can be noticeable long beforehand: the kidneys can no longer concentrate urine as well. The cat excretes more diluted urine and has to drink more to make up for the loss.

## How you can recognize kidney disease

**Early Signs:**

- the cat drinks more than before, you refill the water bowl more often
- the urine clumps in the toilet become larger or more numerous
- slight weight loss

**Later characters:**

- Loss of appetite, picky eating
- Weight loss and muscle loss, especially in the back and hind legs
- Vomiting, often of foam or liquid
- Bad breath, sometimes with a chemical, ammonia-like tone
- shaggy, dull fur
- Tiredness, withdrawal
- Lack of fluids, constipation
- pale mucous membranes due to anemia

If you notice any of these signs, especially in an older cat, make an appointment at the vet's office.

## How the diagnosis is made

The vet will examine your cat and analyze blood and urine. Important values are:

- **Creatinine and urea:** classic kidney values that only increase when a large part of the kidney function is lost
- **SDMA:** a newer marker that often rises earlier and helps detect the disease at an earlier stage
- **Urine specific gravity:** shows how well the kidneys concentrate urine
- **Protein in urine:** increased protein loss influences the prognosis
- **Phosphorus, potassium and red blood cells**
- **Blood pressure:** High blood pressure is a common comorbidity

An ultrasound examination of the kidneys may also be useful. Based on the values, the disease is divided into stages according to an internationally accepted system, the International Renal Interest Society (IRIS) scheme. The stage determines which treatment is recommended.

## The treatment

Treatment aims to protect remaining kidney function, slow progression and relieve symptoms. It consists of several building blocks that are combined depending on the stage.

### Kidney diet

Special kidney diets contain less phosphorus and adjusted amounts of high-quality protein, as well as often more omega-3 fatty acids and potassium. Studies have shown that such a diet can help cats with kidney disease live longer with fewer crises. It is one of the most important parts of the treatment.

Some cats don't like kidney food. Switch slowly, try different types and consistencies, and speak to the practice if your cat refuses. A cat that doesn't eat at all is in greater danger than one that eats a regular diet. If the diet is not acceptable, phosphate binders mixed into the food may be an alternative.

### liquid

Cats with kidney disease lose a lot of fluid through their urine and become dehydrated easily. Wet food, several water points and drinking fountains help. In advanced stages, veterinarians sometimes show owners how to give their cat fluid under their skin at home. This sounds difficult, but many cats find it easy to bear after a short period of getting used to it and can significantly improve their well-being.

### Blood pressure

High blood pressure is common in cats with kidney disease. It further damages the kidneys and can lead to retinal detachments with sudden blindness, heart problems and neurological deficits. Therefore, blood pressure is measured regularly and reduced with medication if necessary.

### Protein loss

If the kidneys are losing protein through urine, a medication may be prescribed to reduce this loss.

### Accompanying complaints

Nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, anemia and potassium deficiency are specifically treated. Appetite-stimulating and anti-nausea medications can significantly improve the quality of life.

## Living with a cat with kidney disease

The diagnosis is a shock for many owners. However, most cats live a long time after diagnosis, often several years in the early stages. Regular checks are crucial, usually every few months, so that the treatment can be adjusted.

There's a lot you can do at home:

- Offer food in small, fresh, warmed portions.
- Make sure there are plenty of water points and a high proportion of wet food.
- Weigh your cat regularly.
- Keep stress to a minimum because stress can trigger crises.
- Record eating behavior, amount of water consumed, vomiting and weight so that you can report accurately during checks.
- Administer medication reliably as prescribed.

If the condition worsens significantly despite treatment, the cat no longer eats, becomes very thin and no longer shows any zest for life, the vet will also talk to you about when it is time to say goodbye. This is one of the hardest decisions in life with a cat, and it's good to not have to make it alone.

## Prevention

Age-related kidney disease cannot be reliably prevented. But you can reduce the risk and detect the disease early:

- sufficient liquid via wet food and water points
- no lilies in the apartment, no painkillers for people
- Regular check-ups with blood and urine tests from around seven to ten years old
- Pay attention to increased drinking and address it early

## Acute kidney injury: an emergency

In addition to the gradual, chronic form, there is acute kidney damage, which develops within hours to days. Common triggers include poisoning, for example from lilies, antifreeze or painkillers for humans, a urethral obstruction in cats, severe infections or severe loss of fluids. The cat suddenly stops eating, vomits, is weak and often barely passes any urine. Acute kidney injury must be treated immediately, usually with infusions in the clinic. If detected early, kidney function can recover partially or completely. If you suspect poisoning, don't wait and go to the veterinary clinic immediately.

## Infusions under the skin: this is how it works at home

If the vet recommends infusions at home, she will show you exactly how to do it in practice. A few tips make the routine easier. Warm the infusion bag to body temperature beforehand, for example in a water bath, because cold liquid is unpleasant. Choose a quiet place where the cat feels comfortable, such as its favorite blanket on the sofa, and have a second person help you at the beginning. The liquid is usually given into the loose fold of skin between the shoulder blades. A soft cushion then forms there for a few hours and slowly spreads out. Reward your cat with food or attention after each infusion. Many cats get used to it so well that they lie down purring during the infusion.

## Hereditary kidney diseases

Some breeds have hereditary kidney diseases. The best known is polycystic kidney disease, in which cysts form in the kidneys over time. It occurs primarily in Persian cats and related breeds. There is a genetic test for this disease, and responsible breeders have their breeding animals tested. If you buy a pedigree cat, ask for the test results of the parents.

## Kidney and thyroid: a difficult pair

Many older cats have both hyperthyroidism and kidney disease at the same time. Hyperthyroidism increases blood flow to the kidneys and can mask the onset of kidney disease. If the thyroid is treated, the kidney values ​​sometimes increase and only then can it be seen how well the kidneys are actually working. That's why the veterinarian checks the kidney values ​​particularly closely after starting thyroid therapy and adapts both treatments to each other.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a damaged kidney recover?

Lost kidney tissue does not grow back. However, acute deterioration, for example due to dehydration, can often be treated and the values ​​can then improve again.

### Should I give kidney food as a precaution?

No. A healthy cat does not need kidney food, and reducing protein too early can lead to muscle loss in older cats. Renal diet is intended for cats diagnosed with the disease.

### Is kidney disease painful?

The disease itself usually does not cause severe pain, but nausea, loss of appetite and weakness affect well-being. These complaints can be treated.

### Can a cat with kidney disease be given treats?

Many commercial treats are high in phosphorus and do not fit a kidney diet. Instead, use individual pieces or kibble of diet food as a reward, or ask the practice for snacks that are suitable for cats with kidney disease.
