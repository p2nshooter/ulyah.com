There are many good reasons to change a cat's food: the vet recommends a diet food, the cat is too fat, the favorite type is no longer manufactured, or you want to feed more wet food. But anyone who has ever tried to give a cat a new food overnight knows the result: it sniffs, turns around and leaves. Or she eats and the next day there is diarrhea in the toilet.

Cats are creatures of habit when it comes to their food, and their digestive tract doesn't like sudden changes. With patience and the right approach, the changeover is almost always successful.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Change gradually, over about seven to ten days, or longer for sensitive cats.
> - Mix the new food into the usual food in increasing proportions.
> - Never starve a cat so it can eat the new food. Prolonged fasting can cause dangerous fatty liver disease.
> - If diet food is prescribed: Discuss any problems with the veterinary practice instead of switching back on your own.

## Why cats are skeptical about new food

Cats are imprinted with certain tastes, smells and consistencies in their first few months of life. A cat that only knew dry food as a kitten sometimes doesn't consider wet food to be food at all, and vice versa. There is also a natural caution towards the unknown: in nature, new food can be dangerous.

At the same time, a cat's intestines are adjusted to what they eat regularly. The intestinal flora, i.e. the community of bacteria in the intestine, needs a few days to adapt to new compositions of protein, fat and fiber. An abrupt change can therefore lead to loose stools, diarrhea, flatulence or vomiting.

## The transition plan

A seven to ten day plan in which the new food is gradually mixed into the old has proven to be successful:

| days | old food | new food |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 2 | three quarters | a quarter |
| 3 to 4 | half | half |
| 5 to 6 | a quarter | three quarters |
| from day 7 | nothing more | everything |

For sensitive cats, older animals or when switching between very different types of food, you can extend each stage to three, four or more days. If the poop becomes soft, take a step back and stay there for a few days longer.

Mixing wet food is easy. For dry food, mix the kibble in the bowl. If you want to switch from dry to wet food, mixing is more difficult because many cats pick out the kibble from the wet food. Then another way helps.

## From dry to wet food

This change is particularly difficult for many cats because the consistency, smell and eating habits change completely. These steps will help:

1. **Introduce solid meals.** If dry food was previously freely available all day, switch to two to four solid meals first. A cat that has some appetite is more likely to try new things.
2. **Offer wet food first.** Offer a small amount of wet food at the beginning of each meal and only then the usual dry food.
3. **Use kibble as a bridge.** Place a few kibble on top of the wet food or crumble them over the top. So the new smells familiar.
4. **Adjust the temperature.** Wet food from the refrigerator hardly smells. When warmed to body temperature, for example with a spoonful of warm water, it smells more intense and is more likely to be eaten.
5. **Test different consistencies.** Some cats like pate, others like pieces in sauce, others like mousse. Try out what your cat likes.
6. **Slowly reduce the amount of dry food.** Over weeks, not days.

This change can take several weeks for very imprinted cats. That's okay.

## What you should never do: starve yourself

With dogs you sometimes hear the advice to just wait until they are hungry enough. This is dangerous in cats. If a cat doesn't eat anything or eats too little for a long time, its body mobilizes fat reserves and the liver can be overwhelmed with the breakdown. This so-called hepatic lipidosis, fatty liver disease, is a serious disease that requires intensive veterinary treatment. Overweight cats are particularly at risk.

Therefore: If your cat refuses the new food and hardly eats at all, give him the old food again and try it more slowly. If she doesn't eat for more than a day, call the vet's office.

## Tips for picky cats

- **Small quantities, offered fresh:** Wet food that sits in the bowl for hours loses its appeal. Offer small portions and put away any leftovers after about half an hour.
- **Quiet feeding place:** A cat that is disturbed while eating or is afraid of another cat eats worse.
- **Flat plate instead of bowl:** Some cats prefer to eat from a flat plate.
- **Not a huge selection:** If you constantly offer your cat new varieties whenever it hesitates, you're teaching your cat to picky. Stick to one line and change the presentation, not the product all the time.
- **Treats sparingly:** A cat that gets lots of snacks in between isn't hungry at mealtime.

## Special features of diet food

If the vet has prescribed a diet food, for example because of kidney disease, urinary stones or a food allergy, the change is part of the treatment. Some diets taste less good to cats than their usual food, for example because they contain less phosphorus or protein.

Patience is particularly important here. Often the same diet comes in different flavors and textures, and it's worth trying several. However, with allergy diets, nothing else should be fed, not even treats, otherwise the result will be distorted. If the cat does not accept the diet, speak to the practice. It's better to find a slightly less optimal diet that the cat will eat than a perfect one that it refuses.

## When a change has to happen quickly

Sometimes there is no time for a slow change, for example if a food is recalled or the vet recommends an immediate change due to acute symptoms. Then switch immediately, but watch the cat particularly carefully. Light, soft stools in the first few days are not unusual. If you have severe diarrhea, vomiting or loss of appetite, contact the practice.

## How you can tell that the change has been successful

- your cat eats the new food willingly
- the feces are formed and of normal color and quantity
- there is no vomiting and no bloating
- the weight remains stable or develops as desired
- the fur remains shiny

Weigh your cat regularly in the weeks following a change in diet. New types of feed often have a different energy density and the feeding amount must be adjusted.

## Intolerance or just a bumpy transition?

Light, soft stools or a single vomit in the first few days of a change are usually harmless and will subside if you go slower. The situation is different if a cat has diarrhea for weeks, vomits frequently, constantly scratches itself or develops crusts and bald spots, especially on the head and neck. Then there could be a food allergy or intolerance behind it. To detect this, the vet prescribes an exclusion diet with a protein source that the cat has never eaten before or with a specially broken down protein. She is usually fed strictly for about eight weeks, with no treats and no exceptions. Only then does a targeted provocation test show which ingredient is causing problems.

## Changes in a multi-cat household

It becomes difficult if only one cat has to get a new or diet food. Then feed the cats at fixed times in separate rooms and put the bowls away after the meal. A practical help are automatic feeders with chip recognition, which only open for the cat whose microchip is registered. This way, the elderly woman with kidney disease gets her diet and the young cat doesn't eat her portion. Slowly get the cats used to the noise and the opening of the lid.

## A food diary helps

During a change, note down every day what food was offered and in what quantity and how much was eaten, as well as the consistency of the stool, vomiting and abnormalities such as itching. Weigh your cat once a week. These notes will show you whether the transition is successful and will help the vet if there are problems. Especially when you change food several times in a row, you quickly lose track of what was fed and when.

## Change if you are overweight

If you want your cat to lose weight, switching to a lower-energy food is often an important step. It's not just the food itself that counts, but above all the quantity. Have the vet calculate the daily ration, weigh it with a kitchen scale and distribute it throughout the day. Healthy weight loss occurs slowly, over months. How you do this is described in [Obesity in Cats](/katzenwissen/uebergewicht-bei-katzen/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I feed several varieties alternately?

Yes, if your cat tolerates it well. Many cats like to eat a variety of foods, and switching between varieties from the same manufacturer is usually not a problem. For sensitive cats, consistent feeding is better.

### My cat has diarrhea after the change. What to do?

Take a step back to the previous mixing ratio and stay there for a few days. If the diarrhea lasts longer than two days, contains blood or the cat appears weak, it should be examined by a veterinarian.

### How do I switch a kitten to adult food?

From the age of about one year, when growth is largely complete, in the same gradual manner. For large breeds, kitten food may be useful for a little longer.

### How long does opened food last during the changeover?

An opened can of wet food will last, covered, in the refrigerator for about one to two days. When making the change, buy small packs so that nothing spoils while your cat only gets the new food in small quantities. Opened dry food stays fresh for a few weeks when tightly closed. Always pay attention to the best-before date on the package.
