Sooner or later, almost every cat has to take medication: a dewormer, an antibiotic after a bite wound, painkillers after an operation or long-term medication for a chronic illness. And almost every cat owner knows the scene: the tablet is in its mouth, the cat apparently swallows it, and a minute later it is lying unharmed on the carpet.

Cats have a keen sense of taste, are suspicious of new things and are surprisingly adept at defending themselves. With the right technique, a little practice and patience, entry is successful in most cases. This guide shows how to do it.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Give medication exactly as the vet tells you: dose, time, duration.
> - Ask whether the medication can be given with food and whether there are other dosage forms.
> - Always add some water or food after a tablet without food so that it doesn't get stuck in the esophagus.
> - Never give medication to humans or dogs without a veterinarian’s instructions.
> - If there is a lot of resistance: let us show you the technology in practice or ask about alternatives.

## Before you start: ask the right questions

Before you give medication for the first time, clarify in the practice:

- **With or without food?** Some medications need to be given on an empty stomach, others with a meal.
- **Can the tablet be divided or crushed?** Some tablets have a protective coating or release their active ingredient with a delay and must not be crushed. Others taste very bitter when crushed.
- **Are there alternatives?** Many active ingredients are also available as a liquid, as a paste, as a tasty chewable tablet, as a spot-on or as a long-term injection. Some pharmacies can convert medications into a form more suitable for cats.
- **What to do if a dose is missed or spit out?** Don't just give double the amount, ask.

## Hide tablets in food

The easiest method if it works: the tablet is hidden in something tasty.

- **Special pill hiding places:** There are soft snacks available in stores that are shaped around a pill.
- **A dollop of pate or liver sausage for cats**, a piece of soft wet food, a little cream cheese in small quantities.

**Here's the best way:**

1. First give a small bite without the tablet. This way the cat won't become suspicious.
2. Then the bite with the tablet.
3. Then take another bite without the tablet.

Don't hide the tablet in the whole meal. If the cat only eats half, you don't know how much active ingredient it received and it could refuse the food in the future.

Many cats are very good at sorting out tablets from food. If this happens, don't keep trying for weeks, but switch to direct input.

## Enter tablets directly

This method seems difficult at first, but with practice it can often be done quickly and safely.

1. **Preparation:** Have the tablet, some water in a syringe without a needle, and a treat ready. If your cat struggles, wrap him loosely in a towel so that only his head is sticking out.
2. **Position:** Sit on the floor or place the cat on a table with its rear facing you or against your body so that it cannot move backwards.
3. **Hold the head:** Place your hand from above the cat's head, thumb and index finger behind the canines at the corners of the mouth.
4. **Tilt your head back:** Gently tilt your head up so that your nose is pointing towards the ceiling. Many cats already open their mouths slightly.
5. **Open mouth:** With the middle finger of your other hand, holding the tablet between your thumb and index finger, gently press on the front incisors of the lower jaw.
6. **Place the tablet:** Place the tablet as far back in the middle of the tongue as possible. The further back, the more likely it is to be swallowed.
7. **Close mouth:** Close the mouth and keep it closed briefly, head slightly raised. Gently stroke the throat or blow lightly on the nose to encourage swallowing.
8. **Rinse:** Use the syringe to put some water into the side of the mouth or offer a bite of food immediately afterwards.
9. **Reward:** A treat and praise end the procedure on a positive note.

**Why rinsing is important:** Tablets swallowed without liquid can get stuck in the esophagus. Some active ingredients, such as certain antibiotics, can cause inflammation and constriction. Therefore, add some water or food after each dry tablet.

There are also tablet dispensers, a type of syringe with a soft tip that is used to place the tablet far back on the tongue without having to put the fingers in the mouth. Let us show you in practice how to use them correctly.

## Liquid medications

Liquid medications are given using a dosing syringe without a needle:

1. Draw up the exact amount.
2. Keep the cat's head still, but do not tilt it far back, otherwise it may choke.
3. Insert the syringe into the side of the mouth behind the canines.
4. Dispense the liquid slowly in small portions, giving the cat time to swallow.
5. Reward.

If the cat is salivating or foaming heavily, the medication probably tastes bitter. Ask for a different dosage form or a preparation with improved taste.

## Eye drops and eye ointment

1. Wash hands.
2. Hold the cat's head from behind with one hand and tilt it slightly upwards.
3. Use your thumb to gently lift the upper eyelid.
4. Let the drop fall into the eye from above and slightly from behind without touching the eye with the bottle. If the cat doesn't see the bottle directly in front of him, he will be less startled.
5. If using ointment, place a small strand in the lower conjunctival sac.
6. Allow the eye to close briefly and gently stroke over it so that the product is distributed.

If you are taking multiple eye medications, leave a few minutes between each medication unless otherwise prescribed.

## Ear drops

1. Gently pull the auricle upwards to stretch the ear canal.
2. Drip the prescribed amount into the ear canal.
3. Gently massage the base of the ear from the outside to create a smacking sound.
4. The cat usually shakes its head afterwards. Wipe off excess product on the auricle with a soft cloth. No cotton swabs in the ear canal.

## Spot ons

Spot-ons are liquids that are dripped onto the skin, usually against parasites, sometimes other active ingredients.

1. Part the fur on the back of the neck between the shoulder blades, in a place that the cat cannot reach with its tongue.
2. Apply the liquid directly to the skin, not to the fur.
3. Do not pet the cat in this area for a while and do not let children cuddle with it until the area is dry.
4. In multi-cat households, make sure that the cats do not lick each other until the product has been absorbed.

Only use products that are specifically approved for cats. Products for dogs may contain active ingredients that are fatal to cats.

## If your cat resists strongly

- **Keep calm:** Hecticness is passed on to the cat.
- **Work in pairs:** One person holds the cat, the other gives the medication.
- **Towel Method:** A cat loosely wrapped in a towel cannot scratch and sometimes even feels safer.
- **Training outside of treatment time:** Practice holding the head and opening the mouth with treats when medication is not necessary.
- **Don't give up, just ask:** If it doesn't work despite everything, tell the vet honestly. There are almost always alternatives, such as long-term injections or other preparations. A medication that doesn't work doesn't help.

## Store medications properly

- Store out of the reach of children and animals.
- Observe cooling instructions.
- Opened eye drops and liquids often only have a limited shelf life after opening.
- Do not stop treatment on your own, even if the cat is feeling better, especially with antibiotics.
- Do not save leftovers for later illnesses or pass them on to other animals without consultation.

## Long-term therapy: if the cat needs medication every day

For chronic illnesses such as hyperthyroidism, kidney disease, osteoarthritis or diabetes, many cats receive medication every day for months or years. Then a fixed routine helps. If possible, always give the medication at the same time, combine it with something pleasant such as a small meal, a brushing session or cuddles, and keep the process as calm and short as possible. An alarm clock on your cell phone prevents you from forgetting a dose when everyday life goes differently.

Keep a simple treatment plan, on paper or in an app: date, time, dose and abnormalities such as appetite, amount to drink, vomiting or weight. These notes are very valuable during check-ups because the vet often adjusts the dose based on blood values ​​and your observations. Most owners also learn insulin injections for diabetes quickly. The needles are very fine, and many cats hardly notice the sting if it is placed calmly and with a reward. Let us show you the technique in practice and practice it there under supervision.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I crush the tablet and mix it with food?

Only if the vet allows it. Many crushed tablets taste bitter, so the cat refuses all food. Some tablets may not be crushed at all.

### My cat spit out the tablet. Should I give a new one?

Once the tablet has all come out, you can usually try again. If you are not sure how much was swallowed, ask the practice.

### Can I give my cat a painkiller from my medicine cabinet?

No. Many human painkillers, especially paracetamol, but also ibuprofen and aspirin, can be fatal to cats even in small amounts.

### What do I pay attention to after entering?

Observe your cat initially after taking a new medication. Vomiting, excessive salivation, diarrhea, noticeable tiredness or restlessness can be side effects. If you have facial swelling, shortness of breath or collapse, go to the vet's office or animal clinic immediately. If the symptoms are milder, call and discuss whether the medication should be continued.
