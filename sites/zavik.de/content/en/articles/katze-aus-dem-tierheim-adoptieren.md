Every year, tens of thousands of cats wait for a new home in German animal shelters and animal welfare associations. Some were given away because their people died, had to move or developed an allergy. Others were brought as found animals, others were born in a colony of feral cats. Anyone who adopts a cat from an animal shelter gives an animal a second chance and in most cases gets a cat that has already been vaccinated, chipped, neutered and veterinary examined.

This guide explains step by step how adoption works, what animal shelters look for, what questions you should ask and how to find out which cat is right for your life.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Plan several visits before you decide. Reputable animal shelters do not provide placement at the first meeting.
> - Talk openly about your living situation, your everyday life and your experiences. The animal shelter wants to find the right cat for you, not test you.
> - Expect to pay a protection fee and a protection contract that regulates, among other things, castration and keeping conditions.
> - Young cats and indoor cats are often only adopted in pairs.

## Why from the animal shelter?

The most important reason is obvious: the cats are already here and need a home. Each adoption also creates free space that the animal shelter needs for the next found animal.

But there are also very practical advantages. The employees have often known their protégés for weeks or months. They know whether a cat is cuddly or reserved, whether it gets along well with other cats, whether it likes children or whether it runs under the bed when there are loud noises. This experience is valuable because with a kitten from the farm or from a classified ad, you usually don't know what character will develop.

In addition, shelter cats usually receive veterinary care: they are vaccinated against cat flu and cat disease, microchipped, dewormed and, if they are old enough, neutered. Many animal shelters also test their cats for the viral diseases FIV (cat infections) and FeLV (leukosis).

## Before you go: ask yourself honest questions

A cat can live fifteen to twenty years. Before you visit an animal shelter, it's worth answering a few questions honestly:

- **How much time do you have?** Cats are not loners who are happy alone all day. Indoor cats in particular need daily activity and attention. If you work a lot, a pair of cats is often a better choice than a single cat.
- **Are you allowed to keep a cat?** Keeping cats can be regulated in the rental agreement. Clarify this with your landlord beforehand, preferably in writing.
- **Apartment or outdoor access?** Do you live on a busy street, in a city apartment on the fourth floor or in a quiet house with a garden? This depends on which cat is right for you.
- **Who else lives in the household?** Small children, a dog, other cats or people with allergies influence the choice.
- **Can you afford the costs?** In addition to food and litter, there are annual vaccinations, checkups and unexpected vet bills. Since the fee schedule for veterinarians was adjusted in 2022, many treatments have become significantly more expensive. A reserve or health insurance for the cat can make sense.

## This is how the mediation works

Every animal shelter has its own procedures, but the basic principles are similar almost everywhere.

### 1. Find out more and get in touch

Most animal shelters and animal welfare associations present their cats on their website with photos and a short description. Read the texts carefully: Phrases like “looking for a quiet home without children” or “only in the outdoors” are not empty phrases, but are based on observations. Many animal shelters ask that you call or fill out a short questionnaire before a visit.

### 2. The first conversation

On your first visit, you usually speak to an employee first. You will be asked about your apartment, your everyday life, your experiences with cats and your ideas. This can feel like a job interview, but it's well-intentioned: the more the team knows about you, the better they can suggest a cat that's really right for you. Honesty is worth it. If you're looking for a quiet cat for a small apartment, you won't be happy with a young, spirited tomcat, even if he looks particularly pretty in the photo.

### 3. Getting to know each other, several times

Then you can get to know the cats that are suitable. Many cats don't show their typical side when they first meet you. A cat that retreats to the back corner of the animal shelter can become a cuddly companion at home once it has peace and quiet. Conversely, not every cat that immediately jumps onto your lap is suitable for every family. So plan at least two or three visits. Sit quietly on the floor, let the cat come to you and don't force yourself on it.

### 4. Pre-check

Some animal shelters and many animal protection associations carry out a so-called pre-inspection before adoption. A person from the club will look at your apartment, often with a view of secured windows and balconies. This is not mistrust, but part of animal protection. You will often also get useful tips.

### 5. Protection contract and protection fee

If everyone agrees, you sign a protection contract. In this agreement, you undertake to keep the cat in a species-appropriate manner, to provide it with veterinary care if necessary and not to give it away without consultation. The contract often contains regulations for castration, securing windows and balconies or keeping cats as outdoor cats or indoor cats. Some clubs reserve the right to make a follow-up visit after a few weeks.

You pay a nominal fee for the placement. For cats, depending on the animal shelter, region and age of the animal, it is often in the range of around 80 to 150 euros and only covers part of the costs for vaccination, chipping, castration and accommodation.

### 6. Pickup

On the day of collection you bring a sturdy transport box with you. Ask about the food the cat has been fed and the litter it is used to. You should maintain both in the first few weeks. You will also receive a pet ID card with vaccinations and, if available, results from veterinary examinations.

## Which cat is right for you?

The most important question is not what color fur or what eyes you like, but what character suits your life. The following overview can help with orientation:

| Your situation | Which often works well |
|---|---|
| Small apartment, job | Two adult, calm cats who already know each other |
| Family with small children | Adult, calm cat who knows children; not very shy animals |
| House with garden in a quiet location | Cat that is used to being outdoors |
| First cat, little experience | Adult, uncomplicated cat; Ask an animal shelter for a recommendation |
| Lots of time, quiet household | Senior cat or cat with special needs |

### Kitten or adult cat?

Kittens are cute and find homes quickly. But they require a lot of time, patience and activity, and their future character is still unclear. Adult cats have already found their personality. So you know what you're getting into. Seniors aged around ten and over often wait the longest for a home, even though many of them are quiet, affectionate companions.

### Individually or in pairs?

Many animal shelters only place young cats and indoor cats in pairs. Cats are social animals, even if they don't hunt in packs. Two cats who get along well play with each other, groom each other, and become less bored when their humans are at work. But there are also cats who don't like other cats and are happier as an only cat. The animal shelter team usually knows this well too.

## Questions to ask at the animal shelter

Use the conversations to learn as much as possible about the cat:

- Where does the cat come from and how long has it been in the animal shelter?
- How does she behave around people, other cats, dogs and children?
- Was she previously an indoor cat or an outdoor cat?
- What vaccinations has she had, is she neutered and chipped?
- Has she been tested for FIV and FeLV?
- Does she have any known illnesses, allergies or food intolerances?
- What food and litter does she get?
- Are there any peculiarities, such as fear of certain noises or unusual behavior?

## After moving in

The first days in a new home are exciting for a shelter cat. Give her a quiet room with a toilet, food, water and a place to hide, and let her explore at her own pace. Many cats need a few days, some a few weeks, until they really feel safe. We explain in detail how to help you get used to the cat in our guide to the first days with your new cat.

Register your cat's microchip with a pet registry such as TASSO or FINDEFIX if the shelter has not already done so, or change the contact information there to your name. Your cat can only be assigned if it ever escapes.

And if, despite all your efforts, it doesn't fit? Reputable animal shelters will take a cat back if the placement doesn't work. Talk to the team early, before the situation becomes stressful for you and the cat.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I take a cat with me on the first visit?

Not in most animal shelters, and that's a good thing. Several meetings will help you and the team make the decision calmly.

### Are cats also given to working people?

Yes. Many animal shelters provide homes to working people, but they often prefer two cats rather than a single indoor cat. It is important that you realistically describe how long the cat will be alone.

### What if I live in rent?

Then the animal shelter will usually ask for confirmation that your landlord agrees to keeping cats. It's best to clarify this before you fall in love with a cat.

### Can I also adopt a cat from abroad?

Foreign animal protection organizations also bring cats to Germany. Make sure that the association works reputably, that the cat has been examined by a vet before leaving the country, that it has been vaccinated and chipped, and that you have a contact person in Germany beforehand. Adopting from an animal shelter near you has the advantage that you can get to know the cat in person beforehand.
