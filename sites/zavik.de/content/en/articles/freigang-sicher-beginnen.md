For many cats, being outdoors is the greatest joy: sun on their fur, mice in the grass, trees to climb, an area full of smells and stories. Outdoor animals move more, are less likely to be bored and can express their natural behavior. At the same time, the world outside is not without danger: traffic, territorial wars, diseases, parasites, poisons and sometimes people who are not kind to cats.

Whether outdoor access is an option for your cat depends on where you live, the age and health of the cat and its personality. If you decide to do this, good preparation is crucial. This guide shows you how to design it step by step.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Before the first release: castration, vaccinations including rabies, microchip and registration with a pet registry.
> - A newly moved cat stays indoors for at least four to six weeks so that it knows home as a safe place.
> - The first trips are short, during daylight and just before a meal.
> - Outdoor animals should be indoors at night if possible.
> - A cat flap with chip recognition keeps strange cats out.

## Is release the right choice?

Outdoor access is good for cats who live in quiet residential areas with little traffic, gardens and green spaces. The risk is significantly higher on busy streets, in densely built-up city centers or on high floors without direct access to the outside. For such situations, a secured balcony or an enclosure is often a better solution.

The cat itself also plays a role. Very anxious cats, cats with limited vision or hearing, chronically ill and very old animals are at greater risk outdoors. Cats that have tested positive for FIV or FeLV should generally not be allowed outside to avoid infecting other cats.

## The requirements

### Castration

Outdoor dogs must be neutered. Unneutered cats contribute to the proliferation of homeless cats; unneutered tomcats roam widely, fight and injure themselves. Many cities and municipalities explicitly require the castration of outdoor animals.

### Vaccinations

In addition to basic protection against cat flu and cat disease, outdoor cats should be vaccinated against rabies. Vaccination against leukosis is often recommended for cats in contact with other cats, after prior testing. Discuss the vaccination schedule with your veterinary practice.

### Microchip and registration

A microchip placed under the skin makes it possible to match a found cat with its human. However, this only works if the chip is registered with a pet registry, such as TASSO or FINDEFIX. After inserting it, check whether the registration is completed and keep your contact details up to date, even after moving.

### Parasite protection

Outdoor animals are more likely to have worms, fleas and ticks. Schedule regular deworming or fecal examinations and appropriate protection against fleas and ticks with your veterinary practice.

### The right age

Kittens should not go outside until around six months of age at the earliest, when they are neutered, fully vaccinated and physically adept enough. Very young cats underestimate dangers such as cars and dogs.

## First arrive, then leave

A cat that has just moved into your home must first get to know its new home as its territory and safe haven. If you let her out too early, she may try to return to her old territory or not find her way home.

The rule of thumb is to stay indoors for at least four to six weeks, or longer if cats are insecure. How you can tell if your cat is ready: He moves around the entire apartment in a relaxed manner, sleeps in open places, comes when you call him and eats reliably at fixed times.

## The first excursion

Prepare your first trips well:

1. **Choose the right time:** in daylight, in calm weather, at a time when there is little traffic and no fireworks or construction work are disturbing, for example on a quiet weekend morning.
2. **Just before eating:** Let your cat go out a little hungry. Then she will be motivated to come back when you call her to eat.
3. **Practice calling:** Train your cat in the apartment beforehand to respond to a certain sound, such as the rustling of a food bag or a whistle, and reward him for it.
4. **Accompaniment:** Go outside with you the first time, sit in the garden or on the stairs and let the cat explore at its own pace. Don't carry it far away, let it go itself.
5. **Keep it short:** The first few trips only last ten to twenty minutes. Then call the cat in and reward it with food.
6. **Increase slowly:** Increase the time over days and weeks. Many cats initially explore their territory cautiously and often return.

Some owners get their cat used to a harness before their first free trip and go out into the garden together a few times. This can help, but is not suitable for every cat. Never leave a cat unattended on a leash as it can become entangled.

## The cat flap

A cat flap gives your cat freedom to come and go when they want. There are different models:

| Art | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| simple flap | cheap, easy | Strange cats and other animals can come in |
| Flap with magnet on the collar | only cats with matching magnets come in | Collar required, magnet can get lost |
| Flap with chip recognition | recognizes your cat's microchip, no collar required | more expensive, requires batteries |

The safest option is a flap with chip recognition. Many models can be adjusted so that the cat can only go in but not out, for example at night. Calmly get your cat used to the flap: first with the flap open, then with food on the other side, then with the flap slightly raised.

## Collar: yes or no?

Collars can snag on branches or fences, causing a cat to strangle itself or put a leg through it and injure itself. If you use a collar, only use one with a safety lock that opens when pulled. A registered microchip is more reliable for identification.

GPS trackers on collars can help get to know the cat's territory or find a missing cat. Look for a lightweight model and a collar with a safety clasp.

## Safety in everyday life

- **Indoors at night:** The risk of traffic and turf wars is higher in the dark. Many owners therefore bring their outdoor dogs in in the evening, for example with a solid evening meal.
- **New Year's Eve and loud festivals:** On days with fireworks, the cat should stay indoors.
- **Garden poisons:** Slug pellets, rat poison, weed killers and antifreeze are dangerous to cats. Talk to your neighbors if you notice such dangers.
- **Garages and Garden Sheds:** Cats are sometimes accidentally locked up. If your cat goes missing, ask neighbors to check sheds and basements.
- **Check regularly:** Check your outdoor animal regularly for ticks, wounds and abscesses, especially after fights.

## When the cat doesn't come home

Many cats occasionally stay away longer than usual. But if your cat stays away significantly longer than usual:

- First search in the surrounding area, in cellars, garages and garden sheds, and call them with their usual signal, especially in the evening when it is quiet.
- Report her missing to the pet registry where she is registered.
- Find out about animal shelters, veterinary practices and veterinary clinics in the area.
- Put up wanted posters with photos and ask neighbors.
- Put something with your scent on it, such as a worn item of clothing, and her toilet in front of the door.

## Territory and neighborhood

Outdoor birds have a territory that, depending on the environment, can only include a few gardens or a large area. In residential areas, many cats' territories overlap and encounters, threatening behavior and occasional fights occur. Bite wounds in cats easily become infected and form abscesses. A warm, swollen area, limping or fever after a fight should be taken to the vet's office.

Not all neighbors are happy about cats visiting, especially when freshly planted beds are used as toilets. Speak openly with your neighbors and offer your cat an attractive spot in your own garden with loose sand or fine soil where it can relax. This defuses many conflicts before they arise.

## Outdoor access and wildlife

Cats are hunters, and well-fed outdoor cats also catch mice and sometimes birds. However, you can reduce hunting success. During the breeding season from around May to July, keep your cat indoors in the early morning hours and at dusk, when young birds are particularly at risk. A British study has also shown that daily play with toys that imitate prey and a meat-rich diet can significantly reduce the number of prey animals brought home. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can I subsequently allow an indoor cat to go outside?

Yes, if the conditions are right. Adult cats that have never been outside are often cautious and need more time. Be particularly attentive to your first excursions.

### Is a bell on a collar useful to warn birds?

For the cat, a bell means constant noise directly in the ear. Whether it really protects birds is controversial. It is more effective to keep the cat indoors and play with it a lot in the morning and evening during the breeding season, when birds are particularly active.

### Can I let my cat out immediately after moving?

No. Even after moving, your cat needs at least four to six weeks to get to know the new apartment as a home before being allowed out.

### What do I do if my cat brings home prey?

Don't scold him, as this is completely natural behavior for the cat. Take away the loot calmly and with gloves. If the animal is still alive, take the cat to another room and release the animal outside. An injured bird can be cared for at a wild bird station or at a veterinarian.
