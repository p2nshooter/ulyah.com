Cats are living longer today than ever before. Fifteen, eighteen, sometimes over twenty years are no longer uncommon. They owe this to better veterinary care, safer housing and higher quality food. But over the years, a cat's body changes, and with it the nutritional requirements.

A common misconception is that older cats generally need less food and less protein. That's only partially true. This guide explains what really changes as you get older and how you can feed your senior citizen well.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - From around ten years old, cats are considered seniors, from around fifteen they are considered very old.
> - While middle-aged cats are more likely to be overweight, many very old cats lose weight.
> - Healthy seniors need enough easily digestible protein. Protein is only specifically adjusted for certain diseases.
> - Plenty of liquid via wet food protects the kidneys and urinary tract.
> - Regular weighing and two check-ups a year are the best prevention.

## What changes with age

### The energy requirement

Between the ages of seven and eleven, many cats become calmer, move less and tend to be overweight. In this phase, it may make sense to slightly reduce the amount of food or choose a lower-energy food.

For cats aged around twelve and over, the trend is often reversed. Digestion becomes less efficient, and fat and protein in particular are less absorbed. At the same time, the sense of smell and taste decrease and the food becomes less interesting. Many very old cats therefore lose weight and muscle mass. Energy-rich, easily digestible food is important for them.

### Muscles and protein

As cats age, they lose muscle mass, similar to humans. Sufficient high-quality protein helps to slow down this breakdown. A healthy older cat should therefore not be fed a blanket diet that is reduced in protein. Low-protein foods are intended for cats with certain illnesses, especially advanced kidney disease, and should only be fed as recommended by a veterinarian.

### Kidneys

Chronic kidney disease is one of the most common diseases affecting older cats. It develops gradually, and early signs such as increased drinking are often overlooked. If kidney disease is diagnosed, a kidney diet with reduced phosphorus content is one of the most important components of treatment. However, without a diagnosis, you should not switch to kidney food as a precaution.

### teeth

Many older cats have dental problems: tartar, gum inflammation or painful tooth resorption. A cat with a toothache often eats less, eats only wet food, chews on one side, or drops food from its mouth. Soft food can help, but does not replace dental treatment.

### senses and joints

A diminished sense of smell makes the food less attractive. Osteoarthritis can make it difficult to bend down to the bowl or jump onto the elevated feeding area where the cat has eaten for years.

## This is how you feed a senior citizen properly

### Lots of fluids

Older cats are more susceptible to dehydration. A high proportion of wet food is therefore particularly useful. Distribute several water bowls around the apartment, including in her favorite places, and make it easier for her to access.

### Small, frequent meals

Many older cats prefer to eat several small portions throughout the day rather than two large ones. This also helps with less efficient digestion. Freshly offered food smells more intense.

### Warm the food slightly

Wet food that has been warmed to body temperature smells stronger and stimulates the appetite. A spoonful of warm water is enough. Do not heat food in the microwave without stirring thoroughly to avoid hot spots.

### Comfortable feeding places

Raise the bowl slightly, for example with a sturdy stand, so that the cat does not have to bend down far. Make sure food and water are accessible without jumping or climbing. A non-slip surface provides security.

### Choose a suitable food

Food for older cats, often referred to as “senior” or “7+”, does not have a uniform composition. Some are lower in energy to prevent obesity, others are higher in energy and are particularly easy to digest. Which food is suitable depends on whether your cat is likely to gain or lose weight. Discuss this at the check-up.

### Calm while eating

In multi-cat households, older cats are sometimes pushed out of the bowl by younger ones. Make sure that the senior can eat in peace, if necessary in her own room.

## Take weight loss seriously

Weight loss in an older cat is not a normal sign of aging, but is almost always an indication of a cause that should be looked for. Possible reasons are:

| possible cause | typical other characters |
|---|---|
| Hyperthyroidism | large appetite, restlessness, increased drinking, shaggy fur |
| chronic kidney disease | increased drinking and urination, loss of appetite, vomiting |
| Diabetes mellitus | very thirsty, lots of urine, appetite often good |
| Dental diseases | Bad breath, drooling, careful chewing |
| intestinal diseases | Diarrhea, vomiting, changing appetite |
| Tumor diseases | different, often gradual progression |
| Pain, for example due to osteoarthritis | less movement, stiff gait, withdrawal |

Weigh your older cat once a month and record the weight. A loss of five percent or more, or two hundred grams for a cat weighing four kilograms, is a reason for a visit to the vet, even if it otherwise seems lively.

## Prevention: twice a year

Many veterinarians recommend two examinations a year for cats aged around ten and over. Weight, teeth, heart, joints and thyroid are assessed and, depending on the findings, blood and urine are examined and blood pressure is measured. Diseases such as kidney disease or hyperthyroidism can often be detected before they cause significant symptoms. Many of these diseases are easily treatable, and proper nutrition is often an important part of this.

## Nutritional supplements

Numerous nutritional supplements are available for older cats, for example for joints, kidneys or the brain. Some, such as certain omega-3 fatty acids, may be useful in appropriate situations. But only give supplements after consulting the vet. A complete, complete feed covers the basic requirements, and some additives can even be harmful for certain illnesses.

## When the senior woman eats poorly

Older cats often eat more pickily and irregularly than before. A few tricks will help whet your appetite:

- **Increase scent:** Warmed wet food, a little juice from the can or a few drops of unsalted chicken broth without onions make the food more interesting.
- **Plate instead of bowl:** Many old cats prefer to eat from a flat plate because they don't have to lower their heads low and don't bump their whiskers.
- **Hand-held food:** Some seniors eat better when their human offers them the first bites on their finger. The affection alone often stimulates the appetite.
- **Quiet atmosphere:** A feeding area with no through traffic, away from the toilet and loud appliances, is particularly important for older cats.
- **Small portions more often:** It's better to have five small meals than two large ones that are left half in the bowl.

If your older cat doesn't eat for an entire day, eats significantly less than usual for several days, or vomits, don't wait. Nausea, toothache and kidney problems can be treated, and the vet can also use appetite-stimulating and anti-nausea medications if necessary.

## Very old cats from around fifteen years of age

Very old cats often need more energy than you think because they utilize nutrients more poorly. Many become thin even though they appear to be eating normally. Easily digestible, energy-rich food with high-quality protein, divided into many small meals, is suitable for them. If the appetite decreases despite all the tricks, the vet can prescribe appetite-stimulating medication after an examination, some of which is also available as an ointment for the inside of the ear. It is important to look for the cause of the loss of appetite, as nausea, toothache or kidney disease can often be treated specifically.

## Nutrition for kidney disease

For chronic kidney disease, food is an important part of treatment. Kidney diets contain less phosphorus and an adjusted amount of protein and can slow the progression of the disease. Many cats don't like the taste at first. So switch slowly over several weeks and try out different types and consistencies. If your cat doesn't eat the diet at all, talk to the vet about alternatives, such as phosphate binders that are mixed into the usual food. A cat that doesn't eat at all is in greater danger than one that gets its regular food. More information can be found in [Renal Insufficiency in Cats](/katzenwissen/niereninsuffizienz-bei-katzen/).

## Change in diet in old age

Older cats are often particularly conservative when it comes to food. A change should therefore be carried out even more carefully than with younger animals, over two to three weeks, with a slowly increasing proportion of the new food. Do not change several things at the same time, i.e. food, bowl and feeding place at once. Monitor weight, stool and appetite closely during the transition. How to proceed step by step is described in [Switching food correctly](/katzenwissen/futter-richtig-umstellen/).

## Frequently asked questions

### When should I switch to senior food?

There is no fixed age. What matters is your cat's condition. A slim, active twelve-year-old can keep her current food. A ten-year-old who is gaining weight may need a lower-energy food, while a sixteen-year-old who is losing weight may need a higher-energy food.

### My old cat only eats treats. What to do?

Have it examined first, because toothache, nausea or an illness are often behind it. Until then, it's better that she eats at all than not at all. Try warmed, strongly scented wet food and small, frequent portions.

### Can I give my senior citizen milk?

Many older cats cannot tolerate milk just as much as younger ones. Lactose-reduced cat milk is occasionally OK, but is not a substitute for water or food.

### How do I know if my senior is losing muscle?

Run your hand over your back, shoulder blades and hips. If the spine and pelvic bones are more prominent than before, even though the weight has hardly decreased, your cat is losing muscles. Then talk to the vet about protein supply and possible causes.

### Does an old cat need extra vitamins?

If your cat is fed a complete diet, this is usually not necessary. In certain illnesses, such as chronic intestinal diseases, a deficiency in individual vitamins can arise, which the veterinarian detects using blood values ​​and corrects in a targeted manner. Do not give vitamin supplements on your own, as some vitamins can be harmful in too high a dose.
