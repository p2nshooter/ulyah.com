It's a happy day for you: the cat you've been looking forward to for weeks is finally moving in. For the cat it is one of the most exciting days of its life. She is put in a box, carried into a car, rocked and left in a place that smells strange, sounds strange and where she doesn't know anyone. Cats are territorial animals. For them, a new territory initially means uncertainty.

How long it takes for a cat to feel at home varies greatly. Some purr after an hour on the sofa, others stay under the bed for a week. Both are normal. This guide shows how you can help the cat settle in without overtaxing the cat.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Start with a quiet starting room that has everything the cat needs.
> - Let the cat come. Don't take her out of hiding.
> - Initially keep the food, litter and daily routine from the previous home.
> - If the cat doesn't eat anything for more than a day, call the vet's office.
> - Outdoor animals stay indoors for at least four to six weeks before they are allowed out for the first time.

## Preparation: the starting room

Instead of letting the cat into the entire apartment, set up one room as a starting room. A quiet room where people don't constantly come and go, such as a bedroom or study, is well suited. Everything should be ready in this room:

- a litter box with the usual litter, as far away as possible from the food
- Food and water bowl
- at least one hiding place, for example a box with an opening or a cave
- an elevated spot from which the cat can see the room
- a scratching opportunity
- a blanket or pillow from your previous home, if you have one

The advantage of a starter room: The cat can conquer a small, manageable territory before it gets to know the rest of the apartment. She will find her toilet safely and you will be able to better observe whether she is eating, drinking and doing her business.

## The arrival day

Place the transport box on the floor in the starting room, open the door and sit a short distance away. Don't take the cat out. Some cats leave the box immediately and explore the room curiously, others stay in it for hours. Both are fine. The box smells of the journey, but also of herself, and is perhaps the safest place at the moment.

Keep calm for the first time. Visiting friends, loud music and curious children eager to pet the cat should wait. Explain to children beforehand that the cat needs to rest first and that it will come on its own when it is ready.

If the cat is hiding, leave it alone. Hiding is not a sign that something is going wrong, but rather a healthy strategy for dealing with an unfamiliar situation. A cat that feels safe comes out voluntarily.

## Building trust: closeness without pressure

Cats like it when they decide for themselves when and how much contact they have. The most important thing in the first few days is therefore to create opportunities for contact without forcing it.

**Show presence:** Spend time in the starting room without forcing yourself on the cat. Read a book, work on your laptop, or just sit on the floor. Talk to her quietly every now and then. This way she gets used to your voice and your smell.

**Get at eye level:** A person leaning over a cat appears threatening. Better sit or lie on the floor.

**Blink Slowly:** Among cats, slowly closing and opening the eyes is a sign of trust and relaxation. When you look at the cat, blink slowly and then look away slightly. Many cats return this blink after a while.

**Offer the hand:** When the cat approaches, extend a finger or a loosely curved hand and let it sniff. If she rubs her cheek on it, you can gently stroke her head and cheeks. If she withdraws, accept that.

**Food as a bridge:** Feed at regular times and stay close while the cat is eating, but not too close. So she associates your presence with something pleasant. A treat placed on the floor for her can be a friendly greeting.

**Play:** A feather rod is ideal for luring a shy cat out of reserve. You can keep your distance and still experience something together.

## Eating, drinking, toileting: which is normal

Many cats eat little or nothing on the first day. This is usually nothing to worry about. But keep an eye on whether she starts eating again within about 24 hours. A cat that does not eat at all for a long time can become seriously ill, especially if it is overweight: its body begins to break down fat reserves and the liver can become overloaded. If your cat doesn't eat for more than a day, call the vet's office.

You should also keep an eye on the toilet. It can happen that a cat hardly passes any urine on the first day. But if she keeps going to the toilet, pushing and nothing comes out, that's an emergency, especially with tomcats.

Diarrhea in the first few days is common because stress affects the intestines. If it doesn't get better after two or three days, contains blood or the cat appears dull, a veterinarian should take a look at it.

## Conquer the apartment

When the cat feels safe in the starting room and is sleeping, eating, and greeting you, you can open the door and let it explore the rest of the apartment. For some cats this happens after a day, for others after one to two weeks.

Leave the door to the starting room open so the cat can retreat there at any time. It's best to leave the first toilet there for now. Place a second toilet in a quiet place in the home.

Make sure that all sources of danger are secured beforehand: tilted windows, open balcony doors, poisonous plants, cables and small parts. New cats explore particularly thoroughly and like to climb on cupboards and window sills.

## Signs that the cat is arriving

Adjustment is rarely linear. There are good days and setbacks. These signs show you that your cat is trusting:

| behavior | What it means |
|---|---|
| She sleeps in plain view, perhaps even on her back | She feels safe enough to adopt vulnerable positions |
| She rubs her head on furniture or on you | She marks her territory with scents and absorbs you into it |
| She comes towards you with her tail raised | a friendly greeting |
| She blinks at you slowly | Trust and relaxation |
| She eats in your presence | she doesn't see you as a threat |
| She plays and explores curiously | she is relaxed enough to take action |

## If it takes longer

Cats from difficult backgrounds, formerly feral cats or very sensitive animals in particular sometimes need weeks or months. Don't give up if your cat is still shy after a week. Stick to your rituals, be reliable and predictable and don't put pressure on yourself. Many of these cats will later form particularly close bonds precisely because they were given time.

**Atomizers with synthetic cat pheromones** that are plugged into the socket can also be helpful. They mimic scents that cats leave behind when marking with their cheeks, and can make some cats feel like they are in familiar surroundings. They don't work for every cat and aren't a substitute for a quiet environment, but they're worth a try.

If your cat doesn't eat after weeks, just hides, reacts aggressively or is unclean, talk to your veterinarian. Sometimes there is pain or an illness behind it. Advice from a veterinary behavior expert can also help.

## The first vet visit

Even if your cat appears healthy and has been vaccinated and examined from the animal shelter, it makes sense to make an introductory appointment at your veterinary practice in the first few weeks. This way the cat is on file, the practice can record its weight and condition, and in an emergency you don't have to explain everything. But wait until the cat has settled in a bit, unless there is an acute reason.

## Outdoors: first arrive, then leave

If you want your cat to go outside later, he or she is not allowed to go outside immediately. She first needs to know her new home as a safe place to return to. A general rule of thumb is at least four to six weeks, or longer for unsteady cats. Before the first exit, the cat should be neutered, vaccinated, chipped and registered with the pet registry.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I leave the bedroom door open at night?

You can decide however you prefer, as long as you remain consistent. If you don't want the cat in bed at night, you shouldn't let it in for the first week or it will get used to it.

### My cat meows loudly at night. What to do?

Many cats call the first few nights because they don't know their surroundings. Make sure she has played and eaten in the evening and react as calmly as possible. This usually goes away after a few days. If it lasts longer, especially in older cats, have them checked by a veterinarian.

### When can the cat meet other pets?

Only when she feels safe in the starting room. Reuniting with another cat or dog should begin gradually and through smells. You can find out more about this in our guides to second cats and bringing cats and dogs together.
