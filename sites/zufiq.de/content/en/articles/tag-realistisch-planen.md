At eight in the morning, the list still looks doable: finish writing the report, go to the dentist, send the package back, get a present for a birthday at the weekend, call the insurance company back, hang up the laundry, put together documents for the tax advisor, cook for guests in the evening. At twelve the report is half finished, the waiting room was full, and the insurance company put you on hold twice. In the evening, more than half are still on the list, and the feeling of having failed comes with it.

The problem is rarely a lack of hard work, but rather a calculation that doesn't work out. A to-do list knows no time. It shows what needs to be done, but not whether it fits into the day. This guide explains how to create a daily plan that takes into account your real time, includes buffers and still works even if something comes up.

## Why we underestimate the time required

Almost all people underestimate their tasks, even if they actually know better. In psychology, this pattern even has a name: the planning fallacy. There are several reasons behind this:

- **We're thinking of the ideal case.** In our heads, everything runs smoothly: no traffic jams, no questions, no printer going on strike.
- **Setup times are missing.** Getting there, finding a parking space, looking for documents, starting programs, thinking your way back in: none of this is on the list, but it takes time.
- **Tasks are bigger than their name.** “Getting a gift” means finding an idea, driving to the store, looking, paying, wrapping, writing a card.
- **Interruptions are forgotten.** Calls, messages, colleagues with a short question and children with a long one.

### Estimate durations realistically

The best basis for good estimates is your own experience. Here's how to collect them:

1. **Write down for a week.** For the most important tasks, write down beforehand how long you think they will take and then how long they actually took.
2. **Look at the difference.** There is often a recurring pattern. If your tasks usually take about one and a half times as long as estimated, that's your personal factor. Take it into account in the future.
3. **Break down the unknown.** For tasks you've never done before, write down the steps and estimate each one individually. This is more accurate than a gut total.
4. **Treat recurring things as a fixed quantity.** What you do regularly, such as the weekly shop or dinner and cleaning up afterwards, you measure once and then record it with the same duration each time.

An excerpt from such a week could look like this:

| task | appreciated | actually | what came along |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly shopping | 45 minutes | 75 minutes | Arrival, long queue, clearing up at home |
| Dentist appointment | 30 minutes | 90 minutes | There and back, waiting room |
| Write an offer | 2 hours | 3 hours | Ask for prices, two questions |
| Send the package back | 10 minutes | 35 minutes | Find the box, print the label, go to the packing station |
| Birthday card | 10 minutes | 25 minutes | Looking for a card, stamp was missing |

The numbers are just an example. It is important that you collect your own, because they will tell you more than any general rule.

## Make fixed appointments and real free time visible

Before you assign tasks, see how much time is left. First, enter everything that is fixed: working hours, appointments with routes before and after, meals, child care, basic household needs and the time at which you would like to go to sleep.

An example: Lena works in the office from 8:30 a.m. to 4:30 p.m., with a half-hour commute each way. She has a physiotherapy appointment at 5:30 p.m., cooking and eating at 7 p.m., and she wants to relax from 9:30 p.m. On paper, her evening is long. In fact, there is barely more than an hour and a half left between coming home, appointment, meal and rest time, divided into two parts. The insurance callback and the package fit into these gaps, but not the tax documents.

This leads to the most important rule for a realistic day: **Don't plan all your free time.** For example, plan to only fill about two-thirds of it with tasks and see for a week whether that works. You need the rest for buffers, breaks and the unexpected.

## Two methods for daily planning

### The 1-3-5 method

With this method, you set yourself one large, three medium and five small tasks for one day. The big one is your main thing, like writing the offer. Medium tasks may take half an hour to an hour, such as a doctor's appointment or gathering documents. Small ones can be done in just a few minutes: a transfer, a quick call, a message.

For the day from the introduction it could look like this:

- **A big task:** finishing the report.
- **Medium three:** Dentist appointment including directions, compiling documents for the tax advisor, preparing food for the guests.
- **Five little ones:** Call back the insurance company, send the package back, hang up the laundry, send a message to the sister about the gift, write down drinks for the evening.

The gift itself is canceled that day and gets a place the next day.

The advantage lies in the fixed upper limit. If a new task is to be added, another has to give way. On days with a lot of appointments, simply reduce the schedule, for example to one large task, two medium tasks and three small tasks.

### Time blocks

With time blocks, each task has a fixed place in the calendar, like an appointment with yourself: 9 a.m. to 11 a.m. offer, 11 a.m. to 11:30 a.m. emails and callbacks, 2 p.m. to 3 p.m. sorting documents. You collect small things in a separate block instead of spreading them out throughout the day. This method shows immediately whether your plan fits into the day, because a calendar only has 24 hours.

### Which method suits you

| Method | fits well if | Be careful |
|---|---|---|
| 1-3-5 | your day has many small interruptions and you want to remain flexible | big tasks that keep getting pushed aside without a fixed time frame |
| Time blocks | you need to concentrate on bigger things | Days when others have a lot of control over your time |
| Mixed form | you know both | too much planning effort for a single day |

A mixture combines the advantages of both approaches: the big task gets a fixed block, if possible in the hours when you have the most energy. The rest stands next to it as a short 1-3-5 list and fills in the gaps. You decide which task should be the big one using the questions from [Set priorities](/ratgeber/prioritaeten-setzen/).

## Plan buffers and transition times

A plan without air only lasts as long as everything goes according to plan. Buffers are therefore not a luxury, but part of the planning.

- **Buffer after larger blocks:** Plan a small gap after each long task, for example a quarter of an hour. If everything goes smoothly, you've won a break. If things don't go smoothly, the whole afternoon won't fall apart.
- **One buffer block per day:** A free section in the afternoon catches what was left in the morning.
- **Transitional times:** Traveling, changing, getting the children dressed, starting up the computer, gathering after a meeting. These times are short, but they add up and belong in the plan.
- **Real breaks:** Short interruptions help you get through the day. [Breaks in everyday life](/ratgeber/pausen-im-alltag/) describes how you can incorporate them sensibly.

Also pay attention to your energy levels. A challenging task during your afternoon slump takes longer than the same task during your waking hours. How you distribute your energy throughout the day can be found in [Divide Energy](/ratgeber/energie-einteilen/).

## When the day ends: re-sort instead of giving up

No plan survives every day. A sick child, an emergency at work or a delayed train, and by eleven o'clock it's clear that things can't go on like this. The most common mistake is to give up on the plan completely and just react. A quick restart that takes barely five minutes is better:

1. **Stop briefly.** Grab your plan and a pen before rushing forward.
2. **Determine what is still fixed.** Which dates remain the same?
3. **Honestly estimate the remaining time.** How many hours really remain, minus commutes and breaks?
4. **Reselect.** What absolutely has to happen today? Most of the time it's just one thing. You consciously postpone everything else to a specific day or cancel it.
5. **Let us know.** Anyone who waits for something will shortly find out that it will come later.

For very busy phases, a **minimal plan** also helps: a short list of what is enough on a bad day, such as the most important task, food, sleep. A crossed out plan is not a failure. He was a tool, and tools adapt.

## A quick evening comparison for tomorrow

The best time to plan the next day is at the end of today. Five to ten minutes are enough, ideally at a fixed time, such as after work or after dinner.

- **Review of today:** What's done? What has remained open and will it get a new day or will it disappear?
- **Tomorrow's dates:** What is certain and how many paths and crossings are involved?
- **Choose the big task:** What is the one thing that matters tomorrow?
- **Prepare:** Have documents, bag, keys, package ready for the post.

Then you can end the day. [Develop evening routine](/ratgeber/abendroutine-entwickeln/) describes how this comparison fits into a quiet evening ritual. You set the larger line for the whole week in the [Weekly Planning](/ratgeber/wochenplanung-und-wochenrueckblick/) so that one day doesn't have to carry everything.

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

- **The list without a clock:** If you just write down what needs to be done, you won't realize until the evening that it doesn't fit. Estimate a duration for each task.
- **One hundred percent planned:** Every disruption becomes a crisis. Consciously leave gaps.
- **Forgot about routes and waiting times:** The appointment takes thirty minutes, the trip there takes ninety.
- **Simply push on what is left unfinished:** A task that moves every day requires a decision: plan it, reduce it or delete it.
- **Give up at the first failure:** A restart after five minutes often saves half the day.

## Frequently asked questions

### Paper or digital?

Both work. Paper is quick and not distracting, a digital calendar shows blocks of time and appointments in one place. It is important that you only use one system for the day and not keep three lists in parallel.

### What if my job is difficult to plan?

Then plan less, not not at all. A single solid block for your most important task and a short list for the rest is enough. The more unforeseen things happen, the larger the buffers should be.

### Do I also have to plan free time?

Not in detail. However, it helps to mark free time in the plan as occupied so that it is not silently filled with tasks.

## Briefly summarized

A realistic daily plan takes your real time into account. Gather experience of how long things really take for you, first enter fixed appointments and routes and only plan part of your free time. Choose the 1-3-5 method, time blocks or a mix, plan buffers and transitions and reorganize when the day turns. A quick comparison in the evening ensures that the next morning starts with a clear plan.
