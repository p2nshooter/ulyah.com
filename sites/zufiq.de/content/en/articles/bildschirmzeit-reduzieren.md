It's half past ten and you actually just wanted to read the news for a moment. Then there was a video, then another, in between a message in the group chat, and suddenly an hour was gone. At the end of the day, your phone shows a number that scares you and you can hardly tell where the time has gone. This is not a personal failure. Many apps are designed to make it easier to keep going than to stop.

The good news: You can take targeted countermeasures right where the problem arises, on the device itself. This guide will show you how to honestly measure your usage, recognize your triggers, clean up the home screen and remove small hurdles with limits and grayscale. There are also ideas for the empty moments and a plan for four weeks.

## Measure honestly: What your cell phone knows about you

Before you change anything, it's worth taking an honest look at the numbers. Smartphones have a built-in function for this. On the iPhone it's called Screen Time, on many Android devices you'll find it under a name like Digital Wellbeing, labeled slightly differently depending on the manufacturer. The menus change with every update. The quickest way to find the feature is to search for “Screen Time” or “Wellbeing” in Settings.

The overview usually shows:

- how long the screen was active per day and per week
- how much time is spent on individual apps
- How often you unlocked or picked up the device
- how many notifications have been received and from which apps

### This is how you do it

1. **Observe a normal week without changing anything.** Vacation days or sick weeks distort the picture.
2. **Note three values:** your daily average, the three apps with the most time, and the number of unlocks per day.
3. **Don't rate, just sort.** Not every minute on your cell phone is wasted. Navigation, a timetable, a video call with the family or an audio book on the way to work are conscious uses. Mark which time you choose and which one is more likely to happen to you.

The number of unlocks in particular surprises many people. It shows how often the phone interrupts you or you interrupt yourself and is often more revealing than the total time.

## Recognize triggers and patterns

The numbers reveal how much. But what's more interesting is when and why. For three days, briefly note down the situation in which you picked up your cell phone, what you felt before and how you feel afterwards. A few keywords in a notebook are enough.

Recurring patterns often emerge:

| situation | What is often behind it | What can help instead |
|---|---|---|
| Waiting at the stop or at the ticket office | Enduring emptiness is difficult | Be aware of your surroundings, have a book in your pocket |
| Immediately after waking up | Transition into the day, curiosity | an analog alarm clock, the cell phone only after breakfast |
| In the evening on the sofa | Tiredness, desire to switch off | Put something quiet close at hand, such as reading or doing handicrafts |
| When faced with a difficult task | Avoidance, postponement | Make the task smaller, put the cell phone out of sight |
| Reply to a message | one app leads to the next | After answering, consciously put your cell phone away |
| Besides watching TV | double distraction out of habit | one thing at a time, the cell phone out of reach |

If you know your most important pattern, you can start right there instead of generally “using less cell phones”. The guide [Get rid of bad habits] (/ratgeber/schlechte-gewohnheiten-ablegen/) describes in detail how you can change a behavior based on its trigger.

## Declutter the home screen

The start screen is like the display at the supermarket checkout: whatever is there is taken. Every colorful symbol, every red number and every widget with new posts invites you to take a quick look. A quiet home screen is therefore one of the most effective things you can do in half an hour.

1. **Divide your apps into three groups.** Tools like calendar, maps, camera, notes, timetable and banking. Time wasters like social networks, video platforms, news feeds, games and shopping. And quiet companions such as music, audio books or a reading app.
2. **The first page belongs to the tools.** Only what you open specifically is there.
3. **Time wasters are moving further away.** Move them to a folder on the last page or remove them from the home screen entirely so that you only open them via search. Each additional step gives you a moment to ask yourself if this is what you really want right now.
4. **Delete what you don't need.** Apps that you haven't opened in months and those that regularly put you in a bad mood can go. You can reinstall it at any time if necessary.
5. **Log out.** If you have to log in to a service every time, you are less likely to open it out of habit.
6. **Remove widgets with feeds.** Headlines, photos or posts right on the home screen are little bait.
7. **Choose a calm background image.** A plain surface is less distracting than a busy photo.

Notifications are a close part of this, because every red number on an icon attracts the eye. A quick first step: turn off the number badges on all time wasters. The guide [Taming Notifications](/ratgeber/benachrichtigungen-zaehmen/) explains how you can generally organize notifications and set up focus times.

## Install hurdles: app limits, grayscale and more

Hurdles make moving on a little more uncomfortable. They don't prohibit anything, but they interrupt the automatism. You can find most of the functions in your phone's settings in Screen Time or in the accessibility features.

### App limits

You can set a daily limit for individual apps or entire groups. Once it is reached, depending on the system, a message appears or the app is paused for the rest of the day. Start by setting the limit slightly below your previous average, not radically below it. If you've been using an app for a good hour and a half a day, one hour is a feasible first step.

Most limits can be extended with a tap or two. This is not a mistake. A limit is a reminder, not a wall. The moment you decide whether you really want to move on is exactly what it's all about. If you notice that you reflexively push away the limit every day, it helps if someone else sets the code for screen time, if your device offers this.

### Grayscale

Colors make icons, photos and videos more attractive. If you set the screen to grayscale, many things suddenly appear less attractive. You can usually find the setting in the accessibility features under color filters or color correction, and on some Android devices it can also be found as part of a bedtime mode. Many people use a shortcut to switch between color and gray, for example for cameras and maps. Try it for a week. Some people find it liberating, others just find it annoying, and both are fine.

### More small hurdles

- **Turn off automatic playback:** Many video and streaming apps can be set so that the next video does not start automatically.
- **Schedule quiet times:** The Screen Time features can now only allow selected apps at set times. How you can anchor such offline islands in everyday life can be found in [Mobile-free times](/ratgeber/handyfreie-zeiten/).
- **Determine a fixed storage location:** When you're at home, keep your phone in a tray or on a shelf, not in your pocket. Those who have to get up first are less likely to do so out of boredom.

## Replacement for the empty moments

If you just take away, you leave a gap. The moments in which you previously reached for your cell phone need to be filled, otherwise the old habit will return. Think of concrete replacements for your two or three most common triggers.

- **Waiting times:** a thin book or magazine in your bag, a notebook for thoughts or simply observing people and surroundings. A short period of boredom isn't a bad thing, it's often when good ideas come up.
- **In the evening on the sofa:** put something quiet within reach, a book, a puzzle book, knitting or a deck of cards. If the cell phone is further away than the alternative, the alternative usually wins.
- **Short breaks at work:** get up, get a glass of water, look out the window or walk a few steps. This usually provides more relief than just looking at the feed.
- **Need for contact:** call someone instead of scrolling through profiles, or write a message that means something.

## A four-week plan in small steps

Big resolutions often fail after just a few days. Small steps over four weeks have a better chance of becoming a habit.

| week | Focus | Concrete steps |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Measure and observe | Change nothing, write down three values, write down triggers for three days |
| 2 | Clean up the home screen | Divide apps into three groups, move time wasters, delete unused items, turn off number badges |
| 3 | Test hurdles | Limits for two or three time wasters, try grayscale, turn off autoplay |
| 4 | Replacement and review | Determine replacements for the most common triggers, compare values with week 1, decide what remains |

At the end, compare not only the minutes, but also how you feel: Do you fall asleep earlier, do you read more, do you feel less rushed in the evening? This is often more important than any number. Keep what helped and leave out what just annoyed you.

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

- **The radical cure:** Anyone who deletes all apps in one evening often reinstalls them after a few days in frustration. It's better to go step by step.
- **Limits that are too strict:** A limit that you push away every day quickly loses its effect. Start generously and tighten up later.
- **Prohibitions without replacement:** Without an alternative for the empty moments, the old habit returns.
- **Switching to other screens:** If you simply shift your time from your cell phone to a tablet or laptop, you have gained little. Include these devices in your observation.
- **Compare yourself with others:** Your number only has to be right for you. Occupation, place of residence and living situation strongly influence usage.

## If you can't do it alone

Almost everyone uses their cell phone more often than they would like. But if you notice that no matter how hard you try, you can't stop, that your sleep, work or relationships are suffering significantly, or that you become restless when your cell phone is out of reach, talk to someone about it. Addiction counseling centers also provide advice on problematic media use, usually free of charge and confidentially. If you can still help, see [Help & Contact Points](/hilfe-und-anlaufstellen/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How much screen time is normal?

There is no one number that fits everyone. Anyone who does a lot of work on their cell phone will have different values ​​than someone who almost only uses it to make phone calls. The more important question is whether the time fits your own goals and whether you choose it.

### Do I have to delete social networks completely?

No. For many, it's enough to remove them from the home screen, set a limit and turn off notifications. If you notice that an app mostly makes you feel bad, you can leave it alone.

### Does this also help with work cell phones?

Many steps, such as a tidy home screen and silent notifications, also work there. It is best to clarify with your employer what is allowed and expected.

## Briefly summarized

The most effective way to reduce screen time is on the device itself. Measure honestly for a week, find your most important triggers and clear out the home screen so that only tools are there. Build in small hurdles with app limits, grayscale and switched off automatic playback and have a replacement for empty moments. Take it step by step over four weeks and at the end evaluate not just the minutes, but also how your days feel.
