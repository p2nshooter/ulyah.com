The package has been lying unopened in the hallway for three weeks. Inside is a jacket that looked irresistible in the online shop at midnight, reduced and only available in your size. In the kitchen cupboard is the milk frother from the promotional item at the discount store, used twice. And in the closet hangs a shirt with a tag, bought on a frustrating Saturday downtown. Each of these things felt right at the moment of purchase. A few days later there's little left of it except a hint of anger.

Avoiding impulse purchases doesn't mean never buying anything nice again. It means making purchasing decisions yourself again instead of leaving them to the moment. This guide will show you how to recognize impulses, counteract them with some time and deal calmly with bad purchases.

## Why buying impulses are so strong

At the moment, a purchase promises above all a feeling: reward, comfort, anticipation or the image of yourself living with the new thing. This feeling is real, but it usually disappears faster than the item takes up space in the apartment.

In addition, buying is now easier than ever. Saved payment details, one-click orders, countdowns, “only a few left in stock” notices, and advertising that precisely matches your recent searches are designed to shorten the moment between desire and purchase. Anyone who buys spontaneously more often is not weak-willed. He moves in an environment that encourages exactly that. That's why the following steps focus primarily on triggers, time and small hurdles.

## Recognize typical triggers

Most impulse purchases follow recurring patterns. If you know your own, you can take countermeasures sooner.

| Trigger | How he shows himself | What helps instead |
|---|---|---|
| Boredom | Swipe through shop apps on the sofa in the evening | Remove shop apps from the home screen, have something ready for your hands |
| Frustration and stress | after a hard day “I deserve this” | a reward without purchase: a bath, a walk, a call to a loved one |
| Discount promotions | “only today”, sales, promotional weeks around holidays | ask: Would I have bought this at full price? |
| Free shipping limit | a filler item so that shipping costs are eliminated | Paying shipping costs is often cheaper than paying for an item you don't need |
| Advertising on social media | Products in videos, ads matching your searches | Unfollow channels that primarily provide you with buying ideas |
| Promotional goods in the supermarket | Tools or kitchen utensils between milk and bread | Shopping with a shopping list, consciously skipping the action |

### A short purchase log

If you don't know what your triggers are, write down every purchase that wasn't on your shopping list for two to three weeks. Write about when and where it was, how you felt and what the occasion was. After a while you add whether you actually use what you bought. A pattern usually quickly emerges, for example that almost all spontaneous purchases happen on the cell phone after 10 p.m. or always after a stressful appointment.

## The waiting time rule with wish list

The most effective antidote to impulses is time. A genuine purchase desire can survive a few days of waiting. Usually not an impulse.

1. **Create a wish list**, on paper or in a note app, but not as a wish list in the shop. There you will receive reminders, price reduction messages and recommendations that will rekindle your desire.
2. **Enter each unplanned request with a date**, the price and the reason why you want it.
3. **Set your own waiting period.** A possible staggering: two days for small items, one week for medium-sized purchases, one month for everything larger such as furniture, technology or expensive clothing. You decide the amounts from which which level applies, depending on your budget.
4. **Check the list again after the deadline.** Do you still want the item? Then buy it with a good feeling, because the rule is not a ban. However, you will often notice that the entry is now indifferent to you, or that you no longer know why it was so important to you.

A pleasant side effect: The wish list is also a good collection of ideas if someone asks you for a birthday wish.

## Remove hurdles: online and in store

The easier a purchase is, the more likely it is to happen incidentally. Small hurdles give you the seconds back in which you can think.

### Online

- **No saved payment details.** If you have to look for the card number for every order, many impulse purchases will be canceled by themselves.
- **Delete store apps.** Shopping in the browser is a bit more cumbersome, and that's exactly the purpose. It's better to turn off push notifications from shops anyway.
- **Unsubscribe from newsletter.** Every advertising email is an invitation to buy. How you can keep your inbox quieter in the long term can be found in [clean up your email inbox](/ratgeber/e-mail-postfach-aufraeumen/).
- **Leave the shopping cart.** Put the things in, close the window and decide the next day. Expect that some shops will then send you a reminder or a voucher. This is another trigger, not a sign that you should take action now.
- **Be critical of paying later.** Payment methods where the money is only debited weeks later make a purchase seem easier. If you want to buy more consciously, you'd better pay straight away.
- **Limit personalized advertising.** You can limit how closely ads are tailored to you in the settings of many accounts and devices.

### In the store

- **Shop with a note and a goal.** What is not on the note goes on the wish list, not in the basket.
- **Don't go shopping hungry, tired or frustrated** if you can avoid it. In these moments, reward purchases seem particularly tempting.
- **Use a basket instead of a cart** if you only need a few things. You think twice about what will be difficult.
- **Consciously replace strolling.** If you like to meet in the city center in your free time, you can change the meeting point to a café, a park or a museum.
- **Put back instead of taking with you.** An item that you want next week will probably still be there, and if not, you can find a similar one.

## Questions before every purchase

If a wish has survived the waiting period, a few honest questions will help you make the final decision:

1. Do I already have something that does this job?
2. Where exactly will it stand, hang or lie in my apartment?
3. On what specific occasions will I use it in the next few months?
4. Would I buy it at full price?
5. Am I buying the item or image of myself that it promises, such as the yoga mat for a more relaxed me?
6. What kind of work does ownership entail: care, charging, cleaning, storage?
7. Is there an alternative, such as borrowing, buying used or repairing something that already exists?

If you decide to buy it, it's worth taking a look at the durability. [Recognizing quality](/ratgeber/qualitaet-erkennen/) explains how you can recognize good workmanship. And if it doesn't have to be new, [Buy used](/ratgeber/gebraucht-kaufen/) will help you.

## The buying break as an experiment

A voluntary break from buying for a few weeks is a good way to get to know your own habits. It works best when you don't see it as a punishment, but rather as a curious experiment.

Write down in advance what is allowed and what is paused:

| Still allowed | Paused | Goes on the wish list |
|---|---|---|
| Food and drugstore items for normal needs | Clothing, shoes, accessories | anything that comes to mind during the break |
| Replacement for something that has broken and is needed | Decoration, candles, home accessories | Things you would like but don't need |
| gifts already planned | Books, magazines, technology, kitchen aids | Ideas for future purchases |

This is how you do it:

1. **Set time frame.** Four to six weeks is long enough to notice patterns and short enough to persist.
2. **Plan a replacement for the habit.** Read the unread books on the shelf, cook from your supplies, meet up with friends for a walk instead of strolling.
3. **Observe.** Note when the desire to buy was particularly strong and what you did instead.
4. **Evaluate.** At the end, look at your wish list: What else do you really want? What didn't you miss at all? Keep the rules that have worked for you.

## If only it was a bad purchase

Bad purchases happen to everyone. Don't fret for long, but use it as information and deal with it quickly so that the thing doesn't sit around as a silent reproach for months.

- **Purchased online:** As a consumer, you have a legal right of withdrawal for many online purchases. The deadline, exceptions and the question of who pays for the return can be found in the retailer's cancellation policy. Check them early because the deadline usually begins when you receive the goods.
- **Purchased in store:** An exchange without a defect is usually a voluntary service provided by the store. You can find out whether and for how long it is possible at the checkout or on the receipt. So keep your receipts for a while.
- **If you are unsure:** The consumer advice center in your federal state will explain the current rules for your case.
- **Return not possible:** Sell or give away the item soon while it is still new. At least someone else can enjoy it.

Then write down in your purchase log what led to the bad purchase. That's the real savings.

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

- **Total waiver from one day to the next:** Strict bans often end in catch-up purchases. It's better to start with a waiting time and a wish list.
- **Count discounts as savings:** You only saved if you had bought the thing anyway.
- **Keep the wish list in the shop:** There it works for the retailer, not for you.
- **Prohibitions without replacement:** If shopping was your consolation after tiring days, another consolation is needed.
- **Be ashamed of every purchase:** Conscious consumption is not a competition. A well-considered purchase is a success, not a setback.

## When buying becomes a problem

Sometimes frequent impulse purchases are more than just a bad habit. If you keep buying to dampen bad feelings, hide purchases from others, unopened packages pile up, you end up in debt because of it, or arguments arise, get support. Addiction counseling centers also offer advice on shopping addictions, and debt counseling helps with money worries. You can find both on the [Help & Contact Points](/hilfe-und-anlaufstellen/) page.

## Frequently asked questions

### Isn't it economical to buy in advance when on sale?

This can be useful for things that you use regularly and that don't go bad, provided you have space for them. For everything else: an offer is only cheap if you would have needed the item anyway.

### Can I replace something broken during the purchase break?

Yes, if you really need it. But ask yourself beforehand whether repairs, buying used or borrowing are possible. That's part of the experiment.

### What do I do if shopping is my hobby?

Then don't replace the hobby, but rather the purchase. Window shopping without a wallet, flea markets on a budget, or browsing in the library often fulfill the same desire for discovery.

## Briefly summarized

Impulse purchases arise from feelings in the moment and from an environment that makes buying easy. Find your triggers with a short purchase log, give each unplanned wish a waiting time on your own wish list and build in small hurdles: no saved payment data, no shop apps, no newsletters. Before every purchase, ask a few honest questions, try a break from buying as an experiment and deal with bad purchases calmly.
