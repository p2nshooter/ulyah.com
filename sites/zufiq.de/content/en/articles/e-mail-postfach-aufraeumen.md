There is a four-digit number next to the letter symbol on your phone. Somewhere in this mountain lies the appointment confirmation from the citizen's office, the bill that you still have to pay and the message from a friend that you have been trying to respond to for three weeks. Above and below: newsletters, shipping confirmations, notices from networks you hardly use anymore, and advertising from shops you once ordered something from. Every time you open the mailbox, you close it again with a slight pressure in your stomach.

A full mailbox is not a sign of disorder or weakness. It's the result of more coming in every day than anyone could comfortably sort through. This guide will show you how to get rid of the old inventory in a restart without touching each email individually, how to throttle the influx and create a system with three folders, a few filters and fixed times that lasts even on full days.

## Why is a full mailbox so stressful?

Every email requires a small decision: read, reply, keep, delete? When there are hundreds of undecided emails in your inbox, you see each of them as an open question. Important things, things done and advertising mix together and you lose confidence that you haven't overlooked anything. It's exactly this feeling that something could go wrong that makes the mailbox stressful, not the number itself.

The goal is therefore not to have a permanently empty inbox as proof of performance. The goal is an inbox where you know: What's in your inbox is new. What needs to be done is in a fixed place. Everything else is stored and can be found using the search.

## Step 1: Restart with an archive instead of mail by mail

Processing thousands of old emails individually is hardly possible and not necessary. Things that have been left unanswered for months have usually been resolved or come back again. That's why you go to the archive with the old inventory in one go.

1. **Create a folder,** for example “Archive until July 2026”. The date will later show you that the old inventory is there.
2. **Only look at the last few weeks.** Briefly go through the emails from the last two to four weeks and pick out what really needs a response or action.
3. **Move everything else to the archive.** Mark all older emails and move them in one step. Nothing is deleted.
4. **Check the search.** Test search for an old email in the archive so you can be confident that it can still be found.

Try moving a few emails first and check whether they actually arrive in the new folder. For some programs, “archiving” means something different than for others. Your employer's storage rules often apply to work mailboxes. If in doubt, ask the responsible IT department.

If you are short on storage space, sort the archive later by size and delete old emails with large attachments that you no longer need. This is not necessary for the restart itself.

## Step 2: Systematically unsubscribe from newsletters and advertising

A freshly emptied inbox will quickly fill up again if the influx continues. That's why next you take care of everything that comes regularly and without being asked.

- **Look for unsubscribe links.** Enter terms like “unsubscribe”, “unsubscribe” or “newsletter” in the search and see the results from the last few weeks. This way you can see at a glance who writes to you regularly.
- **Decide per sender.** Are you really reading this newsletter, or are you just skimming the subject line? Anything you wouldn't miss can be unsubscribed using the link at the end of the email.
- **Check your customer account.** For shops, you can often opt out of promotional emails in the account settings.
- **Don't click on spam.** Report emails from unknown or obviously dubious senders as spam instead of clicking on an unsubscribe link. The click can confirm that your address is active.
- **Use an unsubscribe week.** If the search is too tedious for you, you can decide immediately with every newsletter that arrives for a week: keep it or unsubscribe.

Newsletters that you enjoy reading can stay. You will later get your own folder using a filter so that they are not between the important emails. Advertising emails often come from accounts that you no longer use. It is best to delete these accounts completely, as described in [Cleaning out passwords and accounts](/ratgeber/passwoerter-und-konten-ausmisten/).

## Step 3: A lean folder system

Many mailboxes fail not because they have too few folders, but because they have too many folders. With thirty subfolders, you have to think about where each email belongs. Today, the search usually finds an email faster than any sophisticated folder structure. Three folders are enough for almost everything:

| folder | What belongs in it | When you look inside |
|---|---|---|
| To do | Emails that require you to do something that takes longer than a few minutes | daily in your email time |
| Waiting for | Emails where you are waiting for someone else, such as a delivery, an offer or feedback | once or twice a week |
| filing | anything done that you might need again | only when necessary via search |

If you keep newsletters, a fourth “Reading” folder will be added. Some services work with labels instead of folders, where an email can have multiple labels. The principle remains the same.

With each new email you then make one of four decisions:

1. **Delete** if it has no value.
2. **Do it immediately** if it only takes a minute or two and then put it down.
3. **Move to To Do or Waiting For** if it takes longer or depends on others.
4. **Dismiss** if you just had to acknowledge them.

You can also save invoices and receipts that you need for taxes or warranties as PDFs in your file storage. [Keep documents](/ratgeber/unterlagen-aufbewahren/) explains how long it is worth keeping.

## Step 4: Rules and filters that do the work for you

Most email programs can automatically sort incoming emails according to rules. You can usually find this in the settings under “Rules” or “Filters”. A rule consists of a condition, such as a specific sender or a word in the subject, and an action, such as moving to a folder or marking.

Useful rules to start with:

- **Newsletters you keep** will automatically end up in the “Reading” folder.
- **Shipping confirmations from shops** go directly to the file. However, it is better to leave bills that you have to pay in your inbox so that no payment goes missing.
- **Emails from important people,** such as family, are marked so that they stand out.
- **At work:** Emails in which you only have a copy go into a separate folder that you look through once a day.

Start with three to five rules and check after a month whether they are doing what they are supposed to. It's best to turn off notifications from social networks and apps directly in their settings instead of filtering them out. And don't automatically forward emails from your work inbox to your private address. This often violates company rules and data protection.

## Fixed email times and short answers

A tidy inbox only stays quiet if it doesn't constantly interrupt you. Set fixed times when you read emails, for example in the late morning and afternoon at work, and once a day at work. In between, the mailbox remains closed. [Tame notifications](/ratgeber/benachrichtigungen-zaehmen/) explains how you can turn off banners, sounds and counters on your cell phone and computer.

In your email time, you work through the inbox from top to bottom without jumping. Keep answers short: first things first, a few sentences, a subject that says what it's about. For recurring answers, it is worth using text modules that you can save as a template in your email program or on your cell phone using the text replacement:

> Thanks for your message. I'll take a look at it until Thursday and then get back to you.

> Thanks for the invitation. Unfortunately I can't make it this time, I wish you a nice evening.

> In order for me to be able to help, I need the following information: …

## Separate work and private life

When private newsletters end up in your work inbox and work emails pop up on your private cell phone in the evening, the boundaries become blurred. A few clear lines help:

- **Separate addresses:** Do not register anywhere privately with your work address.
- **Separate apps or profiles:** If work emails are allowed on your cell phone, then in a separate app that you can mute after work.
- **Out of Office Note:** When you're on vacation, it tells people when you'll be back and who they can contact.
- **One address for shops:** Privately, a second address can only be useful for purchases and customer accounts. This way, your main address remains reserved for people.

## Recognize the basics of phishing emails

A tidy mailbox also helps protect against fraud because anything conspicuous catches the eye more quickly. Fake emails pose as a bank, parcel service, government agency or well-known online shop. These features should make you suspicious:

| feature | What you pay attention to |
|---|---|
| Pressure and haste | Threats such as an alleged account blocking or a deadline of a few hours |
| Querying data | Request to enter password, PIN, TAN or card details via a link |
| Return address | The name displayed sounds legitimate, but the actual address behind it does not match |
| Left | The destination that appears when hovering or long-pressing is different from the expected address |
| Attachments | Unexpected files, such as archives or documents, that want to activate macros |
| Salutation | generic salutation, even though the real sender would know your name |

Spelling errors used to be a typical warning signal. Today, many fake emails are linguistically flawless, so don't rely on it. Reputable banks do not ask you to enter your PIN or TAN by email. If you're unsure, don't click, don't open an attachment, and sign in to the service using your own app or a saved bookmark. ## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

- **Processing the old inventory mail by mail:** This takes weeks. Move it to the archive and start fresh.
- **Creating too many folders:** The finer the structure, the longer each decision takes. Three folders and the search are enough.
- **Use the inbox as a to-do list:** What needs to be done goes after “To Do” so that the inbox only shows new things.
- **Constantly check:** If you check your inbox every few minutes, you won't be working on anything thoroughly. Fixed email times create peace.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I delete or archive old emails?

Archiving is the safer way to restart because nothing is lost. Deleting them is particularly worthwhile for large attachments, when memory is running out, and for pure advertising.

### How often does the inbox have to be empty?

It is enough if it is manageable after each email time and is completely emptied once a week. An empty inbox is a tool, not an end in itself.

## Briefly summarized

Move the old inventory to an archive in one go after you have briefly looked through it over the last few weeks. Systematically unsubscribe from newsletters and advertising and report spam instead of clicking on unsubscribe links. Work with three folders: To Do, Waiting For, and File. Let filters take over the routine work, read emails at fixed times, respond briefly and use text modules. Separate your professional and private life and remain suspicious of emails that put pressure on you and request data.
