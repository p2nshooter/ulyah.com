The cell phone lies next to the plate and is picked up every time there is a break in the conversation. In the evening on the sofa, your hand automatically goes into the bag of chips. The episode is watched “just one more” and then another until it’s past midnight. Almost everyone has habits that they actually want to get rid of. And almost everyone has experienced that simply doing something has little effect. This is not a character flaw. Habits are so powerful precisely because they occur without conscious decision. If you want to change them, you have to understand how they work and start there.

This guide shows you a calm, practical way to get rid of unwanted habits without tormenting yourself with prohibitions and a guilty conscience.

## Why bans rarely work

A habit almost always serves a purpose. The cell phone at the dinner table eliminates boredom or insecurity. The chips in the evening are a reward after a strenuous day. The next episode postpones the moment when the day is over and tomorrow begins. If you simply eliminate the habit, the need behind it remains and it looks for another path, often the old one.

It is more successful to break the habit down into its parts: **What triggers it? What do you do then? What do you get for it?** Only when you know this can you start in the right place.

## Break a habit step by step

### 1. Observe without judging

For a few days, record every time the habit occurs: When was it? Where have you been? How did you feel? Who was there? What happened right before that? Just write it down without criticizing yourself. A pattern usually quickly emerges, such as always at the same time, always when tired or always after a certain conversation.

### 2. Find the need behind it

Ask yourself: What does this habit give me? Relaxation, distraction, comfort, companionship, a sense of control, a break? The need is usually completely justified. Only the way to fulfill it no longer suits you.

| habit | Common need behind it | Possible replacement |
|---|---|---|
| constant cell phone checking | Boredom, restlessness, connection | Have a book with you, consciously look out the window, a fixed cell phone time |
| Snacking in the evening | Reward, relaxation | a special tea, a bath, a fixed evening ritual |
| Series until late at night | prolong the day, recovery | fixed number of episodes, evening routine with an ending |
| Procrastination | Insecurity, excessive demands | Divide the task into very small steps |
| Impulse purchases | Joy, comfort, boredom | Wish list with waiting time, walking instead of shopping |

### 3. Make the old habit uncomfortable

The easier a habit is, the more often you do it. Therefore, consciously create hurdles:

- The cell phone charges in another room, not next to the bed or sofa.
- Social media apps disappear from the home screen or are only used in the browser, where it takes longer to log in.
- Sweets are no longer bought in stock, but only individually if you really want them.
- The automatic start of the next episode is switched off in the streaming service settings.
- Saved payment data will be deleted in online shops.

Just a few seconds more effort is often enough to turn an automatic decision into a conscious decision.

### 4. Choose a replacement for the same trigger

If a trigger remains, it needs a new response. Formulate a clear plan: “When I wait at the stop, I will read my book.” “When I sink onto the sofa exhausted after work, the first thing I do is drink tea.” The replacement doesn't have to be perfect, but it should at least partially fulfill the same need. How to build new habits is found in [Building New Habits](/ratgeber/neue-gewohnheiten-aufbauen/).

### 5. Change the environment where possible

Some habits are closely tied to places. If you always snack on the sofa, you can spend some time at the dining table or in a reading chair. If you're on your cell phone in bed, you don't even take it into the bedroom. Small changes in the environment break the automatic chain.

### 6. Expect relapses

Old habits don't just disappear, they become quieter. They often reappear during periods of stress, on vacation or when tired. That doesn't mean it was all in vain. Take the relapse as information: What was different this time? Which hurdle was missing? Then you start again the next day.

### 7. Get allies

Tell people you trust what you want to change. Maybe someone will join in, for example at a cell phone-free dinner. Common household rules are easier to adhere to than individual fights.

## An example: the cell phone while eating

Anna notices that she constantly looks at her cell phone while eating, even when her partner is sitting at the table. As she observes, she notices: It mainly happens when the conversation stalls or she comes home from work stressed. The need behind it is distraction and a bit of escape from the day. She creates a hurdle: the cell phone ends up in a drawer in the hallway while eating. As an alternative, they both agree to talk about a beautiful and a stressful moment of the day while eating. In the first few days it feels unusual, but after two weeks it's a ritual that both of them would miss. During stressful weeks, the cell phone sometimes ends up on the table again. Then Anna puts it back in the drawer the next evening.

## Stress and fatigue: the secret amplifiers

Many unwanted habits get worse when you're exhausted. This is no coincidence. When energy is scarce, the brain prefers to resort to well-rehearsed processes because they require little energy. That's why it's so much harder to leave the bag of chips in the cupboard after a long day at work than on a relaxed Sunday morning.

This leads to an important insight: If you want to break bad habits, you should also look at sleep, breaks and stress. Sometimes the most effective step isn't breaking the habit, but rather an earlier bedtime or a real break in the afternoon. The guide [Improve sleeping habits](/ratgeber/schlafgewohnheiten-verbessern/) shows you how to ensure more restful nights, and [Breaks in everyday life](/ratgeber/pausen-im-alltag/) helps you recharge your batteries during the day.

Also, plan for difficult situations. If you know that Thursday is always particularly stressful, you can specifically prepare an alternative for that evening: a walk straight after work, a prepared dinner, a date that gets you off the sofa.

## Use rewards correctly

When you break a habit, you give up something that was previously pleasant. So that this doesn't just look like a loss, it helps to make progress visible and consciously appreciate it.

- **Record progress.** On a calendar or [habit tracker](/ratgeber/gewohnheits-tracker/), mark each day that you didn't perform the old habit or consciously replaced it. A growing series of crosses motivates.
- **Repurpose savings.** If you spend less money on impulse purchases or snacks, you can collect the amount and use it after a few weeks for something nice, such as a concert or a trip.
- **Make conscious use of the time you have gained.** If you watch fewer series in the evening, plan your free time for something that is good for you. Otherwise it will quickly fill up with the old stuff again.
- **Celebrate small successes.** The first week without a cell phone at the dinner table is a reason to be happy. Tell someone about it or treat yourself.

Avoid rewards that resemble old habits. If you reduce your snacking in the evening, you shouldn't reward yourself with a large bar of chocolate.

## A plan for the first four weeks

Breaking a habit takes time. This rough plan will help you go step by step without overwhelming yourself:

1. **Week One: Observe.** Note when and why the habit occurs. Don't change anything yet, except that you consciously notice them.
2. **Week two: Add hurdles.** Choose two or three specific hurdles from step three and implement them.
3. **Week three: Practice replacement.** Formulate a clear if-then plan for the most common triggers and try it out.
4. **Week Four: Review.** What worked and what didn't? Adjust hurdles and replacements. The [Weekly Review](/ratgeber/wochenplanung-und-wochenrueckblick/) is a good framework for this.

After these four weeks, the old habit rarely disappears completely, but is usually significantly weaker. And you now know which tools work for you.

## Typical mistakes

| Error | Better that way |
|---|---|
| want to change everything at once | one habit at a time |
| just ban it, without replacement | fulfill the need differently |
| punish yourself for relapses | learn from relapses and move on |
| rely on willpower | Install hurdles and change the environment |
| ignore the trigger | Recognize the trigger and plan specifically |

## When professional help is important

Some behaviors are more than just annoying habits. If you find that you cannot control a behavior even though it is harming you, your health, your finances, or your relationships, such as alcohol, nicotine, medication, gambling, or binge eating, or if you are feeling very distressed, get support. General practitioners, addiction counseling centers and other specialist offices provide confidential help. You can find information on the [Help & Contact Points](/hilfe-und-anlaufstellen/) page.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to break a habit?

This varies greatly and depends on how deeply anchored the habit is and what need it fulfills. Some habits become significantly weaker after just a few weeks, others stay with you for months. More important than a time goal is to stick with it and learn from relapses.

### Should I change multiple habits at once?

Better not. Every change costs attention and energy. Focus on one habit until it becomes stable, then move on to the next one.

### What if others in the household continue the habit?

Be open about what you want to change and ask for support without patronizing others. Common rules can often be found, such as a cell phone-free dining table or sweets in a cupboard that is not constantly in view.

## Briefly summarized

You don't break bad habits by prohibiting them, but by understanding what triggers them and what need they fulfill. Observe without judgment, make the old habit uncomfortable, choose a replacement for the same trigger, and change your environment. Relapses are part of it, they are information, not failure.
