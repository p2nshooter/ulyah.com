Maybe you know the pictures: an almost empty, white apartment, a sofa, a plant, three carefully selected books on the shelf. There are also challenges where someone wants to live with only a hundred items and lists of things that they supposedly don't need. This can be attractive because it promises peace. But it can also be intimidating because your life looks different. You have hobbies, a toolbox, maybe kids or a partner who loves his record collection and don't feel like counting every item.

The good news: Minimalism isn't about these images at all. At its core is a question that you can only answer for yourself: What do I really need, use and love, and what is just lying around? The answer is different for every person. This guide will help you find your own level of “enough” without external rules, without a guilty conscience and without putting other people under pressure.

## What minimalism essentially means

Minimalism means consciously choosing, not doing without. It's not about owning as little as possible, it's about making sure the things around you fit your life. A full bookcase can be minimalist if you like every book in it. It's not a half-empty apartment if there are twenty boxes in the basement whose contents you no longer know.

Three ideas support almost every form of minimalism:

- **Things cost more than their price.** Everything you own needs space, care, attention and sometimes repairs. The less superfluous there is, the more room there is for what is important to you.
- **Enough is one personal size.** There is no correct number of cups, sweaters or cables. There is only the amount with which your everyday life functions well.
- **Less is a means, not a goal.** You don't reduce to achieve a certain look in the end, but to gain time, money, space and peace of mind for other things.

If you internalize this, you no longer need rigid guidelines. The question then is not “Can I keep this?” but rather “Is this good for me and does it have a place here?”

## Common myths and constraints

There are ideas circulating around minimalism that create more pressure than calm. You may come across some of them in videos, books or conversations with friends.

| Myth | What applies instead |
|---|---|
| Minimalists own at most a hundred things. | Numbers are a gimmick. What matters is whether you use or value what you have. |
| A minimalist apartment is white and empty. | Colors, pictures and favorite pieces are included if they mean something to you. |
| You have to clear everything out at once. | Small steps over weeks often last longer than a radical action. |
| Minimalism means doing without. | You give up baggage, not joy, comfort or hospitality. |
| Once you've reduced, you're done. | Life changes, and with it your “enough”. |
| First you need new, consistent things. | If you reduce, you don't buy anything new just to make it look quieter. |

The idea that you have to do minimalism right is particularly treacherous. Anyone who starts counting objects, comparing themselves with others or justifying a new pillow has swapped one set of rules for another. The goal is a more relaxed relationship with things, not a new competitive sport.

## Determine your own “enough.”

“Enough” cannot be determined for the entire apartment at once. It gets easier if you look area by area. For some, a well-equipped kitchen is a matter close to their hearts, while the wardrobe can be small. For the other, it's the exact opposite, and both are fine.

### This is how you do it

1. **Choose an area of life.** For example, clothing, kitchen, books and media, hobbies, technology or decoration.
2. **Get an overview.** Look at what you own in this area, including in closets, boxes, and the basement. You don't have to sort anything out yet.
3. **Answer the basic questions.** You will find them directly in the next section.
4. **Set a boundary that you can see.** The easiest way is to use a container as a measurement: a shelf for decorations, a drawer for cables, a box for craft supplies. What fits in can stay.
5. **Check after a few weeks.** Are you missing something or can you even get by with less? Then you adjust the limit.

### The basic questions for each area

- Which of these have I actually used in the last few months?
- What do I always resort to even though there are alternatives? These are my favorites.
- What do I own more than once without needing the same amount?
- What am I only keeping because it was expensive, was a gift or because I might need it someday?
- How does this area feel when I open or enter it: inviting, neutral or oppressive?
- What would I really miss if it were gone tomorrow?

### Examples of very different “enough”

| area | Key question | A possible measure |
|---|---|---|
| Clothing | What do I wear on a normal week and what on special occasions? | enough to last you until the next wash day, plus a few items for parties |
| Kitchen | What am I really cooking and for how many people? | Dishes for everyone who regularly sits at the table, plus a reserve for guests |
| Books and media | What do I read or hear again, what do I pass on? | a specific shelf that doesn't overflow |
| Hobbies | Which hobby am I currently pursuing and which has been dormant for years? | Material for active hobbies, resting ones in a single box |
| Technology | Which devices and cables are in use? | a drawer for cables and accessories, old devices are given away |
| Decoration | What makes me happy every day, what is there? | a few areas that you consciously design |

These dimensions are suggestions, not specifications. Anyone who cooks for friends every week needs more plates than someone who almost always eats alone. Anyone who is passionate about sewing is allowed to have a supply of fabric. The important thing is that you determine the measure and not the things you.

## Minimalism with your partner, family or shared apartment

Anyone who lives alone can freely determine their “enough”. As soon as you share rooms, a negotiation begins. This is exactly where many good resolutions fail because enthusiasm quickly turns into conversion zeal.

### Start with yourself

Take care of your own things first: your closet, your desk, your hobby box. That's fair, and it's usually more convincing than any discussion. When others see how relaxed you are about your things, they often become curious themselves.

### Decide on common areas together

The following applies to the kitchen, living room, hallway or bathroom: no one makes decisions alone. Sit down together and talk about what bothers you about these spaces and what is important to you. Often the wishes are not that far apart. You both want a work surface that you can cook on, but you disagree about how to get there.

An example from a shared apartment: Three roommates have four pans, three kettles and two toasters because each of them brought their own kitchen. Instead of secretly sorting things out, they put everything on the table and decide together what stays in the kitchen. Everyone takes the rest to their room or passes it on. This way no one feels left out.

### Never give away other people's things

Even though it's tempting, don't give away anything that doesn't belong to you, not even "obvious junk." What looks useless to you may be important to someone else. A single such instance can damage trust, which takes a long time to grow again.

### Allow your own islands

A simple agreement is helpful: Each person has an area in which they can live according to their own taste, whether full or empty. This can be a room, a shelf or a corner. You design the shared spaces together. This takes pressure out of discussions and protects the relationship.

## An attitude for everyday life

Decluttering is a start. So that the apartment doesn't slowly fill up again, you need an attitude that keeps up with everyday life. These habits have proven successful:

- **One in, one out:** If something new comes into the house, something similar goes. You don't have to be rigid about it. Even the question “What replaces this?” changes the way you look at new things.
- **The Waiting List:** Write down your wishes with dates on a list instead of buying them straight away and look at them again after a few weeks. Whatever else is important, you can do in peace. A lot of things have been done by then.
- **Critically check for free:** Promotional gifts, sample packs and things from household clearances cost nothing, but still take up space. A friendly “No thank you” is permitted.
- **Care for instead of replacing:** What you care for and have repaired stays with you longer and new things come along less often.
- **Small passages by the way:** If you open a drawer anyway, take out what no longer fits. This way, your “enough” is retained without any major actions.

## When reducing itself becomes stressful

Minimalism is meant to relieve stress. But sometimes it turns into the opposite. Look out for these signs:

- You feel guilty about every purchase, even things you really need.
- You keep buying what you just gave away.
- You count objects or constantly compare your apartment with other people's pictures.
- You argue with people you live with because they have “too much.”
- Decluttering has become a constant job instead of giving you time.

If you find yourself in this, take a break. Leave the apartment as it is for a while and ask yourself what you originally hoped to achieve by downsizing: more peace, more time, more money? Maybe you have already achieved this. Then you can stop optimizing. A full bookshelf or a drawer full of knick-knacks doesn't make you a worse person.

If thoughts of possessing or letting go bother you greatly, either because you can't part with anything or because you have to part with everything, talk to a professional. Anyone who can help can be found at [Help & Contact Points](/hilfe-und-anlaufstellen/).

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

- **Too much too quickly:** Anyone who empties half the apartment in one weekend often regrets individual decisions. Take your time, especially with things that have feelings attached to them.
- **Buy new things to look minimalist:** Matching boxes or a uniform set of dishes are not a starting point. First reduce it, then see if anything is missing.
- **Adopt someone else's rules:** A number or a look from the internet rarely fits your life.
- **Wanting to convert others:** Living by example is more powerful than persuading.
- **Only look at objects:** Appointments, obligations and digital collections can also become too much. Your “enough” may extend to this, but it doesn’t have to.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is minimalism just for singles in city apartments?

No. Families and people with little space in particular benefit from making conscious choices. The amount that fits is then different.

### Do I have to sort out memorabilia?

No. Memories are often exactly what is allowed to remain. It just helps to store them consciously instead of hiding them in forgotten boxes.

### How do I know that I have reduced enough?

If you find what you are looking for, cupboards can be closed without effort and you feel comfortable in your rooms. From then on it's all about preservation.

## Briefly summarized

Minimalism without dogma means: You decide for yourself what is enough. Look at your things one area at a time, ask yourself honest questions, and set boundaries that you can see, like a shelf or a box. When living together, the following applies: start with yourself, decide on things together, and never give away other people's things. In everyday life, “one in, one out”, a waiting list and a critical look at free things help. And if cutting back is causing more stress than relief, it's time for a break.
