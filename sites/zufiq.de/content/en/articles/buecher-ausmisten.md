The shelf has long been filled in two rows. The novels are at the front, behind them are the non-fiction books that you would like to read at some point. A pile grows on the floor and a second one on the bedside table. You've long since sorted out clothes, kitchen stuff and old cables, but you hesitate when it comes to books. Giving away a book feels somehow wrong, almost disrespectful.

This feeling is widespread and should be taken seriously. Books are more than paper. Nevertheless, a shelf that only contains what really means something to you can be a great joy. This guide will show you why sorting out books is so difficult, how to look at them in categories, which questions will help you with each individual book, what happens to the unread ones and where the books you pass on go.

## Why books are so difficult

Hardly any other object brings together so many feelings. Those who understand what is holding them back make decisions more easily.

- **Books represent education.** Many grew up with the phrase that you don't throw away books. The shelf acts like a business card: someone who reads lives here.
- **Books carry memories.** You read the novel on vacation by the sea, the non-fiction book accompanied you through a difficult time.
- **Unread books are promises.** They represent the person you would like to be: someone who reads philosophy, finally learns Italian or knows the great classics.
- **Gifted books bond with people.** Giving a book away feels like you are rejecting the giver.
- **Books retain their value.** They rarely break and therefore seem as if they could last forever.

A helpful thought: A book is made to be read. If it's just been there for you for years, it can serve its purpose again for someone else. You don't give it up, you pass it on.

## Preparation: all books in one place

Books tend to be spread all over the apartment. Before you decide, you should see how many there really are.

1. **Choose a quiet time.** Depending on the crowd, plan an afternoon or several shorter stages. Books take more time than socks because you should pick each one up briefly.
2. **Gather all the books.** From the living room, bedroom, hallway, basement, boxes from the last move and from your bag.
3. **Sort roughly by category.** Place piles according to the groups from the next section.
4. **Provide boxes.** One for “pass on”, one for “sell” and one for “waste paper”.
5. **Leave memory books for now.** Leave books with dedications from deceased people, old children's books or inherited volumes at the very end.

It is important not to read for hours. Browsing is allowed, reading chapters is not. If a book grabs you so much that you want to read it immediately, the decision has been made: it stays and goes on the bedside table.

## The categories at a glance

Each group of books needs slightly different questions. The following table will help you make a targeted decision.

| Category | Key question | Frequent decision |
|---|---|---|
| Read and loved | Would I read it again or lend it? | stays, especially when you open it again |
| Read but indifferent | Do I even remember the content? | passes on for someone else to read |
| Unread | Would I buy it today? | an honest examination, more on that below |
| Reference books and guides | Is the knowledge still up to date, can I find it quicker? | Outdated editions go, few good ones remain |
| Specialist books for the profession | Do I need it for my work today? | keep what you use, leave the rest to colleagues or offspring |
| Gifts | Do I like the book itself, regardless of who gave it to me? | remains, if so, otherwise pass it on with gratitude in your heart |
| Memory books | Does it remind me of a person or a time that is important to me? | a few selected volumes remain |

Many adults also have old school books and study materials. Most of the time they are outdated in terms of content and can go unless you are actually still working with them.

The same cautious rules apply to books with strong feelings, such as those from your deceased grandfather or your own childhood, as to other keepsakes. You can find out more about this in [Clearing out memorabilia](/ratgeber/erinnerungsstuecke-ausmisten/).

## Questions for each individual book

Hold each book in your hand for a moment. If you are unsure about a category, these questions will help:

1. **Have I opened it in the last few years?** Not read it, just opened it. If not, why not?
2. **Would I recommend it to a friend?** If you're hesitant, it's probably not that important to you.
3. **Would I buy or rent it again?** A resounding no is a resounding answer.
4. **Is it hard to get back?** You're more likely to keep out-of-print copies or rare books than a bestseller that every library carries.
5. **Is the edition itself important to me?** A beautiful old edition, a personal dedication or notes in the margins can have special value.
6. **Do I keep it for myself or for others?** If you're just keeping it because it looks good on the shelf or because you just have the book, it's fine.

An example: Petra comes across a long book about garden design from the time when she still had a garden. Today she lives on the third floor with a balcony. She browses, enjoys some of the pictures and then puts it in the box for a neighbor who has just bought a house with a garden.

## An honest rule for the unread pile

Unread books are often the hardest part because they represent plans. Instead of keeping them all or giving them all away, a clear rule in three parts helps.

- **The store question:** Imagine the book is in front of you in a bookstore today. Would you buy it? If not, it's fine.
- **The Page Test:** For any book you're unsure about, read the first few pages. If it grabs you, it gets a place in the reading pile. If it leaves you cold, that's an honest answer.
- **The space limit:** The unread ones get their own shelf or basket. Whatever doesn't fit in there goes on. If a new book is added, one must be read or passed on first.

This rule takes away the pressure of having to read everything at some point. A manageable reading pile is more inviting to read than a wall full of expectations.

## Where to put the discarded books?

The boxes shouldn't sit in the hallway for months. When sorting, decide which path is best for which book and set yourself a deadline.

| way | Suitable for | Pay attention to this |
|---|---|---|
| Friends, family, neighborhood | Books that make you think of someone specific | Ask specifically, don't hand out boxes without asking |
| Public bookcase | well-preserved novels, crime novels, children's books, current non-fiction books | only a few books at a time, no outdated encyclopedias or damaged volumes |
| Sale | rare, sought-after or very well-preserved titles | Purchasing portals on the Internet, antique shops or flea markets; Often low returns for mass-produced goods |
| Donate | well-preserved books in large quantities | ask beforehand, because not every library, every social department store and every institution accepts books |
| Waste paper | damaged, dirty or outdated books | Follow the rules of your municipality and, if in doubt, ask the waste advice center |

There are public bookcases in many cities and towns. They only work if everyone treats them with care. So only put in what you would like to take with you. Colleagues, training companies or clubs can sometimes use specialist books. Ask here beforehand too.

If you want to sell, be realistic: most books bring in little money. Selling is particularly worthwhile for rare titles. Anything that has not found a buyer after a few weeks is given away or donated. You can find general tips for all sorted out things in [Start clearing out](/ratgeber/ausmisten-anfangen/).

## Arrange the remaining shelf sensibly

If there are only books left that mean something to you, the shelf can also show that they are important to you.

- **Favorite books at eye level.** What you pick up often is where you can easily reach it.
- **Choose a clear order.** By topic or genre, within which alphabetically by author, is most practical for many. Organizing by color looks nice, but makes it harder to find.
- **Dedicated compartment for unread readers.** So you can always see what's next.
- **No double rows.** You'll forget what's at the back. It's better to have one row less.
- **Let it breathe.** A little free space at the end of each row allows new books to be added without everything tipping over immediately. A bookend keeps gaps neat.
- **Display a few books.** An illustrated book, placed frontally, can look like a picture.

## Library and borrowing instead of buying new

So that the shelf doesn't overflow again, it's worth taking a look at the question: Do I have to own this book or do I just want to read it? For many things, reading is enough.

- **Library Card:** Public libraries often offer a large selection of novels, non-fiction books, and magazines. Conditions and fees vary from place to place, check with your library.
- **Reserve and Suggest:** Many libraries allow you to reserve books you have borrowed and sometimes suggest purchases.
- **Borrow eBooks:** Many libraries also lend digital books that you download at home.
- **Exchange among friends:** Pass on a book after reading it and get another one back. You can find more ideas in [Borrow, share, exchange](/ratgeber/leihen-teilen-tauschen/).
- **One in, one out:** If you buy a new book that you want to stay, you check which one can go with it.

Of course, if you buy a book that inspires you, it can still stay. The goal is not to own as little as possible, but rather to have a shelf that suits you. [Minimalism without dogma](/ratgeber/minimalismus-ohne-dogma/) explains how you can find your own measure.

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

- **Read while sorting:** Browse yes, read no. Otherwise you can finish three books in one afternoon.
- **Start with the Memory Books:** Start with the easy categories to get practice.
- **Put everything in the bookcase:** Damaged and outdated books do not belong there.
- **Storage boxes:** Take them away within a few days, otherwise books will go back on the shelf.
- **Declutter by the numbers:** A fixed number of books is not a sensible goal. What matters is that you like the shelf.

## Frequently asked questions

### What about books that belong to me and my partner?

Each person decides on their own books. If you bought volumes together, you sit down together and go through them together. It can be helpful to give each person their own shelf area that they can decide on alone.

### Is it worth replacing books with e-books?

For novels that you only read once, a reading device can save space if you like reading digitally. Many people prefer to keep books with pictures, notes or personal meaning on paper. Keep in mind that purchased e-books are often tied to a specific account.

## Briefly summarized

Books are emotionally charged because they represent education, memories and plans. Gather all the books, sort them into categories and ask yourself honest questions for each book. For the unread, the store question, page test and a fixed place help. Pass on discarded books in a targeted manner, ask before donating and only bring volumes that are in good condition into public bookcases. Arrange the remaining shelves so that favorite books are visible and borrow more often instead of buying. Then you will only have what really means something to you on your shelf.
