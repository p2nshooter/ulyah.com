It's just after three in the afternoon. There are four items left on your list, none of them particularly difficult. Still, everyone feels like a mountain. You read the same email for the third time, grab a coffee, come back and don't know what you wanted to do. In the evening you fall on the sofa and ask yourself why you are so exhausted even though you haven't accomplished that much.

Often this is not due to too little time, but rather due to planning that only the clock knows. An hour in the morning is not the same as an hour in the late afternoon, and one difficult conversation takes more energy than ten transfers. This guide shows you how to get to know your own energy curve, recognize energy thieves and sources of strength, distribute tasks appropriately and plan recovery so that it really works.

## Energy instead of time as a planning factor

If you only plan in time, you treat every hour the same. The calendar says: 2 p.m. to 4 p.m. is free, so the report fits in. Your mind may see things completely differently at 2 p.m. Energy is more than physical strength. It's also about concentration, patience with other people and the ability to make decisions.

A helpful image is a battery that discharges throughout the day and recharges through breaks, sleep and good moments. Some activities draw a lot of electricity, others a little, and some even charge. If you know when your battery is full and what drains it, you can plan your day so that there is still something left in the evening.

## Observe your own daily curve

People are different. Some people are clear-headed straight away in the morning, others don't really get going until midday. Many people are familiar with a low in the early afternoon. Instead of following general advice, it's worth taking an honest look at your own progress.

### This is how you do it

1. **Choose fixed measuring points.** For example, after getting up, in the late morning, after the lunch break, in the late afternoon and in the evening.
2. **Rate your energy on a scale of 1 to 5.** 1 means: hardly any strength left. 5 means: awake, clear, full of energy.
3. **Write down in keywords what you did beforehand.** So meeting, commuting, housework, a walk, screen work.
4. **Hold on, for one to two weeks.** A single day says little, the pattern only becomes apparent over several days, if possible over a weekend.

An observation sheet can look like this:

| Time | Energy (1–5) | What came before | Remark |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7:00 | 2 | got up, slept briefly | need start-up time |
| 10:30 | 5 | Concentrated work for two hours | best moment of the day |
| 13:30 | 2 | Lunch, team meeting | hard to get back in |
| 16:30 | 3 | a short walk into the fresh air | a little better than expected |
| 20:00 | 2 | Shopping, cooking | only enough for quiet things |

At the end of the observation, mark your high phases, your lows and the times in between. Also pay attention to differences between days of the week. The Monday after a full weekend often feels different than the Wednesday.

## List energy robbers and sources of power

In addition to the time of day, the most important thing is what you fill your hours with. Write two lists: What takes energy away from you and what gives you energy back? Be honest and specific. What is relaxing for others may be stressful for you. An evening with many people charges one person and drains another.

| area | Common energy thieves | Possible sources of strength |
|---|---|---|
| work | constant interruptions, unclear tasks, meeting after meeting | a completed work step, clear agreements, a block without disruptions |
| head | Unpleasant tasks postponed, lots of small decisions | a list completed, a decision made |
| people | unresolved conflicts, conversations in which you pretend | intimate conversations, laughing together, time alone when you need it |
| Environment | Noise, clutter in the field of vision, bad light | Daylight, fresh air, a quiet place |
| body | Sitting for long periods of time, rushing between appointments | Movement that is good for you, a moment to breathe deeply |
| Digital | constant messages, endless scrolling | consciously chosen music, a phone call with a loved one |

Choose one point from both lists where you want to change something in the next week. Make an energy robber smaller, install a power source more often. An example: Sabine notices that the unopened letters on the kitchen table bother her every evening. She sets a fixed date for the post. The pile disappears, and with it a constant, quiet unease.

## Place demanding tasks in the peak phases

If you know your curve, you can distribute your tasks accordingly. To do this, it helps to roughly sort activities according to their energy requirements.

- **Thinking work:** Writing texts, calculating, planning, solving problems, learning something new.
- **People work:** important conversations, negotiations, advice, difficult phone calls.
- **Routine:** Filing, transfers, short answers, orders, coordinating appointments.
- **Physical:** Shopping, housework, doing commutes.

### This is how you distribute your tasks

1. **Protect your best phase.** Place the task that needs the most focus during your peak phase. Turn off notifications and don't check your inbox during this time.
2. **Bundle small things.** You can handle callbacks, transfers and short emails in one block, ideally in a middle phase. This way they don't fray all day long.
3. **Use low times for routine or exercise.** In the afternoon low, thinking is difficult, but simple tasks or a trip to the post office work well.
4. **Plan for transitions.** Between two stressful appointments, you need a few minutes to get back to yourself.

Not everyone can organize their day freely. Those who work in shift work, sales or nursing often have fixed procedures. But even then there is leeway: the order of your tasks, the preparation the day before or the question of what you can do after work and what can wait until the day off. The guide [Plan your day realistically](/ratgeber/tag-realistisch-planen/) explains how you can build a feasible plan from all of this.

## Plan recovery consciously

Many people only realize that they need a break when nothing works anymore. However, recovery works better if it comes earlier and matches the type of tiredness. Someone who has been thinking all day needs something different than someone who has been on their feet all day.

| type of exhaustion | Which often helps |
|---|---|
| Head full, thoughts circling | a walk without headphones, handwork, doing something with your hands |
| Physically tired | Sitting, lying down, quiet music, finishing work early |
| Too many people, too many conversations | Time alone, silence, a book |
| Too much screen, too many stimuli | Daylight, nature, a room without noise |
| Listless and empty | something that brings you joy: a hobby, music, meeting a familiar person |

Pay attention to what really refreshes you and what just feels like it. For many, scrolling on the sofa is more of an anesthesia than a break. Afterwards they are often just as tired as before. Instead, consciously try different breaks for a week and see how you feel afterwards. You can find concrete ideas for short breaks in the guide [Breaks in Everyday Life](/ratgeber/pausen-im-alltag/).

The most important recovery is sleep. If you consistently sleep too little or restlessly, you start every day with half the battery. How to strengthen your nights is found in [Improve Sleep Habits](/ratgeber/schlafgewohnheiten-verbessern/).

## Compensate for weeks with peak loads

Some weeks are simply full: a project completion, a move with friends, a visit, an exam, a doctor's appointment that keeps you busy. Such peaks cannot always be avoided. But you can prepare and balance them.

1. **Look ahead.** As you look into the new week, mark the days that will require a lot of energy.
2. **Relieve stress beforehand.** Do what can be done in the days beforehand. For example, cook ahead on the weekend so that you don't have to make any decisions on busy evenings.
3. **Delete volunteer.** You don't have to do the major cleaning, the tax return and the birthday cake in a full week. Postpone what can wait and cancel where you can. How to do this in a friendly manner can be found in [Learning to say no](/ratgeber/nein-sagen-lernen/).
4. **Reduce routines to the minimum version.** Instead of the entire evening routine, three steps are enough in weeks like this.
5. **Plan recovery afterwards.** Consciously set aside a quiet evening or a free morning for the days after the peak before the next appointments fill it up.

Also, avoid having two busy days back to back if you have the choice. A quiet day in between often has more effect than a long weekend afterwards.

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

- **Fill the peak phase with little things:** If you empty your mailbox first in the morning, you use up your best time on tasks that also happen later.
- **Don't take breaks until you can't anymore:** Then you'll need significantly longer to recover. Plan them in advance.
- **Just look at work:** Housework, family organization and worries also take energy. They belong in your bill.
- **Comparing yourself with others:** The fact that a colleague goes to exercise in the evening says nothing about what your battery is capable of.
- **Plan each day the same:** If you know your Monday is hard, put less into it than you did on Wednesday.

## If the exhaustion remains

Being tired after a full day is normal. It's different if you feel exhausted for weeks even though you sleep enough and allow yourself to rest, if your performance noticeably declines or if you experience depression, sleep problems or physical complaints. Then this is not a case for better planning, but rather one for your family doctor's practice. There can be physical or mental causes behind persistent exhaustion, which can be easily clarified and often treated. You can find anyone who can help you under [Help & Contact Points](/hilfe-und-anlaufstellen/).

## Frequently asked questions

### I'm tired all day long. Does observation still help?

Yes, because even small differences show you where you can start. If there are no ups and downs for over two weeks and you feel constantly exhausted, talk to your family doctor about it.

### What do I do when my peak period is during working hours and there is nothing left for private projects?

Then it's worth taking a look at the weekend or days off. Consciously plan your most important private project during your best hours instead of squeezing it into your tired evenings.

## Briefly summarized

Don't just plan with the clock, but with your strength. Observe your daily curve for one to two weeks, list energy robbers and sources of strength and change one point at a time. Put demanding tasks in your high phases, bundle up small things and use low times for routine. Plan rest that suits the type of fatigue you have, and balance full weeks of preparation with rest days afterwards. And if the exhaustion persists despite everything, get it checked out by a doctor.
