It's half past nine in the evening and you've actually been busy all day. You answered twenty emails, unloaded the dishwasher, took a package to the post office, read three parent group messages, and helped a colleague with her spreadsheet. Nevertheless, a stale feeling remains: the call to the health insurance company, the application, the meeting with the old friend you've been wanting to see for months, all of that has been left behind. A lot done, nothing important accomplished.

This is rarely due to laziness. A to-do list almost always grows faster than you can complete it. Setting priorities therefore does not mean getting everything done, but rather deciding what comes first and what can consciously wait. This guide will show you how to do this in everyday life.

## Why a full day can feel empty

Urgent things announce themselves loudly. The phone rings, a message flashes, someone is in the door. Important things, on the other hand, wait quietly: the check-up doesn't complain if you postpone it for another year, and the friendship slowly fades away without there being a deadline. In addition, small tasks provide a quick feeling of success. Ten ticks for small things feels more productive than an hour on a difficult thing that isn't finished yet.

If you don't set priorities, you leave the decision to what is loudest. The aim of this guide is to make the quiet, important things audible again.

## Distinguish between urgent and important

The two terms are often confused, but they mean different things:

- **Urgent** is something that has a deadline or someone is waiting for you. Urgency says something about when.
- **Important** is something that has long-term consequences and has to do with your goals, your commitments, or the people you care about. Importance says something about why.

Combined, the two questions create a four-field grid known as the Eisenhower Matrix. It is not a rigid system, but rather an aid to thinking about your own list with different eyes.

| field | feature | Examples from everyday life | What you do |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: important and urgent | real deadline, tangible consequences | Report to the property management about water damage, an application with a deadline, pick up the sick child from daycare | do it yourself immediately |
| B: important, not urgent | has a long-term effect, no one pushes you | Arrange a preventive appointment, prepare your application, maintain friendships, organize your tax documents before you rush out | reserve a fixed date for it |
| C: urgent, not important | someone is waiting, but it doesn't get you anywhere | lots of calls and chat messages, spontaneous requests, a discount campaign that ends today | keep it short, bundle it, give it away or reject it |
| D: neither important nor urgent | fills time without nourishing | scrolling aimlessly, reading advertising newsletters, comparing products for the third time | delete it or consciously enjoy it as leisure time |

Most people oscillate between field A and field C, i.e. between real emergencies and strangers' urgent requests. The real goal is to spend more time in field B. If you make the workshop appointment for your winter tires in October, you don't have to make hectic calls at the first snow. Field B is precautionary measures instead of fire services.

### This is how you sort your list in fifteen minutes

1. Write down all open tasks on a piece of paper, even the small and unpleasant ones.
2. For each task, ask the question of importance: “What happens if I haven’t done this in a month?” If the answer is “nothing in particular,” it probably isn’t important.
3. Ask the question of urgency: “Who is waiting for this, and until when?” Many perceived deadlines are not real ones. A short message is often enough to clarify whether something has to happen today or whether it will suffice at the end of the week.
4. Write a letter from A to D after each task.
5. Give each B task a specific date in the calendar. Otherwise, without an appointment, she'll keep slipping back.

## Your values and areas of life as a compass

The Matrix has a weakness: it doesn't say what's important to you. For this you need your own compass. A simple path leads through your areas of life.

1. **Name areas.** Write down five to seven areas that make up your life, for example health and relaxation, relationships and family, friendships, work, home and finances, your own interests, commitment.
2. **Formulate one sentence per area.** What should be good there in a year? “I see my closest friends regularly, even if we live in different cities.” “My documents are organized so that mail no longer scares me.”
3. **Honestly assess.** Is the area currently receiving too little, enough or too much attention?
4. **Measure the list against this.** What task serves an area that is being neglected? It's moving up.

The following applies: priorities can shift with the phases of life. When moving, having a newborn child or caring for a relative, other areas inevitably take a back seat. This is not negligence, but a conscious decision for a certain time. It's helpful to name it like this: "The move has priority until the summer, after which I'll take care of the sports club again."

## One main thing per day, few focal points per week

The more things that are prioritized at the same time, the less the word means. A simple countermeasure is the **main thing of the day**: the one task where you can say in the evening that the day counted, even if little else went well.

A good main thing meets three conditions:

- It is important, so it comes from field A or B.
- It can be done in one day or formulated as a clear sub-step. Not “tax”, but “pick out receipts for tradesmen’s invoices and put them in the folder”.
- It's in your hands and doesn't depend on someone else calling back.

Choose it the night before or first thing in the morning, and if possible, give it a time before the inbox and messages take over the day. Anything that happens after that is a win. [Plan the day realistically](/ratgeber/tag-realistisch-planen/) explains how you can then incorporate the main thing into a manageable day with appointments and small things.

At the weekly level, the principle works the same way, just with slightly larger projects. In the [Weekly Planning](/ratgeber/wochenplanung-und-wochenrueckblick/) you set a few priorities, and the main things for each day are simply the next steps there. This way you don't work on something different every day, but rather make progress on the things that are really important to you.

## The don't-do list: consciously leave it out

Setting priorities sounds like choosing. In reality, it is mostly omission. A don't-do list makes these decisions visible so that they don't continue to accompany you as a guilty conscience. There are four ways to get rid of a task:

| Art | Key question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Strike out | Does this serve any area of my life? | Sew the curtains yourself, even though the ones you bought are still good |
| Parking | Is that nice, but not right now? | Put the Spanish course on a later list with a rewatch date |
| Lowering | Is “good enough” enough here? | Buy the cake for the college from the bakery instead of baking it yourself |
| Submit | Can someone else do it just as well? | Organize the birthday party together with your siblings instead of alone |

In addition to individual tasks, the list can also include principles that protect you from new obligations. “I don’t read work emails after 8 p.m.” “I won’t take on any new volunteer positions until the club festival is over.” “For purchases under a certain amount, I don’t compare more than two offers.”

The difference to just leaving it lying there is crucial: a task that you have forgotten is gnawing at you. A task that you consciously canceled or parked is finished, at least for now.

## When priorities collide: work, family, friends

Your priorities do not exist in a vacuum. Other people have their own, and they don't always match yours. Instead of quietly taking on everything, it helps to negotiate conflicts openly.

- **At work:** If a new task comes up even though your table is full, ask about the order instead of just saying yes. "I'm happy to take over. Which of these should I push back?" This makes it clear that time is finite and the decision lies with the person who has to make it.
- **In partnerships and families:** Sit down together briefly and each person says what is most important to them in the coming time. You can often alternate: “This week your exam preparation has priority, next week my project.”
- **With friends:** Honesty usually works better than constant cancellations at the last minute. “I can’t manage long evenings right now, but a walk on Sunday morning works well.”

If you generally find it difficult to refuse, you can find formulations and exercises in [Learn to say no](/ratgeber/nein-sagen-lernen/).

## Readjust regularly

Priorities are not a one-time decision. Schedule specific moments to check them:

- **Daily, two minutes:** Is the main thing chosen for tomorrow?
- **Weekly:** Which areas of life have been neglected this week? Which B tasks have been given an appointment?
- **Approximately quarterly:** Repeat the Life Areas exercise and review the Later list. Some things can be done now, others can be deleted permanently.

Also pay attention to warning signs. If you've been putting off the same task for weeks, it either doesn't have a deadline, is worded too big, or is actually not important to you. Then there are three honest answers: plan, reduce or delete. And if you find that you are constantly exhausted despite having good priorities, the problem may not be with your choices, but with your strengths. More on this in [Divide energy](/ratgeber/energie-einteilen/).

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

- **Everything is a priority:** If ten things are at the top, nothing is at the top. Limit yourself to one main thing per day.
- **Little things first, “to clear your head”:** Afterwards your head is usually full and your energy is gone. Start with the main thing.
- **Only work counts:** Relaxation, relationships and health are not leftovers. Give them appointments just like you would a meeting.
- **Priorities only in your head:** What is not written down is pushed aside by the next loud concern.
- **Never check:** What was right six months ago may be wrong today.

## Frequently asked questions

### What do I do if everything is really urgent?

For each task, ask whether the deadline is real and who set it. If there are still too many real emergencies, this is a sign that you don't have enough time in field B or that you have too much responsibility on your shoulders. Both cannot be solved in one day, but they can be addressed, for example in a conversation with a manager or with the family.

### Is it selfish to pursue your own priorities?

No. If you never set your own priorities, you live according to the priorities of others and at some point you have less energy for them too. It's fair to state your priorities openly and take those of others seriously as well.

### How many areas of life can have priority at the same time?

More like one or two for a given time. The others continue to run at a basic level in this phase. This feels unusual at first, but it creates exactly the peace that is lost when trying to progress everywhere at once.

## Briefly summarized

Setting priorities means deciding what comes first and what can consciously wait. Separate urgent from important, give the important but quiet tasks fixed dates and measure your list against the areas of life that mean something to you. Choose one main thing each day, keep a do-not-do list of tasks that have been crossed out, parked, lowered, and turned in, and handle conflicts openly. Check your priorities regularly because they are allowed to change with your life.
