When clearing out, there are things that are easy: the sweater that scratches, the third cutting board, the cables from devices that have long since been thrown away. And there are things that suddenly make you stop. The children's drawings. Grandmother's letters. The concert ticket from the first date. The teddy bear that was in the basement for thirty years. Memorabilia carry stories, people and feelings within them, which is why every decision about them feels more difficult than about an everyday object.

This guide will show you how to clear out memorabilia without feeling guilty and without giving away something you'll miss later. It's not about getting rid of the past, but about giving it a worthy, manageable place.

## Why memorabilia is so hard to let go of

An object is not the memory itself. It is an anchor that awakens it. When we give away such an item, it feels like we are betraying the person, the moment, or a part of ourselves. There are often other feelings too:

- **Guilt:** “Aunt Inge gave this to me, I can’t give it away.”
- **Fear of Forgetting:** “If I throw this away, I’ll never remember it again.”
- **Feeling of duty:** “My mother already kept this, so I have to too.”
- **Hope:** “Maybe my kids will want it someday.”

All of these feelings are understandable. It is helpful to name them. Often, some of the tension is released as soon as you admit to yourself that you are keeping an item not out of love, but out of guilt.

## The most important rule: memorabilia comes last

Anyone who starts with photo albums almost always fails. When decluttering, start with things that are emotionally neutral: clothes, books, kitchen, bathroom. In doing so, you train your decision-making skills. You learn to feel what is important to you and get used to the feeling of letting go. When you get to the memorabilia afterwards, you'll be more practiced and calmer. You can find a timetable for the correct order in the guide [Start clearing out] (/ratgeber/ausmisten-anfangen/).

## Preparation: time, place and accompaniment

Allow more time for memorabilia than any other category. It's completely normal to get stuck on old letters, read them, laugh or cry. That's part of it.

- **Choose quiet days.** Not under time pressure, not in the evening after a hard day at work.
- **Work in small portions.** One box per session is enough.
- **Get companionship when you need it.** A friend can listen, share stories, and encourage you. Choose someone who won't push you.
- **Respect other people's memories.** Things that belong to other family members or mean something to them are sorted together or returned.

## Step by step through the memory boxes

### 1. Collect everything in one place

Memorabilia is often scattered all over the apartment: in the basement, in the attic, in drawers, in parents' closets. First collect them in one place. It's only when you see everything that you realize how much it is and can decide what the truly special pieces are.

### 2. Sort into groups

Divide the collection into meaningful groups, for example:

| group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Photos | Prints, albums, slides, negatives |
| Letters and cards | Love letters, birthday cards, postcards |
| Children's time | Drawings, certificates, crafts, stuffed animals |
| Heirlooms | Jewelry, dishes, furniture, watches |
| Travel and events | Tickets, programs, souvenirs |
| Documents with emotional value | Diaries, certificates, newspaper clippings |

### 3. Choose the best pieces first

Instead of asking, “What can go?”, ask, “What’s best?” In each group, pick out the pieces that resonate with you the most. Out of fifty children's drawings there may be five that tell a story. Out of a hundred birthday cards, these are the ones with personal words. When you consciously choose the best, the rest suddenly seems less important.

### 4. Ask good questions

For pieces you are unsure about, these questions will help:

- Do I remember this moment even without the object?
- Do I feel happy when I pick up the piece or do I feel heavy?
- Would I miss it if it disappeared today?
- Do I keep it for myself or for my guilty conscience?
- Is there anyone who would be more excited about this than me?

### 5. Set a fixed place

Set a limit for your memories, such as one nice box per person or a specific shelf space. The limit is not a punishment, but a framework. She ensures that every piece in it is truly special. A memento that collects dust in a disorganized box in the basement is not honored, but forgotten.

## Ideas for preserving memories without keeping everything

Letting go doesn't mean the memory disappears. There are many ways to preserve them in a smaller form:

- **Photography:** Take a nice photo of crafts, souvenirs, or large items before giving them away. When collected in a photo book, they hardly take up any space.
- **Digitize:** Letters, drawings and certificates can be scanned. The guide [Digitizing Documents](/ratgeber/dokumente-digitalisieren/) describes how to do this.
- **A piece representative:** Keep a single cup from a large service, a favorite piece from your grandfather's collection.
- **Use instead of hiding:** Beautiful dishes from the heritage belong on the table, not in the box. A piece you use reminds you daily.
- **Write down stories:** Write a few sentences about special pieces: Where does it come from, who owned it, what is the story behind it? For later generations, this story is often more valuable than the object itself.
- **Pass it on:** Ask a sibling, cousin, or friend if they would like a piece. Some things find a better place in a museum, a local history association or an archive.

## Special cases

### Children's drawings and crafts

Children produce endless amounts, and every leaf seems valuable. A proven solution is one folder per child per school year. At the end of the year you choose the most beautiful pieces together. Older children like to make decisions for themselves; this strengthens their sense of what is important to them.

### Photos

Photos are the largest category. Start by eliminating duplicate, blurry, blurry, and meaningless images. You can then organize the best prints into albums or digitize them. Special rules apply to cell phone photos, which are described in the guide [Organize digital photos] (/ratgeber/digitale-fotos-ordnen/).

### Heirlooms after a death

After the death of someone close to you, it is not the right time to make quick decisions. Give yourself time. If an apartment needs to be cleared, it can help to first pack everything with personal meaning into a few boxes and only sort it out in peace months later. Clarify with the other heirs beforehand who is entitled to what.

### Gifts

A gift did its job when it was given: it expressed affection. You don't have to keep it forever to honor that affection. This is especially true for gifts that you never use.

## What happens to the things that are allowed to go

Gently letting go is easier when things are going well:

- You can give away, donate or sell items that are in good condition.
- Books, toys and clothing are happy to find new owners in social department stores.
- Personal letters and documents with data should be shredded, not in waste paper.
- You can offer photos of other people to the people depicted or their families.

## Typical mistakes

**Start with the memorabilia.** The hardest part belongs at the end.

**Too much at once.** Emotional work tires more quickly than physical work.

**Keep for others without asking.** Ask your children or siblings if they really want the items. Often the answer is surprising.

**Throw everything away in order to finally be finished.** Those who radically clear out in a hurry often regret it. Take your time.

## A little ritual for saying goodbye

For some people it helps to consciously let go. It doesn't have to be anything big. Pick up the piece again, remember the story behind it and say thank you internally or out loud. This may sound unusual, but it changes the feeling: you don't throw anything away, you say goodbye. If you like, you can write the memory in a notebook, which over time becomes a small family chronicle.

If you have items belonging to the deceased, an evening together with the family can help. Everyone tells a story about a piece, and at the end everyone gets to choose something. This is how a difficult task becomes a moment of connection.

## If you get stuck

There are phases in which letting go is simply not possible. That's okay. Grief doesn't follow a timetable, and no one has to sort through memories according to a guide. However, if storing or clearing out causes you a lot of stress over the long term, if rooms are no longer usable or you are ashamed of the amount of things, it can be helpful to talk to a doctor or an advice center. Nobody has to be ashamed of that.

## Frequently asked questions

### What if I give something away and regret it later?

This can happen, but it's rarer than you think. In cases of real doubt, a “maybe box” with a date can help. If you haven't opened it for a year, the decision is usually easier.

### How do I deal with memories of difficult times?

Some things remind us of separations, illnesses or losses. You don't have to keep them just because they're part of your story. If it helps, you can consciously say goodbye to them, perhaps with a photo or a few written words.

### How much space should memories get?

There is no correct number. The important thing is that space is limited and that you enjoy looking at the pieces in it. A box that you enjoy opening is worth more than ten that you are afraid of.
