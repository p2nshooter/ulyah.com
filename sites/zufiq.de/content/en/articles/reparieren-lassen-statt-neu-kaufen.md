The zipper on your favorite jacket is stuck, the vacuum cleaner only sucks half-heartedly, and one of the legs on the kitchen chair is loose. The first impulse is often: get rid of it, a new one is needed. Buying new is convenient, the selection is huge and the price is sometimes surprisingly low. And yet there is still an uneasy feeling when an almost functional thing ends up in the trash just because a single small part no longer works.

Repairing is not a hobby for tinkerers, but rather a very practical decision. It often saves money, conserves resources and extends the life of things you actually like. At the same time, not every repair is worth it. This guide will help you honestly consider: When is the repair worth it, what can you do yourself, who can help you, and what rights do you have against dealers and manufacturers?

## Why repairs are part of everyday life again

It used to be normal to take shoes to the cobbler, darn socks and carry the radio to the specialist retailer around the corner. With new goods becoming increasingly cheaper, this habit has been lost in many places. The tide is now changing again: repair cafés have sprung up in many cities and towns, the European Union has adopted rules for more repair-friendly products, and many people are simply fed up with buying the same things every few years.

Repairing has three tangible benefits for you personally:

- **You save money.** A new heel costs a fraction of a new pair of shoes, a replacement battery costs a fraction of a new device.
- **You keep what you like.** The worn-in shoes, the jacket with the perfect fit, the armchair you love to read in. A new purchase is rarely exactly as good.
- **You buy less often.** Every successful repair is one less purchase, one less delivery, one less box in the waste paper.

There is also a quiet but noticeable effect: Anyone who has experienced a few times that something can be repaired will take a closer look the next time they buy. The guide [Identify quality](/ratgeber/qualitaet-erkennen/) shows you what you can pay attention to.

## Is the repair worth it? The honest calculation

Not every repair makes sense. A rough guide: If the costs are significantly less than what an equivalent replacement product would cost and the thing is otherwise in good condition, the repair is almost always worth it. If things get tight, these questions will help you:

1. **How old is the product and how long is it expected to last after repair?** A twelve-year-old washing machine with a rusty drum is a different case than a three-year-old machine with a defective pump.
2. **Is only one part broken or several?** A single defect can usually be easily repaired. If the problems pile up, the end is often in sight.
3. **Would you get an equivalent product again?** Older appliances, furniture and clothing are sometimes built more robustly than today's cheap versions. Then a more expensive repair is worthwhile.
4. **Are there still spare parts?** Without parts, every repair becomes a gamble. A quick look at the manufacturer's website or an inquiry to a workshop will quickly clarify this.
5. **Are you attached to this thing?** Memorability is a legitimate reason. Your grandmother's watch doesn't have to count.

As a rule of thumb, many workshops say: If the repair costs are less than half the new price of a comparable product, repairs are usually the better choice. This isn't a hard limit, but it's a good starting point for your decision.

## Which is typically worth it

| subject | Common defect | Repair usually makes sense? |
|---|---|---|
| Leather shoes | expired sole or heel | Yes, almost always if the basic quality is good |
| Jacket, pants | broken zipper, tear, button | Yes, especially with a good fit |
| Bicycle | Brakes, chain, hose, lights | Yes, almost always |
| Wooden furniture | loose connections, scratches | Yes, often with little effort |
| Upholstered furniture | saggy upholstery, cover | Weigh it up, yes with a high-quality frame |
| Smartphone | Battery, display | Usually yes, if the device is not very old |
| Laptop | Battery, keyboard, fan | Often yes, upgrading RAM or SSD extends life even further |
| Washing machine | Pump, door seal, carbon brushes | Weigh up based on age and condition |
| Fully automatic coffee machine | Calcification, brewing unit, seals | Often yes, maintenance prevents things |
| Cheap small appliances | various | Often difficult, ask Repair-Café |

The table does not replace a case-by-case assessment, but it does show a pattern: something that is solidly built and consists of replaceable parts can be easily repaired. Anything that is glued, riveted or firmly welded becomes more difficult.

## Clothing and shoes: small interventions, big impact

When it comes to clothing, a needle and thread are often enough. You can learn to sew on a button, close an unraveled seam or re-pin a hem in just a few minutes; numerous instructions show the basic stitches. For anything that goes beyond that, tailoring shops are the right place to go: new zippers, patches on trousers, shortening sleeves or mending linings. The costs are usually manageable and bear no relation to a new purchase.

Holes in sweaters can be mended or deliberately emphasized with a visible patch. This technique has gained real fans in recent years because it turns a repair into a detail that gives the garment character. When you [clean out your closet](/ratgeber/kleiderschrank-ausmisten/) and find pieces that only need a little something, put them in their own basket and bring them together to the tailor shop.

When it comes to shoes, the better the basic quality, the more worthwhile the cobbler is. Welted shoes can be resoled several times, glued shoes can at least be given new heels and sole protection. Don't wait until the heel has worn down to the wood or plastic, as the repair will then be more complex and expensive.

## Furniture: often easier than expected

Many furniture problems are smaller than they look. A wobbling chair often just has a loose screw or dry glue in the connection. Tightening screws, wood glue and a screw clamp overnight solve many cases. Scratches in wooden surfaces disappear with wax putty, retouching pens or a little furniture oil. Stuck drawers often run again if you clean the running surfaces and rub them with a little candle wax.

In the event of major damage, carpentry shops, upholstery workshops or restorers can help. This is almost always worthwhile, especially with high-quality old furniture made of solid wood. A newly upholstered armchair with a sturdy frame often lasts longer than a new one made of chipboard.

## Electrical appliances: first check, then decide

Electronics are the category that many people give up on the quickest. The fault often lies in surprisingly simple places: a clogged pump, a worn V-belt, a defective switch or an exhausted battery. Before you abandon a device, go through these steps:

1. **Check operating instructions and error code.** Many devices display error codes, the meaning of which is stated in the instructions or on the manufacturer's website.
2. **Exclude simple causes.** Clean sieves, filters and lint traps, descale, check fuse, inspect cables for damage.
3. **Clarify warranty and guarantee.** If the device is less than two years old, the dealer is responsible first. More on this in the next section.
4. **Get a cost estimate.** Ask whether the cost of the estimate will be credited to the order.
5. **Repair café or specialist workshop.** Volunteer help is often enough for simple errors, but a specialist workshop is the better choice for large appliances.

**Important:** Do not open devices that use mains voltage if you are not familiar with them. Microwaves, power supplies and some large appliances can still store dangerous voltages even when unplugged. Work on gas appliances should only be carried out by specialists. Safety always comes before savings here.

## Your rights: warranty, guarantee and new EU rules

Before you pay yourself, it's worth taking a look at your rights. In Germany, the statutory warranty of two years applies when purchasing new goods from a dealer. If a defect occurs during this time that the product already had when you purchased it, you can request subsequent performance, i.e. repair or replacement. In the first year after purchase, it will be assumed in your favor that the defect was already there when it was handed over. After that, if in doubt, you have to explain it yourself.

The guarantee is different from this. It is a voluntary promise by the manufacturer or dealer, the conditions of which are stated in the guarantee certificate. Some guarantees last significantly longer than two years and are a real advantage if you keep an eye on them. Therefore, keep purchase receipts organized as described in the [Save documents] (/ratgeber/unterlagen-aufbewahren/) guide.

A lot has happened at the European level in recent years. For certain groups of devices, such as washing machines, dishwashers or refrigerators, the eco-design rules require that manufacturers keep important spare parts available for many years. Since 2025, requirements for spare parts, battery life and software updates have also applied to smartphones and tablets, and the energy label of these devices shows a repairability class. The EU directive on the right to repair, which is to be implemented into national law by 2026, also strengthens the position of consumers vis-à-vis manufacturers. The exact details will change with the implementation, the consumer advice centers are currently providing information about this.

## Repair Cafés: Help for self-help

Repair cafés are volunteer-organized meetings where experienced helpers repair broken things together with you. You bring your device, your jacket or your toy and you look for the error together. There is usually coffee and cake, and instead of a bill there is a donation box on the table. You usually have to pay for replacement parts yourself.

Here's how to prepare well:

- **Describe the error in detail.** How long has it been occurring, what exactly is happening, are there any noises or smells?
- **Bring everything that comes with it.** Power supply, remote control, charging cable, if necessary the operating instructions.
- **Allow time.** On busy days there may be a wait and some repairs may take longer.
- **Be open to the outcome.** Not every repair will be successful. But even then, you will know afterwards what the problem was and can make a more conscious decision.

You can find addresses via the repair initiative networks on the Internet, on the website of your city or municipality and often on noticeboards in libraries, district centers and church communities. Many repair cafés are also meeting places where you can get to know neighbors, similar to the ideas in the guide [Borrow, share, exchange](/ratgeber/leihen-teilen-tauschen/).

## Repair yourself: start small

Once you feel like repairing things, you often want to do more yourself. Start with simple, low-risk tasks: sewing on buttons, mending bicycle tubes, tightening screws on furniture, adjusting glasses frames, replacing the battery of an electric toothbrush, if the device is designed for this.

A small amount of basic equipment is enough to get you started:

| tool | For what |
|---|---|
| Sewing set with needles, thread, spare buttons | Clothes, bags, pillows |
| Screwdriver set with small bits | Furniture, toys, small appliances |
| Wood glue and two screw clamps | Chairs, frames, drawers |
| Repair kit and tire levers | Bicycle |
| Superglue and two-component glue | small breaks |
| Flashlight or headlamp | Find faults within |

There are illustrated step-by-step instructions and videos for many devices online. Before you start, read through all of the instructions, take photos of each disassembly step, and sort screws in an egg box or ice cube tray. This way you'll know exactly what goes where later.

## Repairing as a habit

To ensure that repairs don't just remain a good intention, a little routine helps. Set up a designated spot at home for “repairs,” such as a basket or box. Anything that needs a little goes there instead of in the trash or back in the closet. Once a month you take an hour and work through the basket or take the items together to the cobbler, the tailor's shop or the repair café.

It is also helpful to think about the future when buying a new one. Ask for spare parts, choose screwed instead of glued products, and consider whether a good quality [used product](/ratgeber/gebraucht-kaufen/) might not be a better choice. And if the impulse comes to replace something immediately, the trick from the guide [Avoid impulse purchases] (/ratgeber/spontankaeufe-vermeiden/) helps: first sleep on it for a night, then decide.

## If a repair is no longer possible

Sometimes it really is over. Then proper disposal is the last service you can do for the thing. Electrical devices do not belong in the household waste, but rather in the recycling center or in the retailer's collection, which is required by law for many retailers. Rechargeable batteries and batteries are collected separately. Depending on local regulations, clothing that is no longer wearable belongs in the old clothing collection or in the residual waste; not every item is suitable for clothing donations. Individual parts can often be reused: buttons, zippers, screws or intact fabric panels for the next repair.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does a repair approximately cost?

This depends heavily on the item and the region. Small jobs at tailors or cobblers are often in the low double-digit range, while repairs to large appliances can cost significantly more. A cost estimate provides clarity before you make a decision.

### Do I lose my warranty if I repair it myself?

If you intervene yourself within the warranty period, it may be difficult to make a claim against the dealer later. If you have new devices with defects, contact the dealer first and only then repair it yourself.

### Are there subsidies for repairs?

In recent years, some states and cities have offered repair bonuses that cover part of the cost of repairing electrical appliances. Such programs are often temporary and quickly exhausted. It's worth taking a look at the website of your state or city and the consumer advice center.

### Are repair cafés also suitable for beginners?

Yes, that's exactly what they're intended for. You don't have to be able to do anything, just be curious and want to help. Many visitors learn their first steps there and later repair smaller things themselves.
