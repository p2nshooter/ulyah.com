“If you buy cheap, you buy twice,” says an old saying. It's not always true: an expensive product is not automatically good, and some cheap parts last a surprisingly long time. But at the heart of it is a truth that everyone has experienced: the jeans that go out of shape after three washes, the chair that wobbles after a year, the kettle that gives up shortly after the warranty expires. Those who can recognize quality buy less often, are less annoyed and end up with more for their money.

Recognizing quality is not secret knowledge. It's a mix of careful inspection, some basic knowledge of materials and workmanship, and the willingness to take a few minutes before buying. This guide shows you the most important features for clothing, shoes, furniture, kitchen utensils and electrical appliances, explains what seals and labels say, and helps you compare prices honestly.

## What quality actually means

Quality does not mean luxury. A product is of good quality if it fulfills its purpose reliably, for a long time and pleasantly. For a rain jacket this means: tight, breathable, robust. For a cooking pot: even heat distribution, sturdy handle, durable surface. For a cell phone: solid workmanship, long software updates, a replaceable battery or at least a fair repair option.

Three questions help to classify quality in everyday life:

1. **How long is this thing supposed to last?** A party costume can be simple, but a winter coat can't.
2. **How ​​often do I use it?** The more often, the more it pays to do better.
3. **Can it be repaired?** What can be repaired lasts longer, even if something breaks.

## Clothing: fabric, seams and details

When it comes to clothing, even a quick glance can reveal a lot if you know what to look for.

### Check the fabric

- **Hold up to the light:** Very thin, translucent fabrics on pieces that are supposed to be opaque often wear out more quickly.
- **Squeeze lightly:** Good fabric is less likely to wrinkle and bounce back.
- **Read label:** Fiber composition must be stated. Natural fibers such as cotton, wool and linen are comfortable to wear; Blended fabrics can be more robust or easier to care for. What matters is the quality of the fiber and fabric, not just the type of fiber.
- **Watch out for pilling:** If a sweater develops pilling on the sleeves or sides when you get it in the store, it won't get any better with wear.

### Seams and workmanship

- **Stitch density:** Many small, even stitches hold better than a few large ones.
- **Seam allowances:** Generous, overcast seam allowances allow for later alterations and are less likely to tear.
- **Patterns on seams:** With stripes and checks, the patterns on seams and pockets fit together when carefully crafted.
- **Buttons and Zippers:** Tightly sewn buttons, ideally with a spare button, and zippers that run easily and without snags.
- **View inside:** Loose threads, crooked seams and improperly cut edges are warning signs.

## Shoes: sole, shaft and connection

Shoes are a good example of the power of repairability. High-quality leather shoes can often be resoled and last for many years.

- **Connection of sole and shaft:** Sewn soles, for example in welted shoes, can usually be replaced. Glued soles are lighter and cheaper, but often more difficult to repair.
- **Material of the upper material:** Smooth leather, suede or robust textiles; With leather, it helps to look at the cut edges and the evenness of the surface.
- **Inner Lining:** A leather lining is breathable and durable.
- **Heel counter:** A firm heel counter provides support and keeps the shape.

A shoemaker in your area can often honestly tell you which shoes can be repaired when making a purchasing decision. More about this in the guide [Have it repaired instead of buying a new one] (/ratgeber/reparieren-lassen-statt-neu-kaufen/).

## Furniture: material, connections and fittings

Furniture should last for many years and withstand moves. This is what matters:

| feature | High quality | Less durable |
|---|---|---|
| material | Solid wood or solid wood materials with a robust surface | very thin plates, slightly torn edges |
| Connections | Wooden connections, screws in threaded sleeves, metal fittings | just glue or thin plastic dowels |
| drawers | Full extension extensions with ball or roller guides, stable bases | thin floors that sag |
| Upholstery | dense foam, removable, washable covers | soft foam that soaks through quickly |
| Spare parts | Manufacturer offers parts and instructions | no spare parts available |

Sit on chairs and sofas in the store, gently wiggle tables and shelves, and pull drawers all the way out. A sturdy piece of furniture does not creak or wobble. Ask whether there are replacement parts available, such as new covers or fittings. Used furniture made from solid materials is often a very good choice; Tips for this can be found in the guide [Buy used] (/ratgeber/gebraucht-kaufen/).

## Kitchen: pots, knives and little helpers

In the kitchen, quality is particularly evident in everyday use:

- **Pots and Pans:** A thick base distributes heat more evenly. Handles should be firmly riveted or welded. Stainless steel and cast iron last a very long time if cared for properly; Coated pans are practical, but the coating wears off over time.
- **Knives:** A good knife feels balanced in the hand, the blade is firmly connected to the handle, and it can be sharpened.
- **Little helpers:** It's better to have a few sturdy tools made of metal or wood than a lot of plastic parts that break quickly. How to clear out the kitchen can be found in the guide [Clear out the kitchen](/ratgeber/kueche-ausmisten/).

## Electrical appliances: repairability and updates

In devices with electronics, quality is harder to recognize from the outside. These points are all the more important:

- **Guarantee and warranty:** The statutory warranty for new goods is generally two years. A voluntary, longer manufacturer's warranty can be a sign that the manufacturer trusts its product. Read the terms and conditions carefully.
- **Spare parts and repair:** Are there spare parts, repair instructions or a repair service? For some device groups, the EU now stipulates that manufacturers must offer spare parts for a certain period of time.
- **Battery:** Can it be changed? For smartphones and tablets, the EU energy label has also shown a repairability class since 2025.
- **Software updates:** For cell phones, tablets and networked devices, the duration of the security updates determines the length of use. Pay attention to the manufacturer's information.
- **Energy consumption:** The EU energy label helps you compare the running costs, which for a refrigerator, washing machine or dryer often amount to more than the purchase price over the years.

## Seals and labels: what they say and what they don't

Seals can help with your purchase if you know what they stand for. Some common examples:

| Seal | What it stands for |
|---|---|
| Blue Angel | Environmental label from the federal government for more environmentally friendly products and services |
| EU Ecolabel | European Union Ecolabel |
| GOTS | Standard for textiles made from organic natural fibers with environmental and social criteria |
| Green button | State seal for socially and ecologically produced textiles |
| FSC and PEFC | Wood and paper from certified forestry |
| EU energy label | Energy efficiency and, depending on the device, other information such as volume or repairability |

Seals usually say something about environmental and social standards, but not automatically about durability. An organic cotton shirt can still be thin. Seals complement your own view, they do not replace it. The federal government's siegelklarheit.de portal evaluates many seals.

## Compare prices honestly: costs per use

A helpful tool is the “cost per use” calculation. Divide the purchase price by the estimated number of uses over the lifespan. A winter coat for 300 euros that you wear on a hundred days each for ten winters amounts to a thousand days of wear and therefore costs around thirty cents per day. A coat that costs 80 euros and is worn out after two winters costs around forty cents per day for the same use and has to be bought twice. The calculation is never exact, but it shifts the focus from the price tag to the actual value.

## The five-minute check before you buy

In the store there is often no peace and quiet for a thorough inspection. This short sequence will help you make a solid judgment in just a few minutes, whether it's a jacket, a chair, or a kettle.

1. **Touch and weigh.** Take the piece in your hand. Very light products are not automatically bad, but if a pot, tool or coat seems noticeably thin, take a closer look.
2. **Move and strain.** Open zippers, pull out drawers, gently shake chair legs. Things that wobble or snag in the store won't get any better at home.
3. **See the invisible places.** Interior seams, the bottom of a piece of furniture, the back of an appliance. Manufacturers like to save money where customers rarely look.
4. **Ask about replacement.** Are there replacement parts, buttons, covers, batteries or seals? How long are they offered for? A clear answer is a good sign.
5. **Clarify warranty and returns.** The statutory warranty applies anyway, but a longer voluntary warranty shows that the manufacturer has confidence in its product.

If a product fails in two or more of these points, it is usually wiser to continue searching or look for a better version used.

## Maintain quality: care is part of it

Even the best product only lasts as long as it is treated well. Wool sweaters need airing instead of constant washing, leather shoes need shoe trees and cream, wooden boards occasionally need a little oil, knives need a sharpening stone instead of the dishwasher. When purchasing, read the care instructions briefly and ask yourself honestly whether you will stick to them. A product that requires extensive care that you don't want to do is not a good choice for you, even if it is objectively high quality.

## Typical thinking errors

**Expensive equals good.** A high price can represent brand, design or advertising. Check the features, not just the label.

**Many features equal high quality.** More features often mean more parts that can break. A simple, robust device is often more durable.

**Trust online reviews blindly.** Reviews can be helpful, but are sometimes bought-in or based on the first few weeks of use. Independent tests from consumer organizations are more reliable.

**Buy immediately.** Sleeping on it for a night protects against bad purchases. More about this in the guide [Avoid impulse purchases](/ratgeber/spontankaeufe-vermeiden/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I recognize good quality when buying online?

Read the product description carefully: material, workmanship, warranty, spare parts. View detailed photos, use independent testing and plan for returns. When buying online, you usually have a fourteen-day right of withdrawal from retailers in the EU.

### Is quality worth it even on a small budget?

Just then. Those who buy less but better often save money over the years. Good quality doesn't have to be new: used branded products made from solid materials are often cheaper than new, cheap goods.

### What is more important: seal or durability?

Both have their value. Ideally, you will find a product that is manufactured fairly and in a more environmentally friendly way and that lasts a long time. If you have to choose, a product that you will use for many years is often the more sustainable choice.

### How long should everyday things last at least?

There are no fixed numbers, but rough empirical values ​​help with classification: a good winter coat should be wearable for many years, a solid dining table for decades, a washing machine with good care for well over ten years. If something breaks well in advance, it's worth taking a look at the warranty and taking a closer look at the features in this guide the next time you buy.
