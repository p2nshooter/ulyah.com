You're lying in bed, you're actually tired, but your thoughts are still racing. Or you fall asleep quickly, but wake up several times during the night and struggle to get out of bed in the morning. Poor sleep continues throughout the day: concentration declines, mood is weaker, and reaching for the third cup of coffee becomes a habit.

The good news is that many sleep problems are closely related to our everyday habits, and habits can be changed. You don't need expensive gadgets or a perfect routine, but rather a few reliable basic rules, a little patience and the willingness to look honestly at your own day. This guide will show you what adjustments you can make and help you sleep better step by step.

**Important in advance:** This guide does not replace medical advice. If you don't sleep well for weeks, nod off unintentionally during the day, or your partner notices pauses in your breathing, please talk to your family doctor. More on this at the end of the article.

## How much sleep do you actually need?

According to current recommendations, most adults need around seven to nine hours of sleep per night. But that is an average value. Some feel fit with seven hours, others need significantly more. The exact number is less important than the question: Do you feel awake and productive most of the day?

Vacation provides a good indication of your personal needs. If you regularly wake up at the same time after a few days without an alarm clock, that roughly shows how much sleep your body wants. If this value is significantly higher than what you get in everyday life, you are probably carrying a sleep deficit.

Chronotype also plays a role. Some people naturally wake up early and tire quickly in the evening, others find it difficult to get going in the morning and are at their best in the evening. It can't be completely reversed, but you can organize your everyday life so that it fits your rhythm better.

## Rule one: fixed times, even on weekends

The most important lever for better sleep is surprisingly unspectacular: as regular a time as possible for going to bed and, above all, getting up. Your body has an internal clock that is based on recurring signals. If you get up at six during the week and sleep until ten on the weekend, your body experiences something like a little jet lag.

This is how you do it:

1. **Set a fixed get-up time** that you can stick to on all days if possible. A deviation of about an hour on weekends is fine.
2. **Do the math back from there.** If you wake up at 6:30 a.m. and need about eight hours of sleep, you should be in bed by 10:30 p.m.
3. **Plan for buffer time.** Most people take a while to fall asleep. Therefore, allow yourself a little more time in bed than you would like to sleep.
4. **Hang in there.** The internal clock takes a few days to weeks to adjust. Don't give up after the first restless night.

A reliable [evening routine](/ratgeber/abendroutine-entwickeln/) and a calm [morning routine](/ratgeber/morgenroutine-finden/) additionally support this rhythm.

## Light: the most important pacemaker

Light is the strongest signal for your internal clock. Bright daylight in the morning signals to the body: now is day. Darkness in the evening promotes the release of the sleep hormone melatonin.

- **Go outside in the morning.** Even a short walk, the walk to work or a coffee on the balcony bring significantly more light than a morning in the office. Even on cloudy days, it is much brighter outside than inside.
- **Lots of light during the day.** If possible, work near windows and take breaks outside. The guide [Breaks in Everyday Life](/ratgeber/pausen-im-alltag/) provides ideas for this.
- **Dimme in the evening.** One to two hours before going to bed, muted, warm light and floor lamps instead of bright ceiling lighting help.
- **Dark at night.** Blackout curtains or a sleep mask help when street lights or early summer sun shine into the room.

## Screens and cell phones in the evening

Smartphones, tablets and televisions are often suspected of having sleep problems. This is not only due to the light of the screens, but above all to the content. News, social media, exciting series and work emails keep your head awake and make it difficult to calm down. There is also the phenomenon that “just a short time” quickly turns into an hour.

Helpful Habits:

- **A set screen-free time before bed**, for example the last half hour or hour.
- **Charge your cell phone outside the bedroom.** A simple alarm clock replaces the alarm function.
- **Silence notifications in the evening**, for example via a sleep mode with fixed times. The guide [Taming notifications](/ratgeber/benachrichtigungen-zaehmen/) explains how to do this.
- **Consciously choose other evening activities**: reading, listening to music, writing a diary, stretching, a conversation.

You can find more ideas for conscious breaks from the screen in the guide [Cell phone-free times] (/ratgeber/handyfreie-zeiten/).

## Caffeine, alcohol and food

What you eat during the day continues to work well into the night. Caffeine from coffee, black and green tea, cola and energy drinks stays in the body for several hours. Many people sleep better if they avoid caffeinated drinks starting in the early afternoon. How sensitive you react depends on the individual, but trying it yourself over two weeks will bring clarity.

Alcohol often makes you sleepy, but it disrupts the second half of the night. Sleep becomes more restless, you wake up more often and feel less refreshed in the morning. A glass of wine to help you fall asleep is therefore not a good idea.

Dinner also plays a role. A very heavy, fatty or spicy meal just before going to bed can disrupt sleep, but so can a growling stomach. A light dinner two to three hours before bedtime is a good middle ground for many. And if you have to go to the toilet several times at night, it is better to drink the majority of your daily intake before the evening.

## The bedroom as a place of rest

Your bedroom should signal one thing above all: this is where you sleep. The less it looks like an office, closet or TV room, the easier it is to switch off.

| factor | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Temperature | Rather cool, many find around 16 to 19 degrees pleasant |
| Light | As dark as possible, blackout or sleep mask |
| noises | quiet, if there is noise, wear earplugs or there is a constant background noise |
| air | Ventilate well before sleeping |
| bed | matching mattress and pillows, comfortable bed linen |
| Okay | little stuff, no workplace in sight |

The last point in particular is often underestimated. A chair full of clothes, piles of unread documents or the clothes horse in view remind the brain of unfinished tasks. A few minutes of tidying up in the evening is worth it. The guide [Fixed Places and Labeling](/ratgeber/feste-plaetze-und-beschriften/) helps prevent the stuff from ending up in the bedroom.

## Calm your head

Many people lie awake not because their body is tired, but because their head cannot switch off. Thoughts about tomorrow's tasks, conversations of the day or worries keep circling and cannot simply be turned off. These methods help many:

- **Write down thoughts.** Before you go to sleep, write down what is bothering you and write down the next small step for each open task. What is written on paper does not have to be recorded in your head.
- **Plan the next day briefly.** A look at the calendar and a short list of the three most important tasks provide security. The guide [Plan your day realistically](/ratgeber/tag-realistisch-planen/) shows you how to do this.
- **Relaxation exercises.** Calm, slow breathing, progressive muscle relaxation or a body scan help many people to switch off. It's important to practice regularly, not just on bad nights.
- **A fixed evening ritual.** Over time, the same routine, such as making tea, brushing your teeth, reading a few pages, becomes a signal: now it's time to sleep.

## When you lie awake at night

Almost everyone knows nights when sleep just doesn't come. Then it helps not to fight doggedly. Anyone who lies awake for hours looking at the clock increasingly associates the bed with brooding and tension.

A tried and tested recommendation: If you haven't fallen asleep after what feels like a quarter to half an hour, get up, go to another room with dim lighting and do something quiet, like reading or listening to quiet music. Don't go back to bed until you're tired again. And turn the alarm clock so you're not constantly calculating how much sleep you have left.

A short nap during the day can help, but it shouldn't be too long or too late in the afternoon. Otherwise there will be no pressure to sleep in the evening. Many people get along well with ten to twenty minutes.

## Exercise and daily routine

Regular exercise promotes deeper sleep. This doesn't have to be competitive sport: walks, cycling, gardening or climbing stairs count. However, intensive training right before bed is too stimulating for some. Try out what suits you.

The rest of the day also affects the night. If you're under pressure all day, don't take breaks, and take your work into your living room in the evening, it's hard to relax. Clear boundaries after work, a realistic daily plan and the occasional [saying no](/ratgeber/nein-sagen-lernen/) to additional obligations are therefore also sleep hygiene.

## A four-week plan for better nights

Changing everything at once is overwhelming. Make one change per week:

1. **Week one: Fixed wake-up time.** Get up at the same time every day, even on weekends, and go out into the daylight for a few minutes in the morning.
2. **Week Two: Screen-Free Time.** Put your phone away half an hour before bed and charge it outside the bedroom.
3. **Week three: Caffeine and dinner.** Avoid caffeine starting in the early afternoon and eat lighter in the evening.
4. **Week four: Bedroom and ritual.** Tidy up the bedroom, provide darkness and cool air, and establish a short, consistent evening ritual.

During this time, keep a simple sleep diary: when did you go to bed, when did you get up, how rested do you feel on a scale of one to five? After four weeks you will see which changes have had the most effect on you. A [habit tracker](/ratgeber/gewohnheits-tracker/) can help you stick with it.

## When you should seek medical advice

Occasional bad nights are normal. You should seek medical advice if:

- you have trouble falling asleep or staying asleep for several weeks despite good habits,
- you are so tired during the day that you unintentionally fall asleep, for example while reading, watching TV or even driving,
- someone notices loud snoring with pauses in breathing,
- you often have restless or tingling legs at night,
- Sleep problems are accompanied by depression, anxiety or severe stress.

The family doctor's practice is the first point of contact and can refer you to specialized sleep medicine if necessary. You should not take sleeping pills on your own for a long period of time.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I make up for lost sleep on the weekend?

Sleeping a little longer on the weekend can make up for a small deficit. But if you consistently don't sleep enough during the week and spend hours catching up on the weekend, you're disrupting your rhythm. Regular, adequate nights are better.

### Does an evening walk help you fall asleep?

For many, yes. A quiet walk in the early evening relieves tension and provides distance from screens. On the other hand, very strenuous exercise shortly before sleep can be more stimulating for some people.

### Are sleep apps and fitness watches useful?

They can help recognize patterns, such as irregular times. However, the measured sleep phases are only estimates. If the numbers worry you rather than support you, it's better to forego them and rely on how you feel in the morning.

### How long does it take for new sleep habits to take effect?

The first improvements often become apparent after one to two weeks. It can take several weeks for the rhythm to become stable. Patience and regularity are more important than perfection.
