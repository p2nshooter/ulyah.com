The drawer next to the stove can only be opened by shaking it because the whisk got caught in the potato masher. There are storage containers stacked up in the cupboard above, but none of them seem to fit the lid that is on top. Three spatulas, two garlic presses and a waffle iron that comes out once a year share space with the dishes you need every day.

The kitchen is the room you use most often and most purposefully. Every superfluous item doesn't cost you time once a month, but rather a little at every meal. This guide will walk you through an inventory by category, a simple test for anything you're unsure about, and difficult cases like heirlooms. Then you put things away so that cooking becomes easier. This is deliberately not about cleaning and storing food, but rather about the things you work with.

## Preparation: one category per round

Don't clean out the entire kitchen in one evening. Anyone who empties all the cupboards at six o'clock will be standing hungry between mountains of pots at eight o'clock. Set aside one category per round, about an hour on the weekend or a free afternoon.

Prepare four boxes or baskets:

- **Stays:** Things you use regularly and like.
- **Pass it on:** Intact that someone else can use.
- **Dispose of:** Broken, bent or chipped items.
- **Test box:** anything you are unsure about. What happens to this is explained further down in the two-week cooking test.

Get everything together for each category, including the hanging cupboard above the extractor hood, the box in the basement and the box from the last move. Only when all the pans are next to each other on the table will you see how many there actually are.

## Inventory by category

These six categories cover most cuisines. The order is a suggestion: utensils and storage containers are quick and motivating, equipment and dishes need more thought.

| Category | Typical duplications | Key question |
|---|---|---|
| Utensils | multiple spatulas, ladles, sieves and graters, special tools for a single food | Which one should I grab first and which only when the other one is in the dishwasher? |
| Pots and pans | Pot sets that only use two sizes, scratched pans | What sizes are really available on my stove? |
| Dishes and glasses | Advertising cups, mixed leftovers from old sets, chipped plates | How many people do I regularly cover for? |
| storage containers | Cans without lids, lids without cans, collected disposable containers | Does every can have a matching lid? |
| Electrical appliances | several devices that can do the same thing, gifts that are in the box | When did I last use it and what would have replaced it? |
| Kitchen textiles | frayed tea towels, pot holders with holes, never used table linen | How many do I need until the next wash day? |

### Utensils

Place all the utensils on the work surface and sort them by function: turning, stirring, scooping, cutting, grating, sieving. Within each group you usually have a favorite piece that you always reach for. The others are replacements. A replacement can be useful, such as a second wooden spoon if two pots are on the stove at the same time. A third and fourth rare. You only keep special tools like egg cutters or spiral cutters if you actually use them several times a month and a knife wouldn't be just as good.

### Pots and pans

Imagine your most elaborate everyday dish: How many pots and pans are on the stove at the same time, and in what sizes? That's your actual needs, plus maybe a large pot for visitors. Also make sure that everything fits your stove. After moving to a different hob, some pots no longer work. It is better to replace pans whose coating is visibly peeling off. What the manufacturer recommends can be found in the instructions for use.

### Dishes and glasses

Chipped plates and cups that are cracked should be sorted out; you can get injured on sharp edges. When it comes to promotional cups and glasses from promotions, you ask yourself whether you should take them out of the cupboard when the beautiful ones are clean. If not, they are just placeholders.

### storage containers

A little summary helps here: put all the cans and all the lids on the table and find a lid for each can. What's left, on both sides, goes. Preferably keep cans that can be stacked inside each other and form a unified system. You only keep a small, strictly limited amount of disposable containers collected from the delivery service.

### Electrical appliances

Devices take up a lot of space and often make you feel guilty because they cost money. Honestly ask yourself when you last used each and whether another device or simple hand tool does the same job. For example, hand blenders, stand mixers and food processors overlap a lot. Keep what you use most and what you love most.

### Kitchen textiles

Tea towels, pot holders, oven gloves, aprons, tablecloths. Keep as many as you need until the next wash day and a small reserve. Sort out burnt pot holders and gloves with thin spots; they no longer provide reliable protection from heat.

## The two-week cooking test

For many things you simply don't know whether you need them. Instead of pondering for a long time, you let your everyday life decide.

1. **Pack anything unsafe in the test box.** Place it in the kitchen where it is easily accessible, not in the basement.
2. **Write a date on the box.** The evaluation is two weeks later.
3. **Cook as normal.** If you need something from the box, take it out and use it.
4. **Give used things a permanent place.** What you took out of the box can stay.
5. **Evaluate on the deadline.** You haven't missed what's still in the box for two weeks. Just check whether it is a seasonal item, more on that in a moment. Everything else is passed on or thrown away.

Two weeks is long enough for your usual rhythm of working days, weekends and a large purchase, and short enough that the box doesn't become permanent storage.

There is a version without a box for drawers: put a rubber band around each utensil. As soon as you use it, the tape comes off. After two weeks you will immediately see what is still wearing one.

## How much is enough? Quantities as an aid to thinking

Fixed numbers like “six plates per person” don’t fit every household. Those who wash by hand every day need less than those who only turn on the dishwasher when it is full. Someone who often has guests needs more than someone who almost always eats alone. Instead of fixed numbers, these considerations help:

| area | Thinking aid | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| Plates, bowls, cutlery | People in the household have meals until the next round of washing up, plus regular guests | How often do you really wash? |
| Cups and glasses | Favorite pieces for all household members plus a small reserve | Which ones always stay in the closet? |
| Pots and pans | as many as there are on the stove at the same time for the most elaborate everyday dish | What do you need the rest for? |
| storage containers | as many as will fit in the space you give them | What sizes do you really use? |
| Tea towels | Required until the next wash day plus reserve | Which ones do you never take? |

One principle runs through all categories: **Space limits the quantity.** You first decide how much space a category gets, for example a drawer for storage containers. What doesn't fit in goes.

You don't have to permanently store dishes for twenty people for the big family celebration or birthday party twice a year. You can borrow it, rent dishes in some places, or set up a storage box in the basement that doesn't take up the kitchen cupboard. Ideas for this can be found in [Borrow, share, exchange](/ratgeber/leihen-teilen-tauschen/).

## Special cases: gifts, heirlooms, seasonal appliances

### Gifts

A gift served its purpose when it was given. You can pass it on if you don't use it. If the person visits often and might inquire about the fondue pot, be honest and say that it ended up with someone who uses it more often.

### Heirlooms

Grandmother's service or old dough bowl carry memories. Instead of keeping the entire set unused in the cupboard, you can bring individual parts into your everyday life: the cup for the morning, the cake plate for Sundays. Ask the family if anyone else would like individual pieces before giving anything away. Photograph what is allowed to go. You can find out more about this gentle topic in [Clearing out memorabilia](/ratgeber/erinnerungsstuecke-ausmisten/).

### Seasonal devices

Raclette, fondue, cookie cutters, barbecue cutlery: these things are rarely used, but are often used. The question is not whether you used them in the last two weeks, but whether you used them last season. If so, they are given a place outside of the work zones: in the top shelf, in the storage room or in a labeled box. If not, you can lend them or pass them on in the neighborhood.

## Rearrange according to work zones and handle height

Now comes the part that makes clearing out noticeable in everyday life. Arrange things not based on where there is space, but based on where you use them. Most kitchens have three work zones:

| zone | Where | What belongs there |
|---|---|---|
| Prepare | Work surface between sink and stove | Cutting boards, knives, bowls, graters, measuring cups, scales |
| Cooking | around the stove and oven | Pots, pans, wooden spoons, spatulas, ladles, pot holders, oil |
| Rinse | on the sink and dishwasher | Dishes, glasses, cutlery, tea towels |

There is a simple reason why dishes and cutlery are located near the dishwasher: you can then put them in and out in just a few steps.

This is how you do it:

1. **Assign each category to a zone.** On paper or in your head before putting anything away.
2. **Everything you use every day should be placed at a comfortable grip height,** i.e. approximately between eye and hip height and in the front rows.
3. **Weekly used items move up or down.**
4. **Rare items go all the way upstairs or out of the kitchen.**
5. **Keep the work surface clear.** Only what you use every day is written on the surface.

The guide [Fixed Places and Labels](/ratgeber/feste-plaetze-und-beschriften/) explains how to allocate places according to frequency and when labels are worthwhile.

## Where to put the discarded items?

- **Intact dishes, pots and utensils** are happy for social department stores, gift exchanges and people who are currently furnishing their first apartment. Ask beforehand what will be accepted.
- **Electrical devices** do not belong in the residual waste. You hand them in at the recycling center, and many dealers take old devices back. You can find out what applies in individual cases from the dealer. You can also give away working devices.
- **Drinking glasses, porcelain and ceramics** do not belong in the waste glass container. Your municipal waste disposal company will tell you where they belong.
- **Plastic storage containers** are not packaging. Your local authority decides whether they belong in a recycling bin or with residual waste.
- **Knives** should be wrapped securely before passing on or disposing of them, for example in sturdy cardboard, so that no one cuts themselves.

General routes for donations, bulky waste and recycling centers can be found in the timetable [Start clearing out] (/ratgeber/ausmisten-anfangen/).

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

- **Buy organizers first:** You only buy new inserts and cans when you know what's left.
- **Keep double “just in case”:** In the rare case, another tool or a request in the neighborhood is usually sufficient.
- **The test box without a date:** Without a date, it becomes the new storage space. Write the date on it clearly.
- **Decide on your own about common things:** In a shared kitchen, you decide together what stays. You never secretly give away other people's favorite items.

## Briefly summarized

Clear out the kitchen category by category: utensils, pots and pans, dishes and glasses, storage containers, electrical appliances and kitchen textiles. Get everything together and ask yourself what you are really reaching for. Anything unsafe goes into a test box for two weeks. Use quantities as an aid to thinking and let space limit the quantity. Heirlooms are allowed into everyday life, seasonal devices are given a place outside the work zones. After preparing, cooking and washing up, put away your daily essentials at a comfortable reach.
