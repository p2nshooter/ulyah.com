Everyone knows this: in January, after vacation or on a Monday, you make something happen. Move more, sleep earlier, read every day, spend less time on your cell phone. The first few days go well, then comes a stressful week, an infection or a visitor, and the resolution is gone. This is rarely due to a lack of willpower. Most of the time the plan is too big, too vague or doesn't depend on anything that happens in everyday life anyway. Habits are not formed through determination, but rather through repetition within a stable framework.

This guide shows you how to build new habits so that they stick even when initial motivation wanes.

## What a habit actually is

A habit is a behavior that you perform almost automatically in a certain situation, without thinking about it for a long time. You brush your teeth after going to the bathroom. You pick up your cell phone when you wait at the bus stop. The process is always similar: a **situation** triggers a **behavior**, and that is followed by something that **feels good** or at least relieved.

This results in three levers for new habits:

- **A clear trigger** that reliably occurs every day.
- **A behavior** so small that you can do it even on bad days.
- **A pleasant feeling afterwards** so that you will like to repeat it.

How long it takes for a habit to become automatic varies greatly. Simple habits like drinking a glass of water after getting up go faster than demanding ones like regular exercise. Expect weeks to months rather than days, and don't let that discourage you.

## Step by step to a new habit

### 1. Choose a single habit

If you want to change five things at the same time, you are spreading your energy across too many areas. Choose a habit that means the most to you right now. Only when it is running stable will the next one come.

### 2. Make them ridiculously small

Instead of “exercising for thirty minutes every day,” resolve to “put on your sports shoes every day and walk around the block.” Instead of “reading an hour every night,” it’s “reading a page every night.” That sounds almost silly, but there is a reason: the most difficult hurdle is the beginning. If it is low enough, you can start writing even on tired days, and often a page turns into a chapter. It's important that the minimum version counts, even if you don't do more.

### 3. Hang it on a fixed trigger

New habits stick best when they're tied to something you already do every day. Put it as a sentence: “After I…, I will…”.

| Existing habit | New habit |
|---|---|
| After I turn on the coffee machine in the morning | I drink a glass of water |
| After I sit down at the desk | I write down the three most important tasks |
| After I brushed my teeth | I put the cell phone in the kitchen |
| After I unlocked the apartment door | I hang the key on the hook |
| After I lay down in bed | I read a page |

The more precise the trigger, the better. “In the morning” is too vague, “after I turn on the coffee machine” is clear.

### 4. Plan for obstacles with if-then sentences

Think about what might stop you beforehand and decide what you will do next. “If it rains, I do ten minutes of stretching exercises in the living room instead of going for a walk.” “When I’m out and about in the evening, I read a page on the train.” Such plans take the decision out of your hands in the moment when you are tired.

### 5. Design your environment

Your environment is stronger than your intention. You use what is visible and within reach more often. Anything that is hidden or awkward is rarer.

- **Relieve:** The book is on the pillow, the trainers are by the door, the water bottle is on the desk.
- **Complicate it:** The phone is charging in the kitchen, candy is in the top cupboard, the streaming app has disappeared from the home screen.

### 6. Make progress visible

A simple cross on the calendar for each day you complete the habit is amazingly motivating. Over time, the chain of crosses becomes something you don't want to break. How to keep such a log without letting it put you under pressure can be found in [Use a habit tracker](/ratgeber/gewohnheits-tracker/).

### 7. Reward yourself immediately

Many good habits only pay off in the long term. In the short term they sometimes feel tedious. Help yourself with a small, immediate joy: your favorite music while walking, a special cup of tea while reading, a satisfied crossing off of the list.

## An example over six weeks

Let's say you want to move more. For the first week, your plan is: “After I get home from work, I’m going to put on my sneakers and walk around the block.” This takes five minutes. Some days it turns into a longer walk, other days it's just a walk. In the third week you notice that you put the shoes on almost automatically. Now you can extend the minimum version to ten minutes. On the fifth week, it rains three days in a row, and you resort to your if-then plan: ten minutes of stretching in the living room. After six weeks, exercise after work has become a regular part of your day, not because you were particularly motivated every day, but because it was always easy enough to start with.

## If you miss a day

There will be days when it doesn't work out. This is normal and not a failure. It's not the one missed day that's dangerous, but the second and third days when a break turns into a break. A helpful rule is: **Never skip it twice in a row.** If you didn't read yesterday, read at least one page today. This is how the habit stays alive.

Be kind to yourself. If you scold yourself for every slip-up, you associate the habit with bad feelings and give it up more quickly.

## Typical mistakes and what helps

| Error | What happens | Better that way |
|---|---|---|
| too big a start | exhausted after a few days | start with a minimum version |
| vague intentions | no clear moment to act | determine the exact trigger |
| everything at once | no habit becomes stable | one habit at a time |
| just willpower | on tired days everything falls away | Design the environment so that it is easy |
| Perfectionism | one slip becomes a demolition | never miss it twice in a row |
| Goal instead of identity | after the goal everything falls back | see yourself as someone who does this regularly |

## From goal to attitude

A goal like “lose ten pounds” or “read a book a month” comes to an end. If it is reached or missed, it loses its power. It is more helpful to associate the habit with an image of yourself: “I am someone who reads every day”, “I am someone who likes to exercise”. Every small repetition is then a confirmation of this image, even if it was only one page.

## Habits that are good to start with

Anyone consciously building a habit for the first time benefits from starting with something simple. If the first habit succeeds, confidence in the method grows and the next one becomes easier. These examples have proven to be a good introduction because they are short, have an immediate impact and can be easily attached to existing processes:

| habit | Minimum possible version | Appropriate trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Drink more water | a glass of water | after brushing your teeth in the morning |
| Read | one page | after I lay down in bed |
| movement | five minute walk | after coming home |
| Okay | two minutes of cleanup | after the dishwasher is running |
| Gratitude | a sentence in the notebook | after dinner |
| Stretching | three exercises | after getting up |
| Finances in view | Check your account balance briefly | on Sundays with coffee |

Choose one of them that really excites you. A habit that is important to you will last much better than one that you only adopt out of a sense of obligation.

## Let the habit grow

At some point the moment comes when the minimum version almost runs by itself. Then you can carefully expand it. The mistake would be to immediately jump from one page to a whole chapter. Slow growth is better, for example a small step more every two weeks.

It is helpful to differentiate between the minimum version and the desired version. The minimum version always remains valid, even on bad days. The desired version is what you do on good days. If you can do the minimum version every day and the desired version on some days, you will get surprisingly far over the course of months. And because the minimum version never goes away, the chain doesn't break even in stressful weeks.

A good time to check whether the habit can grow is the [Weekly Review](/ratgeber/wochenplanung-und-wochenrueckblick/). Ask yourself there: Is the habit easy for me now? Do I want more? If the answer to both is yes, take a small next step.

## Habits in the family and with others

Habits become easier when others join in. A shared evening walk, a reading evening with the children or a sports date with a friend combine the new habit with company and commitment. At the same time, you shouldn't pressure others into your new habit. Those who are enthusiastic about it themselves often infect others on their own. And if a bad habit gets in the way, you can find help in the guide [Breaking Bad Habits](/ratgeber/schlechte-gewohnheiten-ablegen/).

## When it takes more than a new habit

Some changes cannot be achieved through habits alone. If something is stressing you out, if you constantly feel exhausted or depressed, or if it involves addiction, nutritional problems or health questions, get professional support. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is it true that a habit sticks after 21 days?

This number is widely used but too simple. How long it takes for a behavior to feel automatic varies widely and depends on the habit and the person. Simple habits like drinking a glass of water in the morning are quicker, while more complex ones like regular exercise often take significantly longer. Expect it to take a few weeks to months and don't be discouraged if it doesn't work itself out after three weeks.

### Do I need an app to build habits?

No. A calendar on the wall, a notebook, or a simple paper [habit tracker](/ratgeber/gewohnheits-tracker/) will do just fine. Apps can be helpful if you like working digitally, but they don't replace the clear trigger and small minimum version.

### What to do if motivation starts to fade after a few weeks?

That's normal. The initial enthusiasm rarely lasts long. That's exactly what the minimum version is for: it's so small that you can do it even without motivation. Additionally, remind yourself why the habit is important to you and look at your progress so far.

## Briefly summarized

New habits come from repetition, not big resolutions. Choose a single habit, make it small enough to always fit, and attach it to a fixed trigger. Plan for obstacles, design your environment, visualize progress and reward yourself immediately. If you miss a day, never do it twice in a row.
