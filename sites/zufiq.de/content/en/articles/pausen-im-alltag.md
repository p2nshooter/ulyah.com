At eight o'clock you sit down at your desk, at half past twelve you eat a sandwich next to the keyboard, and around four you notice that your neck is hard, your eyes are burning and you've been working on the same paragraph for an hour. A break? You do it when you're done. But you're never finished, and in the evening you don't have the energy for everything that's actually important to you.

Many people experience breaks as wasted time, as something that has to be earned. Short interruptions are not a luxury, but a tool that allows you to think clearly for longer, stay friendlier and have more left in the evening. This guide will show you what types of breaks there are, how you can take them in the office, in the home office and at home, why the cell phone rarely helps and how you can get rid of that guilty conscience.

## Why breaks are part of performance

Concentration is not a line from which current flows evenly. Anyone who sits on something for a long time without interruption usually notices that their attention is decreasing: they read sentences twice, miss mistakes, and become impatient. The body also reports itself with tension, dry eyes or a dull head. A short break begins here before slight tiredness turns into real exhaustion.

A comparison with sport is helpful: no one would blame a runner for resting between two training sessions. Recovery is part of training. Breaks also belong to work, housework and caring for relatives. A common experience is that breaks work better if they come early and regularly than if you only stop when nothing is working anymore. Then you often need much longer to get back on your feet.

## Four types of breaks

A break is not the same as a break. Depending on what is exhausting you at the moment, something different will help. The following overview shows four shapes that can be easily combined in everyday life.

| Art | Duration | What it's good for | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Micropause | a few seconds to two minutes | Briefly relieve strain on your eyes, breathing and posture | look into the distance, breathe out deeply, circle your shoulders |
| Movement break | about five to fifteen minutes | Get your circulation going and stop sitting for long periods of time | Go up the stairs, get some fresh air, get water |
| Social break | a few minutes to half an hour | Distance from the task, connection with others | a conversation in the kitchen, a call to a friend |
| Real lunch break | as long as work and rules allow | Eating in peace, clear cut in the day | Meal at the table instead of at the desk, then take a few steps |

### Microbreaks

Micro-breaks are so short that you can't find an excuse. Lean back, look out the window into the distance, blink consciously a few times, let your shoulders sink and breathe out slowly. This takes barely a minute and can be installed after every email, phone call or when saving a file.

### Movement breaks

Anyone who sits a lot benefits from getting up regularly. It doesn't have to be a sports program. Walk up and down the stairs, get a glass of water from another room, or talk on the phone while standing. Anyone who works physically needs the opposite: sit down, put their feet up, be quiet for a moment.

### Social breaks

A quick conversation about something other than work can clear your mind surprisingly well. What's important is that it's good for you. Anyone who talks to people all day long, for example in sales, consulting or nursing, is more likely to need a quiet break alone.

### The real lunch break

For many people, the lunch break is the first break that is lost. She is the only one who noticeably divides the day into two halves. Instead of eating at your desk, eat somewhere else, even if it's just the kitchen table. Then take a few minutes to breathe in the air before continuing to work. Which break times you are entitled to and how they are regulated depends on the law, collective agreement and company agreements. Check the rules that apply to you in your employment contract, with the human resources department or with the works or staff council.

## Breaks in the home office and at the screen

A lot of things are missing at home that cause interruptions in the office: the way to the meeting, the colleague who picks up lunch, the walk to the printer. Without these occasions, you can easily sit in the same place for hours. At the same time, the apartment beckons with other tasks. Hanging out the laundry can be a good exercise break, but a quick walk through the tax documents is not.

This is how you can take better breaks when working from home:

- **Leave the workplace.** A break at your desk with the open document in the corner of your eye is only half a break.
- **Create artificial paths.** Put the water glass in the kitchen instead of next to the laptop. Every refill becomes a short walk.
- **Give your eyes a change.** Those who look at the screen often blink less often and look at the same distance for a long time. Every now and then, consciously look out the window and let your gaze wander.
- **Change posture.** Stand for a phone call, move to a different place to read a document, roll your shoulders back.
- **Mark the beginning and end.** A short walk before starting work and after work replaces the commute and helps the mind to switch gears.

An example: Jonas works at home three days a week and regularly has a headache at four o'clock. He sets an alarm clock for half past ten and three o'clock, each time with the message "Open the window, make tea." For lunch he sits down at the dining table and leaves the laptop closed. After two weeks, he noticed that the afternoons were quieter and he was less likely to lie exhausted on the sofa in the evenings.

## Breaks without a cell phone

Picking up your smartphone feels like a break. For many, however, it is more of a transition from one screen to the next: new messages, new stimuli, new small tasks. Your eyes and head can't get any rest, and five minutes quickly turn into twenty. Therefore, consciously try out breaks in which you leave your cell phone lying down, ideally in another room or in your pocket.

A personal pause menu helps you think of something in these moments. Write it on a card and put it where you work.

| time | Ideas without a screen |
|---|---|
| Two minutes | Open the window and take a deep breath, shake out your hands and forearms, and drink a glass of water |
| Ten minutes | Walk around the block, make tea and drink it by the window, water a plant, read a few pages |
| Thirty minutes | Lunch without a sideline, a walk in the park, a conversation with a familiar person |

If you find it particularly difficult to put your cell phone out of your hand during breaks, settings and fixed cell phone-free times can help. You can find out more about this in the guide [Cell phone-free times](/ratgeber/handyfreie-zeiten/).

## Schedule breaks and set reminders

Breaks that only exist as a good intention disappear in the first full day. They hold up better if they have a permanent place.

1. **Look at your daily routine.** When do you typically get tired? Take a longer break just before this low, not in the middle of it. How to find your personal daily curve can be found in [Divide energy](/ratgeber/energie-einteilen/).
2. **Link breaks with fixed occasions.** For example: I get up briefly after every meeting. After every long phone call, I drink a glass of water. Such if-then rules don't need a clock.
3. **Put longer breaks on your calendar.** A lunch block is less likely to be overbooked than an empty spot. [Plan your day realistically](/ratgeber/tag-realistisch-planen/) describes how to incorporate buffers and breaks into a realistic daily plan.
4. **Set friendly reminders.** A quiet alarm, timer, or calendar message with a specific suggestion like “up and down the stairs” works better than a general “break!”
5. **Try out a rhythm.** Some people work in fixed sections, like half an hour of concentration and then a few minutes break, others prefer to work in longer blocks. Try a rhythm for a week and adjust.
6. **Determine the end of the break.** If you know when it will continue, you can enjoy the break more easily and find your way back reliably.

### Breaks even after work

It's not just the working day that needs interruptions. A Saturday with shopping, laundry, government mail and visits can be just as stressful. Even on days off, plan conscious breaks, such as half an hour with a book, before the afternoon begins. Those who care for relatives or look after small children rarely find long breaks. Then the micro-breaks are particularly valuable: a conscious breath at the window, a cup of tea while sitting instead of standing.

## Feelings of guilt and culture of breaks in the team

Breaks often fail not because of the calendar, but because of sentences in your head: “The others are working through it too.” “If I get up now, everyone will think I have nothing to do.” “Only when the list is empty do I deserve a break.” Such thoughts are understandable, but they don't help anyone. A list is rarely empty, and exhausted people make more mistakes and are more irritable, and not just to themselves.

It is helpful to replace the thought instead of fighting it. Instead of "I don't have time for a break," you can say to yourself, "The break is part of the task. It'll be easier after that." If you find it difficult to turn down requests so that your lunch break remains free, you will find friendly formulations in the guide [Learn to say no](/ratgeber/nein-sagen-lernen/).

A lot can be achieved as a team:

- **Don't schedule meetings right after each other.** A few minutes between two appointments gives everyone some breathing space.
- **Keep lunchtime free of appointments.** A short consultation within the team is often enough.
- **Take a break together.** Having lunch together or a short walk for some air after a strenuous round also strengthens solidarity.
- **Make breaks visible.** A status “I'm away for a moment, available again from 1 p.m.” in the chat takes away the pressure of having to answer immediately.
- **Lead the way as a manager.** If the boss takes breaks herself and doesn't send messages at lunchtime, others will also have the courage.

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

- **Don't take a break until you can't do anything anymore:** Set fixed times before you get tired.
- **Take a break at work:** Get up and change location, even if it's just a few steps.
- **Fill breaks with tasks:** A quick transfer, a quick shopping list. This is work in another place.
- **Cancel breaks as soon as it gets busy:** You especially need them on busy days. It's better to shorten them instead of eliminating them completely.

## When breaks are no longer enough

Short breaks help combat the normal tiredness of a full day. If you feel exhausted for weeks despite breaks and enough sleep, if headaches, back problems or sleep problems don't go away or if you can hardly switch off, talk to your family doctor. If you are constantly overloaded at work, depending on the company, a company doctor, works council or an advice center can also help. You can find contact points under [Help & Contact Points](/hilfe-und-anlaufstellen/).

## Frequently asked questions

### I lose track after a pause. What helps?

Before the break, write down in one sentence what the next step is, for example “shorten paragraph two.” When you come back, read the note and get straight back in.

### What do I do if my job hardly allows breaks?

Use the moments that exist for micro-breaks: consciously exhale, relax your shoulders, briefly look into the distance. And clarify which breaks you are entitled to according to the applicable rules. The contact persons are the human resources department, works council or staff council.

## Briefly summarized

Breaks are not a sign of weakness, but a part of good work. Combine microbreaks, exercise breaks, social breaks and a real lunch break, choosing what suits your tiredness. Leave your workstation for breaks, leave your cell phone behind more often and have a break menu ready. Link breaks to fixed occasions, add larger ones to the calendar, set friendly reminders and talk to the team about a shared break culture. If the exhaustion still persists, get it checked out by a doctor.
