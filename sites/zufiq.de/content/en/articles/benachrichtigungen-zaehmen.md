You are sitting on a task that actually only needs half an hour of rest. Then the cell phone vibrates: a package notification. Shortly afterwards, a banner flashes on the computer, the household group chat is about the barbecue evening, an app reminds you of a series that you didn't want to watch. Each individual message only lasts seconds. But every time you do it you have to sort yourself out, and at the end of the day you feel like you never really did anything.

Notifications are a tool. Well adjusted, they tell you exactly what you need to know and remain silent the rest of the time. By default, it's usually the other way around: almost every app can report as often as it wants. Here you'll find out step by step how to turn this around, from your cell phone to group chats to your work computer.

## Why every message costs more than a few seconds

A notification is an interruption that someone else triggered for you. Even if you don't open it, it has distracted your attention for a moment, and then you look for the thread. If you know that something can come at any time, you also remain on standby: your eyes wander to your cell phone, even when it is silent. A quieter cell phone is therefore not the result of willpower, but rather through settings that make the decision for you. The guide [Reduce Screen Time](/ratgeber/bildschirmzeit-reduzieren/) covers how much time you spend in total on the screen. This is about who can interrupt you and when.

## Taking stock: Which apps are the loudest?

Before you switch something off, get an overview. Many smartphones show how many notifications have been received per app in the notification settings or in the screen time overview. If your device doesn't offer this, keep a simple tally sheet for two days.

1. **Note every app that reports** and roughly estimate how often: “rarely”, “a few times a day” or “all the time”.
2. **With each message, ask yourself:** Would I have missed something if she had arrived two hours later?
3. **Pay attention to how you feel.** Some messages are useful, but always cause anxiety, such as breaking news from news apps.

Such a list could look like this:

| app | How often | Would it have been bad later? | First assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Messenger, individual chats | several times a day | sometimes | important, but not every message |
| Group chat house community | constantly | almost never | collected is enough |
| Calendar | a few times a day | yes | immediately |
| Parcel service | occasionally | no | collected or never |
| Online shop | daily | no | never |
| News app | several times a day | no | never, rather read it yourself |
| game | daily | no | never |

As soon as you fill it out, you usually notice that only a handful of apps are really urgent. The rest get in touch because they are allowed to.

## Three levels for each app: immediately, collected, never

Instead of weighing up each app individually, a simple grid helps. Each app ends up in exactly one of three levels.

### Level 1: immediately

Only what really can't wait belongs here: calls, messages from the people who need you in an emergency, calendar reminders of appointments, alarm clocks and timers, security queries from your bank when you're releasing something yourself. These apps are allowed to use sound, vibration and banners. Consciously keep the level small. The less there is here, the more you can trust that a sound means something.

### Level 2: collected

These apps are allowed to report, but quietly: only in the notification overview, without sound, banners or vibration. You check when you pick up your cell phone anyway. Some systems also offer a summary that shows collected messages at fixed times, such as midday and early evening. Typical for this level are group chats, package and delivery instructions, social networks that you want to keep and private emails.

### Level 3: never

Anything that wants to sell you something, get you back into an app or provide you with news that you can read yourself: shopping, games, streaming recommendations, vouchers. You can switch off these messages completely. The app still works, it just no longer reports itself.

### This is how you go through the list

1. **Open your phone's notification settings.** All apps are usually listed there. The menus are called slightly differently depending on the manufacturer and version; you can find them by searching in the settings using terms like “Notifications” or “Notifications”.
2. **Start with level 3.** Switching off is done quickly and immediately brings noticeable calm.
3. **Set level 2 to quiet.** Sound, vibration and banner off, display in the overview on.
4. **Critically check level 1.** Do you really need a sound for every message from every chat, or only for certain people?
5. **Check the app itself.** Many apps let you turn off individual types of notifications, such as advertising, while keeping the delivery status.
6. **For new apps, say no first.** If a newly installed app asks if it can send you notifications, decline. You can always allow it later if you're really missing something.

## Focus and Do Not Disturb modes with exceptions

The three levels regulate everyday life. For times when you don't want to be disturbed, almost all smartphones have a mode that, depending on the device, is called "Do Not Disturb", "Focus" or something similar. How exactly it is set up differs between manufacturers and versions. But the basic idea is the same everywhere: everything is silent except what you explicitly allow.

### Set exceptions

A mode that blocks everything is often quickly turned off again for fear of missing something important. Therefore, set exceptions:

- **Important contacts:** Partner, children, parents you care for, daycare or school. On many devices, you mark them as favorites or add them as allowed contacts directly in the mode.
- **Repeated calls:** You can often set up a second call from the same number to go through within a short period of time. This way, someone who isn't on your list can reach you if it's really urgent.
- **Certain apps:** for example calendar or alarm clock.

Also, keep official alerts turned on. They are one of the few messages that you should never filter out.

### Fixed times instead of pressing a button

The most reliable mode is one that switches on automatically. Typical time windows are:

| mode | When | Who is allowed through? |
|---|---|---|
| night | for example from the evening routine until after breakfast | Favorites, repeat calls, alarm clock |
| Concentration | one or two solid blocks a day | Favorites, calendar |
| Closing time | after work hours | Family and friends, no work apps |

Tell the people you care about that you have times like these and how they can still reach you in an emergency. That takes away everyone's worries. If you would like to expand such quiet windows into real breaks, you can find suggestions in [Cellphone-free times](/ratgeber/handyfreie-zeiten/).

## Mute group chats and messengers without appearing rude

Group chats are often the loudest sources of all: the parent group in the class, the sports club, colleagues, the household. Every message is harmless, but overall the cell phone vibrates constantly. At the same time, many people fear appearing rude if they don't respond immediately.

### Which helps technically

- **Mute groups:** In common messengers you can mute individual chats permanently or for a certain time. The messages continue to arrive, just without sound.
- **Allow mentions:** Some messengers will still notify you if someone addresses you directly by name or replies to your message. Check if yours can do that. This way you won't miss anything that affects you personally.
- **Rethink read receipts:** If you feel pressured because others see that you have read but not responded, you can turn off this feature in many messengers.

### What helps humanly

Silence seems particularly rude when no one knows where they stand. A short sentence provides clarity:

> "I check in on the group twice a day, usually at lunchtime and in the evening. If something is urgent, please call me directly."

Answer collectedly when you look into it anyway. Most groups get used to it quickly. For arrangements such as who will bring which cake to the summer party, a direct call is often quicker than twenty messages.

## Counter icons and lock screen

Don't just interrupt sounds. Red numbers on the app icons and a full lock screen also attract attention every time you pick up the phone.

### Turn off counter symbols

The number on a symbol tells you: Something is waiting here. This is rarely true with a shopping app, it just feels that way. Therefore, switch off the counters for all level 2 and 3 apps; you can keep them for telephone and individual chats. The setting is usually in the notification settings of the respective app, often under terms such as “license” or “badges”.

### Calm the lock screen

- **Hide previews:** Set message content to only appear when the device is unlocked. This also protects your privacy when the cell phone is on the table.
- **Fewer apps on the lock screen:** Level 2 apps don't have to appear there, the notification overview is enough.
- **Turn your phone over or put it away:** A device with the screen facing down or in your pocket attracts less attention than one that lights up with every notification.

If you wear a smartwatch, you should only allow level 1 there. Otherwise the restlessness simply moves from the cell phone to the wrist.

## Notifications on the work computer

There are often even more messages on the computer than on the cell phone: email programs, chat tools, calendars, browsers, updates. You often do exactly the tasks on the computer that require quiet time.

1. **Email without banners and sound.** New emails don't have to interrupt you immediately. Check your inbox at regular times. The guide [clean up your email inbox](/ratgeber/e-mail-postfach-aufraeumen/) explains how to organize your inbox so that it works well.
2. **Target chat tools.** Turn off notifications for major channels and only let direct messages and mentions through. Many programs also allow notifications for certain keywords, such as your project name.
3. **Use statuses.** A status like “Concentrated work until 11 a.m.” shows others why you don't respond immediately.
4. **Decline browser notifications.** Many websites ask if they can send you communications. Decline and check the browser settings to see which sites you previously allowed.
5. **Use the operating system's sleep mode.** Common operating systems have their own mode that suppresses messages for a certain period of time. Combine it with a block in your calendar.

Clarify within the team which method applies to things that are really urgent, for example a phone call instead of a chat message. It is best to discuss directly what availability your employer expects.

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

- **Turn everything off at once:** If you deactivate every message in one evening, you might miss the message from the bank or the message from the school and then turn everything back on in frustration. Follow the three steps.
- **Use the focus mode without exceptions:** Then you're left worrying about missing something important and you're constantly checking again.
- **Constant checking instead of messages:** If you turn off the sounds but check the apps yourself every five minutes, you have just postponed the interruption. Fixed times for checking help.

## Frequently asked questions

### Am I missing something important?

Important things reach you via level 1, your exceptions in focus mode and repeated calls. You'll see what's in level 2 the next time you look at your cell phone.

### What do I do if my job requires constant availability?

Then the work channels during working hours belong to level 1. However, if possible, separate professional and private messages, for example with your own focus profile for work, and turn them off after work, if this has been agreed with your employer.

## Briefly summarized

Take stock of the noisy apps and assign each one to a level: immediately for what is really urgent, collected and quiet for everything that can wait, never for advertising and lure calls. Set up focus times that turn on automatically and let important people through. Mute group chats and be open about when you're checking in. Turn off counter symbols and previews and also put the work computer to rest. So you decide when to turn to whom.
