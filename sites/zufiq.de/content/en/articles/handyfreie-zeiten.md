The smartphone is a great tool. It puts maps, timetables, photos, news, music and the people we love in our pockets. At the same time, it has the ability to slip into every free minute: at the traffic lights, at breakfast, while waiting for the pasta water, in bed, in the middle of a conversation. Many people don't realize how often they use their cell phones until they try not to.

Mobile-free times are fixed islands in the day when the device has a break. It's not about demonizing your smartphone or going on a complete digital detox that you break off after three days. It's about small, reliable spaces in which your attention belongs to yourself and the people around you. This guide will show you which times are particularly suitable, how you can anchor them in your everyday life and what helps if your hand still twitches.

## Why fixed times work better than resolutions

“I want to spend less time on my cell phone” is a good resolution, but a weak plan. He doesn't say when, where or how. Every time your cell phone buzzes, you have to make a new decision, and that takes energy. Fixed cell phone-free times make this decision for you. They work like a habit: once established, they become natural over time.

There is also a second effect. Anyone who is always available is unconsciously constantly waiting for the next message. This constant attention is exhausting, even if the cell phone remains silent. Fixed breaks give the signal to the head: Nothing is coming now, now you can switch off.

## The best moments for cell phone-free times

Not every time of day is equally suitable. Moments when your cell phone replaces something that is actually important to you are particularly rewarding.

| Wait | Why it is suitable | Simple rule |
|---|---|---|
| The first half hour after waking up | The day begins with your thoughts instead of with strangers' news | Phone stays in airplane mode until you get dressed and have breakfast |
| Meals | Conversations and enjoyment instead of casual eating | No cell phones at the table, not even with the display facing down |
| Conversations with partners, children, friends | Full attention is a sign of appreciation | Cell phone stays in your pocket or in another room |
| Concentrated work | Interruptions take time until you get back into it | Focus mode or cell phone out of sight |
| Movement and nature | Walks, sports and gardening become more relaxing | Leave your cell phone at home or only take it with you in emergencies |
| The last hour before bed | Quieter transition into the night | Charge your cell phone outside the bedroom |

You don't have to introduce all the islands at once. Choose two that are particularly important to you and start there.

## This is how you introduce cell phone-free times

### 1. Observe honestly

Most smartphones show how much time you spend on which apps and how often you unlock the device. Look at these numbers for a week without changing anything. Also note the situations in which you reach for your cell phone for no real reason: boredom, restlessness, habit, discomfort in conversations. This observation shows you where cell phone-free times are most beneficial. The guide [Reduce screen time] (/ratgeber/bildschirmzeit-reduzieren/) describes how you can then specifically reduce the numbers.

### 2. Set specific times

The more precise, the better. Instead of “less cell phone in the evening” it would be better to say: “From 9:30 p.m. the cell phone is in the kitchen.” Instead of “don’t look at your cell phone while you eat,” it’s better to say: “At dinner, all cell phones are on the shelf in the hallway.”

### 3. Create a permanent place

A cell phone that is within reach magically attracts your hand. A fixed location out of reach makes cell phone-free time almost effortless. This could be a bowl in the hallway, a charging station in the kitchen or a drawer in the living room. Plus, if the phone is charging there, it has a practical reason to stay there.

### 4. Let the technology work for you

Modern smartphones have functions that support cell phone-free times:

- **Focus or Do Not Disturb Mode:** Activate automatically at fixed times
- **Exceptions for important contacts:** Family or care facility comes through, everything else waits
- **App Limits:** certain apps are blocked after a set time
- **Grayscale mode:** a colorless screen looks significantly less attractive
- **Reduce notifications:** How to do this can be found in the guide [Taming notifications](/ratgeber/benachrichtigungen-zaehmen/).

### 5. Have a replacement ready

We often reach for our cell phones out of habit because our hands or our heads are looking for something to do. It is helpful to have a replacement ready for typical situations: a book on the bedside table, a notebook for thoughts, a crossword puzzle for the train, a classic alarm clock instead of the cell phone alarm clock, a wristwatch instead of looking at the display.

## Remain available where necessary

Many people hesitate to have cell phone-free times because they are afraid of missing something important. This is a legitimate concern, for example for parents, people with relatives in need of care or those on call. The solution is not to always be available, but rather targeted:

- Create a small list of people whose calls always come through.
- Tell the most important people when you are cell phone-free and how they can reach you in an emergency.
- If in doubt, use a simple second device or the landline for emergencies.

After a few weeks, it often becomes clear that really urgent messages are rare, and they still reach you.

## Cell phone-free times in family and relationships

Shared rules have a stronger effect than lonely resolutions because no one has to be the first to pick up their cell phone. The following have proven themselves:

- **A cell phone-free dining table for everyone**, including adults. Children learn more from example than from rules.
- **A shared charging area** outside of the bedrooms, especially for teenagers.
- **Cell phone-free trips** on weekends that include a single cell phone for emergencies and photos.
- **One evening a week without screens**, such as playing board games, cooking or taking a walk.

Talk about the rules instead of prescribing them. Ask yourself together what you miss when everyone is looking at their cell phones and what you would like to do instead. Those who have a say in decisions are more likely to stick to them.

## What to do if your hand still twitches

The impulse to reach for your cell phone doesn't disappear overnight. It will get weaker over time if you don't give in to it every time. These thoughts help:

- **Noticing the impulse without following it.** Say internally: “Ah, there’s the cell phone.” Often this observation is enough for him to pass by.
- **Wait briefly.** A few deep breaths or a glass of water create distance from the impulse.
- **Wondering what's behind it.** Bored? Fatigue? Restlessness? Sometimes the honest answer is a short break or a walk.
- **Take relapses in stride.** If you do pick up your cell phone, nothing is lost. Put it back and move on.

## Accompany children and young people

Children grow up with smartphones, and many parents struggle to establish good rules. Cell phone-free times are often easier to enforce than rigid minute quotas because they are clear and the same for everyone. It is helpful to explain the reasons: we want to talk while we eat, our bodies need rest at night, and concentration helps with homework. The older the children are, the more they can have a say in decisions.

For younger children, specialists usually recommend limiting screen time according to their age and not leaving devices in the children's room overnight. Offers such as the “Look!” initiative offer concrete orientation. or the Federal Center for Health Education, which publish recommendations by age group. A jointly agreed media usage agreement, as offered by various initiatives as a template, makes rules binding and comprehensible.

But the most effective means remains your own role model. When parents look at the display during dinner, no rule works. When they consciously put their cell phones away, children understand without many words that there are times that belong to other things.

## Cell phone-free times at work

Cell phone-free phases are also worthwhile at work, especially for tasks that require concentration. Many people underestimate how long it takes to get back to business after an interruption. Fixed focus blocks of one to two hours during which the private cell phone stays in the pocket and notifications on the computer are paused are helpful. Teams can agree when messages are expected and when they are not, such as no chat messages after hours except in emergencies. If you travel a lot, you can consciously use the train journey as cell phone-free time to read or think instead of filling it with scrolling.

## A plan for the first four weeks

| week | New island | Support |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cell phone-free meals | Fixed storage location in the hallway |
| 2 | Cell phone not in the bedroom | Charging station in the kitchen, analogue alarm clock |
| 3 | Cell phone-free first half hour in the morning | Flight mode overnight, focus mode until 7:30 a.m |
| 4 | One cell phone-free walk per week | Leave your cell phone at home and wear a watch |

After four weeks you look back: Which islands feel good? Which ones need adjustment? The experiences flow well into a weekly review.

## Frequently asked questions

### Isn't a complete digital detox more effective?

A weekend without a cell phone can be a nice experience and shows how it feels. However, small, regular breaks are usually more sustainable in everyday life because they become habits instead of remaining an exception.

### How do I explain to friends that I don't respond immediately?

Honest and friendly: “I don’t look at my cell phone in the evening anymore, I’ll get in touch tomorrow.” Most people react with understanding, some even feel inspired.

### What about music or audio books to help you fall asleep?

If they help you, that's fine. Use a sleep timer, turn off notifications and put your phone out of reach so that the audio book doesn't scroll again.

### Does a simple second cell phone without internet help?

For some people, yes. A simple push-button cell phone for calls and text messages allows you to be reached without the temptation of apps and feeds, for example for hikes, vacations or cell phone-free weekends. In everyday life, however, a well-set focus mode on your smartphone is enough for most people.
