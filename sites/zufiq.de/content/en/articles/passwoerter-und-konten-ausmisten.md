How many online accounts do you have? Most people estimate a number around twenty. If you count, you often come up with a hundred or more: the online shop where you ordered printer cartridges eight years ago, the forum for the old garden hose, the fitness app from your January resolution, three different streaming services, the account at the furniture store, the airline customer account, the competition, the library, the letter from the school to parents. Each of these accounts is a small door to your data, and many of these doors have long been open without you even thinking about it.

Clearing out accounts therefore brings double peace of mind: less digital ballast and more security. This guide will show you how to find your accounts, delete unnecessary accounts, secure the remaining ones well and keep track of them with a password manager.

## Why old accounts are a risk

A forgotten account doesn't bother you as long as nothing happens. It becomes problematic if the provider falls victim to a data leak. Then the email address, password and sometimes also address, date of birth or payment details end up in the wrong hands. If you use the same password in several places, in the worst case scenario you will open your email inbox or bank account. Attackers automatically try out stolen access data on many services.

There's also advertising: Many old accounts send newsletters, reminders and offers that fill your inbox. And some continue as a subscription that you have long forgotten about.

## Step 1: Find your accounts

Nobody has a complete list of their accounts in their head. These sources will help you find:

| Source | How you use them |
|---|---|
| Email inbox | Search for words like “welcome”, “registration”, “confirm account”, “reset password” |
| Browser | Saved passwords in the browser show many accounts at a glance |
| cell phone | List of installed apps and saved logins |
| “Sign in with” services | In the settings of your Google, Apple or other account you can see which services you have signed in to |
| Bank statements | Recurring charges show paid services |

Write all the accounts found in a list, for example in a table with the columns: service, email address, important yes or no, action. You don't have to do it in one evening. Start with the mailbox, you'll find most of them there.

## Step 2: Sort into three groups

Go through your list and assign each account to a group:

1. **Essential:** Email, online banking, authorities, health insurance, mobile communications, important shops that you use regularly
2. **Occasionally useful:** Services that you need from time to time, such as a travel portal or a craftsman portal
3. **Superfluous:** Anything you haven't used in years or no longer need

The third group is usually the largest. This is where the greatest potential for peace and security lies.

## Step 3: Delete unnecessary accounts

Many services allow deletion directly in the account settings. Search for terms like “delete account,” “close account,” or “privacy.” Sometimes the option is well hidden. These ways help:

- **Service Help Page:** Search for “Delete Account”.
- **Data protection declaration:** It tells you how you can request deletion of your data.
- **Email the data protection officer:** According to the General Data Protection Regulation, you have the right to have your personal data deleted in many cases. A short, friendly email asking for deletion and confirmation is usually enough.

Before you delete, check:

- Are there still credits, vouchers or bonus points that you would like to use?
- Do you need invoices or receipts from the account, for example for warranty or tax? Download it beforehand.
- Is there an ongoing subscription tied to the account? Cancel it first. The guide [Check and cancel subscriptions] (/ratgeber/abos-pruefen-und-kuendigen/) provides help.

If an account simply cannot be deleted, at least change the password to a unique one, remove saved payment information, and delete personal information that is not absolutely necessary.

## Step 4: Secure the remaining accounts

### Unique, long passwords

A separate password for each account is the most important rule. A good password is, above all, long. The Federal Office for Information Security lists various ways to create secure passwords, such as long passwords consisting of several words or shorter, more complex character strings. The easiest way is to have passwords generated by a password manager.

### Two-factor login

Two-factor registration, also known as two-factor authentication, requires a second piece of evidence in addition to the password, such as a code from an app, confirmation on the cell phone or a security key. Even if someone knows your password, they can't easily get into your account. Activate it at least on:

- your email account, because almost all other passwords can be reset via it
- Online banking and payment services (here it is usually mandatory anyway)
- Cloud storage with photos and documents
- social networks and messengers

Save the recovery codes that many services display during setup in a safe place, such as your password manager or printed out in the [Emergency Folder](/ratgeber/notfallordner-anlegen/).

### Passkeys

More and more services are offering passkeys. You log in without a password, for example with your device's fingerprint or facial recognition. Passkeys are considered safe against phishing because they are tied to the real website. If a major service supports passkeys, it's worth checking out.

## Step 5: Set up a password manager

A password manager is an encrypted vault for your access data. You just remember a strong master password, the rest is saved, generated and inserted for you.

| Art | Examples of characteristics | Suitable for whom |
|---|---|---|
| Built into the browser or operating system | free, well integrated, synchronized via your own account | everyone who predominantly uses an ecosystem |
| Standalone app | cross-platform, family sharing, emergency access | Households with different devices |
| Local solution without cloud | Data only on your own devices | People who want to secure themselves |

When choosing, pay attention to good encryption, two-factor protection for the safe itself and an option for emergency access by a trusted person. To make the switch, do this:

1. Set master password: long, unique, not used anywhere else. Additionally, write it down and store it in a safe place.
2. Import saved passwords from the browser if the manager supports it.
3. The next time you log in, set a new, generated password for each service. Start with the most important accounts.
4. Disable browser storage if you only use the manager in the future so that there are no two locations.

## Step 6: Check whether data is affected

There are reputable services you can use to check whether your email address has appeared in known data breaches. Well-known offers for this come from the Hasso Plattner Institute. Many password managers also automatically warn if a saved password has been leaked or is being used multiple times. If an account is affected, change the password there and everywhere else you used it.

## Keep track

- **Create new accounts consciously:** Before registering, ask yourself whether guest access is enough.
- **Having your own email address for shops and newsletters** keeps your main inbox quieter. How to clear up your inbox can be found in the guide [clean up your email inbox](/ratgeber/e-mail-postfach-aufraeumen/).
- **Annual check:** Once a year, go through the list in the password manager and delete what you no longer need.
- **Regulate digital inheritance:** Determine who has access in an emergency. Many password managers offer an emergency function for this.

## Recognize phishing: The best security is of little use if you give out your password voluntarily

Many accounts are not cracked, but fished out. Fraudulent emails, SMS or messenger messages pretend to be a bank, parcel service, streaming provider or government agency and lure you to a fake login page. Typical features are:

- **Pressure and Rush:** “Your account will be blocked in 24 hours”, “Last reminder”, “Payment failed”.
- **Impersonal salutation** or noticeable errors in language and design, although good counterfeits are now often error-free.
- **Links whose address does not match the sender.** Hover over the link or press and hold on your phone to see the real address.
- **Requests to enter access data, TANs or codes** or to give them by telephone. Reputable providers never ask you for your password or your TANs.

The safest habit: Don't click on links in unexpected messages, but open the provider's website or app itself as you normally do. A password manager also helps because it automatically inserts access data only on the real website. If the field on a supposed bank page remains empty, this is a warning signal. Consumer advice centers regularly publish current warnings about phishing waves.

## An example: An evening with the account list

A typical evening shows what clearing out feels like. A search of the inbox for “welcome” and “registration” reveals eighty accounts. Of these, twelve are indispensable, fifteen are occasionally useful and over fifty are unnecessary. Around twenty of them can be deleted in two hours, mostly via the account settings, some via a short email to the data protection contact. The rest will have their turn the following evenings. New, generated passwords are set for the twelve most important accounts and two-factor login is activated, starting with the email account. The end result is not a perfect situation, but a significantly smaller and safer one.

## Typical mistakes

**One password for everything.** This is the most common and dangerous mistake.

**Passwords in plain text on your phone.** Note apps are not a safe.

**Two-factor only half set up.** Without secured recovery codes, a lost phone can lock you out.

**Delete accounts without backing up data.** Download invoices and receipts beforehand.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often do I have to change passwords?

Changing it regularly without reason is no longer recommended today if the password is strong and unique. But change it immediately if a service has been hacked or you suspect it.

### Isn't a password manager a risk because everything is in one place?

A good manager encrypts the data so that even the provider cannot read it. The risk of using passwords multiple times or choosing weak ones is usually greater in practice. Protect the vault with a strong master password and two-factor login.

### What if I forget my master password?

Many managers cannot restore it due to security reasons. Therefore, keep it on paper in a safe place and, if possible, set up an emergency contact.
