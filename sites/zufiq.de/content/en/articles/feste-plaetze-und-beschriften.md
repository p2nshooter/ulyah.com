On Saturday you cleaned up, on Wednesday the mail is back on the kitchen table, the scissors have disappeared and the chair in the bedroom has a mountain of clothes on it. This is rarely due to a lack of discipline. Usually something else is missing: a fixed place for every thing. Tidying up means putting things away. Order means that every thing knows where it belongs. Without this home, the mess starts over again every time you clean up.

This guide explains the principle of fixed places, shows you how to allocate places sensibly, when labels are worthwhile and how to include roommates. Finally, you'll learn a short daily routine that holds everything together.

## Why things move without a fixed place

Every time you hold something in your hand that doesn't have a permanent home, you have to make a small decision: what to do with it? After a long day, the simplest answer is “here first”. This is how clipboards are created: the kitchen table, the step, the chest of drawers in the hallway, the corner of the desk. “For now” quickly becomes “for weeks”.

In addition, where there is no fixed place, the same object ends up somewhere else every time. The scissors are sometimes in the kitchen drawer, sometimes on the desk, sometimes in the craft basket. Searching not only takes time but also nerves, especially in the morning when you actually have to go. In households with several people, everyone has their own idea of ​​where something belongs, and things literally wander around the apartment.

A fixed place takes this decision away from you. Putting things back becomes a habit that hardly requires any thought.

## The basic principle: Every thing has a home

The principle is simple: everything you own has exactly one place where it lies when it is not in use. To make this work in everyday life, three rules help:

1. **The place is where you use the thing.** The key belongs on the apartment door, not in the bedroom. Stamps and envelopes are where you do mail.
2. **Putting it back must be easier than leaving it lying down.** A space that you can only reach by opening a door, removing a lid, and pulling out a box is not being used. One handle is ideal, two is fine.
3. **Space limits quantity.** When the cable drawer is full, it is full. New things only come in when old things go. This means that the fixed space also becomes a natural upper limit.

Before you allocate places, it's worth taking a look at what should stay. The fewer things, the easier it is for each one to find its place. How to do this is described in [Start clearing out](/ratgeber/ausmisten-anfangen/).

## Spaces allocated according to usage and grip height

Not every place is equally accessible. The most valuable storage space is between waist and eye level and close to where you need something. What you use every day belongs there. Everything else moves up, down or further away.

| zone | Where | How often used | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Close | between hip and eye level, directly at the point of use | daily or several times a week | Keys, favorite mugs, charging cable, notepad |
| Means | upper and lower compartments, cupboards in the same room | weekly to monthly | Guest towels, tools, baking pans, spare batteries |
| Fern | at the top, at the bottom, basement, storage room | less often than monthly or only seasonally | Christmas decorations, suitcases, winter clothes in summer, documents from previous years |

Heavy things belong on the bottom, even if you use them often. This protects your back and is safer than pulling it upside down from a high compartment.

### Step by step to new places

1. **Choose an area.** For example, a kitchen cabinet, the hallway dresser or the desk.
2. **Observe for a week.** What do you reach for every day and what rarely? Where do you put things after you use them? These storage areas tell you where there is missing space.
3. **Distribute by zone.** Frequently forward and at handle height, rarely backwards, up or down.
4. **Test the new places.** Leave them like this for at least a week. If you keep looking at the old spot or something gets left behind, the location may be inconvenient.
5. **Correct without ambition.** A place that doesn't work isn't a failure, it's information. Move it to where the thing ends up anyway.

An example from everyday life: Lena always puts her glasses on the armrest of the sofa in the evening and looks for them the next morning. Instead of admonishing herself every day, she puts a small bowl on the side table next to the sofa. The glasses have been there ever since because the place is exactly where she puts them.

## Create categories and store them together

Fixed places work best when not every single thing has its own place, but rather every category. All batteries in one place, tape and scissors in one drawer, all charging cables in one box. So you don't have to remember a hundred places, just a few.

So that categories carry in everyday life:

- **Choose understandable names.** Everyone in the household understands “office”, “first aid” or “wrapping paper”. “Miscellaneous” and “junk,” on the other hand, quickly become a collection point for everything.
- **Do not divide too finely.** One box of “cables and chargers” works better than five boxes for different types of plugs. You can still sort more finely if a category becomes too large.
- **Gather supplies.** Spare batteries, light bulbs, tissues: Whatever you have on hand is in one place. This way you can see what's there and don't buy anything twice.
- **Allow deliberate duplication.** One pair of scissors in the kitchen and one at the desk can be useful if you often need them in both places. The only important thing is that everyone has their own fixed place.

### A place for things in transit

Some things shouldn't stay at all: the borrowed book that goes back to the neighbor, the package to be returned, the shoes to the shoemaker, the bottles to the deposit machine. They often lie in the way for days because they don't belong anywhere. So give them their own category: an exit basket or a compartment near the apartment door. Whatever is there, you take with you on the next suitable route. Take a look inside once a week so that the basket doesn't become storage itself.

## Labels: Methods and when they are worthwhile

Labels make places visible. They help with searching, but especially with putting things back, and they also show others in the household where something belongs. Not everything needs to be labeled: plates in the plate cupboard are self-explanatory.

Labels are particularly worthwhile for:

- opaque crates and boxes
- shared areas such as pantry cupboards, hallway cupboards or tool compartments
- rarely used things in the basement and storage room
- Drawers whose contents your roommates don't know

| Method | Good for | Boundaries |
|---|---|---|
| Labels, handwritten or printed | permanent categories, uniform image | more complicated to change |
| Masking tape and pen | Test phases, quick changes, moving boxes | looks temporary, comes loose over time |
| Photo of the contents on the outside of the box | Basement, attic, large opaque boxes | must be renewed if the content changes |
| Colors, such as glue dots or colored boxes | Assignment to people or general areas | the meaning must be known to everyone |
| Symbols or small images | People who don't read yet or don't read well | need a little more space |

### Practical tips

- **Start with masking tape.** Only after a place has proven itself for a few weeks is a nicer label worth it.
- **Label the category, not each item.** “Sewing kit” is enough. You only need a contents list for boxes in the basement.
- **Place the label prominently.** On the side you see when the box is on the shelf, not on the lid of a stacked box.
- **Stick with the same words.** If one box is called “tools,” don’t call the second “craftsman’s stuff.”
- **Write big enough.** A label that you can only read with your nose on the shelf doesn't help anyone.

## Involve roommates and family

A system that only one person knows doesn't last long. If you live with others, decide together where shared things should fit. Based on everyone's habits: If everyone leaves their bags at the bottom of the stairs, that might be exactly the right place for a hook.

Explain why fixed spaces are important to you instead of just setting rules. “I don’t want to look for the key in the morning” is more convincing than “It belongs there.” Labels help anyone who doesn't know where something belongs and make the system independent of the one person who has everything in their head.

It is also helpful to have a separate storage space for each person, such as a basket or a compartment where things that are on the move can end up. This prevents things lying around from being spread everywhere. Children do well with pictures on boxes and low places that they can reach themselves.

## The daily clean-up routine

Even the best system needs a few minutes of maintenance. A short walk in the evening ensures that every day begins in a quiet apartment. This is what it can look like:

1. **Take a basket.** Walk around the apartment with it and collect everything that is out of place.
2. **Return each thing.** Room by room without getting lost in other tasks.
3. **Empty the clipboards.** The kitchen table, hallway dresser and sofa are freed up.
4. **Note missing spaces.** If you don't know where to put something in your hand, it needs a space. Write it down and worry about it over the weekend.

With a little practice, this round often only takes a few minutes. It fits well into an [evening routine](/ratgeber/abendroutine-entwickeln/), for example between dinner and the end of the day on the sofa. During the day, a simple sentence helps: don't put it down, put it away. If you already have something in your hand, put it straight into its place.

## Typical mistakes and how to avoid them

- **Buy boxes first, then think about it:** Clean them out first and decide what remains. Then you know which containers you really need.
- **Allocate spaces based on appearance rather than usage:** A pretty shelf in the living room is of little use if you need the item in the hallway.
- **Too many levels:** Box within a box in the closet behind a door. The more actions you take, the more likely something will get left behind.
- **Simply expand full spaces:** If a category is constantly overflowing, it is usually not the space that is too small, but the inventory that is too large.
- **Expect a system for all time:** Life circumstances change, and with them places. A quick look per season keeps the system up to date.

## Briefly summarized

Order remains intact when every thing has a permanent home. Assign spaces where you use something and according to frequency: everyday items at arm's length, rare items upstairs, downstairs or in the basement. Group similar things into categories and label anything that isn't obvious at the beginning with masking tape. Get everyone in the household involved and give the apartment a quick clean-up every evening. Then you will have the peace and quiet that you earned while tidying up.
