Aerothermal energy — *aerotermia*, as it is sold in Spain — has become one of the buzzwords of heating and cooling. It is presented as the alternative to gas and oil boilers, it is required in much new-build housing and it appears in many grants for energy renovation. But what exactly is it, what does it cost, how much does it save and in which homes does it make sense?

This guide explains how an air-source heat pump works, the types of system, the efficiency ratings, installation and running costs, the most suitable emitters, and the cases in which it is worth it and those in which it is not.

## What aerothermal heating is

Aerothermal heating is a technology that uses a **heat pump** to extract thermal energy from the outdoor air and move it into the home, both for space heating and for domestic hot water and, in many cases, for cooling in summer.

Surprising as it may seem, outdoor air holds usable energy even at sub-zero temperatures. Using a circuit with a refrigerant gas, a compressor and heat exchangers, the heat pump "pumps" that heat indoors at a useful temperature.

### Why it is so efficient

A heat pump does not generate heat by burning fuel or with an electric element: it **moves** heat. For every kWh of electricity it uses, it can deliver between 3 and 5 kWh of heat, depending on conditions. That performance is expressed with the following ratings.

## COP, SCOP and other ratings

| Rating | What it measures |
|---|---|
| COP | Instantaneous heating performance under specific test conditions |
| SCOP | Average seasonal heating performance over a typical winter |
| EER | Instantaneous cooling performance |
| SEER | Seasonal cooling performance |

A **SCOP of 4** means that over the season the unit delivers on average 4 kWh of heat for every kWh of electricity. Performance drops when it is very cold outside and when the emitters are asked for very hot water.

## Types of aerothermal system

### Air-to-water

This is what is normally meant by "aerothermal". The heat pump heats water that circulates through:

- **Underfloor heating:** the ideal emitter, because it works with low-temperature water (30 to 40 °C), which maximises efficiency.
- **Low-temperature radiators or fan coils:** suited to water at 40 to 50 °C.
- **Conventional radiators:** these can work in well-insulated homes or with oversized radiators, though less efficiently.

It also produces **domestic hot water** through a storage cylinder.

**Configurations:**

- **Monobloc:** the whole refrigerant circuit sits in the outdoor unit; only water runs through the house.
- **Split (bibloc):** an outdoor unit and an indoor unit connected by refrigerant pipes.

### Air-to-air

These are air-conditioning units with a heat pump (splits and ducted systems). They heat and cool the air directly. In the broad sense they are aerothermal too, and they are very efficient as heating. See [air conditioning: consumption and efficient use](/guias/aire-acondicionado-consumo-y-uso-eficiente/).

### Heat pump water heaters

Compact units that only produce domestic hot water, with a built-in cylinder. They are a very efficient alternative to an electric water heater. See [hot water: electric water heaters and the alternatives](/guias/agua-caliente-termo-electrico-y-alternativas/).

## What installation costs

| System | Indicative installed cost |
|---|---|
| Air-to-water heat pump for heating and hot water in a home (6 to 12 kW), keeping the radiators | €8,000 to €14,000 |
| With new underfloor heating | €14,000 to €22,000 or more |
| Heat pump for hot water only | €1,500 to €3,000 |
| Air-to-air with splits (2 or 3 indoor units) | €2,500 to €6,000 |

The cost depends on the output, the brand, whether the emitters need adapting, the space for the cylinder and how complex the work is.

## What it costs to run

Let us compare the cost of producing 10,000 kWh of useful heat a year — a reasonable figure for an average-sized home in a cold climate — at indicative prices:

| System | Efficiency | Energy used | Price | Annual cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Old oil boiler | 85 % | 11,765 kWh | €0.10/kWh | €1,180 |
| Condensing natural-gas boiler | 95 % | 10,526 kWh | €0.08/kWh | €840 |
| Electric radiators | 100 % | 10,000 kWh | €0.16/kWh | €1,600 |
| Heat pump with a SCOP of 3.5 | 350 % | 2,857 kWh | €0.16/kWh | €460 |

Energy prices change, but the order usually holds: a heat pump is far cheaper to run than electric radiators and heating oil, and it is usually cheaper than gas, especially when combined with solar panels or a well-chosen electricity tariff. And without gas, the fixed gas charge and the boiler inspections disappear.

## When it pays off

A heat pump is usually a good investment when:

- **It replaces electric radiators, heating oil or propane.**
- **The home is reasonably well insulated** or is about to be insulated.
- **There is underfloor heating** or radiators that can work at low temperature.
- **It is combined with solar self-consumption.** See [solar self-consumption](/guias/autoconsumo-solar-guia-completa/).
- **It is also used for cooling** in summer.
- **It is part of a full renovation,** where the extra cost is smaller.

It may not pay off, or may need a closer look, when:

- The home is very poorly insulated and has small radiators that need water at 70 °C.
- Heating demand is very low, for example in very mild climates.
- There is no room for the outdoor unit or the cylinder, or the owners' association will not allow the outdoor unit on the façade.

## Sizing and installation

A badly sized heat pump installation loses much of its advantage. Ask the installer to:

- Calculate the home's **heat losses** room by room.
- Check whether the existing **emitters** can work at the planned temperature.
- Size the hot-water **cylinder** for the number of people in the household.
- Place the **outdoor unit** somewhere ventilated, where air does not recirculate, with a drain for the defrost water.
- Take **noise** into account: the outdoor unit sounds much like an air conditioner; keep a sensible distance from neighbours' windows.
- Set up the **heating curve**, which adjusts the water temperature to the outdoor temperature.

## Using it efficiently

- **A steady temperature:** a heat pump works best keeping the house at a stable temperature rather than with long on and off periods.
- **The lowest water temperature** that keeps you comfortable.
- **Hot water at 45 to 50 °C,** with regular anti-legionella cycles.
- **Use the sunny hours or the off-peak hours** to heat the cylinder.
- **Maintenance:** clean the filters and check the circuit pressure and the outdoor unit.

## Electrical capacity

Installing a heat pump may mean reviewing your contracted electrical capacity. Many units have a backup electric element that is best left off except in emergencies. See [how much power to contract](/guias/que-potencia-contratar/).

## Case study: replacing an oil boiler

A 140-square-metre detached house in Soria had an oil boiler from 2003 and cast-iron radiators. It used about 1,900 litres of heating oil a year, around €1,900 depending on each season's price.

After insulating the loft and replacing the ground-floor windows, the owners installed a 12 kW air-to-water heat pump with a 200-litre cylinder, keeping the radiators, which were large. The heating curve was set to send water at 45 °C on mild days and at 55 °C on the coldest days.

In the first winter the heat pump used about 6,200 kWh of electricity for heating and hot water, around €1,100 on their tariff. Combined with 4 kWp of solar panels that cover much of the hot water in summer, the home's yearly energy spending fell to less than half, and the oil tank disappeared from the garage.

## Checklist before you sign

- Ask for a heat-loss calculation for each room.
- Check whether your radiators can work at low temperature.
- Choose the position of the outdoor unit with noise in mind.
- Review your contracted electrical capacity.
- Check which grants and tax deductions are available before signing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does it work below freezing?

Yes. Current units work down to -15 to -25 °C, although with lower efficiency. In very cold climates, sizing has to be done carefully.

### Do I need to change my radiators?

Not always. In well-insulated homes the existing radiators can work with water at 45 to 55 °C. In others it is worth replacing some with larger or low-temperature radiators.

### Is it noisy?

The outdoor unit makes a moderate noise. Modern units are fairly quiet, but where you put it matters.

### Are there grants for installing a heat pump?

There have been regional grants and tax deductions linked to improving the energy performance certificate. Check which ones are currently open. More in [the energy performance certificate of a home](/guias/certificado-energetico-de-la-vivienda/).

## In short

An air-source heat pump extracts energy from the outdoor air to produce heating, hot water and often cooling, delivering 3 to 5 kWh of heat for every kWh of electricity. It pays off best when it replaces electric radiators, heating oil or propane, in insulated homes with low-temperature emitters, and combined with solar panels. Insist on rigorous sizing, place the outdoor unit with care and run it at steady temperatures.
