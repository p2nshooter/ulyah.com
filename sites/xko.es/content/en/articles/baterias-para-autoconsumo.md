Home batteries promise something very attractive: storing the solar energy left over at midday to use at night and depending as little as possible on the grid. In practice they are an expensive component whose payback depends heavily on your consumption pattern, the size of the solar installation and what you are paid for your surplus.

This guide explains how batteries for self-consumption work, which types exist, what they cost, how to calculate the real saving and when they make sense. It also covers cheaper alternatives for making more of your solar energy.

## How a home battery works

During the day, when the panels produce more than the home is using, the spare energy charges the battery instead of being fed into the grid. When solar output falls, in the evening or at night, the battery discharges to power the house. Only when the battery runs out is energy taken from the grid.

### Components

- **Battery modules:** almost always lithium, specifically lithium iron phosphate (LFP), for its safety and lifespan.
- **Battery management system (BMS):** it controls the charging, discharging and temperature of the cells.
- **Hybrid inverter or battery charger:** it manages the flow of energy between panels, battery, house and grid.

### Hybrid inverter or AC coupling

- **Hybrid inverter:** a single unit manages both panels and battery. This is usual in new installations that include a battery or plan to add one.
- **AC coupling:** a battery inverter is added to an existing installation. Useful for adding a battery to panels already in place.

If you install panels without a battery but think you will add one later, ask for a compatible hybrid inverter.

## Capacity and power

Two figures define a battery:

- **Capacity (kWh):** how much energy it can store. Home batteries usually hold 5 to 15 kWh.
- **Power (kW):** how much energy it can deliver at once. It determines how many appliances it can run at the same time.

These matter too:

- **Usable depth of discharge:** LFP batteries let you use 90 % or more of their nominal capacity.
- **Round-trip efficiency:** 5 to 15 % of the energy is lost in the charge and discharge cycle.
- **Cycle life:** LFP batteries usually guarantee 6,000 to 10,000 cycles, or a percentage of capacity after 10 years.

## What they cost

| Capacity | Indicative installed cost, VAT included |
|---|---|
| 5 kWh | €3,000 to €5,000 |
| 10 kWh | €5,500 to €8,500 |
| 15 kWh | €8,000 to €11,500 |

The price per kWh has fallen in recent years and keeps falling, but per euro saved it is still the most expensive component of a self-consumption installation.

## What a battery really saves

A battery's saving is not the value of all the energy it stores, but the **difference** between:

- The price you would pay for that energy if you bought it from the grid at night.
- What you would have been paid for exporting it as surplus.

### Example

A home stores an average of 6 kWh a day for 300 days a year (in winter the battery does not always fill up): 1,800 kWh a year.

- Night-time purchase price with taxes: €0.14/kWh.
- Surplus compensation price: €0.06/kWh.
- Difference: €0.08/kWh.
- Efficiency losses of 10 %.
- Annual saving: 1,800 × 0.9 × 0.08 ≈ €130.

At a cost of €4,500 for a 7 kWh battery, simple payback is more than 30 years, longer than the life of the equipment. In this scenario the battery never pays for itself.

### When the numbers improve

- **Surplus that is lost:** if in many months you go over the compensation limit and have no virtual battery, every kWh stored is worth the full purchase price.
- **High night-time prices,** or a big gap between purchase and compensation prices.
- **Cheaper batteries** in future.
- **Grants** specifically for storage.
- **Backup during outages:** if you suffer frequent power cuts, the value of having electricity during a blackout is not measured in euros alone.

## Batteries and power cuts

Many people buy a battery thinking they will have power during a blackout. That is not always the case:

- For safety, a grid-connected inverter disconnects when the grid fails.
- Only systems with a **backup function** (backup or EPS) and the right wiring can keep powering the house or some circuits.
- The backup function may have limited power and a brief interruption when it switches over.

If backup matters to you, ask for it explicitly and check which circuits it will cover.

## Cheaper alternatives for making use of the sun

Before investing in a battery, consider these options:

### A virtual battery

Many retailers bank in euros the surplus that cannot be compensated. They store no physical energy, but you do not lose its value. Some charge a monthly fee; compare. See [surplus compensation](/guias/compensacion-de-excedentes/).

### A thermal battery: hot water

An electric water heater programmed to heat at midday, or better still a **heat pump water heater**, stores solar energy as heat at a far lower cost than a battery. See [hot water: electric water heaters and the alternatives](/guias/agua-caliente-termo-electrico-y-alternativas/).

### Load management

Programming the washing machine, dishwasher, dryer and oven for the sunny hours, or using smart plugs that switch on with surplus power, increases self-consumption at no cost.

### An electric car

Charging the car during the day with surplus power is the best "battery" there is, if the car is at home at those times.

## How to choose a battery if you decide to install one

- **LFP chemistry:** safer and longer-lasting than other lithium chemistries for home use.
- **Capacity matched to your night-time consumption:** work out how many kWh you use between sunset and morning. A battery that never fully discharges is oversized; so is one that never fills.
- **Compatibility with the inverter.**
- **Guarantee:** years, cycles and guaranteed residual capacity.
- **Location:** a ventilated spot, out of direct sun and away from extreme temperatures.
- **Modularity:** some batteries let you add modules later.

### Work out your night-time consumption

Using the hourly consumption from your distribution company, add up the consumption between 20:00 and 8:00 for several days in each season. In many flats and houses without electric heating it ranges from 3 to 7 kWh. A battery of 5 to 7 usable kWh would cover much of that in spring and summer.

### Example: the same house in summer and in winter

A home uses about 5 kWh each night, and its panels leave it about 10 kWh of surplus a day in the long days, but only 2 or 3 kWh in midwinter.

| Usable battery | Long days | Midwinter |
|---|---|---|
| 5 kWh | Fills and empties almost every day | Charges halfway |
| 10 kWh | Fills, but only half is used each night | Never gets past a quarter of its capacity |

The extra 5 kWh of the big battery hardly work all year: they cost money and add no saving. For this house, the sensible choice is the small battery, with the rest of the surplus going to hot water or load management.

## Inverter operating modes

A battery performs according to how it is set up. Hybrid inverters usually offer several modes:

- **Maximum self-consumption:** charges with surplus and discharges when the house uses more than the panels produce. This is the usual mode.
- **Backup reserve:** keeps a minimum percentage of charge in case of a power cut. It takes away usable capacity every day, so set it to what you really need.
- **Charging from the grid in off-peak hours:** some units can charge in the early hours to discharge at peak times. It only pays if the price difference exceeds the cycle losses and wear; it can make sense in winter, when the battery gets little sun. See [time-of-use periods](/guias/tramos-horarios-punta-llano-valle/).

Review the settings after installation and after every change of tariff: a badly chosen mode can leave the battery idle.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying a big battery** that never fills because the solar installation is small.
- **Expecting backup during blackouts** without having ordered and installed it.
- **Not calculating the real saving** from the difference between purchase and compensation.
- **Putting it somewhere hot,** which shortens its life.
- **Choosing an inverter** that is not compatible with future extensions.

## Checklist before buying a battery

- Work out your average night-time consumption by season from the hourly data.
- Check how much surplus you lose each month because of the compensation limit.
- Compare with the cost of a virtual battery.
- Ask for the estimated annual saving and payback in writing.
- Confirm whether it includes a backup function and which circuits it covers.
- Check the guarantee in years, cycles and residual capacity.

## When waiting may be the best decision

The price of home batteries has fallen steadily in recent years. If the numbers do not add up today, a sensible option is to install the panels with a **hybrid inverter** ready for a battery and add one later, when its price or your circumstances — an electric car, say, or a tariff with big differences between hours — justify it. That way you do not lose the panels' saving while you wait, and you avoid changing the inverter later.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does a home battery last?

Good-quality LFP batteries usually guarantee around 10 years, with a residual capacity of about 70 % or more. Their real life can be longer.

### Can I add a battery to panels that are already installed?

Yes, with an AC-coupled battery inverter or by replacing the inverter with a hybrid one. Ask for a compatibility study.

### With a battery, can I go off grid?

In a grid-connected home it does not usually pay. An off-grid installation needs much bigger batteries, a backup generator and very carefully managed consumption, especially in winter.

### Does the battery need to be legalised?

The battery is part of the self-consumption installation and must appear in the technical documentation. The installer should include it in the legalisation.

## In short

Batteries store solar surplus for the night, but their real saving is the difference between the purchase price and the compensation price, which makes payback very long in most homes. They pay off better when surplus is being lost, when there are big price differences or when backup during power cuts matters. Before buying one, use the cheaper alternatives: a virtual battery, solar hot water, load management and charging the car during the day.
