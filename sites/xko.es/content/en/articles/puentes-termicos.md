If you have ever seen a dark patch of mould in the corner of a bedroom, a line of damp on the ceiling next to the outside wall or condensation on the metal frame of a window, you were very probably looking at a **thermal bridge.** These are parts of a home's envelope where heat escapes more easily than through the rest, and as well as increasing heating consumption, they create cold surfaces where moisture in the air condenses.

This guide explains what thermal bridges are, where they appear most often, how to detect them, why they cause mould and which solutions exist, from renovation works to the daily habits that reduce their effects.

## What a thermal bridge is

A thermal bridge is a part of the envelope (wall, roof, floor) where resistance to the flow of heat is lower than in the surrounding areas. It can be caused by:

- **A change of material:** a concrete pillar or a steel beam inside an insulated brick wall.
- **A break in the insulation:** the insulation stops where it meets a floor slab or around a window.
- **Geometry:** at corners, the outer surface losing heat is larger than the inner surface receiving it.

In those areas, the temperature of the inside face of the wall in winter is lower than elsewhere.

## Where they appear most often

| Location | Why it happens |
|---|---|
| Slab edges | Each floor's concrete slab runs through the façade to the outside |
| Pillars in the façade | Concrete conducts heat far better than brick |
| Around windows | Uninsulated junction between frame and wall, sills and lintels |
| Shutter boxes | A hollow, uninsulated box at the top of the window |
| Corners between façades | The geometric effect |
| Junction of façade and roof | A break in the insulation |
| Cantilevered balconies and terraces | The slab sticks out like a fin that sheds heat |
| Metal frames without a thermal break | Aluminium conducts heat from outside to inside |

In buildings put up before the insulation rules, slab edges, pillars and balconies are very pronounced thermal bridges.

## Consequences

### Energy losses

In a home that is insulated but has unresolved thermal bridges, they can account for a significant share of total heat loss. The better insulated the rest is, the greater the relative weight of the thermal bridges.

### Condensation and mould

This is the most visible consequence. Indoor air contains water vapour from breathing, cooking, showers or clothes drying. When that air touches a surface that is cold enough, the vapour condenses. If the surface stays damp for days, mould grows.

Mould is not just a cosmetic problem: it can affect the health of people with asthma or allergies and spoils paint, plaster and furniture.

### Discomfort

Cold walls make you feel cold even when the air is at the temperature you want, which leads you to turn the heating up.

## How to detect them

- **Recurring patches of mould** in corners, on ceilings next to the façade, behind wardrobes against outside walls or around windows.
- **Paint that darkens** in horizontal lines at slab level or vertical lines over pillars.
- **Condensation** on window frames or at the top of outside walls.
- **Thermal imaging:** a thermal camera clearly shows the cold areas in winter, with the heating on. There are inexpensive cameras that plug into a phone, and energy-efficiency technicians use them in their surveys.
- **Touch:** in winter, some parts of the wall feel colder than others.

### Telling it apart from other damp

Not every stain is condensation. Rule out:

- **Leaks** of rainwater or from pipes, which usually appear after rain or have a source you can find.
- **Rising damp** on ground floors, which climbs from the ground and leaves salt deposits at the bottom of the walls.

Condensation is worse in winter, in rooms with a lot of steam and little ventilation, and in the cold areas described above.

## Building solutions

### External wall insulation

It is the most complete solution, because it wraps slab edges and pillars continuously. See [wall and roof insulation](/guias/aislamiento-de-fachada-y-cubierta/).

### Dealing with windows

- Replace metal frames without a thermal break with insulating frames.
- Insulate the shutter box.
- Seal the junction between frame and wall properly.
- More in [efficient windows](/guias/ventanas-eficientes/).

### Continuous internal lining

If you insulate from inside, pay special attention to the junctions with floor slabs and partitions, carrying the insulation a few centimetres along the ceiling and inner walls to reduce the effect of the thermal bridge.

### Balconies

In new buildings, thermally broken connectors are used. In renovations, the balcony slab is sometimes wrapped in insulation.

### Thermal and anti-condensation paints

Paints advertised as "thermal" have a very limited insulating effect. Anti-mould paints with fungicides help slow the growth of mould, but they do not solve the cause. Use them as an extra, not as the solution.

## Habits that reduce the problem

Until building work is done, condensation can be reduced a great deal:

- **Ventilate every day:** 5 to 10 minutes with the windows open, ideally with a through-draught, especially in the morning and after showers and cooking.
- **Use the extractor fans** in the kitchen and bathroom.
- **Avoid drying clothes indoors** without ventilation or in a closed room.
- **Keep the temperature steady:** homes that cool down a lot during the day and are heated sharply in the evening encourage condensation.
- **Move furniture** a few centimetres away from outside walls so that air can circulate.
- **Keep an eye on relative humidity:** ideally between 40 and 60 %. A cheap hygrometer lets you monitor it. A dehumidifier can help at times.
- **Do not cover the ventilation grilles.**

## How to clean mould

1. Wear gloves and a mask and ventilate the room.
2. Clean the surface with an anti-mould product or a solution of bleach diluted in water, following the manufacturer's instructions. Do not mix bleach with other products.
3. Let it dry thoroughly.
4. If you are going to paint, use a paint with a fungicide.

If the mould keeps coming back, the underlying problem is still there: you have to act on ventilation, temperature or insulation.

## Winter checklist

- Check corners, ceilings next to the façade and the backs of wardrobes on outside walls.
- Measure relative humidity with a hygrometer over several days.
- Check that the kitchen and bathroom extractor fans work.
- Ventilate every morning for 5 to 10 minutes.
- Move furniture a few centimetres away from outside walls.
- If the mould comes back, ask for a survey with thermal imaging.

## Case study: the north-facing bedroom

In a flat in Oviedo, the north-facing bedroom had mould every winter in the top corner next to the façade. Thermal imaging showed that the surface temperature of that corner fell to 11 °C with the air at 20 °C, because of where the slab and the pillar meet.

While the owners' association considered external insulation, the family took several steps: ventilating every morning, keeping the bedroom at 17 °C instead of letting it go cold during the day, moving the wardrobe away from the wall and using a dehumidifier in the dampest weeks. Relative humidity fell from 70 to 55 % and the mould stopped appearing, even though the thermal bridge remained until the renovation.

## When buying or renting: how to spot them on a viewing

Thermal bridges are easiest to see in winter, but they leave traces all year round. When viewing a home, look out for:

- **Dark stains or recent repainting** in the corners of the ceiling, behind wardrobes and around windows.
- **A musty smell** in bedrooms and built-in wardrobes.
- **Blackened silicone seals** on windows and in bathrooms.
- **The energy performance certificate,** compulsory for sales and lettings, and whether the building has been renovated or has external insulation.

If you rent and serious condensation damp appears, tell the landlord in writing: tenancy law requires them to carry out the repairs needed to keep the home habitable, although the tenant's ventilation habits also play a part. Record the problem with photos and dates.

## Common mistakes

- **Painting over the mould** without cleaning it or fixing the cause.
- **Mistaking condensation** for a leak or rising damp.
- **Stopping ventilating in winter** so as not to lose heat.
- **Pushing wardrobes** against cold outside walls.
- **Relying only on miracle paints** for a building problem.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do I have mould in only one bedroom?

Bedrooms usually have little ventilation at night, a lot of moisture from breathing and, often, lower temperatures. If they also have outside walls with thermal bridges, they are the first to show mould.

### Does a dehumidifier solve the problem?

It reduces the moisture in the air and therefore condensation, but it uses electricity and does not fix the cause. It is a temporary help.

### Is the problem the building's or mine?

Thermal bridges are a building problem. Habits can make it worse or better. In rented homes, it is best to tell the landlord about the problem.

### How much does a thermal-imaging survey cost?

A survey with thermal imaging by a technician can cost from around a hundred euros, depending on the home and the report.

## In short

Thermal bridges are parts of the envelope where heat escapes more easily, such as slab edges, pillars, window surrounds, shutter boxes and balconies. They increase heating consumption and create cold surfaces where moisture condenses and mould grows. The fundamental solution is continuous insulation, above all from outside, and better windows. In the meantime, ventilating every day, controlling humidity and keeping temperatures steady greatly reduces their effects.
