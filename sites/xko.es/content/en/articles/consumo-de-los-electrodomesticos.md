Knowing how much each appliance in your home uses is the first step to cutting the electricity bill sensibly. Without figures, it is easy to fret about unplugging the phone charger while the electric water heater or an old fridge quietly uses hundreds of kilowatt-hours a year.

This guide brings together indicative power ratings and consumption figures for the most common appliances in a Spanish home, explains how to work out the cost of any appliance with a simple formula, and points out which ones to keep an eye on first.

## Power versus consumption

Two ideas that are often confused:

- **Power (W or kW):** how much electricity the appliance draws at any moment while it runs. A 2,200 W iron is powerful.
- **Consumption (kWh):** how much energy it uses in total over a period. It depends on power and hours of use.

**Consumption (kWh) = power (kW) × hours of use**

A 2,200 W iron used for half an hour a week uses 1.1 kWh a week. A 10 W router left on all year uses 88 kWh. The powerful appliance is not always the one that uses the most.

## How to work out the cost of an appliance

**Cost = kWh × price per kWh**

For a quick calculation including taxes, you can use an indicative price of €0.18 to €0.22/kWh for the energy term with taxes. Your bill shows your exact price. See [how to read your electricity bill](/guias/como-leer-la-factura-de-la-luz/).

### Example

A 2,400 W oven used for 3 hours a week:

- 2.4 kW × 3 h × 52 weeks ≈ 375 kWh a year (a little less in practice, because the thermostat switches the element on and off).
- 375 kWh × €0.20 ≈ €75 a year.

## What each use costs

At €0.20/kWh, this is what each use costs:

| Use | Indicative energy | Approximate cost |
|---|---|---|
| Boiling 1 litre in a kettle (2,000 W, about 3 minutes) | 0.1 kWh | 2 cents |
| An hour of oven at 180 °C (2,400 W with thermostat) | 1 to 1.5 kWh | 20 to 30 cents |
| An hour of air conditioning at a moderate rate (about 800 W) | 0.8 kWh | 16 cents |
| An hour of electric radiator at full power (1,500 W) | 1.5 kWh | 30 cents |
| An hour of a 55-inch television (about 100 W) | 0.1 kWh | 2 cents |
| A fan all night (50 W, 8 hours) | 0.4 kWh | 8 cents |

A night of the fan costs the same as half an hour of air conditioning, and an hour of radiator the same as fifteen hours of television: whatever makes heat or cold is what weighs.

## The big users

In most homes without electric heating, the appliances that weigh most on the bill are:

1. **The electric water heater,** if there is one. See [hot water: electric water heaters and the alternatives](/guias/agua-caliente-termo-electrico-y-alternativas/).
2. **The fridge and freezer,** especially if they are old. See [fridges and freezers: consumption and tips](/guias/frigorifico-y-congelador-consumo-y-trucos/).
3. **Heating and cooling:** air conditioning in summer and heaters in winter.
4. **A resistive tumble dryer.**
5. **The accumulated standby consumption** of many appliances. See [standby consumption](/guias/consumo-en-standby/).

## Resistive element or heat pump: the rule that saves most

The big loads in a home usually come from appliances that heat with a **resistive element**: water heaters, radiators, fan heaters, conventional dryers and ovens. An element turns each kWh into at most one kWh of heat. A **heat pump** takes heat from the air and delivers several times the energy it uses: it is the principle behind air conditioning with a heat pump, [air-source heat pumps](/guias/aerotermia/), heat pump dryers and heat pump water heaters.

When one of these appliances needs replacing, ask first whether there is a heat-pump version; in the washing table, the heat pump dryer uses around half as much as the resistive one. For ovens and kettles there is no such alternative, and what saves is how you use them: shorter times and portions to match.

## How to measure real consumption

The tables give an idea, but every appliance and every home is different. To find the real consumption:

- **A plug-in energy meter:** it sits between the socket and the appliance and measures instantaneous power and accumulated kWh. Leave it on the fridge or the router for a week for a reliable figure.
- **The energy label:** it shows estimated annual consumption under standard conditions. See [the energy label](/guias/etiqueta-energetica/).
- **The home's meter:** switch everything off except the appliance you want to measure and watch the instantaneous power.
- **Hourly data from the distribution company:** it lets you see peaks and background consumption.

## Saving measures by appliance

| Appliance | The measure with most impact |
|---|---|
| Electric water heater | Lower it to 50 to 55 °C and schedule it for cheap hours |
| Fridge | Keep it away from heat sources, check the seals, set it to 4 to 5 °C |
| Washing machine | Wash at 30 °C or cold with eco programmes and full loads |
| Dishwasher | Eco programme with a full load, no pre-rinsing by hand under hot water |
| Tumble dryer | Spin well first, hang clothes out when you can, clean the filter |
| Oven | Avoid unnecessary pre-heating, use the residual heat |
| Television and console | Switch off fully, adjust the brightness |
| Air conditioning | 25 to 27 °C, sun protection, clean filters |

## When it is worth replacing an appliance

Subtract the annual consumption of the new model, shown on its label, from the measured consumption of your appliance, and multiply the difference by your price per kWh: that is the annual saving. Divide the price of the new appliance by that saving and you will know how many years it takes to pay for itself.

Early replacement usually pays for appliances that run all year, such as old fridges and freezers. For little-used ones, like an oven switched on twice a month, it makes sense to wait until it breaks. And the second fridge or the nearly empty chest freezer in the garage have the cheapest solution of all: unplug them.

## Case study: a home energy audit in an afternoon

With a plug-in meter and the meter display, a couple in Bilbao went over their home in an afternoon:

1. **Base load in the early hours:** 210 W on the meter, more than expected.
2. **A 2008 fridge:** 1.6 kWh a day measured over a week, about 580 kWh a year.
3. **A desktop computer** that was never switched off: 65 W at idle.
4. **A dehumidifier** forgotten in the storeroom: 230 W, running on and off all day.

What they did: they replaced the fridge with one using about 160 kWh a year, set the computer to switch off at night and unplugged the dehumidifier except in the damp months. Their base load fell to 90 W and their annual consumption by about 900 kWh.

## Checklist

- Measure the base load in the early hours.
- Measure the fridge for a week.
- Check appliances that run unattended: water heater, pumps, dehumidifiers, heaters.
- Note the power and hours of use of the big appliances.
- Prioritise the measures that tackle the biggest loads.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a phone charger left plugged in without the phone use much?

Very little, usually less than 0.5 W. But many small loads together do add up.

### Does switching on and off use more than leaving things on?

For most appliances and LED lights, switching off saves. The exceptions are systems with a lot of inertia, such as some heating systems.

### Does an air fryer use less than the oven?

For small portions, yes, because it heats a much smaller space for less time.

### How do I know how much my whole home uses?

Look at the annual consumption on your bills or on your distribution company's website. See [average consumption of a home in Spain](/guias/consumo-medio-de-una-casa-en-espana/).

## In short

An appliance's consumption depends on its power and its hours of use. Multiply kW by hours to get kWh, and kWh by price to get euros. In most homes, the electric water heater, the fridge, heating and cooling, the tumble dryer and accumulated standby are the big users. Measure with a plug-in meter, start with the appliances that run for many hours and apply the measures with the most impact to each.
