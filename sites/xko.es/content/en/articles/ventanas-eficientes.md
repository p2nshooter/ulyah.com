Windows are the thermal weak point of most homes. A well-built wall lets through far less heat than an old single-glazed window, and in many Spanish flats built before the 1980s the windows account for a large share of heat loss in winter and of heat gain in summer. Draughts also get in through their gaps, forcing you to keep the heating higher to feel comfortable.

This guide explains how windows affect consumption, which types of frame and glazing exist, how to read the technical values, what to choose for your climate and when replacing them is worth it compared with cheaper improvements.

## Why windows matter

A window loses or gains energy in three ways:

- **Conduction through the glass and frame:** heat passes from the warm side to the cold side.
- **Air leaks:** outdoor air comes in and indoor air goes out through joints and gaps.
- **Solar radiation:** sun passing through the glass heats the home, which is welcome in winter and a nuisance in summer.

A good window reduces conduction, is airtight and manages the sun coming in according to orientation and climate.

## The technical values you need to know

### Thermal transmittance (U)

It shows how much heat passes through the window per square metre and per degree of temperature difference. It is expressed in W/m²K. **The lower it is, the better it insulates.**

| Type of window | Indicative U value |
|---|---|
| Single glazing in a metal frame without a thermal break | 5.5 to 6 |
| Old double glazing (4-6-4) in aluminium without a thermal break | 3.5 to 4 |
| Double glazing with a 12–16 mm cavity in thermally broken aluminium | 2.2 to 2.8 |
| Low-emissivity double glazing with argon in PVC or high-performance aluminium | 1.2 to 1.6 |
| Low-emissivity triple glazing in a high-performance frame | 0.7 to 1.0 |

The U value of the whole window (Uw) combines that of the glass (Ug) and of the frame (Uf). Look at the whole-window figure.

### Solar factor (g)

It shows what share of solar energy passes through the glass. It ranges from 0 to 1. A high value lets in more solar heat; a low one protects against heat.

- **In cold climates and on south façades:** a high solar factor is useful for making the most of the winter sun.
- **In hot climates and on west façades:** a lower solar factor or, better, good external sun protection.

### Air permeability

It is classed from class 1 (worst) to class 4 (best). A good-quality modern window should be class 3 or 4.

## Types of frame

| Material | Advantages | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| PVC | Very good insulation, good value, little maintenance | Thicker profiles, less rigid in large sizes |
| Thermally broken aluminium | Strong, slim profiles, a wide range of finishes | Insulates worse than PVC except at the top end |
| Timber | Good insulation and appearance | Needs regular maintenance |
| Composite (timber-aluminium, PVC-aluminium) | Combine advantages | Higher price |

**Aluminium without a thermal break,** very common in homes from the 1970s to the 1990s, is a poor insulator: the frame conducts heat and condensation forms on its inner face in winter.

## Types of glazing

- **Double glazing:** two panes separated by a cavity filled with air or gas. It is today's minimum standard.
- **Low-emissivity glass:** it has an almost invisible coating that reduces heat loss by radiation. It improves insulation a great deal for little extra cost.
- **Argon in the cavity:** it insulates better than air.
- **Solar control glass:** it reduces solar heat gain; useful on very sunny façades in hot climates.
- **Acoustic or laminated glass:** it improves sound insulation and safety.
- **Triple glazing:** maximum insulation, more weight and cost; it makes most sense in cold climates.

## What to choose for your climate

Spain has very different climate zones. The Technical Building Code classifies them with letters (α to E, warmest to coldest) and numbers (1 to 4, by the severity of the summer).

| Climate | General recommendation |
|---|---|
| Mediterranean coast and south (mild winters, hot summers) | Low-emissivity double glazing, solar control on west façades, good external sun protection |
| Central interior (cold winters, hot summers) | Low-emissivity double glazing with argon, well-insulating frames, external sun protection |
| North and mountains (cold winters, mild summers) | Low-emissivity double or triple glazing with a high solar factor on south façades |
| Canary Islands | Priority to sun protection and ventilation; moderate insulation |

## How much you save

The saving depends on the state of the old windows, the climate, the glazed area and the heating system. As a reference, replacing single glazing or old double glazing in non-thermally-broken aluminium with modern low-emissivity windows can reduce:

- Heating consumption by 10 to 30 % in cold climates.
- Draughts and the feeling of cold next to the windows.
- Condensation on the frames.
- Outdoor noise, noticeably.

### Indicative cost

A medium-sized PVC or thermally broken aluminium window with low-emissivity double glazing, installed, can cost €400 to €900, depending on size, opening type, glazing and how complex the work is. Shutters and shutter boxes are quoted separately.

With moderate heating savings, payback on energy alone is usually long, 15 to 25 years or more. But the extra comfort, less noise and the added value of the home are part of the benefit.

## Do not forget the shutter box

In many flats, the **shutter box** is an uninsulated hollow box through which heat escapes and air gets in. When you replace the windows, insulate or replace the box. If you are not replacing the windows, insulating it is a cheap and effective improvement. More ideas in [draught strips and air leaks](/guias/burletes-y-corrientes-de-aire/).

## Cheaper alternatives

If you cannot replace the windows now:

- **Draught strips and sealing** of joints to stop air leaks.
- **Insulate the shutter box.**
- **Low-emissivity or solar control films** stuck to the glass.
- **Secondary glazing** inside, or replacing only the glass in frames that are in good condition.
- **Heavy curtains** at night in winter.
- **External sun protection** in summer: awnings, shutters, window shutters. See [shutters, awnings and sun protection](/guias/persianas-toldos-y-proteccion-solar/).

## How to compare window quotes

Two quotes at the same price can hide very different windows. Ask for each to state, in writing:

| Item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Transmittance of the whole window | The figure for frame and glass together determines the losses; the glass alone is only part of it |
| Glazing make-up | Number of panes, cavities, gas fill and low-emissivity or solar control coatings |
| Frame profile | Material, number of chambers and type of thermal break |
| Glass spacer | Warm-edge spacers reduce condensation around the edges |
| Installation | Removal, sealing between frame and wall, finishing and waste disposal |
| Shutter box | Whether it is insulated or replaced |
| CE marking and documents | Windows must be sold with CE marking and their declaration of performance |
| Guarantee and timing | For the product and the installation |

If you live in a block of flats, check the building's rules first: changing the colour, material or layout of the windows can alter the façade and need the owners' agreement. And ask about any energy-renovation grants in your autonomous community, which usually have to be applied for before the work starts.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing on the frame alone** without looking at the glass, or the other way round.
- **Poor installation:** a good window badly fitted, with unsealed gaps between frame and wall, loses much of its effectiveness.
- **Forgetting ventilation:** making the home more airtight means making sure the air is renewed, by ventilating every day or with ventilation systems, to avoid damp.
- **Not asking for the technical values** in writing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need permission to replace the windows in a flat?

It usually needs a municipal notification or licence, and in buildings with façade rules, respecting the colour and exterior design set by the owners' association.

### PVC or aluminium?

PVC usually insulates better for the same price. Top-end thermally broken aluminium also insulates well, with slimmer profiles. Compare U values.

### Are there grants for replacing windows?

There have been energy-renovation grants and income-tax (IRPF) deductions linked to improving the energy certificate. Check the current ones in your autonomous community. More in [the energy performance certificate of a home](/guias/certificado-energetico-de-la-vivienda/).

### Why is there condensation on my new windows?

If it appears on the inside face of the glass, it is usually due to high indoor humidity and little ventilation. Ventilate every day and look for sources of moisture.

## In short

Old windows are one of the main points of energy loss in a home. When replacing them, look at the U value of the whole window, the right solar factor for your climate and orientation, and the air permeability. Combine low-emissivity double glazing with well-insulating frames, take care over the installation and the shutter box, and do not forget ventilation. If you cannot replace them, draught strips, films and sun protection offer cheap improvements.
