Adjusting your contracted power is one of the simplest ways of cutting the electricity bill for good. If you have more power than you use, lowering it saves money every month without changing any habit. If, on the other hand, the power keeps tripping because you have installed a heat pump or a car charger, raising it may be necessary.

This guide explains how power is changed, who handles each step, what lowering and raising it costs, what happens with the electrical installation certificate and how to check that the new value is right.

## Before you change: work out the power you need

The first step is knowing how much power you really need. There are two ways to find out, explained in detail in [how much power to contract](/guias/que-potencia-contratar/):

- **Add up the appliances** you use at the same time at your moment of highest consumption.
- **Look up the maximum power recorded** by your smart meter on your distribution company's website.

With those figures, choose a value with a small margin over your real maximum. You do not have to pick one of the old standard values; with smart meters many distribution companies accept intermediate ones.

### How to read your data without getting it wrong

The distribution company's website shows the **hourly consumption curve,** in kWh, and the **maximum power demanded,** in kW. Only the second is any use for deciding your power. An hour with 1.2 kWh means an average of 1.2 kW, but within it there may have been ten minutes at 4 kW with the oven and the kettle on together.

- **Look at a full twelve months.** Peaks usually show up in winter, with a heater, or in summer, with the air conditioning. Spring gives a misleadingly low picture.
- **Separate P1 and P2.** Because the whole weekend counts as P2, the peaks of Sunday lunch do not show up in P1.
- **Ignore the exceptional.** An isolated peak during building work does not count; one that comes back every Sunday does.

## Who does what

Two different companies are involved in the Spanish electricity system:

- **The retailer:** the company your contract is with, which sends you the bill.
- **The distribution company:** the company that owns the networks and the meter in your area. It carries out the technical change.

**You make the request to the retailer,** which passes it on to the distribution company. In most cases the change is made remotely through the smart meter, with no visit needed.

### If you rent or the contract is not in your name

The retailer only processes the change at the request of the **contract holder** or someone they have authorised:

- **You are a tenant and the contract is in your name:** you can request it yourself. Nothing else is needed to lower it; if raising it means changing cables or the fuse board, talk to the landlord first, because it is work in their property.
- **The contract is in the landlord's name:** ask them to request it or to authorise you in writing, or transfer the contract to your name and ask whether both changes can be processed together.
- **The holder is a former tenant or a relative who has died:** sort out the holder first; any later step will be simpler.

## What lowering your power costs

Lowering your power is cheap. The distribution company charges a **connection fee** (*derechos de enganche*), a regulated amount of around €9 plus VAT. Some retailers cover this cost in occasional campaigns.

There are no extension or access fees to pay, because you are not asking the grid for more capacity.

### How much you get back

If you lower P1 by one kilowatt, the annual saving including taxes is usually €35 to €55, depending on the tariff. The cost of the change is recovered within a few months.

## What raising your power costs

Raising your power is dearer, because you are asking the grid for more capacity. The regulated charges are:

| Charge | What it is | Indicative amount |
|---|---|---|
| Extension fee | A contribution to the network for each extra kW | About €17 per kW |
| Access fee | For each extra kW | About €20 per kW |
| Connection fee | The switching operation | About €9 |
| VAT | On the total | 21 % |

For example, going from 3.45 to 5.75 kW (2.3 kW more) can cost around €100 to €110 with VAT. These are regulated amounts that may be updated; your retailer must tell you before carrying out the change.

### Power already paid for in the past

If in recent years the home had more power and it was lowered, the extension fees already paid are kept for a time. In that case, going back up to the earlier value may cost less. Ask the distribution company.

## The electrical installation certificate

The **electrical installation certificate** (*certificado de instalación*, popularly the *boletín*) states the maximum power your home's internal wiring can take. It matters when you raise your power:

- **If the new power is below the maximum allowed** on the certificate, nothing more is needed.
- **If it is above it,** or the certificate is very old and the distribution company requires it, you will need an authorised installer to check the installation and issue a new certificate. The cost varies widely with the state of the installation.
- **In older homes,** raising the power may mean replacing cables, the main switch or the fuse board.

No certificate is needed to lower your power.

## Step by step

1. **Work out the new power** from your real data.
2. **Check the certificate** if you are going to raise it.
3. **Contact your retailer** by phone, online or through its app and ask for the power change in P1, in P2 or in both.
4. **Give the details** they ask for: holder, CUPS and the power you want.
5. **Accept the quote** if there are costs.
6. **Wait for it to be carried out.** With a smart meter the change is usually made within a few days, sometimes in less than a week.
7. **Check the next bill** to make sure the new power appears and the cost of the change is the one you were told.

## How often you can change it

The rules allow the distribution company to refuse a further change of power if less than a year has passed since the previous one. That is why it matters to calculate carefully before asking: if you go too low and the power keeps tripping, you may not be able to go back up straight away, or you may have to pay the costs of raising it.

## How to check you got it right

In the first few weeks after lowering your power:

- **Watch for trips.** If the power only cuts out in exceptional situations, the value is right.
- **Check the maximum power recorded** on the distribution company's website: if you are often very close to the limit, consider changing a habit.
- **Adjust simple habits:** do not run the oven and the washing machine together, programme the dishwasher for the night or limit the power of the induction hob.

### What to do if the power trips

1. Switch off the appliance with the highest power.
2. If your meter resets automatically, wait a few seconds. If not, reset the power-control switch or the main switch.
3. Note which appliances were on so as to avoid that combination.

## When it is worth reviewing your power

- **When you move house:** the previous power reflects someone else's habits.
- **When you change your heating:** moving from electric radiators to a heat pump or gas usually lets you go lower.
- **When you change your cooker:** moving from gas to induction may mean going higher, unless you limit the hob.
- **When you install an electric car charger:** raising the power in P2 is usually enough. See [tariffs for electric cars](/guias/tarifa-para-coche-electrico/).
- **When you install solar panels:** self-consumption does not reduce the power you need at night, but it may change your habits.

## Case study: from 5.75 to 4 kW

A couple in Granada inherited a 5.75 kW contract. On their distribution company's website they found that their maximum power in the past year had been 3.6 kW, one day in August with the air conditioning, the oven and the washing machine on together. The rest of the year they rarely went above 3 kW.

They decided to lower P1 to 4 kW and keep 4 kW in P2. The change cost about €11 with VAT and was carried out remotely in four days. In the first summer the power tripped once, when the oven coincided with the washing machine heating water; since then they programme the washing machine for the night. With a power price of €0.09/kW per day in P1, the annual saving is over €55 before taxes and around €70 with them, every year and without changing any other habit.

## Checklist before you call

- Have the CUPS and the holder's details to hand.
- Check your maximum power over the last twelve months.
- Decide the value for P1 and, separately, for P2.
- If you are raising it, check the certificate or have an installer check the installation.
- Ask for the total cost with VAT before accepting.
- Note the date of the change and check the next bill.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I change only the off-peak power?

Yes. You can ask for a different value for P1 and P2. Raising only P2 is the cheapest option for charging an electric car at night.

### Can they charge me for lowering my power?

Only the regulated connection fee, around €9 plus VAT. Be wary of higher charges and ask for a breakdown.

### Does a technician need to come?

With a smart meter, usually not. The change is made remotely. You will only need an installer if you go above what your installation allows.

### How long does the change take?

It is usually completed within a few working days of acceptance, although it may vary with the distribution company.

### Can I change my power at the same time as my company?

Yes. When you sign up with another retailer you can state the power for P1 and P2, and both requests reach the distribution company together, with the same regulated costs as any power change. See [switching electricity supplier step by step](/guias/cambiar-de-comercializadora-paso-a-paso/).

## In short

A power change is requested from the retailer and carried out by the distribution company, almost always remotely. Lowering it costs little and pays for itself within months; raising it has regulated costs for each kW and may require a new installation certificate. Work out your power from your meter's data before asking for the change, because the distribution company may refuse another change within a year, and adjust your habits to make the most of the new value.
