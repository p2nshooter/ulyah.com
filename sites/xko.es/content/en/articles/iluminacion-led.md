LED lighting has completely changed how much electricity homes use for light. An LED bulb uses a fraction of what an incandescent or halogen bulb did for the same light, lasts many years and is now the only option for most uses, following the gradual phasing-out of the old technologies in the European Union.

But choosing an LED bulb well means understanding a few ideas: lumens rather than watts, colour temperature, colour rendering, compatibility with dimmers. This guide explains everything you need to choose, how much you save and which mistakes to avoid.

## How much LED saves

| Technology | Power for about 800 lumens | Indicative lifespan |
|---|---|---|
| Classic incandescent | 60 W | 1,000 hours |
| Halogen | 42 to 46 W | 2,000 hours |
| Compact fluorescent | 13 to 15 W | 8,000 to 10,000 hours |
| LED | 7 to 9 W | 15,000 to 25,000 hours |

Replacing a 46 W halogen bulb with an 8 W LED used for 3 hours a day saves about 42 kWh a year, around €8 per bulb. In a home with 15 to 20 old light points, the saving can be more than €100 a year.

If your home already has LED in almost every light point, lighting will weigh little on the bill and the room for saving lies in other appliances. See [how much each appliance uses](/guias/consumo-de-los-electrodomesticos/).

## Lumens, not watts

For decades bulbs were chosen by watts: "a 60". With LED, watts show consumption, not light. The amount of light is measured in **lumens (lm).**

| Incandescent equivalent | Approximate lumens |
|---|---|
| 25 W | 250 lm |
| 40 W | 470 lm |
| 60 W | 800 lm |
| 75 W | 1,050 lm |
| 100 W | 1,500 lm |

**Luminous efficacy** (lumens per watt) shows how efficient a bulb is. Good-quality LEDs exceed 100 lm/W; the most efficient, a good deal more.

### How much light each room needs

| Room | Indicative lighting |
|---|---|
| Living room (general light) | 100 to 150 lm per m² |
| Kitchen (worktop) | 300 to 500 lm per m² in the work area |
| Bedroom | 100 lm per m² |
| Bathroom (mirror) | 300 to 500 lm at the mirror |
| Study or desk | 300 to 500 lm per m² on the desk |
| Hallway | 50 to 100 lm per m² |

## Colour temperature

It is measured in kelvin (K) and shows whether the light is warm or cool:

| Temperature | Appearance | Usual uses |
|---|---|---|
| 2,700 K | Warm white, yellowish | Living room, bedroom, cosy atmosphere |
| 3,000 K | Neutral warm white | General use at home |
| 4,000 K | Neutral white | Kitchen, bathroom, work areas |
| 5,000 to 6,500 K | Cool white, bluish | Garages, workshops, technical lighting |

At home, most people feel more comfortable with 2,700 to 3,000 K in living areas and 3,000 to 4,000 K in kitchens and work areas. Mixing very different temperatures in the same room usually feels uncomfortable.

## Colour rendering (CRI)

It shows how faithfully the light shows colours, on a scale up to 100.

- **CRI 80:** acceptable for general use.
- **CRI 90 or more:** colours look natural. Recommended in the kitchen, bathroom, dressing area and anywhere it matters to see skin, food or clothes properly.

## Bases and formats

| Base | Typical use |
|---|---|
| E27 | Large screw, ceiling and floor lamps |
| E14 | Small screw, wall lights and small lamps |
| GU10 | 230 V recessed spotlights with two twist-lock pins |
| GU5.3 / MR16 | 12 V spotlights with a transformer |
| G9 | Small capsules in designer lamps |
| T8 tubes | Replacements for linear fluorescent tubes |

When replacing 12 V spotlights (MR16), check that the LED is compatible with the existing transformer, or replace the fitting with a 230 V GU10.

## Dimmable LEDs

Not every LED can be dimmed. To use them with a dimmer:

- Buy bulbs marked as **dimmable.**
- Check that the **dimmer is compatible** with LEDs. Old dimmers designed for incandescent bulbs can cause flicker or buzzing.
- Alternatively, use smart bulbs dimmed from an app or with a dedicated switch.

## The energy label on bulbs

Since September 2021, light sources have used the new A to G scale. Many ordinary LED bulbs are in classes E or F of the new scale, which means good efficiency; classes A to C are reserved for very efficient products. Look at the lumens per watt. See [the energy label](/guias/etiqueta-energetica/).

## Quality and eye health

- **Flicker:** poor-quality LEDs can flicker imperceptibly, which causes eye strain in some people. Look for products that declare low flicker.
- **Glare:** choose suitable diffusers or shades for visible fittings.
- **Blue light at night:** in bedrooms, warm temperatures are more pleasant before sleep.

## Habits and control

- **Switch lights off** when you leave a room: with LED, switching on and off does not shorten their life significantly.
- **Make use of daylight.**
- **Presence sensors** in hallways, garages and storerooms.
- **Zoned lighting:** task light where you work rather than lighting the whole room at high power.
- **Good-quality LED panels and strips** for indirect lighting, with suitable power supplies.

## Lifespan: why some LEDs fail early

LED packaging advertises lifespans of many thousands of hours, but that figure is measured in the laboratory. At home, the real lifespan depends mainly on three things:

- **Heat:** the diode and its electronics suffer with temperature. A bulb inside a closed lamp or a sealed ceiling fitting gets hotter and lasts less. If the fitting is enclosed, choose bulbs the manufacturer declares suitable for it.
- **The internal electronics:** the part that fails most is not the LED but the small circuit that drives it. In very cheap products it is usually the weak point.
- **The dimmer:** a non-dimmable bulb on a dimmer can flicker, buzz and fail early. Always check compatibility.

Keep the receipt: bulbs have the same legal guarantee as any other product, and one that fails after a few months can be returned.

## Recycling old bulbs

LED bulbs and fluorescent lamps, including energy-saving ones, are waste electrical and electronic equipment and must not go in the general rubbish bin. Fluorescent lamps also contain a small amount of mercury. Take them to a recycling point (*punto limpio*) or to the collection bins found in many shops: stores above a certain size must accept very small appliances, such as bulbs, even if you buy nothing. That way materials are recovered and hazardous substances stay out of landfill.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing by watts** instead of lumens.
- **Mixing colour temperatures** in the same room.
- **Using non-dimmable LEDs** with dimmers.
- **Buying very cheap, poor-quality bulbs** that flicker or do not last.
- **Enclosing LEDs in unventilated fittings** if the manufacturer does not allow it: heat shortens their life.
- **Forgetting the transformer** in 12 V installations.

## Case study: relighting a flat

A three-bedroom flat had 18 halogen GU10 spotlights of 50 W in the kitchen, hallway and bathrooms, and 6 energy-saving bulbs elsewhere.

| Area | Before | After | Hours of use a day |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen (6 spotlights) | 300 W | 36 W | 3 |
| Hallway (6 spotlights) | 300 W | 30 W | 1.5 |
| Bathrooms (6 spotlights) | 300 W | 36 W | 1 |
| Elsewhere (6 bulbs) | 90 W | 54 W | 3 |

Annual consumption for lighting fell from about 840 kWh to about 160 kWh. At €0.20/kWh, the saving is over €130 a year, against an outlay of about €120 on good-quality bulbs. The kitchen also gained useful light by choosing 4,000 K spotlights with CRI 90 over the worktop.

## Checklist

- Make an inventory of the bulbs at home: base, power and hours of use.
- Start with those that are on for the most hours.
- Choose the lumens equivalent to what you had.
- Use one colour temperature per room.
- Check for dimmers and buy dimmable LEDs where there are any.
- Recycle the old bulbs at a recycling point.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does an LED really last?

The figures of 15,000 to 25,000 hours refer to the useful life until the light falls to a given percentage. In hot conditions or with poor-quality components they can last less.

### Is it worth replacing bulbs that still work?

Halogen and incandescent ones, yes: the saving pays back within a few months. Compact fluorescents when they fail, or if they are on for many hours.

### Where do old bulbs go?

Fluorescent lamps and LEDs are electrical waste: take them to a recycling point or to the collection bins in shops. Fluorescent lamps contain mercury.

### Do smart bulbs save energy?

When on, they use the same as an ordinary LED, plus a little on standby. They save if you use them to schedule and switch off lights that would otherwise stay on.

## In short

LED bulbs use up to 85 % less than incandescent ones and last for many years. Choose by lumens, not watts; pick 2,700 to 3,000 K for living areas and up to 4,000 K for kitchens and work areas; look for CRI 90 where colours matter; check the bases and compatibility with dimmers; and combine good bulbs with the habit of switching off and with presence sensors.
