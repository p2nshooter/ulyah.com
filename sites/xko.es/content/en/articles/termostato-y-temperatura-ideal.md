The thermostat is the cheapest part of the heating system and, at the same time, the one with the most influence on what you pay. One degree's difference in the setting is hardly noticeable in comfort, but it shows on the bill over a winter. And a thermostat that is badly placed, badly programmed or untouched since it was installed may be heating the house when nobody is in or to temperatures nobody needs.

This guide explains which temperature to set in winter and summer, how much each degree saves, how to programme the times, whether it is better to switch the heating off at night or keep it on, where to put the thermostat and what smart models add.

## Which temperature to set

### In winter

The most common recommendation for homes is to keep **19 to 21 °C** during the day when people are at home and **15 to 17 °C** at night or when the house is empty.

In public and commercial buildings, Spanish rules have at times set temperature limits, for example a maximum of 19 °C for heating in certain spaces during energy-saving plans. There is no obligation in homes, but those values are a good reference.

### In summer

With air conditioning, **25 to 27 °C** is a comfortable temperature in light clothing and allows a substantial saving. See [air conditioning: consumption and efficient use](/guias/aire-acondicionado-consumo-y-uso-eficiente/).

### Special cases

Babies, older people or people with certain illnesses may need somewhat higher temperatures in winter. Comfort and health come before saving.

## How much each degree saves

The rule often quoted is that **each degree lower on the heating saves 5 to 10 %** of heating consumption. The percentage depends on the climate, the insulation and the outdoor temperature: in mild climates, where the difference between inside and outside is small, a degree weighs proportionally more.

| Change | Indicative saving on heating |
|---|---|
| From 23 to 21 °C | 10 to 20 % |
| From 21 to 20 °C | 5 to 10 % |
| Down to 16 °C at night instead of keeping 20 °C | 5 to 15 % |
| Heating only when someone is at home | 10 to 25 % |

In a home spending €700 a year on heating, lowering it by two degrees can save more than €100.

## Scheduling: the key to saving

A programmable thermostat lets you set different temperatures by time and day. An example for a family who work away from home Monday to Friday:

| Time | Temperature |
|---|---|
| 6:30 to 8:00 | 20 °C |
| 8:00 to 17:30 | 16 °C |
| 17:30 to 22:30 | 20 to 21 °C |
| 22:30 to 6:30 | 16 °C |
| Weekends during the day | 20 to 21 °C |

### Switch off at night or keep a low temperature?

It depends on the system and the home:

- **Boilers and radiators in poorly insulated homes:** lowering the setting to 15 or 16 °C at night usually saves. Switching off completely may be even better in mild climates, but in cold climates the house cools so much that it takes a long time to recover in the morning.
- **Underfloor heating:** it has a lot of inertia. Night-time setbacks should be small, 1 to 2 °C, or you lose comfort without a clear saving.
- **Heat pumps:** they work best at steady temperatures. Gentle setbacks of 1 to 2 °C.

The idea that "it costs more to heat up again than to keep it warm" is generally false: a cooler house loses less heat to the outside, so keeping the temperature high all night usually costs more. The exceptions are high-inertia systems and heat pumps, where a sharp recovery can switch on backup elements or reduce efficiency.

### Bring the start time forward

Schedule the heating to start 30 to 60 minutes before you get up or arrive home, depending on your system's inertia. That way you avoid turning the setting up to 24 °C "so that it heats faster", which speeds up nothing and just ends in overheating.

## Where to put the thermostat

A badly placed thermostat measures a temperature that is not the home's:

- **On an inside wall,** about 1.5 metres above the floor.
- **In the main room** where you spend most time, usually the living room.
- **Away from heat sources:** radiators, fireplaces, lamps, the kitchen or direct sun through a window.
- **Away from draughts:** outside doors, windows.
- **Not inside a cupboard** or covered by curtains.

If the thermostat is in a cold hallway, the boiler will overheat the rest of the home. If it is near a sunny window, it will switch off too early.

## Thermostatic radiator valves

Thermostatic valves let you set a different temperature in each room. They are especially useful for:

- Keeping bedrooms cooler than the living room.
- Turning the heating down in rooms that are not used.
- Preventing sunny or crowded rooms from overheating.

In buildings with central heating, the rules on individual metering have led to thermostatic valves and heat cost allocators being installed in many buildings. Using them well lets you pay less according to what you use.

## Smart thermostats

Connected thermostats offer extra features:

- **Control from your phone:** switch off or on when plans change.
- **Geolocation:** it lowers the temperature when everyone has left and raises it when someone comes back.
- **Learning:** some models learn the household's habits and the home's inertia.
- **Open-window detection:** they stop the heating if they detect a sudden drop in temperature.
- **Consumption reports.**
- **Modulation** with compatible boilers (protocols such as OpenTherm), which improves the efficiency of condensing boilers. See [condensing boilers](/guias/caldera-de-condensacion/).

### Do they really save?

They save if they replace a thermostat that was never programmed or was used badly. If you already programmed your thermostat well, the extra saving is smaller. The biggest saving comes from the schedules and settings, not from the technology itself.

| Type | Indicative cost | Main advantage |
|---|---|---|
| On-off thermostat | €20 to €40 | Simple |
| Digital programmable | €30 to €80 | Weekly schedules |
| Smart | €100 to €250 | Remote control, geolocation, reports |
| Manual thermostatic valve | €15 to €30 per radiator | Room-by-room control |
| Smart thermostatic head | €40 to €80 per radiator | Schedules by room |

## Other habits that help

- **Close shutters and curtains at dusk** in winter to cut losses through the windows.
- **Open the shutters on sunny façades** during the day to make use of the sun.
- **Ventilate briefly:** 5 to 10 minutes is enough; ventilating for an hour with the heating on wastes energy.
- **Do not cover radiators** with furniture or clothes.
- **Bleed the radiators** at the start of the season.
- **Seal draughts.** See [draught strips and air leaks](/guias/burletes-y-corrientes-de-aire/).
- **Dress for the season** at home: a jumper lets you be comfortable a degree lower.

## Comfort is not just the number on the thermostat

Two homes with the thermostat at the same temperature can feel very different. The feeling of comfort depends on several factors at once:

| Factor | How it affects comfort |
|---|---|
| Air temperature | What the thermostat measures |
| Surface temperature | Cold walls, floors and windows "steal" heat from the body by radiation and force you to turn the thermostat up to feel comfortable |
| Draughts | They make you feel colder even when the air is warm |
| Humidity | Very dry air feels uncomfortable; very humid air encourages condensation and mould |
| Clothing and activity | Reading on the sofa is not the same as cooking |

That is why insulating, replacing windows or getting rid of draughts lets you lower the thermostat without feeling cold: with warmer surfaces, the body loses less heat. And it is also why you should not lower the temperature without limit to save money. The World Health Organization recommends keeping homes at about 18 °C at least in winter, and warmer in households with babies, older people or people who are ill. In poorly insulated homes, a temperature that is too low also sends condensation on cold walls and mould soaring. We explain this in the guide to [thermal bridges](/guias/puentes-termicos/).

## Common mistakes

- **Turning the thermostat to maximum** thinking the house will warm up sooner.
- **Keeping the heating on** at the same temperature day and night.
- **Putting the thermostat** next to a heat source, a window or a draught.
- **Covering radiators** with furniture, curtains or clothes.
- **Ventilating for a long time** with the heating on.
- **Lowering the temperature so far** that condensation and mould appear.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it true that setting 25 °C heats faster?

No. The boiler or heat pump delivers the same output; a higher setting just makes it run longer and leaves the house warmer than necessary.

### What temperature should the bedroom be?

16 to 18 °C is usually comfortable for sleeping with suitable bedding.

### Is a smart thermostat worth it in a small flat?

If you already programme the heating well, the extra saving will be limited. If nobody programmes anything or your timetable changes a lot, it may pay.

### How do I know whether the thermostat measures correctly?

Compare its reading with a reliable thermometer placed at the same height in the middle of the room.

## In short

19 to 21 °C by day and 15 to 17 °C at night in winter, and 25 to 27 °C in summer, are comfortable temperatures that save energy. Each degree lower on the heating saves 5 to 10 %. Schedule the times to fit your routine, put the thermostat somewhere representative, use thermostatic valves room by room, and combine it all with good ventilation habits and protection for your windows.
