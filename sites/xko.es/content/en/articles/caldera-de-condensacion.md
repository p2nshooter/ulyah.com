In millions of Spanish homes, heating and hot water depend on a natural-gas boiler on the kitchen or utility-room wall. Since 2015, European ecodesign rules have in practice required new boilers installed in homes to be **condensing** boilers. If your boiler is more than fifteen years old, it is probably a conventional or low-NOx model and uses a good deal more gas than a modern one.

This guide explains how a condensing boiler works, how much it really saves, what has to be done for it to actually condense, which servicing the law requires and when it makes more sense to replace it with a heat pump than with another boiler.

## How a condensing boiler works

Burning natural gas produces water vapour as well as heat. In a conventional boiler, that vapour goes up the flue with the exhaust gases at 120 to 200 °C, and a noticeable amount of energy goes with it.

A condensing boiler has a larger heat exchanger that cools the exhaust gases until the water vapour **condenses**. As it turns from vapour into liquid, the water gives up its latent heat, which the boiler uses to pre-heat the water returning from the heating circuit. The exhaust leaves much cooler, at 40 to 60 °C, and the condensed water runs away down a drain.

### Visible differences

- **A condensate drain:** a plastic pipe connected to the waste-water system.
- **A plastic flue** or one made of acid-resistant materials, often coaxial.
- **A white plume** at the flue outlet on cold days: it is water vapour.

## How much it saves

Manufacturers advertise efficiencies of up to 108 or 109 % on the net calorific value. That number is confusing: no energy is being created. It means the boiler recovers part of the latent heat that the conventional calculation leaves out.

In practical terms:

| Type of boiler | Indicative seasonal efficiency |
|---|---|
| Old conventional boiler (more than 20 years) | 70 to 80 % |
| Modern conventional room-sealed boiler | 82 to 88 % |
| Well-adjusted condensing boiler | 92 to 98 % |

Replacing an old conventional boiler with a well-installed condensing one can cut gas consumption by 15 to 25 %. If the old boiler was badly adjusted or oversized, the saving can be greater.

### Example

A home uses 9,000 kWh of gas a year with a boiler from 2004. With a well-adjusted condensing boiler it could fall to about 7,200 kWh. At an energy price of €0.08/kWh, the saving would be around €145 a year, plus taxes. With a new installation costing about €2,000, the payback on energy alone would be around ten years, although the old boiler might be near the end of its life anyway.

## To condense, the water must come back cool

A condensing boiler only condenses when the water returning from the radiators is below about 55 °C. If it is set to send out water at 80 °C, like the old boilers, the return comes back too hot, the exhaust does not cool enough and the boiler works almost like a conventional one.

### Settings that make the difference

- **A low flow temperature:** 50 to 60 °C is enough in many homes, especially on mild days.
- **An outdoor sensor and heating curve:** with an outdoor temperature sensor, the boiler automatically adjusts the water temperature to how cold it is. It is one of the cheapest and most effective improvements.
- **Modulation:** modern boilers adjust their output. A modulating thermostat (for example with the OpenTherm protocol) makes better use of that ability than an on-off thermostat.
- **Balancing the radiators:** adjusting the lockshield valves so that every radiator gets the right flow lets you lower the temperature without leaving cold rooms.
- **Big enough radiators:** if you need very hot water on the coldest days, a radiator may be too small for its room.

### How to lower the flow temperature step by step

If your boiler has no outdoor sensor, you can find by hand the lowest temperature that keeps you comfortable:

1. **Find the heating control,** usually marked with a radiator symbol, separate from the hot-water control, marked with a tap. Your model's manual explains how to change it.
2. **If it was at maximum, start at about 60 °C.**
3. **Wait for a few days of normal cold weather** and check whether every room reaches the thermostat temperature.
4. **Lower it 5 °C at a time** as long as the house still heats up well.
5. **If a room falls short,** go back up a step and look at that room: bleed the radiator, open it fully or balance the circuit.
6. **During a cold snap,** raise it a little and lower it again when it passes.

If the boiler has an outdoor sensor, you adjust the heating curve rather than the water temperature; the procedure is the same, lowering the curve one step at a time.

## Domestic hot water

Combi boilers produce hot water on demand. A few points:

- Set the hot water to about 45 to 50 °C; mixing very hot water with cold at the tap wastes energy.
- Boilers with a small built-in store deliver hot water faster, at the cost of a little standing consumption.
- For homes with several bathrooms, a model with a storage cylinder can give more comfort.

## Compulsory servicing

Spain's Regulation on Thermal Installations in Buildings (RITE) requires gas boilers to be serviced regularly. In addition, the gas distribution company carries out a **periodic inspection** of the installation every five years.

| Check | Who | How often |
|---|---|---|
| Boiler servicing | A registered installation or maintenance company | Depending on output and regional rules; in homes, usually every one or two years |
| Periodic gas inspection | A company appointed by the distribution company | Every five years |

Be wary of unannounced visits from supposed technicians who demand immediate payment for inspections: the distribution company's inspection is announced in advance and its cost is charged on the gas bill.

### What a service includes

- Cleaning the burner and the heat exchanger.
- A combustion analysis.
- Checking the flue and the condensate drain.
- Checking the circuit pressure and the expansion vessel.
- Checking the safety devices.

## Faults and warning signs

- **Frequent loss of pressure:** it may point to a leak in the circuit or a faulty expansion vessel.
- **A blocked or frozen condensate drain:** the boiler locks out. Outdoors in very cold places, the pipe must be protected.
- **Noises or knocking:** scale in the heat exchanger or air in the circuit.
- **A yellow flame or soot:** poor combustion; call the service engineer at once.
- **A smell of gas:** turn off the main tap, open the windows, do not touch any switches and call the distribution company's emergency number from outside the home.

Fit a **carbon monoxide detector** if you have combustion appliances inside the home.

## If your building has central heating

In many buildings the boiler is shared. Everything above applies just the same: a central condensing boiler only performs well if the water comes back cool. In addition:

- **Thermostatic valves** on the radiators let each neighbour set the temperature of each room without depending on the general timetable.
- **Heat cost allocators or energy meters:** the rules require them in many buildings with central heating, unless it is not technically or economically feasible, so that each home pays according to its consumption. Ask the property manager how this affects your building.
- **The installation's timetable and temperatures** are decided by the owners' association; proposing a lower flow temperature in the mild months is a measure that costs nothing.
- **Renovating the boiler room:** if it comes up, ask for a comparison with centralised heat pumps or hybrid systems as well.

## Another boiler or a heat pump?

When the boiler needs replacing, more and more households consider moving to a heat pump. Some criteria:

| Situation | The option that usually fits |
|---|---|
| A flat with radiators, little outdoor space, an association that does not allow units on the façade | A condensing boiler |
| A well-insulated home or one with underfloor heating | An air-to-water heat pump |
| A home with solar panels, or planning them | A heat pump |
| A very tight budget and a boiler that breaks down in midwinter | A condensing boiler |
| A full renovation on the way | A heat pump, taking advantage of the work |

European rules increasingly discourage boilers that run only on fossil fuels, and many public grants are aimed at heat pumps. More in [air-source heat pumps](/guias/aerotermia/).

## Choosing and installing a new boiler

- **The right output:** many homes have oversized boilers. A 24 kW boiler is usually enough to heat a flat; it is the hot-water output that tends to set the size.
- **A wide modulation range:** the lower it can run, the fewer on-off cycles.
- **An outdoor sensor and a modulating thermostat.**
- **A suitable flue** in line with the rules and the building's statutes.
- **A registered installer** who issues the installation certificate and registers the boiler.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I install a condensing boiler in place of an old one without building work?

Normally the flue has to be adapted and a condensate drain added. In buildings with shared flues it may need specific solutions.

### Is the condensate water dangerous?

It is slightly acidic and must go down the drain. It is not dangerous in the quantities a home produces.

### How often should I bleed the radiators?

At the start of the heating season, or when you notice the top of a radiator is cold.

### What pressure should the boiler show?

On most wall-hung boilers, with the system cold, between 1 and 1.5 bar; with the heating on it rises a little. After bleeding radiators it is normal for it to drop and for water to need topping up with the filling loop, following the manual. If you have to top it up often without having bled anything, check the warning signs described above.

### Is a regulated gas tariff right for me?

It depends on your consumption. See [the gas bill explained](/guias/factura-del-gas-explicada/).

## In short

A condensing boiler recovers the heat in the water vapour of its exhaust and can use 15 to 25 % less than an old conventional boiler, as long as it works with cool return water. To get there, lower the flow temperature, fit an outdoor sensor, use a modulating thermostat and balance the radiators. Keep up with the compulsory servicing and, when the time comes to replace it, compare it with a heat pump, especially if your home is insulated or you have solar panels.
