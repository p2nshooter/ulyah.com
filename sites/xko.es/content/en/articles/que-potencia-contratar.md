Contracted power is the part of the electricity bill you pay even if you never switch on a single bulb. Many households have more power than they need because they inherited it from the previous tenant, because someone raised it "just in case" or because it was never reviewed after the appliances changed. Each spare kilowatt costs several tens of euros a year, taxes included.

This guide explains what power means, how to work out what you need with two complementary methods, which values are usually enough for each type of home and which mistakes to avoid.

## What contracted power is

Power, measured in kilowatts (kW), is the maximum amount of electricity you can use at any one moment. It is not what you use in a month, which is measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh), but the "size of the pipe". If you run the oven, the washing machine and the hairdryer at the same time and go over that limit, the smart meter cuts the supply and you have to reset it.

On the 2.0TD domestic tariff you can contract two different power levels:

- **P1 (peak and mid-peak):** Monday to Friday from 8:00 to 24:00.
- **P2 (off-peak):** from 0:00 to 8:00, and all day at weekends and on national holidays.

P1 power is the expensive one. P2 is much cheaper, so raising it has little impact on the price. To understand the difference on the bill, see [the power term and the energy term](/guias/termino-de-potencia-y-termino-de-energia/).

## Method 1: add up the appliances you use at the same time

The first method is to think about which appliances run at the same time at the worst usual moment of the day and add up their power.

### Indicative power of common appliances

| Appliance | Typical power |
|---|---|
| Electric oven | 2,000 to 3,000 W |
| Induction hob (per zone in use) | 1,200 to 2,500 W, up to 7,000 W for the whole hob |
| Washing machine (heating water) | 2,000 W |
| Dishwasher (heating water) | 2,000 W |
| Resistive tumble dryer | 2,500 W |
| Electric water heater | 1,500 to 2,000 W |
| Microwave | 800 to 1,200 W |
| Electric kettle | 2,000 W |
| Hairdryer | 1,500 to 2,000 W |
| Split air conditioner (per unit) | 800 to 1,500 W |
| Electric radiator | 1,000 to 2,000 W |
| Fridge | 100 to 200 W on average, with peaks at start-up |
| Television, computer, LED lights | 50 to 300 W in total |

### A practical example

A family has dinner at nine: the oven is on at 2,000 W, an induction zone at 1,500 W, the dishwasher starts heating water at 2,000 W, and the television, lights and fridge add about 400 W. In total, 5,900 W, or 5.9 kW. With 4.6 kW contracted, the power would trip.

But the same family can avoid the cut simply by programming the dishwasher for later. Then the peak falls to 3.9 kW and 4.6 kW would be enough. The power you need depends as much on your habits as on your appliances.

### Watch out for induction hobs

Many induction hobs have a maximum power of 7 kW but almost never use all of it. Most let you **limit the power** from the menu, for example to 2.8 or 3.7 kW. Setting that limit is a simple way of avoiding contracting more power.

## Method 2: look at your meter's real data

Smart meters record the **maximum power demanded** in each period. You can look it up in two ways:

- **On the bill:** many retailers show the "maximeter" or the maximum power recorded in the period.
- **On your distribution company's website:** with your CUPS (the supply-point code on the bill) you can register and download hourly consumption and maximum power for recent months.

### How to interpret the data

1. Find the maximum power for the last twelve months, especially in winter and summer, when heating or air conditioning is on.
2. If the maximum recorded is well below your contracted power, you have room to go lower.
3. Leave a small safety margin, for example 10 to 15 % above the real maximum.

A household with 5.75 kW contracted whose maximum power over the past year was 3.4 kW could go down to about 4 kW without noticing any difference, with a worthwhile saving every year.

## Indicative power by type of home

| Type of household | P1 power that is usually enough |
|---|---|
| Studio or small flat with gas for cooking and hot water | 2.3 to 3.45 kW |
| Flat for two or three people, electric cooker, no electric heating | 3.45 to 4.6 kW |
| Family with induction, oven, dishwasher and air conditioning | 4.6 to 5.75 kW |
| Home with a heat pump for heating | 5.75 to 8 kW depending on size |
| Home heated by electric radiators | 6.9 to 9.2 kW or more |

These are references, not rules. Two identical homes may need different power depending on how the appliances are used.

## Different power in P1 and P2

Contracting more power in the off-peak period is worthwhile in particular cases:

- **An electric car:** overnight charging can need an extra 3.7 or 7.4 kW. Raising only P2 costs little. More in [tariffs for electric cars](/guias/tarifa-para-coche-electrico/).
- **An electric water heater programmed for the night** together with other night-time loads.
- **Weekends with a lot of simultaneous use,** since all of Saturday and Sunday is P2.

The opposite, contracting less power in P2 than in P1, rarely pays because the saving is very small.

## How much lowering the power saves

The saving depends on the price per kW on your tariff. As an order of magnitude, each kilowatt less in P1 is worth €30 to €45 a year before taxes, and a little more with the electricity tax and VAT. Going down from 5.75 to 4.6 kW, for example, can save around €40 to €60 a year with taxes.

That is not a spectacular figure on its own, but it repeats every year without changing any habit and adds to other measures. To see how to change it, see [changing your contracted power](/guias/cambiar-la-potencia-contratada/).

## Living with less power: load managers and habits

Before raising your power because it keeps tripping, it is worth trying solutions that let you stay where you are or even go down a step:

- **A load manager or limiter:** a small device installed in the fuse board monitors total power and, if it gets close to the limit, disconnects a non-priority circuit, such as the water heater or car charging, for a few minutes and then reconnects it. It must be installed by an authorised electrician.
- **Car chargers with dynamic load management:** they adjust the charging power to whatever is free at each moment.
- **Delayed start on appliances:** the washing machine, dishwasher and dryer take turns rather than running together.
- **A scheduled water heater:** have it heat in the early hours, when nobody is cooking or using the oven.
- **A limit on the induction hob,** as explained above.

If the meter cuts the supply, first switch off a high-power appliance and then reset following your distribution company's instructions; on smart meters, resetting is usually automatic or very simple. If the cuts keep happening even when you organise your loads, then it is time to review your power, as we explain in [changing your contracted power](/guias/cambiar-la-potencia-contratada/).

## Common mistakes

- **Raising the power after a single trip.** Sometimes it is enough not to run two high-power appliances together or to programme the washing machine.
- **Not reviewing the power after changing heating.** Someone who replaces electric radiators with a heat pump may need considerably less power.
- **Forgetting motor start-up.** Some appliances, such as water pumps or old compressors, have brief start-up peaks. If you have equipment like this, leave a little more margin.
- **Going too low.** If the power trips often, the inconvenience is not worth the saving. Adjust with data.
- **Sticking to the old standard values.** With smart meters, many distribution companies accept intermediate values, which lets you fine-tune.

## Frequently asked questions

### What happens if I go over my contracted power?

The smart meter cuts the supply after a few seconds. Just switch something off and reset the main switch, or wait for the meter to reset itself, depending on the model.

### Can I find out my maximum power without waiting for the bill?

Yes. On your distribution company's website or app you can see the maximum-power records and your hour-by-hour consumption, usually a day or two behind.

### Does power affect the price of energy?

Not directly. The price per kWh does not depend on contracted power, but the bill total does, because the power term is a large fixed part.

### Is it better to contract a little extra?

A small margin over your real maximum is sensible. Contracting much more than you need only makes the bill dearer.

## In short

The power you need is what covers the appliances you use at the same time at your moment of highest consumption, with a small margin. Work it out by adding up power ratings and, above all, by checking your meter's real records on the distribution company's website. Adjust the habits that cause peaks, limit the induction hob if you can and take advantage of cheap off-peak power if you charge an electric car. Every spare kilowatt is money you pay each month without using it.
