Electric heating is common in many Spanish homes, especially in areas with mild winters, in flats without mains gas and in second homes. But very different systems hide behind that name, and the advertising does not always help: "low-consumption" radiators, "smart" heaters or panels that "save 70 %" promise figures that do not stand up.

This guide explains, with numbers, how each electric heating system works, how much it really uses, what each one's advantages are and which suits each use of a home.

## The truth about "low consumption"

Every resistive electric heater — oil-filled radiator, panel heater, convector, infrared panel or electric underfloor heating — has the same efficiency: **it turns 1 kWh of electricity into 1 kWh of heat**. There is no resistive radiator that gives more heat for the same electricity.

The differences between them lie in:

- **How they spread the heat:** by convection (warming the air) or by radiation (warming surfaces and people).
- **Thermal inertia:** how long they store and release heat.
- **Control:** accurate thermostats, scheduling and open-window detection.

These differences can improve comfort and cut consumption through better control, but they do not change the physics.

The only electric technology that delivers more heat than the electricity it uses is the **heat pump**, which moves heat in from outside.

## The systems compared

| System | How it heats | Inertia | Advantages | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Convector or fan heater | Fast convection | Very low | Cheap, instant heat | Dries the air, little comfort, high consumption if used a lot |
| Oil-filled radiator | Convection and some radiation | Medium | Cheap, pleasant heat | Slow, bulky, basic control |
| Fluid-filled or dry-core panel heater | Convection and radiation | Medium | Good electronic control, programmable | Mid-range price, same efficiency as the rest |
| Storage heater | Stores heat at night in refractory bricks | Very high | Uses cheap night-time tariffs | Inflexible, bulky, hard to adjust |
| Infrared panel | Radiation | Low | Warms people and surfaces, discreet | Localised heat, uneven feel |
| Electric underfloor heating | Radiation from the floor | High | Very comfortable, invisible | Expensive to install, slow |
| Air-to-air heat pump (split) | Convection with air | Low | 3 to 5 times more efficient | Upfront investment, outdoor unit |
| Air-to-water heat pump | Hot water in radiators or underfloor | Medium to high | Very efficient, hot water too | Bigger investment |

## What heating with each costs

Suppose a home needs 5,000 kWh of useful heat in a winter — reasonable for a flat in the interior of the peninsula — and the average price of electricity including taxes is €0.17/kWh:

| System | Electricity used | Indicative cost |
|---|---|---|
| Any well-controlled resistive heater | 5,000 kWh | €850 |
| Storage heaters on an off-peak tariff at €0.11/kWh | 5,000 kWh | €550 |
| Air-to-air heat pump with a SCOP of 4 | 1,250 kWh | €210 |
| Air-to-water heat pump with a SCOP of 3.5 | 1,430 kWh | €245 |

The difference between resistive heating and a heat pump is enormous. That is why, if your home already has air conditioning with a heat pump, using it for heating instead of electric radiators is usually the quickest saving.

### How to work out what your radiator uses

The power on the label is not what it uses every hour. A 1,500 W heater with a thermostat runs at full power until it reaches the set temperature and then switches on and off to hold it.

- **Formula:** consumption (kWh) = power (kW) × hours the element is actually on.
- **Example:** a 1,500 W heater switched on for 5 hours that heats, on average, for half that time uses about 1.5 × 5 × 0.5 ≈ 3.75 kWh. At €0.17/kWh, about €0.64 a day.
- **Measure it:** a plug-in energy meter between the appliance and the socket gives you a week's real kWh, as long as it can handle the appliance's power. Repeat the measurement on cold days and mild days, because the difference is large.

## When each system makes sense

### Convectors and fan heaters

Useful for very occasional use: a bathroom for ten minutes, a room used for an hour a week. They are not suitable as main heating.

### Oil-filled radiators and panel heaters

A reasonable option in mild-climate homes with low demand, in second homes or as a back-up. A panel heater with a good electronic thermostat and scheduling lets you control consumption better than an old radiator with a dial, but it does not use less per kWh.

### Storage heaters

They make sense with a **time-of-use tariff** with a very cheap off-peak period and in homes that are occupied all day. Their limitations are their inflexibility and weight. See [time-of-use periods](/guias/tramos-horarios-punta-llano-valle/).

### Infrared panels

Suitable for warming people in particular spots, such as a home office, or spaces with high ceilings. Their radiant heat can feel comfortable with somewhat lower air temperatures.

### Electric underfloor heating

Very comfortable, but expensive to install and to run if it is used as the main heating. It suits bathrooms or renovations where comfort is highly valued.

### Heat pumps

The most efficient option for main heating. A heat-pump split in each main room, or an air-to-water system with radiators or underfloor heating, cuts consumption to a quarter or a third. See [air-source heat pumps](/guias/aerotermia/).

## Contracted power

Resistive heating needs a lot of power at the same time: four 1,500 W radiators add up to 6 kW. That means contracting more power, which raises the fixed part of the bill all year round.

A heat pump delivering the same heating uses a fraction of that power, which may let you lower your contracted power. See [how much power to contract](/guias/que-potencia-contratar/).

## How to cut consumption with what you already have

If you cannot change system, these measures cut the cost:

- **Heat only the rooms you use** and close the doors.
- **Schedule the heaters** so they run only when needed.
- **Set the temperature:** 19 to 20 °C in living areas and lower in bedrooms. See [the thermostat and the ideal temperature](/guias/termostato-y-temperatura-ideal/).
- **Use the heat pump in your air conditioning** if you have one.
- **Seal draughts and insulate the shutter boxes.** See [draught strips and air leaks](/guias/burletes-y-corrientes-de-aire/).
- **Make use of the sun** by opening the shutters on sunny façades during the day.
- **Review your tariff:** if your heaters have a lot of inertia, a tariff with a cheap off-peak period can help.

## Safety with electric heating

- **Plug heaters straight into the wall,** not into power strips or extension leads, which can overheat at 1,500 or 2,000 W.
- **Do not cover them** or dry clothes on them, except on towel rails designed for it.
- **Keep a distance** from curtains, sofas, beds and papers.
- **In the bathroom,** use only appliances made for that use, installed at the proper distances from the bath and shower.
- **A hot or blackened plug or socket** points to a bad connection: stop using that socket and have an electrician check it.
- **With children or older people,** choose heaters whose surface does not burn to the touch and, for portable ones, with automatic tip-over cut-off.

## In a second home

- **Frost-protection mode:** in cold areas it keeps the home a few degrees above freezing to protect the pipes, with low consumption.
- **Remote-controlled thermostats or plugs:** they let you switch the heating on a few hours before you arrive instead of leaving it on all week.
- **Contracted power:** if you only use the house a few weeks a year, the power term may weigh more than the energy on your bill. Set it to what you really switch on at the same time. See [changing your contracted power](/guias/cambiar-la-potencia-contratada/).

## Common mistakes when buying

- **Believing in miraculous saving percentages** for resistive heaters.
- **Buying more power than you need:** a heater that is too powerful uses the same for the same heat demand, but it may force you to raise your contracted power.
- **Not looking at the controls:** a good thermostat and scheduling matter more than what the radiator is made of.
- **Ruling out a heat pump** because of its upfront price without working out the running cost.

## Case study: from radiators to a split

A 70-square-metre flat in Zaragoza was heated with four 1,500 W panel heaters. The previous winter, heating came to about 3,800 kWh, more than €650 with taxes. The flat already had two heat-pump splits for the summer, in the living room and the main bedroom.

The family decided to use the splits as their main heating and keep the panel heaters only for the bathroom and a small bedroom. With an estimated real SCOP of 3.5, the following winter's heating used about 1,300 kWh, around €230. They were also able to lower their contracted power from 6.9 to 4.6 kW, saving more than €80 a year on the power term as well.

## Checklist

- Check whether your air conditioners have a heat pump.
- Heat only the rooms you use.
- Schedule moderate times and temperatures.
- Review your contracted power after changing system.
- Seal draughts before buying more heaters.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do panel heaters use less than oil-filled radiators?

At the same temperature and for the same hours, they use the same per kWh of heat. They can save if their control is more precise and they are scheduled better.

### Is it better to leave the radiator on low all day?

Usually not. It is more efficient to heat when the room is in use, except with systems that have a lot of inertia.

### Does air conditioning heat well in winter?

Modern heat-pump units heat well even with low outdoor temperatures, with far greater efficiency than electric radiators.

### Do infrared panels save money?

Their efficiency is the same as any resistive heater. They may let you feel comfortable with slightly cooler air, but they work no miracles.

## In short

Every resistive electric heater has the same efficiency: 1 kWh of electricity gives 1 kWh of heat. Their differences lie in how they spread heat, their inertia and their control. The only way to multiply efficiency is a heat pump, which can cut the cost to a quarter or a third. If you already have air conditioning with a heat pump, use it to heat; if not, heat only what you need, schedule carefully and improve the insulation.
