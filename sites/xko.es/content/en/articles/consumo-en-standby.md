The television with its red light, the microwave showing the time, the set-top box, the console, the smart speaker, the charger plugged in without a phone, the printer waiting for jobs: a modern home is full of appliances that use electricity even when nobody is using them. Each one uses little, but together they run 24 hours a day, 365 days a year.

This guide explains how much standby consumption amounts to, how to measure your home's base load, which appliances use most while "off" and which solutions work without making your life complicated.

## What standby consumption is

We speak of **standby consumption** when an appliance is connected to the mains but is not doing its main job. It covers several situations:

- **Standby proper:** the appliance waits for a command from the remote or a button.
- **Networked or "quick start" mode:** the appliance keeps its internet connection to update itself or switch on faster. It uses a good deal more than classic standby.
- **"Switched-off" appliances that still draw power:** internal transformers that stay connected.
- **Permanent functions:** clocks, displays, indicator lights.

## How much it amounts to

European ecodesign rules limit the standby consumption of many new appliances to 0.5 W or 1 W in basic modes, and set limits for networked standby. But many homes have older appliances or appliances in higher-consumption modes.

| Appliance | Indicative standby consumption |
|---|---|
| Modern television on standby | 0.3 to 1 W |
| Television with quick start or networked standby | 5 to 15 W |
| TV set-top box | 5 to 20 W |
| Console in rest mode with downloads | 5 to 15 W |
| Hi-fi or soundbar | 1 to 10 W |
| Microwave with a clock | 1 to 3 W |
| Printer on standby | 2 to 6 W |
| Computer switched off with the power supply connected | 1 to 5 W |
| Smart speaker | 2 to 4 W |
| Chargers without a device | 0.1 to 0.5 W |
| Router (always on) | 8 to 15 W |

In a home with many appliances, standby consumption can add up to **30 to 60 W continuously,** which is **260 to 525 kWh a year.** At €0.20/kWh, that is **€50 to €100 a year** for appliances nobody is using.

### The one-watt rule

An appliance that draws power continuously runs for all 8,760 hours of the year. So **every permanent watt means about 8.8 kWh a year,** around €1.75 at €0.20/kWh. With this rule you can value what the plug-in meter shows in an instant:

| Continuous consumption | Energy per year | Cost per year |
|---|---|---|
| 1 W | 8.76 kWh | €1.75 |
| 5 W | 43.8 kWh | €8.76 |
| 10 W | 87.6 kWh | €17.52 |
| 25 W | 219 kWh | €43.80 |
| 50 W | 438 kWh | €87.60 |

If the appliance is only on standby for part of the day, multiply by those hours: a 14 W set-top box on standby 20 hours a day costs about €20 a year.

## Measure your home's base load

The **base load** is what your home uses when nobody is doing anything: in the early hours, with everyone asleep. It includes the fridge, the router and all the standby.

### How to measure it

1. **On the meter display:** in the early hours, or with everything switched off, look at the instantaneous power. See [estimated readings and smart meters](/guias/lecturas-estimadas-y-contador-inteligente/).
2. **In the distribution company's hourly data:** consumption between 3:00 and 5:00 in the morning is a good approximation. If you use 0.15 kWh in each of those hours, your base load is about 150 W.
3. **With a monitor in the fuse board,** if you have one.

### What it means

| Base load | What it means |
|---|---|
| 50 to 100 W | Efficient: fridge, router and little standby |
| 100 to 200 W | Normal, with room for improvement |
| 200 to 400 W | High: there are appliances or permanent loads to check |
| More than 400 W | Very high: look for water heaters, pumps, heaters, aquariums or equipment running for no reason |

## How to track down hidden loads

### The fuse-board method

1. Look at the instantaneous power on the meter.
2. Switch the circuit breakers off one by one (except the fridge's, for long) and watch how much the power drops.
3. That tells you which part of the home the load is in.

### A plug-in energy meter

Plug the meter into each suspect appliance or power strip and measure its standby consumption. Within a few minutes you will know which appliances use the most.

### The usual suspects

- Set-top boxes and pay-TV equipment.
- Consoles with active rest mode.
- Desktop computers that are never switched off.
- Old hi-fi systems.
- Pumps for water tanks or swimming pools.
- Heated towel rails, forgotten dehumidifiers.
- Electric water heaters without a schedule. See [hot water: electric water heaters and the alternatives](/guias/agua-caliente-termo-electrico-y-alternativas/).

### Beyond the living room

The TV unit is the classic suspect, but standby consumption is spread all over the home:

- **Kitchen:** microwaves and ovens with clocks, coffee machines that keep water hot, food processors and cooker hoods with indicator lights.
- **Home office:** printer, monitors that never fully switch off, computer speakers, network drives and laptop docks.
- **Bedrooms and bathroom:** second televisions, charging docks, lamps with transformers and electric toothbrushes on their base.
- **Garage, storeroom and outdoors:** chargers for tools, bikes or scooters that stay plugged in, doorbells with transformers and lights on timers.
- **Smart home devices:** every smart bulb, plug, camera or speaker draws a little to stay connected even when it is off. Individually it is little, but a home with twenty connected devices adds up to several permanent watts. Bear it in mind before automating something a switch would solve.

## Solutions that work

### Power strips with a switch

Group the appliances in one area — the television, set-top box, console and soundbar — on a power strip with a switch and turn it off at night and when you go out. It is the cheapest and most effective solution.

### Master-slave power strips

They detect when you switch off the main appliance, such as the television or the computer, and automatically cut power to the peripherals.

### Timer or smart plugs

They let you set schedules: for example, cutting the set-top box and the console from 1:00 to 17:00. Smart ones are controlled from your phone, but they use a little themselves (0.5 to 1 W), so use them where the saving justifies it.

### Switching off quick start

On televisions, consoles and computers, look in the settings for options such as "quick start", "networked standby" or "rest mode". Turning them off cuts standby consumption a great deal at the cost of a slightly slower start.

### Switching computers off fully

Shut the computer down at night instead of leaving it in extended sleep, unless you need it.

### The router

The router uses little, but continuously. Some let you schedule the wifi to switch off at night. Consider whether it is worth it, bearing in mind devices such as alarms or home automation that may depend on it.

### Power strips: how to use them safely

- **Respect the maximum power** shown on the strip and do not plug heaters, ovens, irons or other high-power appliances into it.
- **Do not daisy-chain strips** into one another.
- **Keep them visible and ventilated,** never under rugs or behind curtains, and replace them if they get hot, discoloured or the switch fails.
- **Put the switch where you can reach it easily.** A strip hidden behind the furniture ends up never being switched off.

## What you should not unplug

- **Fridges and freezers.**
- **Alarm and security equipment.**
- **Medical devices.**
- **Boilers and thermostats** that need power to protect themselves from frost.
- **Equipment that loses its settings** or needs long updates if it is disconnected often; judge case by case.

## Case study: a family living room

A family used a plug-in meter to measure the living-room TV unit with everything "off":

| Appliance | Standby consumption measured |
|---|---|
| Television with quick start | 11 W |
| Set-top box | 14 W |
| Console in rest mode with downloads | 9 W |
| Soundbar | 4 W |
| Lamp with transformer | 2 W |
| **Total** | **40 W** |

Running like that for about 20 hours a day, the unit used nearly 290 kWh a year on standby alone, about €55 to €60 with taxes.

After grouping the appliances on a switched power strip that goes off at bedtime, turning off the television's quick start and scheduling the console's downloads for a set time, standby consumption fell to under 5 W during the hours everything is off. The saving was over €45 a year for an outlay of €15 on a power strip.

## Checklist

- Measure your base load in the early hours.
- Walk round the home noting appliances with a light, clock or display on.
- Measure the suspect groups with a plug-in meter.
- Fit switched power strips in the living room and at the desk.
- Check the energy-saving settings of televisions, consoles and computers.
- Unplug appliances you only use occasionally.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it really worth unplugging chargers?

A modern charger without a device uses very little. The saving is small, but unplugging them also reduces risks. The big standby loads are usually in the audiovisual and computer equipment.

### Is switching the television off with the remote enough?

On modern televisions without quick start, standby consumption is very low. With networked functions on, it can be several times higher.

### How much do I save with a power strip?

If it groups appliances drawing 20 W on standby for 16 hours a day, it saves about 117 kWh a year, more than €20.

### Do smart plugs use power?

Yes, around 0.5 to 1 W each. Use them where the load they cut is larger.

## In short

Standby consumption can cost €50 to €100 a year in a home with many appliances. Measure your base load in the early hours on the meter or in the distribution company's data, track down hidden loads with a plug-in meter or by switching circuits off, group your audiovisual and computer equipment on switched power strips, turn off quick-start modes and leave alone the appliances that must stay connected.
