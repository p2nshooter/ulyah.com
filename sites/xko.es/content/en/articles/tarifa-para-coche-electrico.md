Charging an electric car at home is by far the cheapest way to cover kilometres in it. But the saving depends a great deal on how you do it: what time you charge, what power you have contracted, which tariff you are on and whether you make use of solar panels. The same car can cost less than €2 per 100 km charging at night on a good tariff, or three times as much charging at peak times or at public rapid chargers.

This guide explains what charging at home costs, which power and tariff suit you, how to install a charge point in a house or a shared garage and how to make use of solar energy.

## How much an electric car uses

An electric car uses between **14 and 20 kWh per 100 km,** depending on the model, speed and temperature. Add to that the charging losses, 10 to 15 % for home charging.

| Kilometres a year | Approximate energy needed (with losses) |
|---|---|
| 8,000 km | 1,300 to 1,800 kWh |
| 12,000 km | 1,950 to 2,700 kWh |
| 20,000 km | 3,200 to 4,500 kWh |

For an average household, an electric car can mean a 50 to 100 % increase in electricity consumption. That is why the tariff and the time of charging matter so much.

## What 100 km costs

| How you charge | Indicative price per kWh | Cost per 100 km (17 kWh + losses) |
|---|---|---|
| At home, off-peak on a time-of-use tariff | €0.08 to €0.12 | €1.50 to €2.30 |
| At home, single fixed price | €0.13 to €0.18 | €2.50 to €3.50 |
| At home, with solar surplus | Opportunity cost of €0.05 to €0.07 | €1.00 to €1.40 |
| Public rapid charger | €0.40 to €0.65 | €7 to €11 |
| Equivalent petrol car (6 l/100 km) | — | €9 to €11 |

The difference between charging at home overnight and at a rapid charger is enormous. Most drivers who can charge at home do so 80 to 90 % of the time.

## Power: raise only the off-peak period

On the 2.0TD domestic tariff you can contract different power in two periods:

- **P1 (peak and mid-peak):** 8:00 to 24:00 on working days.
- **P2 (off-peak):** 0:00 to 8:00, and all weekend and on national holidays.

P2 power is much cheaper than P1. The usual strategy is to **raise only P2** so that you can charge the car at night without tripping the power, while keeping P1 matched to the home's needs during the day. See [how much power to contract](/guias/que-potencia-contratar/) and [changing your contracted power](/guias/cambiar-la-potencia-contratada/).

### Which charging power to choose

| Charging power | Type | Range recovered in an 8-hour night |
|---|---|---|
| 2.3 kW | Household socket (portable charger, not recommended for daily use) | About 100 km |
| 3.7 kW | Single-phase wallbox at 16 A | About 160 to 190 km |
| 7.4 kW | Single-phase wallbox at 32 A | About 330 to 380 km |
| 11 kW | Three-phase wallbox | More than most people need |

For most daily use, **3.7 kW is enough:** it recovers a normal day's kilometres in a few hours. With 7.4 kW you will have more headroom, but you will need more power in P2.

### Dynamic power management

Many chargers include **dynamic load management:** they measure the home's consumption and adjust the charging power so as not to exceed the contracted power. It lets you contract less power and avoid trips when, for example, the dishwasher coincides with charging.

## Which tariff suits you

### Time-of-use pricing with a cheap off-peak period

Free-market offers with three periods and a low off-peak price are usually the most attractive if you charge at night. Some retailers offer tariffs specifically for electric cars with a particularly cheap off-peak period or "super off-peak" hours.

### The PVPC

The regulated price changes every hour. The early hours are usually cheap, and on very sunny days the middle of the day too. If you can schedule charging for each day's cheapest hours, the PVPC can be competitive. See [PVPC or the free market](/guias/pvpc-o-mercado-libre/).

### A single fixed price

It only pays if you cannot charge at night or at midday, or if the off-peak price on other offers is not much cheaper.

### How to decide

Work out the car's consumption and add it to your current consumption split by period: most of the car's will go to off-peak. Compare offers with that profile and with the higher P2 power. See [time-of-use periods](/guias/tramos-horarios-punta-llano-valle/).

## Installing a charge point

### In a house

- **An authorised installer:** the installation must comply with the specific technical instruction for electric-vehicle charging in Spain's Low Voltage Electrotechnical Regulation.
- **A dedicated circuit** with its own protection.
- **Indicative cost:** €800 to €2,000 including the wallbox, depending on the distance to the fuse board and the model.

### In a shared garage

Spain's Horizontal Property Act allows you to install a charge point for private use in your parking space **by notifying the owners' association beforehand,** without needing its approval, as long as you pay the cost. There are several connection arrangements:

- **From your home's own meter:** a cable is run from your meter to the space. It is the most common where the bank of meters is close by.
- **An individual meter at the space:** a new supply with its own contract.
- **A collective installation:** the association installs a shared line ready for several points, with individual meters.

The distance between the bank of meters and the parking space is what pushes the cost up most.

### Grants

There have been grant schemes for buying electric vehicles and installing charge points, as well as income-tax (IRPF) deductions. The conditions change; check the current ones in your autonomous community.

## Charging with solar panels

If you have solar self-consumption, charging the car during the day with surplus power is the cheapest option. Some wallboxes have a **solar mode** that adjusts the charging power to the surplus available at each moment.

- It works best if the car is at home during the day: working from home, weekends or a second car.
- It can be combined with off-peak charging to top up whatever the sun does not cover.

See [solar self-consumption](/guias/autoconsumo-solar-guia-completa/) and [solar surplus compensation](/guias/compensacion-de-excedentes/).

## Good charging practice

- **Schedule charging** from the car or the charger so that it starts in the off-peak period.
- **Do not always charge to 100 %:** many manufacturers recommend charging to 80 % for daily use, which extends battery life.
- **Pre-heat or pre-cool the car** while it is plugged in, especially in winter and summer.
- **Avoid the household socket** as a permanent solution: it is not designed for long, repeated charging.

## Charging away from home: when it makes sense

Although home charging at off-peak times is usually the cheapest, there are situations in which it is worth charging elsewhere:

| Type of point | When it makes sense | Bear in mind |
|---|---|---|
| Slow destination charging | Hotels, car parks or shopping centres where the car stays for hours | Sometimes free or included in the parking; low power |
| Fast charging in town | When you have no garage of your own | The price per kWh varies widely between operators |
| Rapid charging on the road | Long journeys | The dearest option per kWh; use it only for what you need |

Some tips to avoid paying too much:

- **Compare the price per kWh,** not per minute, whenever the operator allows.
- **Check the fees and subscriptions** of charging apps: they only pay if you charge often with that operator.
- **On journeys, charge only what you need** at rapid points to reach the next leg or your destination; the final stretch of charging, above a high battery level, is slower.
- **If you have no garage,** look into public points near your home or work and the terms of car parks with charging.

## Common mistakes

- **Raising the power in every period** when it is only needed off-peak.
- **Charging without a schedule,** as soon as you get home, at peak time.
- **Using the household socket** as a permanent solution.
- **Signing up to a fixed-price tariff** without comparing it with one with a cheap off-peak period.
- **Always using rapid charging** when you could charge at home.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to raise my power to charge the car?

Usually raising P2 (off-peak) power is enough. With a charger that has dynamic control, sometimes not even that.

### How much does the bill go up with an electric car?

At 12,000 km a year charging off-peak, the increase may be around €200 to €300 a year, against more than €1,000 for petrol.

### Can I charge from an ordinary socket?

Occasionally yes, with the car's portable charger, but for daily use a dedicated charge point is recommended.

### What if I have no garage?

You will have to rely on public points, which are dearer. Some town halls and companies offer charging in public car parks at intermediate prices.

## In short

Charging an electric car at home at night, in the off-peak period, can cost less than €2 per 100 km, against €7 to €11 at a rapid charger or in petrol. Raise only your off-peak power, choose a tariff with a cheap off-peak period or the PVPC if you can schedule charging, install a wallbox with power control and, if you have solar panels, use the midday surplus.
