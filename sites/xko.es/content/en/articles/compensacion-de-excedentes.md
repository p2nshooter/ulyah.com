When a solar installation produces more electricity than the home is using at that moment, the spare energy goes out to the grid. That energy has value, and the way you are paid for it goes a long way to deciding how profitable your panels are. In Spain, most homes with self-consumption are on **simplified surplus compensation**, a mechanism that deducts the value of the exported energy from the electricity bill.

This guide explains how compensation works, how it is calculated on the bill, what its limits are, how the PVPC and free-market offers differ and what so-called virtual batteries are.

## What simplified compensation is

Simplified compensation is an arrangement governed by Royal Decree 244/2019. In each billing period, the energy you feed into the grid is valued at a price and **deducted from the energy term** of your bill.

To use it, several conditions must be met:

- The installation must be **renewable**.
- The installation's total output cannot exceed **100 kW**.
- There must be a **single supply contract** for the associated consumption.
- The installation cannot receive any other form of payment.

In homes, all these conditions are almost always met.

## How it is calculated on the bill

Each month (or billing period), the bill shows:

1. **Energy taken from the grid:** the kWh you drew from the grid when your panels were not producing enough.
2. **Energy exported:** the kWh you supplied to the grid.
3. **Value of the exported energy:** the kWh exported multiplied by the compensation price.
4. **Net energy term:** the cost of the energy used minus the value of the surplus.

### The main limit

Compensation **cannot make the energy term negative.** At most it can bring it to zero. The power term, the meter rental and the taxes are still paid.

That is why, in summer months with high output and little grid consumption, it is common for part of the surplus to go unused: compensation hits its ceiling and the rest is lost.

### Example

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Energy taken from the grid | 150 kWh × €0.14/kWh = €21.00 |
| Energy exported | 300 kWh × €0.06/kWh = €18.00 |
| Energy term after compensation | 21.00 − 18.00 = €3.00 |

Had 450 kWh been exported, it would have been worth €27, but only €21 could be compensated. The remaining €6 would not be paid, unless the retailer offers some way of banking it.

## The compensation price on the PVPC and on the free market

### On the PVPC

If you are on the regulated tariff, the surplus is valued at a regulated price linked to the wholesale market in each hour. In the middle of sunny days, when there is a lot of solar output across the country, the wholesale price can be very low, so compensation per kWh is low too.

### On the free market

Each retailer sets its own compensation price:

- **A fixed price:** for example €0.05 to €0.08/kWh for the life of the contract.
- **An indexed price:** linked to the wholesale market, similar to the PVPC.
- **With conditions:** some offers require a tie-in period or add-on services.

The compensation price must be compared together with the purchase price of energy and power. An offer that pays very well for surplus but charges a lot for energy or power can work out worse.

## Virtual batteries

To solve the problem of lost surplus, many retailers offer a **virtual battery** (also called a solar wallet or solar piggy bank). It works like this:

- The surplus that cannot be compensated in the month is banked in euros in a virtual account.
- That balance is used in later months to reduce other items on the bill, such as power, or consumption in months with less output.

### What to check in a virtual battery

| Aspect | Question |
|---|---|
| Cost | Is there a monthly fee? Does it pay off with your surplus? |
| Expiry | Does the banked balance expire? |
| What it can be set against | Energy only, or power and taxes too? |
| Portability | What happens to the balance if you change retailer? |
| Valuation price | At what price is the banked surplus valued? |

A virtual battery with a monthly fee may not pay off if your unused surplus is small.

## How to compare offers for self-consumption

1. **Work out your surplus and your grid consumption month by month.** The inverter and the distribution company's website give you these figures.
2. **Price them with each offer:** energy price, power price, compensation price and virtual-battery conditions.
3. **Apply the compensation limit** month by month.
4. **Add taxes** and compare the total annual cost.
5. **Check tie-in periods and linked services.**

In many cases the best offer for a household with self-consumption is not the one with the highest compensation price but the one that balances every item well for your pattern.

### Example: two offers with the monthly limit applied

Imagine a household with the same power price in both offers and two typical months: a winter one with 300 kWh bought from the grid and 80 kWh exported, and a summer one with 150 kWh bought and 350 kWh exported.

| Month | Offer A (energy €0.13/kWh; surplus €0.08/kWh) | Offer B (energy €0.11/kWh; surplus €0.05/kWh) |
|---|---|---|
| Winter | 39.00 − 6.40 = €32.60 | 33.00 − 4.00 = €29.00 |
| Summer | 19.50 − 28.00 → €0.00 (€8.50 lost) | 16.50 − 17.50 → €0.00 (€1.00 lost) |
| Total | €32.60 | €29.00 |

Offer A pays more for surplus, but in summer the ceiling wipes out much of that advantage, and in winter, with little surplus, the purchase price weighs more. Repeat the sum with your own twelve months before deciding.

## Self-consumption and time-of-use periods

With panels, your grid consumption changes shape: it drops sharply in the middle of the day, and what you buy is concentrated in the evening, at night and on cloudy days. That affects which tariff suits you:

- **With different prices by period,** the panels cover much of the 10:00 to 14:00 peak, but not the 18:00 to 22:00 one. If you can move consumption to the night or the weekend, off-peak works in your favour.
- **With a single price all day,** every kWh you buy costs the same. It can suit you if your night-time consumption is small and hard to move.
- **On the PVPC,** the price changes every hour, and the evening and night hours, when the panels no longer produce, are usually dearer than midday.

See [time-of-use periods: peak, mid-peak and off-peak](/guias/tramos-horarios-punta-llano-valle/) to see which loads can be shifted.

## How to make better use of the surplus

The most profitable way to use surplus is not to have it: every kWh you use directly is worth the full purchase price plus taxes, while an exported kWh is worth much less.

- **Shift loads to the sunny hours:** washing machine, dishwasher, dryer, oven.
- **Use hot water as a battery:** programme the water heater or heat pump water heater for midday.
- **Heat or cool in the sunny hours:** pre-heat or pre-cool the house with the heat pump.
- **Charge the electric car during the day,** if possible.
- **Use smart plugs or relays** that switch appliances on when there is surplus.

## The paperwork for compensation

Compensation is activated once the installation has been legalised. Usually the installer:

1. Registers the installation with the autonomous community's self-consumption register.
2. The distribution company receives the information and assigns or updates the self-consumption code.
3. The retailer is notified and signs a **compensation agreement** with the holder.

This can take weeks or months. During that time the surplus may not be compensated. More in [paperwork and legalising self-consumption](/guias/tramites-y-legalizacion-del-autoconsumo/).

## Case study: the summer the surplus was lost

A home in Córdoba with 4 kWp produced 680 kWh in July and exported 470 kWh, but only drew 160 kWh from the grid. At a compensation price of €0.06/kWh, the surplus was worth €28.20, but the month's energy term was only €22.40. €22.40 was compensated and €5.80 lost. Over June, July and August, the losses added up to more than €15.

The family weighed two options:

- A virtual battery with a fee of €2 a month: it would have recovered about €15 a year, but cost €24. Not worth it.
- Programming the air conditioning, the electric water heater and the washing machine for midday to raise direct self-consumption: no cost and a bigger saving.

They chose the second. The following year, the energy exported in summer fell and hardly any surplus was lost.

## Checklist

- Check month by month the energy exported and the energy compensated.
- Work out whether you lose surplus in summer.
- Compare the cost of a virtual battery with what you would recover.
- Raise your direct self-consumption before anything else.

## Frequently asked questions

### Am I paid in cash for my surplus?

Not with simplified compensation: it is deducted from the bill. To be paid in cash you would have to use the sales arrangement, with more paperwork and tax obligations.

### Why does my bill not fall as much as I expected?

Because compensation only reduces the energy term and cannot take it below zero. Power, the meter and taxes are still paid.

### Can I change retailer if I have self-consumption?

Yes. Check the new compensation agreement and what happens to your virtual-battery balance if you have one.

### Is surplus taxed for income tax (IRPF)?

For simplified compensation in homes, the Tax Agency has generally considered that the saving is not taxable income. Check with a tax adviser if you have doubts about your case.

## In short

Simplified compensation deducts the value of your solar surplus from the energy term of each bill, without being able to take it below zero. The compensation price varies between the PVPC and free-market offers, and virtual batteries let you use leftover surplus, sometimes for a fee. Compare offers with your real monthly figures and, above all, raise your direct self-consumption, which is the most profitable way of using your installation.
