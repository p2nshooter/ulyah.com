Every fridge, washing machine, television or light bulb sold in the European Union carries an **energy label.** It is a tool designed to compare appliances' consumption simply, but many buyers look only at the letter and miss the most useful information: the annual consumption in kWh, which lets you work out what each appliance will cost you over its life.

This guide explains how to read the label, what changed with the new 2021 scale, which data appear for each type of appliance, how to use the European EPREL database and how to work out whether paying more for a better class is worth it.

## The new A to G scale

For years, labels ended up with classes such as A+, A++ and A+++, because most appliances had already passed the old class A. That made it hard to pick out the genuinely efficient models.

Since **March 2021,** the European Union has used a rescaled **A to G** scale for:

- Fridges and freezers.
- Washing machines and washer-dryers.
- Dishwashers.
- Televisions and electronic displays.

And since **September 2021,** for **light sources** (LED bulbs and lamps).

With the rescaling, the new class A is very demanding and, at the time of the change, was left almost empty, to leave room for future improvements. An appliance that used to be A+++ may now be C or D without having got any worse.

### Appliances that still use the old scale

Some products, such as **air conditioners,** **ovens,** **cooker hoods** or **tumble dryers** during certain periods, still use old scales or their own transition timetables. Always check the scale on the specific label.

## How to read the label

A label in the new format includes:

1. **A QR code:** it links to the product's entry in the European EPREL database.
2. **Brand and model.**
3. **The efficiency scale** from A (dark green) to G (red), with the appliance's class highlighted.
4. **Energy consumption:** in kWh per year, per 100 cycles or per 1,000 hours, depending on the appliance.
5. **Pictograms** with information specific to the type of appliance.

### Data specific to each type

| Appliance | Main data |
|---|---|
| Fridge and freezer | Annual consumption in kWh, compartment volumes, noise in dB and noise class |
| Washing machine | Consumption per 100 cycles of the eco programme, capacity in kg, eco programme duration, water per cycle, spin class, noise |
| Dishwasher | Consumption per 100 cycles, number of place settings, water per cycle, eco programme duration, noise |
| Television | Consumption per 1,000 hours in SDR and HDR, screen size, resolution |
| Light bulb | Consumption per 1,000 hours, luminous flux |

### The eco programme

For washing machines and dishwashers, the label's figures refer to the **eco programme,** which is longer but much more efficient. If you always use quick or intensive programmes, your real consumption will be higher. See [efficient washing machines and dishwashers](/guias/lavadora-y-lavavajillas-eficientes/).

## The EPREL database

Scanning the QR code takes you to the product's entry in EPREL, the European energy-labelling database. There you can:

- See the official label and the product information sheet.
- Look up additional technical data.
- Compare models.

It is useful for checking the label of a product you are buying online or for comparing before you go to the shop.

## How much a better class saves

The letter does not tell you how much you will save; the **annual consumption in kWh** does. To compare:

**Annual saving = (consumption of model A − consumption of model B) × price per kWh**

### An example with similar fridge-freezers

| Model | Class | Annual consumption | Annual cost at €0.18/kWh | Cost over 12 years |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | E | 250 kWh | €45 | €540 |
| B | C | 170 kWh | €31 | €367 |
| C | A | 110 kWh | €20 | €238 |

If the class C model costs €120 more than the class E one, the difference is recovered in about 8 or 9 years. If the class A model costs €400 more, it may not pay off on energy alone. That is why it is worth doing the sum each time.

### Which appliances most deserve a good class

- **Those that run for many hours:** fridges and freezers, which are on 24 hours a day.
- **Those used often:** washing machines and dishwashers in large families.
- **Those with high consumption:** tumble dryers (heat pump models use far less) and air conditioners.

For appliances used very occasionally, the class difference matters less.

## Tips for choosing

- **Choose the right size:** a big class A fridge can use more than a small class C one. Compare absolute consumption, not just the letter.
- **Look at the noise** if the appliance will be near bedrooms or the living room.
- **Consider water use** for washing machines and dishwashers.
- **For tumble dryers,** choose a heat pump over a resistive element: consumption can be less than half.
- **For televisions,** compare SDR and HDR consumption; bright picture modes use more.
- **Check for grants:** some appliance-replacement schemes have subsidised the purchase of high-efficiency appliances.

## The label and real consumption

The label is measured under standard laboratory conditions. Real consumption depends on:

- **The room temperature:** a fridge in a very hot kitchen uses more.
- **How you use it:** opening the door often, quick programmes, part loads.
- **Maintenance:** worn seals, dirty filters, limescale.

To find an appliance's real consumption, you can use a plug-in energy meter. See [how much each appliance uses](/guias/consumo-de-los-electrodomesticos/).

## Case study: choosing a washing machine with the label

A family of four does about 220 washes a year and is torn between two 8 kg washing machines:

| Label data | Model 1 | Model 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Class | C | A |
| Consumption per 100 eco cycles | 62 kWh | 48 kWh |
| Water per cycle | 52 litres | 46 litres |
| Eco programme duration | 3 h 20 min | 3 h 40 min |
| Price | €420 | €560 |

At 220 washes a year, model 1 uses about 136 kWh and model 2 about 106 kWh. The 30 kWh difference is worth about €6 a year, plus a water saving of about 1,300 litres. On energy and water alone, the €140 price difference would take many years to recover.

The lesson is not that class A never pays, but that you have to do the sum: in a fridge, which runs all year, the consumption gap between classes usually weighs far more than in a washing machine. If the family often washes at 60 °C, the real difference between the two models may be larger than the label suggests.

## Checklist before buying

- Note the kWh consumption on the label of each model you are comparing.
- Estimate how many years you will use the appliance, usually 10 to 15.
- Multiply the difference in annual consumption by your electricity price and by the years of use.
- Compare that saving with the price difference.
- Check noise, capacity and, for washing appliances, water use.
- Scan the QR code to confirm the data in EPREL.

## Other labels worth knowing

The European energy label is not limited to kitchen and laundry appliances. In the same spirit, though not always on the same scale, you will find it on:

| Product | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Light bulbs and light sources | Luminous efficiency and consumption, on the new A to G scale |
| Televisions and monitors | Consumption in normal use and in high dynamic range |
| Air conditioners and heat pumps | Seasonal efficiency in cooling and heating, by climate zone |
| Boilers and water heaters | Efficiency in space heating and in hot water |
| Ovens and cooker hoods | Consumption per cycle and extraction efficiency |
| Tyres | Rolling resistance, which affects a car's fuel use, wet grip and noise |
| Homes | The energy performance certificate, compulsory when selling or letting |

Beyond the labels, European ecodesign rules set minimum efficiency requirements and, for several types of appliance, require manufacturers to keep spare parts available for years. Before you give up on a broken appliance, ask whether the part exists: repairing it is usually the most efficient option of all.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing on the letter alone** without looking at the annual consumption in kWh.
- **Comparing old-scale and new-scale labels** as if they were equivalent.
- **Buying a bigger appliance than you need** because it has a better class.
- **Forgetting water use** for washing machines and dishwashers.
- **Ignoring the eco programme,** which is the one used to measure the label.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is my old A+++ fridge now a D?

It probably corresponds to an intermediate class on the new scale. The rescaling made the classes more demanding; it did not make your appliance worse.

### Do all appliances carry a label?

No. Microwaves, toasters and small kitchen appliances have no compulsory energy label.

### Is class A always the best buy?

It is the most efficient, but not always the most profitable if its price is much higher. Work out the saving with the annual kWh.

### Where is the label in online shops?

Shops must show it next to the price, often as an icon with the letter that expands when you click it.

## In short

Since 2021 the energy label has used an A to G scale for fridges, washing machines, dishwashers, televisions and light bulbs. Beyond the letter, look at the consumption in kWh, which lets you work out the cost over the appliance's life. Use the QR code to check EPREL, choose the right size and put efficiency first for appliances that run for many hours.
