"How many panels do I need?" is the first question almost everyone asks when considering solar self-consumption. The intuitive answer is "enough to cover all my consumption", but that is rarely the most profitable. Because most solar output is concentrated in the middle of the day and surplus energy is paid far less than the energy you buy, an installation that is too big can add years to the payback.

This guide explains how to size a self-consumption installation sensibly from your real data, with a five-step method, examples by type of home and the most common mistakes.

## Step 1: know your annual consumption and how it is spread

It all starts with your data. You need to know:

- **Annual consumption in kWh:** it is on your bills or on your distribution company's website.
- **The spread by hour:** download the last twelve months' hourly consumption from the distribution company's website with your CUPS.
- **The spread by month:** winter and summer consumption can be very different if you have electric heating or air conditioning.

With the hourly data you can see how much energy you use between 9:00 and 18:00, which is when the installation produces. That is the key figure.

### Reference consumption

| Type of home | Indicative annual consumption |
|---|---|
| Flat for 1 to 2 people without electric heating | 1,800 to 2,500 kWh |
| Family flat with an electric cooker | 2,500 to 3,500 kWh |
| Detached house with air conditioning | 3,500 to 5,000 kWh |
| Detached house with a heat pump | 5,000 to 8,000 kWh |
| With an electric car (10,000 km a year) | + 1,500 to 2,000 kWh |

To learn more about your consumption, see [average electricity use of a home in Spain](/guias/consumo-medio-de-una-casa-en-espana/).

## Step 2: estimate your daytime consumption

Work out what share of your consumption happens in the sunny hours. Some references:

- **A home empty during the day** (everyone works or studies away): 25 to 35 % of consumption is during the day, mainly the fridge, standby and the odd wash.
- **Working from home, or people at home:** 40 to 55 %.
- **A household that schedules appliances and the water heater for midday:** it can exceed 55 %.

The higher this percentage, the more of the installation you can use directly.

## Step 3: work out the output per kWp where you live

Each kilowatt-peak (kWp) produces between 1,100 kWh a year in the north and 1,800 kWh in the south. You can get a more precise estimate for your location, orientation and pitch with the European Commission's free PVGIS tool, which uses several years of radiation data.

| Area | kWh per kWp a year |
|---|---|
| North | 1,100 to 1,300 |
| Centre | 1,400 to 1,600 |
| South and Levante | 1,600 to 1,800 |

## Step 4: choose a size that balances self-consumption and surplus

A practical rule is to size the installation so that its annual output is between **70 and 100 % of your annual consumption,** and no more, unless you have firm plans to use more electricity (an electric car, a heat pump) or a particularly good surplus compensation.

### Why you should not overdo it

- Simplified compensation **cannot take the energy term below zero.** Surplus beyond your grid consumption in the month is lost, unless your retailer offers a "virtual battery".
- The compensation price is a good deal lower than the purchase price.
- Each extra panel produces mostly in the hours when you already have surplus.

### How many panels that is

With 450 W panels:

| Installation size | Number of panels | Approximate area |
|---|---|---|
| 1.8 kWp | 4 | 8 to 9 m² |
| 2.7 kWp | 6 | 12 to 13 m² |
| 3.6 kWp | 8 | 16 to 18 m² |
| 4.5 kWp | 10 | 20 to 22 m² |
| 5.4 kWp | 12 | 24 to 26 m² |

## Step 5: check the roof and the inverter

- **Usable area:** leave out chimneys, skylights and shaded areas.
- **Orientation:** south is ideal; east-west spreads output across morning and afternoon.
- **Structure:** the roof must bear the weight, which is around 15 to 20 kg per square metre including the frame.
- **Inverter:** its rating is usually somewhat lower than the panels', since they rarely produce at 100 % of peak power. A panel-to-inverter ratio of 1.1 to 1.3 is common.

## Examples

### A flat in Madrid with collective self-consumption

A family uses 3,000 kWh a year and takes part in a shared installation. Their share is 1.5 kWp, producing about 2,250 kWh. Because the association shares out the output by coefficients, their direct self-consumption depends on how much the family uses during the day.

### A detached house in Seville with home working

Consumption of 4,500 kWh a year with air conditioning in summer and 50 % daytime consumption. A 3 kWp installation produces about 5,100 kWh. About 2,300 kWh are used directly and 2,800 exported. If an electric car charged at midday were added, the same installation would raise self-consumption considerably.

### A house in Asturias with a heat pump

Consumption of 6,500 kWh concentrated in winter. A 4 kWp installation produces about 4,800 kWh, but mostly in summer. Direct self-consumption in winter will be low. In this case, a somewhat more modest installation, combined with a good tariff and better insulation, may be more profitable.

## Common sizing mistakes

- **Sizing by the roof space available** instead of by consumption.
- **Not considering future plans.** If you are thinking of buying an electric car or replacing the boiler with a heat pump within two years, take it into account from the start; extending an installation later is possible but costs more.
- **Ignoring winter shading,** when the sun is lower.
- **Calculating with total consumption** instead of daytime consumption.
- **Trusting output estimates without a source.** Ask the company to state how it calculated the output.

## How batteries affect the size

With a battery, part of the day's surplus is stored for the night. That can justify a somewhat larger installation. But the battery costs several thousand euros. Work out the installation without a battery first, and then study whether adding one improves the payback. See [batteries for solar self-consumption](/guias/baterias-para-autoconsumo/).

## Checklist before asking for quotes

- Download your hourly consumption for twelve months.
- Work out your annual consumption and your consumption between 9:00 and 18:00.
- Simulate your roof's output in PVGIS with the real orientation and pitch.
- Note your plans for the coming years: electric car, heat pump, pool, home working.
- Measure the roof's usable area and any winter shading.
- Decide whether you want the inverter ready for batteries.

## How to read the quotes you receive

With your figures in hand, ask for at least three quotes and compare them point by point, not just on the final price:

| Section | What it should state |
|---|---|
| Peak power | Total kWp, the number of panels and their make and model |
| Inverter | Make, model and rating, and whether it is battery-ready |
| Mounting | The type of roof fixing and how watertightness is guaranteed |
| Protection and wiring | The DC and AC protection devices included |
| Estimated output | kWh a year and the expected self-consumption percentage, with the tool used to calculate it |
| Paperwork | Whether it includes the municipal permit, certificate, registration and notification to the distribution company |
| Guarantees | For panels, inverter and installation, in writing |
| Timing and payments | The installation date and payment schedule |

Be wary of quotes that do not detail the estimated output, of those that promise savings without saying which electricity price they used, and of offers that press you to sign the same day. If an installer proposes a very different size from the one your own figures give, ask them to justify it with numbers.

## When a somewhat larger installation does make sense

Although the general rule is not to oversize, there are situations in which an installation somewhat larger than current consumption is worthwhile:

- **You are going to electrify your heating** with a heat pump in the next few years.
- **You are going to buy an electric car** that will be at home during the day.
- **Your retailer offers a virtual battery** that lets you use the surplus at no or low cost.
- **The marginal cost of a few more panels is small** because the frame, inverter and labour are already included.

In these cases the installer should show with numbers how much of the extra output will actually be used.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it better to install more panels just in case?

Not necessarily. Each extra panel produces mostly surplus, which is paid little. Size to your consumption and leave room to extend if your needs change.

### Can I extend the installation later?

Yes, but it may need a new or additional inverter and new legalisation. If you expect to extend, ask for an inverter with headroom.

### What inverter rating do I need?

Usually somewhat below the panels' total. The installer should justify it according to the orientation and climate.

### How many panels fit on a typical roof?

It depends on its shape and orientation, but a detached house usually has room for 6 to 15 panels on the well-oriented slope.

## In short

To know how many panels you need, start from your annual consumption and, above all, your daytime consumption. Work out the output per kWp for your area with reliable data, size the system to produce 70 to 100 % of your annual consumption, check the roof and the shading and take your future plans into account. An installation matched to your consumption pays for itself sooner than one that fills the roof.
