Domestic hot water — the water for showers, basins and the kitchen sink — is one of the big invisible loads in a home. In a home without electric heating it can account for 15 to 25 % of all the energy used, and in a flat with an electric water heater (a *termo*) it is often the appliance that uses the most electricity after the fridge and heating or cooling.

This guide explains how much energy it takes to heat a family's water, how to cut consumption with the system you already have, especially an electric water heater, and how to compare the alternatives: a boiler, a heat pump water heater and solar thermal.

## How much energy hot water uses

Heating water takes a lot of energy. Raising 1 litre of water by 1 °C needs about 1.16 Wh. For an 8-minute shower with a flow of 9 litres a minute, at 38 °C from mains water at 15 °C:

72 litres × 23 °C × 1.16 Wh ≈ 1.9 kWh

A family of four who shower every day can need 5 to 8 kWh a day for showers alone, that is, 1,800 to 2,900 kWh a year, before counting the sink or the basin.

| Household | Indicative annual hot-water demand |
|---|---|
| 1 person | 600 to 900 kWh |
| 2 people | 1,100 to 1,600 kWh |
| 4 people | 2,000 to 3,000 kWh |

On top of this come the system's **losses**: heat escaping from the tank, the pipes and the appliance itself.

## The electric water heater: how it works and what it uses

An electric water heater is an insulated tank with an element, usually of 1,500 to 2,000 W, that heats the water and keeps it at the temperature set on the thermostat.

Its consumption has two parts:

- **Heating the water that is used:** this depends on the litres and the temperature.
- **Keeping the tank hot:** losses through the insulation. In an 80 to 100-litre heater these can be 1 to 2 kWh a day in older models, less in modern ones with better insulation.

For a family of four, an electric water heater can use 2,500 to 3,500 kWh a year. At €0.16/kWh, that is €400 to €560.

### How to save with your electric water heater

- **Set the temperature:** 50 to 55 °C is enough for daily use. Higher temperatures increase losses. But do not keep it much below 50 °C permanently because of the risk of legionella growing; some heaters have automatic anti-legionella cycles.
- **Programme the heater:** with a timer or a programmable plug, make it heat in the cheapest hours of your tariff — at night if you have time-of-use pricing, or around midday if you have solar panels. See [time-of-use periods](/guias/tramos-horarios-punta-llano-valle/).
- **Switch it off when you are away for a while:** holidays or weekends away.
- **Check the magnesium anode:** it protects the tank against corrosion. Replacing it every few years extends the heater's life.
- **Remove the limescale:** in hard-water areas, scale builds up on the element and lowers efficiency.
- **Insulate the hot-water pipes** that run through cold spaces.

### Switch the heater off every day?

Switching the heater off during the day and on again before use reduces standing losses, but the tank then has to be reheated. In well-insulated heaters the saving is modest. What works best is **concentrating the heating in the cheap hours** with a schedule.

### How to measure what your heater uses

The smart meter records your consumption hour by hour, and you can download it from your distribution company's website with your CUPS supply-point code. See [smart meters and estimated readings](/guias/lecturas-estimadas-y-contador-inteligente/).

- **If it is programmed for a fixed slot,** say 2:00 to 6:00, add up the consumption in those hours and subtract the home's base load at that time (fridge and appliances on standby). The result is, quite closely, what the heater uses each day.
- **To measure the standing losses,** leave the heater on once while you are away for a day or two: whatever is used above the base load is heat escaping from the tank, because nobody is using hot water. That figure tells you how much you save by switching it off when you are away, and whether it is worth replacing.

### Common water-heater problems and what they mean

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| The safety valve drips while heating | The water expands; a small drip is normal | Lead it to a drain; never block or disable the valve |
| It drips all the time, even when idle | High mains pressure or a faulty valve | Have a plumber assess a pressure reducer or a new valve |
| The hot water runs out sooner than before | Scale on the element or a faulty thermostat | Service and descaling |
| The residual-current breaker trips when it starts heating | A current leak in the element or the wiring | Disconnect the heater and call a technician |
| Cloudy or reddish water, or a rotten-egg smell | A spent anode, corrosion or bacteria in a lukewarm tank | Check the anode and heat to a high temperature if the manufacturer says so |

Any work on the element, the thermostat or the anode means first cutting the power at the fuse board and draining the tank. If you have no experience, leave it to a professional.

## Habits that cut hot-water use

- **Shorter showers:** each minute less saves about 0.25 kWh in a typical shower.
- **Efficient shower heads:** they cut the flow from 12 to 15 litres a minute to 6 to 9 without losing comfort. They are one of the cheapest and most effective measures.
- **Aerators on the taps.**
- **Single-lever or thermostatic mixer taps:** they avoid wasting water while you adjust the temperature.
- **Washing clothes at 30 °C** instead of 40 or 60 °C when you can. See [efficient washing machines and dishwashers](/guias/lavadora-y-lavavajillas-eficientes/).
- **Using the dishwasher** rather than washing up by hand under running hot water.

## The systems compared

Indicative annual cost for a family of four with a hot-water demand of 2,500 kWh:

| System | Indicative efficiency | Energy used | Indicative annual cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electric water heater | 85 to 90 % (with losses) | 2,800 kWh of electricity | €450 |
| Condensing gas boiler | 85 to 95 % | 2,750 kWh of gas | €220 plus part of the fixed gas charge |
| Heat pump water heater | Average COP of 2.5 to 3.5 | 800 to 1,000 kWh of electricity | €130 to €160 |
| Solar thermal with electric backup | Covers 50 to 70 % | 900 kWh of electricity | €145 |
| Electric water heater with solar PV panels | Depends on the solar surplus | Partly free | Varies widely |

## Heat pump water heaters

This is a unit with a built-in tank (100 to 300 litres) and a small heat pump on top that draws heat from the surrounding air. It uses between a third and a half of the electricity of an electric water heater.

**Advantages:**

- A big saving compared with an electric water heater.
- A fairly simple swap in the same place as the old heater, if there is enough space and ventilation or the air can be ducted.
- It fits very well with solar panels: heating the water at midday is like having a thermal battery.

**Drawbacks:**

- A higher price: €1,500 to €3,000 installed.
- It needs a room with a large enough volume of air, or ducts to the outside.
- It makes some noise and slightly cools the space it stands in.

## Solar thermal

Solar thermal collectors heat water directly with the sun. Spain's Technical Building Code requires a minimum solar contribution to hot water in new buildings and major renovations, unless it is replaced by other renewables such as a heat pump.

- **Advantages:** it covers much of the annual demand in sunny climates, and the technology is proven.
- **Drawbacks:** it needs maintenance (antifreeze fluid, inspections), it can overheat in summer if no water is used, and it needs roof space.

Today, in many homes, the combination of solar PV with a heat pump water heater, or with an electric water heater programmed for midday, competes very well with solar thermal, with less maintenance.

## Hot water from a heat pump or a boiler

If you have a heat pump for heating, it will usually also produce the hot water with a cylinder. See [air-source heat pumps](/guias/aerotermia/). If you have a gas boiler, instantaneous hot water is efficient; set it to 45 or 50 °C. See [condensing boilers](/guias/caldera-de-condensacion/).

## What to choose

| Situation | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| A flat with an old electric water heater that needs replacing | A heat pump water heater if there is space; if not, a new, well-insulated heater with a schedule |
| A home with solar panels | A water heater or heat pump programmed for midday |
| A home with a gas boiler in good condition | Keep it, set the temperature, fit efficient shower heads |
| A detached house heated by a heat pump | Hot water from the same heat pump |
| A new build or a full renovation | A heat pump or solar thermal, depending on the project |

## Before you install a new unit

- **Capacity to match your habits:** count how many showers are taken one after another in the morning and whether anyone fills the bath. A tank that runs short forces you to raise the temperature and increases losses.
- **The load profile on the label:** water heaters carry an energy label with a load profile (M, L, XL…) that shows the demand they are designed for. Compare models with the same profile. See [the energy label](/guias/etiqueta-energetica/).
- **A position close to the taps:** the longer the pipe to the shower, the more cold water is wasted while you wait and the more heat stays in the pipe after each use.

## Frequently asked questions

### What size of water heater do I need?

As a guide, 50 litres for one or two people, 80 litres for three and 100 to 150 litres for four or more, depending on your habits.

### How long does a water heater take to heat up?

An 80-litre heater going from 15 to 55 °C needs about 80 × 40 × 1.16 ≈ 3.7 kWh; with a 1,500 W element it takes about two and a half hours. If the tank is emptied every day, the programmed slot must last at least that long.

### Does a water heater use much on standby?

An old, poorly insulated heater can lose more than 1 kWh a day. Modern ones lose a good deal less. If your heater is very old, replacing it may pay off.

### Is legionella a danger at home?

The risk exists when water sits for a long time between 25 and 45 °C. Keeping the tank at 50 to 60 °C, or running regular cycles at 60 °C, reduces the risk.

### How much does an efficient shower head save?

Cutting the flow from 12 to 8 litres a minute saves a third of the energy of every shower, for an outlay of a few euros.

## In short

Hot water is a big invisible load in the home. With an electric water heater, set it to 50 to 55 °C, programme it for cheap or sunny hours, keep the anode and the scale in check and fit efficient shower heads. If the unit needs replacing, a heat pump water heater can cut consumption by more than half, and combined with solar panels it becomes a very profitable thermal battery.
