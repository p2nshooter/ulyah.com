A large part of Spain's housing stock was built before there were any thermal insulation requirements at all. There were no rules until the end of the 1970s, and until the Technical Building Code of 2006 the requirements were modest. The result is millions of homes whose walls and roofs let heat escape in winter and soak it up in summer, which means high heating and air-conditioning bills and little comfort.

Insulating the building envelope — the façades, roofs and floors in contact with the outside — is one of the most effective efficiency improvements in the long run. This guide explains the main techniques, what they cost, how much they save, which permissions they need and how to choose the right one for each case.

## Where the energy is lost

In an uninsulated home, heat losses in winter are roughly shared out like this:

| Element | Indicative share of losses |
|---|---|
| Roof (in top-floor flats or detached houses) | 25 to 35 % |
| External walls | 25 to 35 % |
| Windows and doors | 15 to 25 % |
| Air leaks | 10 to 20 % |
| Floors | 5 to 15 % |

The percentages vary a great deal with the type of home. In a mid-floor flat, with neighbours above and below, losses through the roof and floor are small and the façades and windows weigh more. In a detached house, the roof is often the weakest point.

## Insulating external walls

### External wall insulation (SATE)

SATE (*Sistema de Aislamiento Térmico por el Exterior*, an external thermal insulation composite system) means bonding and fixing insulation boards to the outer face of the wall and covering them with a reinforced render and a decorative finish.

**Advantages:**

- It is the most effective solution: it wraps the building and corrects most of the **thermal bridges** at floor-slab edges and pillars. See [thermal bridges](/guias/puentes-termicos/).
- It does not reduce the floor area inside the homes.
- The work is done from outside, with no disruption inside the homes.
- It gives the façade a new look.

**Drawbacks:**

- In blocks of flats it needs the agreement of the owners' association and a municipal licence.
- It needs scaffolding, which makes the work more expensive.
- It is not possible on listed façades or ones with ornamental features that must be kept.

**Indicative cost:** €50 to €100 per square metre of façade, depending on thickness, materials, height and access.

### Cavity-wall fill

Many buildings put up between the 1960s and the 1990s have double-leaf brick walls with an air cavity between them. Cavity fill means filling that cavity with loose insulation (mineral wool, cellulose or polystyrene beads) blown in through small holes.

**Advantages:**

- Cheap and quick: it takes one or two days per home.
- It does not reduce the space inside or change the façade.
- It can be done from inside or from outside.

**Drawbacks:**

- It is only possible if there is a continuous cavity in good condition.
- It does not correct the thermal bridges at pillars and floor slabs.
- The thickness of insulation is limited by the width of the cavity.

**Indicative cost:** €15 to €30 per square metre of façade.

Before filling, a technician must check with a small drill hole or an endoscopic camera that the cavity exists, that it is clean and that there is no damp.

### Internal lining

This means adding a layer of insulation on the inside of the home, covered with plasterboard or a partition wall.

**Advantages:**

- It can be done home by home, without the owners' association's agreement.
- It is useful when the façade cannot be altered.
- It also improves sound insulation.

**Drawbacks:**

- It reduces the usable floor area: 5 to 10 cm per wall.
- Sockets, radiators and furniture have to be taken down and put back.
- It does not correct the thermal bridges at floor slabs.
- Done badly, it can cause condensation inside the wall. It needs a proper design, often with a vapour barrier.

**Indicative cost:** €30 to €60 per square metre.

## Insulating roofs

### Flat roofs and roof terraces

- **Inverted roof:** insulation boards are laid on top of the waterproofing and protected with gravel or tiles. It is a durable solution that also protects the waterproofing.
- **Insulation under the slab:** from inside the top floor, with a false ceiling and insulation. It reduces the ceiling height a little.

### Pitched roof with an unused loft

This is the easiest and most profitable case: you simply spread a thick layer of insulation over the loft floor, either mineral-wool rolls or blown insulation. The cost is low and the saving is considerable.

### Pitched roof with a habitable attic

- **From outside:** lifting the tiles, fitting insulation panels and retiling. Ideal if the roof needs redoing anyway.
- **From inside:** between or under the timber rafters, with a lining. It reduces the height of the room.

**Indicative cost of insulating a roof:** from about €15 to €25 per square metre in an accessible loft to more than €100 per square metre if the roof is rebuilt.

## The most common insulating materials

| Material | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Expanded polystyrene (EPS) | Economical, widely used in external insulation |
| Extruded polystyrene (XPS) | Resistant to damp, suitable for inverted roofs and plinths |
| Mineral wool (rock or glass) | Non-combustible, good sound insulation |
| Polyurethane (PUR/PIR) | Very good insulation with little thickness |
| Wood fibre and cork | Natural materials, good thermal mass |
| Cellulose | Recycled material, common in blown insulation |

**Thermal conductivity** (λ) shows how well a material insulates: the lower it is, the better it insulates. To compare solutions, look at the resulting **thermal resistance** (R), which depends on both the material and the thickness.

## Before you choose: what your building has

The right technique depends on how the building was built, and much of that can be found out before asking for quotes:

- **The original project:** the property manager or the municipal archive may keep plans and a specification showing how the façades and roof are made up.
- **Year of construction:** a building older than the first thermal regulations rarely has any insulation, one from the 1980s or 1990s usually has little, and one built under the Technical Building Code starts from a reasonable level, so the improvements that pay off there are more targeted.
- **Thermal imaging in winter:** it shows up cold pillars, slab edges and shutter boxes, and helps you decide where to start.
- **Damp:** stains, salt deposits or flaking paint must be diagnosed and solved before insulating. Covering a damp wall with insulation makes the problem worse.

## Common mistakes when insulating

- **Forgetting the junctions with the windows:** if the insulation does not reach the frame, jambs and sills remain cold spots with a risk of condensation. Ideally, coordinate external insulation with replacing the windows or at least wrap the openings well.
- **Treating the plinth like the rest of the façade:** the bottom strip takes knocks and damp from the ground; it is usually solved with a water-resistant insulation such as XPS and a reinforced finish.
- **Not reviewing ventilation after the work:** an insulated home with new windows is more airtight; if the air is not renewed, indoor humidity rises.
- **Comparing only the price per square metre:** ask each quote for the final thermal resistance, the finishing details included and the guarantees.

## How much you save

The saving on heating and air conditioning from insulating the envelope depends on the starting point and the climate. Some indicative references:

- Insulating an unused loft in an uninsulated house: a 15 to 25 % saving on heating.
- Cavity fill in a flat: 10 to 25 %.
- Full external insulation of an uninsulated building: 30 to 50 % across the whole building, even more if combined with new windows.

Besides the saving, insulation improves comfort, reduces condensation and mould on cold walls and adds value to the home. It usually improves the rating on the [energy performance certificate](/guias/certificado-energetico-de-la-vivienda/) by one or more letters.

## Permissions and the owners' association

- **External insulation and work on the façade:** these need the agreement of the owners' meeting and a municipal licence. Spain's Horizontal Property Act makes it easier to approve energy-efficiency improvements with smaller majorities in certain cases; the property manager should confirm which majority applies.
- **Occupying the public pavement:** external insulation can add a few centimetres to the façade. Many town halls expressly allow it.
- **Cavity fill:** if it is done from inside, notifying the town hall is usually enough; from outside, it may need the association's agreement.
- **Internal lining:** minor works inside the home.

## Grants and tax deductions

In recent years there have been grant programmes for the energy renovation of buildings and homes, run by the autonomous communities, and income-tax (IRPF) deductions for works that reduce heating and cooling demand or improve the energy rating, proven with energy certificates before and after the work. Conditions and deadlines change; check the current ones before signing a contract.

## Case study

A 1975 building in Valladolid with 20 flats and a brick façade with an air cavity weighs up two options:

- **Cavity fill:** about €20 per square metre, a quick job, an estimated 15 to 20 % saving on heating.
- **10 cm of external insulation:** about €75 per square metre, several months of work, an estimated 35 to 45 % saving, thermal bridges corrected and a renewed façade.

The association chooses external insulation combined with insulating the roof, taking advantage of public grants, because the façade needed repairing anyway. Had the façade been in good condition, cavity fill would have been a very profitable option.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I put external insulation on my flat alone?

Technically it would be possible in some cases, but it is rarely allowed because it alters the shared façade. It is normally decided by the association for the whole building.

### Does insulation help in summer too?

Yes. It reduces the heat coming in through walls and roof. In summer it should be combined with sun protection on the windows.

### How thick does it need to be?

It depends on the climate and the material. In mild climates, 6 to 10 cm of insulation on the façade is usually reasonable; in cold climates, more. A technician should calculate it.

### Does cavity fill cause damp?

Not if it is done in a suitable cavity with the right material. That is why the prior diagnosis matters.

## In short

Insulating the envelope is one of the most effective ways of cutting heating and cooling consumption. On façades, external insulation is the most complete solution, cavity fill is the cheapest when it is possible, and internal lining works home by home. On roofs, insulating a loft is cheap and very profitable. Ask for a technical diagnosis, compare solutions, check the grants available and combine insulation with efficient windows and good ventilation.
