In Spain, any household with contracted power of up to 10 kW can choose between two broad ways of paying for electricity: the **Voluntary Price for Small Consumers (PVPC),** known as the regulated tariff, and **free-market offers,** where each retailer sets its own prices and conditions. There is no universal answer to which is better: it depends on your consumption, your tolerance for price swings and how much you value predictability.

This guide explains how each option works, what has changed in recent years, the advantages and drawbacks of each and how to compare them using your own data instead of trusting the adverts.

## What the PVPC is

The PVPC (*Precio Voluntario para el Pequeño Consumidor*) is a government-regulated price that only **reference retailers,** a small group of designated companies, can offer. Its main feature is that the price of energy **changes every hour,** following the wholesale electricity market, with network tolls, system charges and other regulated costs added on top.

### How the price is formed

Each hour's PVPC price combines:

- **The cost of energy:** it comes from the wholesale market. Since 2024 the formula gradually includes a share of forward (futures) prices, not just the day-ahead market price, to smooth out sharp rises and falls.
- **Network tolls and charges:** the costs of the transmission and distribution networks and other system costs, which vary by time-of-use period.
- **Other regulated costs:** such as financing system services.

The result is a price you can look up each day for the following day on Red Eléctrica's website or in apps that show the price hour by hour.

### Requirements

- Contracted power of 10 kW or less.
- A contract with a reference retailer.
- It is compulsory for the **electricity social discount.** See [the electricity social discount](/guias/bono-social-electrico/).

## What the free market is

On the free market, each retailer designs its own offers. The most common are:

| Type of offer | How it works |
|---|---|
| A single fixed price | The same price per kWh at any hour, usually for 12 months |
| Time-of-use pricing | Three fixed prices for peak, mid-peak and off-peak |
| An indexed price | The price follows the wholesale market plus a margin, similar to the PVPC |
| A flat rate | A fixed monthly fee based on estimated consumption, with catch-up adjustments |
| Offers with services | The energy price combined with maintenance, insurance or other services |

Power is also priced on the free market: some retailers charge per kW at the regulated toll-and-charge rate plus a margin, others apply their own, higher prices.

## The advantages and drawbacks of each option

### The PVPC

**Advantages:**

- No tie-in period or penalty for switching.
- No compulsory add-on services.
- When wholesale prices are low, it is usually among the cheapest.
- Off-peak hours and, with a lot of solar output, the middle of the day can be very cheap.
- It gives access to the social discount.

**Drawbacks:**

- The price changes every hour and every day, which makes the bill hard to predict.
- In episodes of high prices, as in 2022, the bill can shoot up.
- Making the most of the cheap hours takes some attention.

### The free market

**Advantages:**

- A predictable price for the agreed period, usually a year.
- The chance to choose the type of offer that best fits your habits.
- Some offers include discounts for direct debit, e-billing or signing up for gas as well.

**Drawbacks:**

- When wholesale prices fall, you may keep paying a high fixed price until the contract ends.
- It is common for the price to rise on automatic renewal.
- Paid services you do not need may be included.
- Some offers, especially on higher-power supplies, include tie-in periods.

## How to compare with your own data

The most common mistake is to compare the price per kWh in an advert with the one on your bill without taking into account power, temporary discounts or taxes. This method is more reliable:

1. **Download your hourly consumption** for the last twelve months from your distribution company's website. You will need your CUPS, which is on the bill.
2. **Work out the cost on the PVPC:** several public and private tools accept the consumption file and price it with each day's real hourly prices.
3. **Work out the cost with each free-market offer:** multiply your consumption in each period by the offer's prices and add the power charge.
4. **Add taxes** to compare totals: the electricity tax, the meter rental and VAT. See [taxes on the electricity bill](/guias/impuestos-de-la-factura-de-la-luz/).
5. **Compare the annual cost,** not a single month, because winter and summer change the results a great deal.

The National Markets and Competition Commission (CNMC) offers a public comparison tool where you enter your consumption and power to see estimated annual costs.

## Which profile suits which option

| Profile | The option that usually fits best |
|---|---|
| Low, flexible consumption, with appliances scheduled for cheap hours | PVPC or time-of-use pricing |
| A household that values knowing what it will pay each month | A fixed price on the free market |
| An electric car charged overnight | Time-of-use pricing with a cheap off-peak period, or the PVPC |
| Working from home with high consumption at peak times | A single fixed price |
| Potentially eligible for the social discount | The PVPC, which is compulsory for the discount |
| A home with solar self-consumption | Depends on the surplus compensation each retailer offers |

If you have solar panels, the choice changes considerably, because the way surplus is valued differs between the PVPC and each free-market retailer. See [solar surplus compensation](/guias/compensacion-de-excedentes/).

## How to switch from one to the other

Switching between the PVPC and the free market, or between retailers, is free and needs no building work or new meter. All you need is:

- Your CUPS and personal details.
- A bank account for the direct debit.
- To accept the new contract.

The new retailer handles leaving the old one. The switch usually takes effect within a few days or with the next reading. See the full process in [switching electricity supplier step by step](/guias/cambiar-de-comercializadora-paso-a-paso/).

### Check before you sign

- **How long the price lasts:** is it fixed for 12 months or only for the first few?
- **Renewal:** how does the price change on renewal?
- **Tie-in period:** is there a penalty for leaving early?
- **Add-on services:** are any included by default?
- **The power price:** is it the regulated rate or a higher one?

## Common myths

### "The PVPC is always more expensive"

No. There have been years when it was the cheapest option for most households and others when it was the dearest. It depends on how the market moves and on how you use energy.

### "There are more charges on the free market"

Taxes and regulated tolls are the same. What changes is the price of energy, sometimes the price of power, and the add-on services.

### "Changing company could leave me without power"

No. The physical supply is guaranteed by the distribution company, which is the same whatever your retailer. The switch is purely administrative.

### "Flat rates always save money"

A flat rate spreads the payment across the year, but if you use more than estimated there will be catch-up bills, and if you use less you may be paying too much. What it gives you is predictability, not necessarily savings.

## How to interpret the result of the comparison

Once you have the estimated annual cost for each option, the decision is not always obvious:

- **If the difference is small,** choose by preference: a stable price with a fixed offer, or flexibility with no tie-in on the PVPC.
- **If the difference is large,** check that it is not down to a temporary welcome discount that will vanish on renewal.
- **Think about risk:** during the 2021 and 2022 energy crisis, the PVPC soared and fixed-price contracts protected many households; in periods of low prices, the opposite happens. Nobody can predict with certainty how the market will move.
- **Look at seasonality:** an offer may win in summer and lose in winter, depending on your consumption.
- **Repeat the comparison every year,** ideally a month before your contract renews, and put it in the calendar.

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing prices without taxes** or power.
- **Choosing for a welcome discount** that lasts only a few months.
- **Forgetting automatic renewal** and moving to a higher price without noticing.
- **Not checking the power price,** which on some offers is above the regulated rate.
- **Accepting add-on services** that make every bill dearer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I go back to the PVPC if I move to the free market?

Yes, as long as your power is 10 kW or less. Just sign up with a reference retailer, first checking whether your free-market offer has a tie-in period.

### Where can I see tomorrow's PVPC price?

Red Eléctrica publishes the next day's hourly prices every afternoon. Many free apps show them, with alerts.

### Is an indexed free-market tariff a good idea?

It can be worthwhile if its margin is low and it includes no add-on services, but compare it with the PVPC using your own data: they work in a similar way.

### How often should I review my tariff?

At least once a year, when the guaranteed-price period ends, and whenever your habits change, for example when you buy an electric car or install solar panels.

## In short

The PVPC offers hourly prices linked to the market, with no tie-in and access to the social discount, at the cost of less predictable bills. The free market offers fixed or time-banded prices and more variety, but requires you to watch renewals, tie-in periods and add-on services. The right decision is not made from an advert but from a year of your real hourly consumption priced with each option, taxes included.
