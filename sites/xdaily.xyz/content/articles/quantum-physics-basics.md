At the scale of atoms and subatomic particles, nature behaves in ways that seem to defy common sense. Particles can act like waves, energy comes in tiny packets, an electron can be in a combination of states at once, and two particles can remain mysteriously connected across great distances. These are not speculative ideas; they are the predictions of quantum mechanics, the most precisely tested theory in the history of science. Quantum physics explains why atoms are stable, how the Sun shines and how lasers, computer chips and MRI machines work. This explainer introduces the key ideas of quantum physics in plain language, without equations.

## Why we needed a new physics

At the end of the nineteenth century, many physicists believed that physics was almost complete. Newton's laws described motion, and Maxwell's equations described electricity, magnetism and light. But a few experiments did not fit. Classical physics could not explain the colors of light given off by hot objects, the way light knocks electrons out of metals, or why atoms do not collapse.

Solving these puzzles required a completely new way of thinking about nature at small scales.

## Energy comes in packets

In 1900, the German physicist **Max Planck** studied the light emitted by hot objects. To explain the observed spectrum, he made a radical assumption: energy is not emitted continuously but in small, discrete packets, which he called **quanta**. The energy of each packet depends on the frequency of the light.

In 1905, **Albert Einstein** went further. To explain the **photoelectric effect**, in which light shining on a metal releases electrons, he proposed that light itself consists of particles of energy, later called **photons**. Whether electrons are released depends on the frequency of the light, not its brightness. Dim blue light can release electrons, while bright red light cannot. This only makes sense if light arrives in packets, each with energy tied to its frequency. Einstein received the Nobel Prize in 1921 for this work.

## Quantized atoms

In 1913, **Niels Bohr** applied quantum ideas to the atom. He proposed that electrons can only occupy certain allowed energy levels around the nucleus, not any energy in between. When an electron jumps from a higher energy level to a lower one, it releases a photon with a specific energy, and therefore a specific color.

This explains why each element gives off a unique pattern of colors, called its **spectrum**, when heated. Astronomers use these spectra to identify the elements in distant stars. The word "quantum leap" comes from these jumps, which are actually incredibly small.

## Wave-particle duality

One of the strangest discoveries of quantum physics is that light and matter behave both like waves and like particles.

In 1924, the French physicist **Louis de Broglie** proposed that particles such as electrons also have wave properties. This was soon confirmed: electrons fired at a crystal produce interference patterns, just like waves.

### The double-slit experiment

The famous **double-slit experiment** shows this duality clearly. When light or electrons pass through two narrow slits onto a screen, they create an interference pattern of light and dark stripes, as waves do when they overlap.

Remarkably, this happens even when particles are sent through one at a time. Each electron arrives at a single point on the screen, like a particle, but over time the dots build up an interference pattern, as if each electron passed through both slits and interfered with itself. When scientists measure which slit each particle goes through, the interference pattern disappears. The act of measurement changes the outcome.

The physicist Richard Feynman said the double-slit experiment contains "the only mystery" of quantum mechanics.

## The wave function and probability

In 1926, **Erwin Schrödinger** developed an equation describing how the **wave function** of a quantum system changes over time. The wave function contains all the information about a particle, but it does not tell us exactly where the particle is. Instead, it gives the **probability** of finding the particle in different places when we measure it.

This probabilistic nature is fundamental. Quantum mechanics cannot predict the exact result of a single measurement, only the probabilities of different outcomes. Einstein was uncomfortable with this, famously saying that God "does not play dice," but experiments have consistently supported the probabilistic view.

## Superposition

According to quantum mechanics, a particle can exist in a **superposition**, a combination of multiple states at the same time, until it is measured. An electron can be in a superposition of spinning in two directions. When measured, it is found in one state or the other, with probabilities given by the wave function.

To illustrate how strange this seems when applied to everyday objects, Schrödinger proposed his famous thought experiment of a cat in a box whose fate depends on a quantum event. According to a naive reading, the cat would be both alive and dead until someone opens the box. Schrödinger intended it as a critique, highlighting the puzzle of how quantum rules connect to the everyday world.

In practice, large objects do not show superposition because they constantly interact with their environment, a process called **decoherence**, which effectively destroys delicate quantum effects.

## The uncertainty principle

In 1927, **Werner Heisenberg** formulated the **uncertainty principle**. It states that certain pairs of properties, such as a particle's position and momentum, cannot both be known precisely at the same time. The more precisely you know one, the less precisely you can know the other.

This is not due to clumsy instruments. It is a fundamental property of nature, arising from the wave-like behavior of particles. A wave that is sharply localized in space is made of many different wavelengths, and therefore many different momenta.

## Quantum tunneling

In classical physics, a ball cannot roll over a hill unless it has enough energy. In quantum physics, a particle has a small probability of passing through an energy barrier even when it does not have enough energy to go over it. This is called **quantum tunneling**.

Tunneling is essential to nuclear fusion in the Sun: protons tunnel through the barrier of their electrical repulsion to fuse. Tunneling also makes certain electronic components work and is used in scanning tunneling microscopes, which can image individual atoms.

## Entanglement

**Quantum entanglement** occurs when two or more particles become linked so that their properties are correlated, no matter how far apart they are. Measuring one particle instantly tells you something about the other.

Einstein called this "spooky action at a distance" and believed it showed that quantum mechanics was incomplete. In the 1960s, the physicist John Bell devised a way to test this experimentally. Experiments since the 1970s, including work by John Clauser, Alain Aspect and Anton Zeilinger, who shared the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics, have confirmed that entanglement is real and that nature behaves as quantum mechanics predicts.

Importantly, entanglement cannot be used to send messages faster than light. The results of measurements appear random on each side; the correlation only becomes visible when the results are compared through ordinary communication.

## Quantum physics in everyday technology

Quantum physics may seem abstract, but it underpins much of modern technology:

- **Transistors and computer chips** depend on the quantum behavior of electrons in semiconductors.
- **Lasers** rely on stimulated emission, a quantum process, and are used in fiber-optic communication, barcode scanners and surgery.
- **LEDs** produce light when electrons drop between energy levels in semiconductors.
- **MRI scanners** use the quantum property of spin in atomic nuclei to create images of the body.
- **Atomic clocks** use quantum transitions in atoms to keep time with extraordinary precision, essential for GPS.
- **Solar cells** convert photons into electric current.

## The quantum future

Researchers are developing new quantum technologies:

- **Quantum computers** use quantum bits, or qubits, which can exist in superpositions. For certain problems, such as simulating molecules or factoring large numbers, they could vastly outperform ordinary computers, though building large, reliable machines is very challenging.
- **Quantum cryptography** uses quantum principles to create communication that reveals any attempt at eavesdropping.
- **Quantum sensors** can measure magnetic fields, gravity and time with unprecedented precision.

## Questions readers ask

**What is quantum physics?**
The branch of physics that describes the behavior of matter and energy at the scale of atoms and subatomic particles.

**What is wave-particle duality?**
The idea that light and matter show both wave-like and particle-like properties, depending on how they are observed.

**What does the uncertainty principle say?**
Certain pairs of properties, like position and momentum, cannot both be known with perfect precision at the same time.

**What is quantum entanglement?**
A connection between particles in which their properties remain correlated no matter how far apart they are.

**Is quantum physics used in daily life?**
Yes. Computer chips, lasers, LEDs, MRI machines, atomic clocks and solar cells all rely on quantum physics.

## In short

Quantum physics describes a world where energy comes in packets, particles behave like waves, outcomes are governed by probabilities, particles can be in superpositions, properties are limited by the uncertainty principle and entangled particles stay connected across distances. Strange as it sounds, quantum mechanics is extraordinarily accurate and forms the foundation of chemistry, electronics and much of modern technology. Its mysteries continue to inspire physicists and drive new technologies like quantum computers.
