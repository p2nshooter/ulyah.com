Rocks seem like the very definition of permanence. We say "solid as a rock" and build monuments of granite and marble to last forever. Yet on the timescale of Earth's history, rocks are constantly changing. Mountains rise and wear down to sand. Sand is buried and pressed into stone. Stone is heated, squeezed and folded into new forms, or melted and erupted from volcanoes. This endless recycling of Earth's materials is called the **rock cycle**. Understanding it reveals how landscapes form, where resources come from and how geologists read Earth's history in stone.

## What is a rock?

A **rock** is a naturally occurring solid made of one or more **minerals**. A mineral is a naturally occurring, inorganic solid with a specific chemical composition and an orderly crystal structure. Quartz, feldspar, mica and calcite are common minerals. Granite, for example, is a rock made mostly of quartz, feldspar and mica crystals.

Geologists classify rocks into three main groups based on how they form: **igneous**, **sedimentary** and **metamorphic**.

## Igneous rocks: born from fire

Igneous rocks form when molten rock cools and solidifies. The word "igneous" comes from the Latin word for fire.

- **Intrusive (plutonic) igneous rocks** form when magma cools slowly deep underground. Because it cools slowly, there is time for large crystals to grow. **Granite** is the classic example, with visible crystals of different minerals. Over time, erosion can expose these rocks at the surface, as in the granite domes of Yosemite National Park.
- **Extrusive (volcanic) igneous rocks** form when lava cools quickly at Earth's surface. Rapid cooling produces small crystals or none at all. **Basalt**, a dark, fine-grained rock, forms most of the ocean floor and many volcanic islands. **Obsidian** is volcanic glass that cooled so quickly that no crystals formed. **Pumice** is so full of gas bubbles that it can float on water.

The composition of igneous rocks also varies. Rocks rich in silica, like granite and rhyolite, tend to be light-colored. Rocks lower in silica and richer in iron and magnesium, like basalt and gabbro, tend to be dark.

## Sedimentary rocks: layers of time

Sedimentary rocks form from pieces of other rocks, minerals, or the remains of living things that accumulate and harden over time.

### How they form

1. **Weathering:** Rocks at the surface are broken down by water, ice, wind, temperature changes, plants and chemical reactions. For example, water freezing in cracks expands and splits rocks apart, while rainwater containing dissolved carbon dioxide slowly dissolves limestone.
2. **Erosion and transport:** Rivers, glaciers, wind and waves carry the broken pieces, called sediments, away from their source.
3. **Deposition:** When the transporting agent slows down, sediments settle in layers, often in lakes, river deltas and oceans.
4. **Compaction:** As more layers pile up, the weight of the overlying sediments squeezes out water and presses the grains together.
5. **Cementation:** Minerals dissolved in groundwater, such as calcite or silica, crystallize between grains and glue them together into solid rock. Together, compaction and cementation are called **lithification**.

### Types of sedimentary rocks

- **Clastic rocks** are made of fragments of other rocks. Depending on grain size, they include **conglomerate** (pebbles), **sandstone** (sand) and **shale** (clay and silt).
- **Chemical rocks** form when minerals precipitate from water, such as **rock salt** and some types of **limestone**, which form when seawater evaporates or chemicals precipitate out of solution.
- **Organic (biogenic) rocks** form from the remains of living things. Many limestones are built from the shells and skeletons of marine organisms. **Chalk** is made of the microscopic shells of tiny marine algae. **Coal** forms from compressed plant material.

Sedimentary rocks often show visible layers, called strata, and they are the rocks most likely to contain **fossils**. By studying these layers, geologists can reconstruct ancient environments, climates and the history of life. In undisturbed layers, the oldest rocks are at the bottom and the youngest at the top, a principle called superposition.

## Metamorphic rocks: transformed by heat and pressure

Metamorphic rocks form when existing rocks are changed by heat, pressure or chemically active fluids, without melting completely. The word "metamorphic" comes from Greek words meaning "change of form."

Metamorphism happens deep underground, often where tectonic plates collide and mountains form, or near hot magma. The minerals in the original rock recrystallize into new minerals that are stable under the new conditions.

### Examples

| Original rock | Metamorphic rock | Changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shale | slate, then schist, then gneiss | increasing heat and pressure produce larger crystals and banding |
| Limestone | marble | calcite crystals grow larger and interlock |
| Sandstone | quartzite | quartz grains fuse into a very hard rock |
| Granite | gneiss | minerals separate into light and dark bands |

Some metamorphic rocks, such as slate and gneiss, have a **foliated** texture, with minerals aligned in layers or bands because of directed pressure. Others, like marble and quartzite, are **non-foliated**.

Marble has been used by sculptors for thousands of years, and slate splits into thin sheets that make excellent roof tiles.

## The cycle: how rocks change from one type to another

The rock cycle shows that any type of rock can be transformed into any other type, given the right conditions and enough time.

- **Igneous rocks** exposed at the surface are weathered and eroded into sediments, which can become **sedimentary rocks**. If they are buried deep and subjected to heat and pressure, they become **metamorphic rocks**.
- **Sedimentary rocks** can be uplifted and eroded into new sediments, buried and transformed into **metamorphic rocks**, or melted into magma.
- **Metamorphic rocks** can be uplifted and eroded, or heated further until they melt into magma, which cools into new **igneous rocks**.

There is no fixed order. A rock might follow many different paths through the cycle over hundreds of millions of years.

## Plate tectonics: the engine of the rock cycle

The rock cycle is powered by two main energy sources:

- **The Sun**, which drives the water cycle and weather, causing weathering and erosion at the surface.
- **Earth's internal heat**, which drives plate tectonics, volcanism and metamorphism.

Plate tectonics plays a central role. At mid-ocean ridges, new igneous rock forms as magma rises between separating plates. At subduction zones, oceanic plates carrying sediments sink into the mantle, where they are heated, metamorphosed and partly melted, feeding volcanoes. When continents collide, rocks are folded, uplifted into mountain ranges and metamorphosed. Mountains are then eroded, sending sediments into the sea to begin the cycle again.

## How fast does the rock cycle work?

Most stages of the rock cycle are extremely slow. Weathering a mountain range down to low hills can take tens of millions of years. Turning sediment into sedimentary rock may take thousands to millions of years. Some processes are fast, though: a volcanic eruption can create new igneous rock in minutes, and a landslide can move large amounts of rock in seconds.

Earth's oldest known minerals, tiny zircon crystals from Western Australia, are about 4.4 billion years old. The oldest known rocks are about 4 billion years old. Most of Earth's early crust has been recycled by the rock cycle and plate tectonics.

## The rock cycle and people

The rock cycle provides many resources:

- **Building materials** such as granite, marble, limestone, sandstone and slate.
- **Cement and concrete**, made from limestone and other rocks.
- **Metals**, concentrated in ores by igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic processes.
- **Fossil fuels** such as coal, oil and natural gas, formed in sedimentary rocks.
- **Soil**, formed by weathering rocks and mixing them with organic matter, which supports agriculture.
- **Groundwater**, stored in porous rocks like sandstone.

## Reading rocks in everyday life

You can see the rock cycle around you. A river carrying sand and pebbles is transporting sediments. Layers in a road cut or cliff tell the story of ancient seas and rivers. A granite countertop formed from magma cooling slowly underground, and a marble floor began as limestone on an ancient seafloor. Even the pebbles on a beach, rounded by waves, are part of a journey that began millions of years ago.

## Questions readers ask

**What are the three main types of rocks?**
Igneous rocks, formed from cooled magma or lava; sedimentary rocks, formed from compacted and cemented sediments; and metamorphic rocks, formed when existing rocks are transformed by heat and pressure.

**Can a rock change from one type to another?**
Yes. Through weathering, burial, heat, pressure and melting, any type of rock can become any other type over time.

**Where are fossils usually found?**
Mostly in sedimentary rocks, because the gentle accumulation of sediments can preserve the remains of living things.

**What is the difference between a rock and a mineral?**
A mineral is a naturally occurring solid with a specific chemical composition and crystal structure. A rock is made of one or more minerals.

**What drives the rock cycle?**
The Sun, which powers weathering and erosion, and Earth's internal heat, which powers plate tectonics, volcanism and metamorphism.

## In short

The rock cycle describes how Earth's rocks are continuously formed, broken down and transformed. Igneous rocks form from cooling magma and lava, sedimentary rocks from layers of sediment that harden over time, and metamorphic rocks from existing rocks changed by heat and pressure. Weathering, erosion, burial, melting and uplift move rocks from one type to another, powered by the Sun and Earth's internal heat through plate tectonics. Every stone you see is a chapter in this slow, endless story, and learning to read it reveals the long history of our planet.
