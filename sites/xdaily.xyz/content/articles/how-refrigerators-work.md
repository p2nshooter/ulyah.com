A refrigerator seems simple: food goes in warm and comes out cold. But a fridge does not actually create cold. Instead, it moves heat from inside the cabinet to the air in your kitchen. It does this using a clever trick of physics, the fact that liquids absorb heat when they evaporate and release heat when they condense. The same principle powers freezers, air conditioners and heat pumps, which together keep food safe, homes comfortable and medicines stable around the world. This explainer describes how a refrigerator moves heat "uphill," from a cold place to a warm one.

## Heat flows from hot to cold

Heat naturally flows from warmer objects to cooler ones. A cup of hot coffee cools down because heat flows into the cooler air around it. A cold drink warms up because heat flows into it from the warmer room. This one-way tendency is described by the second law of thermodynamics.

A refrigerator's job is to do the opposite: to move heat from the cold interior to the warmer kitchen. This does not happen on its own. It requires energy, just as pumping water uphill requires a pump. That is why refrigerators need electricity, and why they are sometimes described as **heat pumps**.

## The key idea: evaporation absorbs heat

You have experienced the basic principle of refrigeration many times. When you step out of a swimming pool, you feel cold, even on a warm day. Water evaporating from your skin absorbs heat from your body. Sweating cools you for the same reason.

When a liquid turns into a gas, it needs energy to break the bonds between its molecules. This energy, called the **latent heat of vaporization**, is absorbed from the surroundings, cooling them. When the gas turns back into a liquid, it releases that same heat.

The boiling point of a liquid also depends on pressure. At lower pressure, liquids boil at lower temperatures; at higher pressure, they boil at higher temperatures. Refrigerators use this to control where evaporation and condensation happen.

## Refrigerants

Refrigerators use special fluids called **refrigerants**, chosen because they boil at very low temperatures at low pressure and condense easily when compressed. Over the years, several types have been used:

- Early refrigerators used toxic gases like ammonia, sulfur dioxide and methyl chloride, which were dangerous if they leaked.
- In the 1930s, **chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)** such as Freon were introduced as safer alternatives. Decades later, scientists discovered that CFCs were destroying the ozone layer, and they were phased out under the Montreal Protocol of 1987.
- **Hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs)** replaced them. They do not harm the ozone layer but are powerful greenhouse gases, and they are now being phased down under the Kigali Amendment.
- Many modern household refrigerators use **isobutane (R-600a)**, a hydrocarbon with very low impact on climate and ozone, used in small sealed amounts.

## The four main parts of the refrigeration cycle

A typical refrigerator uses a process called the **vapor-compression cycle**, which has four main components connected by a closed loop of tubing.

### 1. The compressor

The **compressor**, usually located at the bottom back of the fridge, is the heart of the system. It is an electric pump that draws in cool, low-pressure refrigerant gas and compresses it. Compressing a gas raises its pressure and temperature, just as a bicycle pump becomes warm when you pump up a tire. The refrigerant leaves the compressor as a hot, high-pressure gas.

### 2. The condenser

The hot gas flows into the **condenser**, a network of coils usually on the back or underneath the fridge. Because the gas is hotter than the kitchen air, heat flows from the refrigerant into the room. As it loses heat, the high-pressure gas condenses into a liquid, releasing its latent heat. This is why the back or bottom of a refrigerator feels warm.

### 3. The expansion valve

The high-pressure liquid then passes through an **expansion valve** or a thin **capillary tube**. This narrow passage causes a sudden drop in pressure. As the pressure drops, the refrigerant's boiling point falls dramatically, and part of it begins to evaporate, cooling the mixture to a temperature well below the temperature inside the fridge.

### 4. The evaporator

The cold, low-pressure refrigerant flows into the **evaporator**, coils located inside the fridge or freezer compartment, often hidden behind a panel. Here, the refrigerant absorbs heat from the air and food inside the fridge and fully evaporates into a gas. This is where the cooling happens. A small fan often circulates air over the evaporator to distribute the cold air.

The low-pressure gas then returns to the compressor, and the cycle starts again.

## The cycle at a glance

| Stage | Component | What happens | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Compressor | gas is compressed | pressure and temperature rise |
| 2 | Condenser | gas releases heat and condenses to liquid | heat goes into the kitchen |
| 3 | Expansion valve | pressure drops suddenly | refrigerant becomes very cold |
| 4 | Evaporator | liquid absorbs heat and evaporates | inside of the fridge cools |

## The thermostat

A refrigerator does not run constantly. A **thermostat** senses the temperature inside and turns the compressor on when it becomes too warm and off when it reaches the set temperature. That is why you hear the fridge humming on and off throughout the day. Many modern refrigerators use variable-speed compressors that run more steadily at lower speeds, which saves energy and keeps temperatures more stable.

## Frost and defrosting

When warm, humid air enters the fridge, for example when you open the door, water vapor in the air can freeze on the cold evaporator coils, forming frost. Frost acts as an insulator and reduces cooling efficiency. Older freezers needed to be defrosted manually. Most modern refrigerators are **frost-free**: they periodically run a small heater on the evaporator coils to melt the frost, and the water drains into a pan near the compressor, where it evaporates.

## Why the kitchen does not get colder with the door open

Opening the refrigerator door does not cool the kitchen. The fridge removes heat from inside and releases it, plus the energy used by the compressor, from the condenser into the same room. With the door open, it simply pumps heat around in a circle, and the kitchen actually gets slightly warmer because of the energy the compressor consumes.

## Air conditioners and heat pumps

Air conditioners use exactly the same cycle. The evaporator sits inside the house, absorbing heat from indoor air, and the condenser sits outside, releasing that heat. A **heat pump** for heating a home runs the cycle in reverse: it absorbs heat from the outdoor air, ground or water, even when it is cold outside, and releases it indoors. Because it moves heat rather than creating it, a heat pump can deliver two to four or more units of heat energy for each unit of electricity it uses, making it an efficient way to heat buildings.

## Keeping your fridge efficient

Refrigerators run around the clock, so efficiency matters. A few habits help:

- **Set the right temperature:** About 37 to 40 °F (3 to 4 °C) for the fridge and 0 °F (−18 °C) for the freezer.
- **Keep the condenser coils clean:** Dust on the coils makes it harder for the fridge to release heat. Vacuum them once or twice a year.
- **Check the door seals:** A leaky seal lets warm air in. If a piece of paper closed in the door slides out easily, the seal may need replacing.
- **Leave space for airflow:** Allow some room behind and above the fridge for heat to escape.
- **Avoid placing it next to heat sources** such as ovens, dishwashers or direct sunlight.
- **Do not overfill or leave it nearly empty:** Air must circulate inside, but a reasonably full fridge holds its temperature better when the door is opened.
- **Let hot food cool briefly** in shallow containers before refrigerating, but do not leave it out longer than two hours.

## Questions readers ask

**Does a refrigerator create cold?**
No. It moves heat from inside the fridge to the kitchen using a refrigerant that absorbs heat when it evaporates and releases heat when it condenses.

**Why is the back of my fridge warm?**
That is where the condenser coils release the heat removed from inside the fridge, plus heat from the compressor.

**What is a refrigerant?**
A fluid that boils at low temperatures and changes easily between liquid and gas, allowing it to absorb and release heat in the refrigeration cycle.

**Why does my fridge make noise?**
The compressor, fans and refrigerant flowing through the tubes make humming, clicking and gurgling sounds as the system turns on and off.

**Is a heat pump the same as a refrigerator?**
They use the same principle. A heat pump moves heat from outdoors into a building for heating, or the other way around for cooling.

## In short

A refrigerator works by moving heat, not creating cold. A compressor squeezes refrigerant gas, making it hot; the condenser releases that heat into the kitchen as the refrigerant becomes a liquid; an expansion valve drops its pressure so it becomes very cold; and the evaporator inside absorbs heat from the food as the refrigerant evaporates again. A thermostat controls the cycle, and frost-free systems melt ice automatically. The same physics powers air conditioners and heat pumps, quietly keeping food fresh, homes comfortable and energy use efficient.
