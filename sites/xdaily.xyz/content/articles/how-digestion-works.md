Every bite of food you eat begins a remarkable journey. Over the next day or two, it travels through a tube about 30 feet (9 meters) long, is mashed, mixed, soaked in acid, broken into tiny molecules by enzymes and absorbed into the bloodstream, while trillions of bacteria help process what is left. The digestive system turns a sandwich, an apple or a bowl of rice into the fuel and building blocks your body needs. This explainer follows food through the digestive system and describes what each organ does. It explains how the body works and is not medical advice.

## Why we need to digest food

Food contains large, complex molecules: carbohydrates such as starch, proteins and fats. These molecules are too large to pass through the walls of the intestines into the blood. Digestion breaks them down into smaller units the body can absorb and use:

| Nutrient | Broken down into | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carbohydrates | simple sugars, such as glucose | energy |
| Proteins | amino acids | building and repairing tissues, enzymes, hormones |
| Fats | fatty acids and glycerol | energy, cell membranes, hormones |

Digestion involves two kinds of processes: **mechanical digestion**, which physically breaks food into smaller pieces, and **chemical digestion**, in which enzymes break the chemical bonds in food molecules.

## The mouth: where digestion begins

Digestion starts before food even enters the mouth. The sight and smell of food can trigger saliva production.

- **Teeth** cut, tear and grind food into smaller pieces, increasing its surface area for enzymes.
- **Saliva**, produced by salivary glands, moistens food and contains the enzyme **amylase**, which begins breaking down starch into sugars. That is why a piece of bread starts to taste slightly sweet if you chew it long enough.
- **The tongue** mixes food with saliva and shapes it into a soft ball called a **bolus**.

The salivary glands produce about 1 to 1.5 liters of saliva per day.

## Swallowing and the esophagus

When you swallow, the tongue pushes the bolus into the throat. A flap called the **epiglottis** covers the windpipe so food goes down the **esophagus**, a muscular tube about 10 inches (25 centimeters) long that connects the throat to the stomach.

Food does not simply fall down the esophagus. Muscles in its walls contract in coordinated waves called **peristalsis**, squeezing food downward. Peristalsis is so effective that you could swallow even while upside down. At the bottom of the esophagus, a ring of muscle called the lower esophageal sphincter opens to let food into the stomach and closes to keep stomach contents from flowing back. When it does not close properly, stomach acid can rise into the esophagus, causing heartburn.

## The stomach: a muscular mixer

The **stomach** is a J-shaped muscular sac that can expand to hold about 1 to 1.5 liters of food in adults. It has three main jobs:

- **Mixing:** Strong muscular contractions churn food and mix it with digestive juices.
- **Breaking down proteins:** The stomach lining releases **hydrochloric acid** and the enzyme **pepsin**. The acid is very strong, with a pH of about 1.5 to 3.5. It unfolds proteins so pepsin can break them into smaller pieces, and it kills many microbes in food.
- **Storing and releasing food gradually:** The stomach releases its contents into the small intestine in small portions.

The stomach protects itself from its own acid with a thick layer of mucus and by constantly renewing its lining.

After a few hours in the stomach, food becomes a thick, soupy liquid called **chyme**.

## The small intestine: the main event

The **small intestine** is where most digestion and almost all nutrient absorption take place. Despite its name, it is the longest part of the digestive tract, about 20 feet (6 meters) long in adults, coiled tightly in the abdomen. It has three sections: the **duodenum**, the **jejunum** and the **ileum**.

### Helpers from the pancreas, liver and gallbladder

As chyme enters the duodenum, it is mixed with juices from three organs:

- **The pancreas** produces digestive enzymes that break down carbohydrates, proteins and fats. It also releases bicarbonate, which neutralizes stomach acid.
- **The liver** produces **bile**, a greenish fluid that breaks fat into small droplets, a process called emulsification, so enzymes can digest it more easily.
- **The gallbladder** stores and concentrates bile and releases it when fatty food arrives.

Enzymes on the surface of the intestinal lining complete the breakdown of carbohydrates and proteins.

### Absorption

The inner wall of the small intestine is covered with folds and millions of tiny finger-like projections called **villi**. Each villus is covered with even smaller projections called **microvilli**. This structure creates an enormous surface area for absorption, roughly the size of a small apartment, often estimated at about 30 square meters.

Inside each villus are tiny blood vessels and a lymph vessel. Sugars and amino acids pass into the blood, while fats are absorbed into the lymphatic system and later enter the bloodstream. Vitamins and minerals are also absorbed here.

## The liver: processing center

Nutrient-rich blood from the intestines travels first to the **liver** through a large vessel called the hepatic portal vein. The liver processes nutrients, stores glucose as glycogen, makes proteins, detoxifies harmful substances, including alcohol and some medicines, and stores vitamins and iron. It performs hundreds of functions and is the largest internal organ.

## The large intestine: water and microbes

What remains after the small intestine, mostly water, fiber and undigested material, moves into the **large intestine**, also called the colon. It is about 5 feet (1.5 meters) long and wider than the small intestine.

The large intestine has several important roles:

- **Absorbing water and salts**, turning liquid waste into more solid stool.
- **Hosting the gut microbiome:** The large intestine contains trillions of bacteria and other microorganisms. They ferment dietary fiber that the body cannot digest, producing short-chain fatty acids that nourish the cells of the colon. Gut microbes also produce some vitamins, such as vitamin K, and play roles in immune function and metabolism. Scientists are actively studying how the microbiome influences health.
- **Storing and eliminating waste:** Stool is stored in the rectum until it is eliminated through the anus.

## How long does digestion take?

Transit times vary widely between people and depend on the type of food:

| Section | Approximate time |
| --- | --- |
| Mouth and esophagus | seconds to a minute |
| Stomach | 2 to 5 hours |
| Small intestine | 2 to 6 hours |
| Large intestine | 10 to 59 hours |

In total, food typically takes about one to three days to pass through the entire digestive tract. Fatty and protein-rich meals tend to stay longer in the stomach, while fiber generally speeds up movement through the intestines.

## Control of digestion

Digestion is controlled by both nerves and hormones. The digestive tract has its own network of nerves, sometimes called the "second brain" or **enteric nervous system**, containing hundreds of millions of neurons. It coordinates peristalsis and the release of digestive juices and communicates with the brain.

Hormones also play a role. For example, **gastrin** stimulates stomach acid production, **secretin** signals the pancreas to release bicarbonate, and **cholecystokinin** triggers bile release and enzyme secretion. Other hormones, such as **ghrelin** and **leptin**, influence hunger and fullness.

## Supporting digestive health

Health organizations generally recommend a varied diet with plenty of fiber from fruits, vegetables, whole grains and legumes, drinking enough water, staying physically active and eating at regular times. Fiber supports regular bowel movements and feeds beneficial gut bacteria. Persistent digestive symptoms, such as ongoing pain, changes in bowel habits or unexplained weight loss, should be checked by a doctor.

## Common digestive experiences explained

Many everyday sensations come from normal digestive processes:

- **Stomach growling:** The rumbling sound, called borborygmi, comes from muscles in the stomach and intestines squeezing gas and fluid along. It is often louder when the stomach is empty, because there is less food to muffle the sound, and because a pattern of contractions called the migrating motor complex sweeps through the gut between meals.
- **Hiccups:** Sudden, involuntary contractions of the diaphragm, followed by the vocal cords snapping shut, which produces the "hic" sound. They are often triggered by eating quickly, swallowing air or carbonated drinks.
- **Gas:** Swallowed air and gases produced by gut bacteria fermenting fiber are normal. Foods like beans, onions and some vegetables produce more gas because they contain carbohydrates that reach the large intestine undigested.
- **Feeling full:** Stretch receptors in the stomach and hormones released by the intestines signal the brain that you have eaten enough. These signals take some time, which is one reason eating slowly can help people notice when they are full.

## Questions readers ask

**How long is the digestive tract?**
In adults, about 30 feet (9 meters) from mouth to anus, with the small intestine making up about 20 feet (6 meters).

**Where does most digestion happen?**
In the small intestine, with help from enzymes from the pancreas and bile from the liver.

**Why doesn't the stomach digest itself?**
A thick layer of mucus protects the stomach lining, and the lining is constantly renewed.

**What does the large intestine do?**
It absorbs water and salts, hosts gut bacteria that ferment fiber and forms and stores stool.

**What are gut bacteria for?**
They ferment fiber, produce some vitamins and beneficial compounds, support the immune system and help keep harmful microbes in check.

## In short

Digestion turns food into nutrients the body can absorb. It begins in the mouth with chewing and saliva, continues in the stomach with acid and churning, and reaches its peak in the small intestine, where enzymes from the pancreas and bile from the liver finish breaking down food and villi absorb the nutrients into the blood. The liver processes these nutrients, and the large intestine absorbs water while trillions of gut bacteria ferment fiber. Coordinated by nerves and hormones, this journey of one to three days quietly fuels every part of the body.
