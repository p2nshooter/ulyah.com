Science has given us vaccines, electricity, smartphones, space travel and an understanding of everything from the structure of DNA to the age of the universe. But science is not just a collection of facts. It is a way of thinking, a method for asking questions about the world and finding reliable answers. At its heart is a simple but powerful idea: test your ideas against evidence, and be willing to change your mind when the evidence demands it. This explainer describes how the scientific method works, why it is so effective and how you can use its principles to evaluate claims in everyday life.

## What makes science different

Humans have always tried to understand the world through stories, traditions, authority and personal experience. Science differs in a few important ways:

- **Evidence over authority:** In science, an idea is accepted because of evidence, not because of who proposed it.
- **Testability:** Scientific ideas must make predictions that can be tested and could, in principle, be shown to be wrong.
- **Self-correction:** Scientists expect that some ideas will turn out to be incomplete or mistaken, and they actively look for errors.
- **Openness:** Methods and results are shared so that others can check, repeat and build on them.

The philosopher Karl Popper emphasized **falsifiability**: a scientific claim must be able to be proven false by some possible observation. "All swans are white" is a scientific claim because finding a single black swan would disprove it, and black swans do exist in Australia.

## The steps of the scientific method

The scientific method is often described as a series of steps. In practice, scientists move back and forth between them, but the basic pattern looks like this.

### 1. Observation and questions

Science begins with curiosity. A scientist notices something and asks a question. Why do some patients recover faster than others? Why does this plant grow better in one place? Why do stars in some galaxies move faster than expected?

### 2. Background research

Before investigating, scientists study what is already known. They read published studies to find out whether the question has been answered and what methods others have used.

### 3. Hypothesis

A **hypothesis** is a proposed explanation that can be tested. It is often stated as a prediction: "If plants receive more light, then they will grow taller." A good hypothesis is specific and testable.

### 4. Experiment or observation

Scientists design ways to test the hypothesis. In an **experiment**, they change one factor and measure its effect:

- The **independent variable** is the factor that is deliberately changed, such as the amount of light.
- The **dependent variable** is the outcome that is measured, such as plant height.
- **Controlled variables** are kept the same, such as water, soil and temperature, so they do not influence the results.

Not every science can use controlled experiments. Astronomers cannot change stars, and geologists cannot repeat earthquakes. These sciences rely on careful observations and natural experiments, comparing different situations that occur naturally.

### 5. Analysis

Scientists analyze the data, often using statistics, to see whether the results support the hypothesis. Statistics help determine whether an effect is likely to be real or could be due to chance.

### 6. Conclusion

Based on the analysis, scientists decide whether the evidence supports or contradicts the hypothesis. If it contradicts it, they revise or abandon the hypothesis. If it supports it, they test it further. A single experiment rarely proves anything conclusively.

### 7. Sharing and peer review

Scientists publish their findings in scientific journals. Before publication, other experts in the field review the work in a process called **peer review**, checking the methods, analysis and conclusions. Peer review is not perfect, but it helps catch mistakes and weak arguments.

### 8. Replication

Other scientists then try to repeat the experiment. If different teams, using different methods, get similar results, confidence in the finding grows. If results cannot be replicated, the original finding may have been a fluke or an error.

## Controls and blinding

Good experiments include **control groups**, which are treated exactly the same as the experimental group except for the factor being tested. In a medical trial, for example, one group receives a new drug and a control group receives a placebo, an inactive substance that looks identical.

Researchers often use **randomization**, assigning participants to groups by chance, to avoid bias. In **double-blind** studies, neither the participants nor the researchers know who received the real treatment until the end. This prevents expectations from influencing the results. The **placebo effect**, in which people feel better simply because they expect to, shows why these controls are so important.

## Correlation is not causation

One of the most important lessons of science is that **correlation does not imply causation**. Two things can happen together without one causing the other.

For example, ice cream sales and drowning incidents both rise in summer. Ice cream does not cause drowning; hot weather leads to both more ice cream eating and more swimming. A hidden factor that influences both is called a **confounding variable**. Controlled experiments help separate correlation from causation.

## Hypothesis, theory and law

In everyday language, "theory" often means a guess. In science, it means something very different.

| Term | Meaning in science | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hypothesis | a testable proposed explanation | "this drug lowers blood pressure" |
| Theory | a well-tested, comprehensive explanation supported by a large body of evidence | theory of evolution, germ theory, theory of general relativity |
| Law | a description of a pattern in nature, often mathematical, that holds under certain conditions | law of gravity, laws of thermodynamics |

Theories do not become laws with more evidence. They serve different purposes: laws describe what happens; theories explain why. Scientific theories are among the most reliable forms of knowledge we have.

## Certainty and uncertainty

Science does not offer absolute certainty. Every conclusion is open to revision if new evidence appears. That is a strength, not a weakness. Scientists express uncertainty through error bars, confidence intervals and probabilities. When scientists say they are "95 percent confident," they are being precise about the limits of their knowledge.

At the same time, some scientific conclusions are supported by such overwhelming evidence from so many independent sources that they are accepted as established facts, for example that Earth orbits the Sun, that germs cause many diseases and that species evolve.

## When science changes its mind

The history of science includes many revisions:

- Scientists once believed that the continents were fixed. Evidence from rocks, fossils and the ocean floor led to the theory of plate tectonics in the 1960s.
- Doctors once believed that stomach ulcers were mainly caused by stress. In the 1980s, Barry Marshall and Robin Warren showed that most are caused by the bacterium *Helicobacter pylori*, earning them a Nobel Prize.
- Newton's laws of motion were refined by Einstein's theories of relativity for very high speeds and strong gravity.

These changes show science working as intended: new evidence leads to better explanations.

## Judging science in the news

Headlines often exaggerate or misrepresent scientific findings. A few questions help you evaluate claims:

- **Who did the study, and where was it published?** Peer-reviewed journals are more reliable than press releases or social media posts.
- **How many people or samples were involved?** Small studies are more likely to produce misleading results.
- **Was it done in humans, animals or cells?** Results in mice or petri dishes do not always apply to people.
- **Was there a control group?**
- **Is it correlation or causation?**
- **Has it been replicated?** One study is rarely the final word.
- **What do other experts say?** Look for the broader scientific consensus.

## Science as a community

Science is not the work of lone geniuses alone. It is a global community of researchers who check each other's work, argue about interpretations and build on one another's discoveries. This social side of science is part of what makes it reliable. A single scientist can be mistaken or biased, but when many independent researchers, with different backgrounds and sometimes competing interests, reach the same conclusion from different directions, the result is far more trustworthy.

Scientists also guard against their own biases with practices such as pre-registering studies, which means publishing the plan for an experiment and its analysis before collecting data, so that results cannot be selectively reported afterward. Sharing raw data and computer code allows others to check analyses. Large collaborations combine data from many laboratories to test whether a result holds across different populations and conditions.

Science also has limits. It can tell us how the world works and what the consequences of our choices are likely to be, but it does not by itself decide what we should value. Questions of ethics, meaning and policy draw on scientific evidence while also depending on human values and judgment.

## Using scientific thinking every day

The habits of the scientific method are useful far beyond the laboratory. When you try a new recipe, adjust one ingredient at a time to see what makes the difference. When choosing between two products, look for fair comparisons rather than testimonials. When you hear a surprising claim, ask what evidence supports it and what would change your mind. Thinking this way does not require a lab coat, just curiosity, honesty and a willingness to follow the evidence.

## Questions readers ask

**What is the scientific method?**
A process of observing, asking questions, forming testable hypotheses, testing them with experiments or observations, analyzing data and sharing results for review and replication.

**What is the difference between a hypothesis and a theory?**
A hypothesis is a testable idea. A theory is a well-tested, comprehensive explanation supported by extensive evidence.

**Why is replication important?**
It confirms that a result is reliable and not due to chance, error or bias.

**Can science prove things?**
Science provides strong evidence and well-supported explanations, but conclusions remain open to revision if new evidence appears.

**Why do scientists use control groups?**
To compare outcomes and isolate the effect of the factor being tested.

## In short

The scientific method is a way of learning about the world by testing ideas against evidence. Scientists observe, ask questions, propose testable hypotheses, design experiments with controls, analyze data and share their results for peer review and replication. Theories are powerful, evidence-based explanations, not guesses, and science corrects itself as new evidence emerges. Understanding how science works helps us appreciate its achievements and think more clearly about the claims we encounter every day.
