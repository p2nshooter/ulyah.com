Open your eyes and the world appears instantly: colors, shapes, depth, movement, faces and words, all seemingly effortless. Yet seeing is one of the most complex things the body does. Light must be focused onto a thin layer of tissue at the back of the eye, converted into electrical signals by millions of specialized cells and interpreted by large areas of the brain. What we experience as "seeing" is not a simple photograph of the world but a picture constructed by the brain. This explainer follows light on its journey through the eye and into the brain. It explains how the body works and is not medical advice.

## Light and the eye

We see objects because light from them enters our eyes. Some objects, like the Sun or a lamp, produce light. Most objects simply reflect light that falls on them. An apple looks red because it reflects mostly red light and absorbs other colors.

The human eye detects **visible light**, a narrow band of electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths from about 380 to 700 nanometers. Shorter wavelengths look violet and blue, longer wavelengths look orange and red. We cannot see ultraviolet or infrared light, although some animals can.

## The parts of the eye

The eye is roughly a sphere about an inch (2.5 centimeters) across. Its main parts include:

- **Cornea:** The clear, dome-shaped front surface. It does most of the focusing of light.
- **Iris:** The colored part of the eye. It is a ring of muscle that controls the size of the pupil.
- **Pupil:** The opening in the center of the iris through which light enters. It becomes smaller in bright light and larger in dim light.
- **Lens:** A flexible, transparent structure behind the iris that fine-tunes the focus.
- **Vitreous humor:** A clear, jelly-like substance that fills the eye and helps maintain its shape.
- **Retina:** A thin layer of light-sensitive tissue lining the back of the eye, where light is converted into nerve signals.
- **Optic nerve:** A bundle of more than a million nerve fibers that carries signals from the retina to the brain.

## Focusing light

For us to see clearly, light must be focused precisely onto the retina. The **cornea** bends, or refracts, incoming light and provides about two-thirds of the eye's focusing power. The **lens** provides the rest and can change shape to adjust the focus.

When you look at something far away, small muscles around the lens relax, and the lens becomes flatter. When you look at something close, the muscles contract, and the lens becomes rounder and more powerful. This process is called **accommodation**.

The image formed on the retina is actually upside down and reversed left to right, just like the image in a camera. The brain interprets it so that we perceive the world the right way up.

## Common focusing problems

- **Nearsightedness (myopia):** The eye focuses light in front of the retina, often because the eyeball is slightly too long. Close objects are clear, but distant ones are blurry. Myopia has become much more common worldwide in recent decades, and research suggests that spending more time outdoors in childhood may reduce the risk.
- **Farsightedness (hyperopia):** The eye focuses light behind the retina, often because the eyeball is slightly too short. Close objects may be blurry.
- **Astigmatism:** The cornea or lens is unevenly curved, causing blurred or distorted vision at all distances.
- **Presbyopia:** With age, usually starting in the 40s, the lens becomes less flexible, making it harder to focus on close objects. This is why many people need reading glasses later in life.

Glasses, contact lenses and laser surgery correct these problems by adjusting how light is focused onto the retina.

## The retina: turning light into signals

The retina contains two main types of light-sensitive cells called **photoreceptors**: rods and cones.

### Rods

There are about 120 million rods in each eye. They are extremely sensitive to light and allow us to see in dim conditions, but they do not detect color. That is why, at night, the world appears in shades of gray. Rods are concentrated around the edges of the retina, which is why faint stars are sometimes easier to see when you look slightly to the side of them.

### Cones

There are about 6 million cones in each eye. They work best in bright light and allow us to see color and fine detail. Cones are concentrated in a small central area of the retina called the **fovea**, which provides our sharpest vision. When you read or look directly at something, you are using the fovea.

### How photoreceptors work

Rods and cones contain light-sensitive pigments. When a photon of light strikes a pigment molecule, it changes shape. This triggers a chain of chemical reactions that changes the electrical activity of the cell. The signal is passed to other cells in the retina, which process it and send it through the optic nerve to the brain.

The retina is not just a passive sensor. It contains several layers of nerve cells that begin processing visual information, detecting edges, contrast and movement before signals even leave the eye.

## How we see color

Human color vision is based on three types of cones, each most sensitive to different wavelengths of light:

- **S-cones** are most sensitive to short wavelengths, perceived as blue.
- **M-cones** are most sensitive to medium wavelengths, perceived as green.
- **L-cones** are most sensitive to long wavelengths, perceived as red.

The brain compares the signals from these three types of cones to determine the color of light. For example, yellow light stimulates both M- and L-cones, and the brain interprets this combination as yellow. This system, called **trichromatic vision**, is why screens can create all the colors we see using just red, green and blue pixels.

**Color vision deficiency**, often called color blindness, happens when one type of cone is missing or works differently. The most common form makes it difficult to distinguish red and green. It is much more common in men than women, affecting about 8 percent of men of Northern European descent, because the genes for the red and green cone pigments are on the X chromosome.

## The blind spot

Where the optic nerve leaves the eye, there are no photoreceptors. This creates a **blind spot** in each eye. We do not normally notice it because the brain fills in the missing information, and because the blind spots of the two eyes are in different places in the visual field.

You can find your blind spot with a simple test. Draw a small cross and a dot about 3 inches (8 centimeters) apart on a piece of paper. Close your left eye and look at the cross with your right eye. Slowly move the paper toward or away from your face. At a certain distance, the dot will disappear.

## From the eye to the brain

Signals from the retina travel along the optic nerves. At a crossing point called the **optic chiasm**, fibers from the inner half of each retina cross to the other side of the brain. As a result, information from the left side of the visual field goes to the right side of the brain, and vice versa.

The signals then pass through a relay station in the thalamus and reach the **visual cortex** at the back of the brain, in the occipital lobe. There and in many other brain areas, visual information is processed in stages:

- Some areas detect simple features such as edges, lines and orientation.
- Others process color, motion and depth.
- Higher areas recognize objects, faces and places.

Roughly a third of the brain's cortex is involved in processing vision in some way.

## Seeing in three dimensions

Because our eyes are about 2.5 inches (6 centimeters) apart, each eye sees the world from a slightly different angle. The brain compares the two images to judge depth and distance, a process called **stereopsis**. The brain also uses other clues, such as the relative size of objects, perspective, shading, overlap and motion, which is why we can still judge depth with one eye closed.

## The brain constructs what we see

Vision is not a perfect recording of reality. The brain constantly interprets and fills in information based on experience and expectations. **Optical illusions** reveal this. For example, two lines of the same length can appear different depending on the arrows at their ends, and colors can look different depending on their surroundings. Our eyes also make small, rapid movements called **saccades** several times per second, yet we perceive a stable world because the brain stitches the images together.

## Questions readers ask

**Why do we see colors?**
Three types of cone cells in the retina respond to different wavelengths of light, and the brain compares their signals to create the perception of color.

**Why is it hard to see color at night?**
In dim light, mainly rods are active, and rods do not detect color.

**What causes nearsightedness?**
Light focuses in front of the retina, often because the eyeball is slightly too long.

**Why don't we notice our blind spot?**
The brain fills in the missing information, and each eye covers the other's blind spot.

**Is the image on the retina upside down?**
Yes. The lens projects an inverted image, and the brain interprets it correctly.

## In short

Vision begins when light enters the eye through the cornea and pupil and is focused by the cornea and lens onto the retina. There, rods detect dim light and cones detect color and detail, converting light into electrical signals. The optic nerve carries these signals to the brain, where the visual cortex and other regions analyze edges, color, motion and depth and assemble them into the images we experience. Seeing is a partnership between eye and brain, an ongoing act of interpretation that turns light into a rich, three-dimensional world.
