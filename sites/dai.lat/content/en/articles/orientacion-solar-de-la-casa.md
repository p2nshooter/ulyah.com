Two identical houses, with the same materials and the same size, can feel completely different inside. One is cool in the afternoon and the other looks like an oven. One receives sun on winter mornings and the other spends the year in humid shade. The difference is often not in what was built, but in **where** each facade faces and how its windows were protected.

Solar orientation is one of the cheapest decisions and most difficult to correct later. It doesn't cost anything to think carefully about where the bedrooms or large windows go, but changing them when the house is already built does cost. In this guide you will see how the sun moves in the different regions of Latin America, which facades should be opened and which ones to protect, how to locate each environment and what to do if your lot or your apartment already has a difficult orientation.

## The path of the sun is not the same throughout the continent

Latin America extends from more than 30 degrees north latitude to more than 50 degrees south latitude. That means that the sun behaves very differently in Tijuana, Panama and Punta Arenas.

| Region | Examples | Where is the sun at noon | Facade that gets hotter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Northern Mexico and areas outside the tropics to the north | Monterrey, Chihuahua, Hermosillo | towards **south** almost all year round | west in the afternoons, south in winter |
| tropical strip | Mexico City, Caribbean, Central America, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, northern Brazil | very high, sometimes to the north and sometimes to the south depending on the season | **roof**, east and west |
| Southern Cone and areas outside the tropics to the south | Santiago, Buenos Aires, Montevideo, southern Brazil | towards **north** almost all year | west in the afternoons, north in winter |

Everywhere the sun rises in the east and sets in the west, but its height changes. Near the equator it passes almost overhead, so the roof receives the most radiation and the east and west facades receive a low sun, which is difficult to block. Far from the equator, the winter sun travels low and enters deep through windows facing the equator, while the summer sun travels high.

Therefore, the classic recommendation to "orient the house to the south" only makes sense in the northern hemisphere outside the tropics. In Santiago or Buenos Aires, the privileged façade is the **north**, and in Quito or Bogotá the most important thing is to protect the roof and the east and west façades.

## The four facades, one by one

### The facade that looks at the equator

In northern Mexico it is the south facade. In the Southern Cone it is the northern façade. It is the easiest to handle: in winter it receives a low sun that warms, and in summer the sun passes high and is blocked by an eave or a relatively short overhang. It is ideal for the living room, dining room or spaces where you spend more hours of the day.

### The east façade

Receives the morning sun. That sun comes in low, but it comes when the air is still cool, so it's usually pleasant. It is a good orientation for bedrooms and kitchens where breakfast is eaten. In very hot climates it is also advisable to protect it, because by mid-morning it already heats up strongly.

### The west facade

It is the most problematic in almost the entire continent. The afternoon sun arrives low, direct and at a time when the air is already warmer. A large unprotected west window can turn a room into the hottest place in the house. It is advisable that it have few windows, that they be protected with **vertical elements** such as lattices, exterior blinds or vegetation, and that there are spaces of little use behind.

### The façade that faces the poles

In the north of Mexico it is the north; in the Southern Cone, the south. It receives almost no direct sun, so it offers an even and soft light, good for studios and workshops. In cold climates it is the coldest and most humid façade, so it is advisable to locate bathrooms, storage rooms, the staircase or the garage there.

## What environment goes in each orientation

| Environment | warm climate | temperate or cold climate |
|---|---|---|
| Living room and dining room | towards the more ventilated side, with wide eaves | towards the equator to gain winter sun |
| Bedrooms | east or towards the pole, far from the west | east or towards the equator |
| Kitchen | ventilated, far from the west | this to have light in the morning |
| Bathrooms, laundry, storage | west, as a thermal mattress | towards the pole, as a cushion against the cold |
| Study or workshop | towards the pole, with even light | towards the pole or east |

The idea of using service spaces as a **thermal mattress** works very well: a bathroom, a cellar or a staircase on the west façade absorb the afternoon heat before it reaches the rooms.

## Eaves, overhangs and protections

A well-oriented window still needs shade in the hot season. The protections are chosen according to the façade:

- **Horizontal screenings** such as eaves, overhangs, pergolas or overhead balconies work best on the equator-facing façade, because they block the high summer sun and let in the low winter sun.
- **Vertical protections** such as lattices, sunshades, exterior blinds or side walls work best on the east and west facades, because there the sun arrives low and from the side.
- **Vegetation**: a deciduous tree protects in summer and lets in the sun in winter. In tropical areas, trees and vines on the west side greatly reduce the heat load.

Exterior protections are more effective than interior curtains. A curtain stops the light, but the heat has already entered through the glass. An exterior blind or lattice stops the sun before it reaches the window.

## The roof, the fifth facade

In the tropical strip, the roof receives more sun than any wall. An uninsulated concrete slab or sheet metal roof can radiate heat inward for hours. Therefore, in Mérida, Barranquilla, Guayaquil or Manaus, the most important decision for comfort is not only the orientation of the windows, but how the roof is protected: light color, insulation, double ventilated roof or a shaded terrace. ## The wind also counts

Orientation is not just a matter of sun. In hot climates, breezes are as valuable as shade. Before defining where the windows go, observe where the dominant wind blows in the hot season, for example the sea breeze on the coast or the valley winds in mountain areas. Ideally, the main openings face these breezes and there are exits on the opposite side. How to achieve this is explained in the [cross ventilation](/articulos/ventilacion-cruzada/) guide.

## If the lot or department is already given

In the city the orientation is rarely chosen. The lot has the shape it has and the apartment looks where it looks. Still, there is a lot of room:

1. **Find out the actual orientation.** Use a compass or the map on your phone and see where the sun comes in at different times and at different times of the year.
2. **Locate the rooms according to use.** If you have to choose, sleep on the cooler side and leave the west side for passage or service spaces.
3. **Protects from the outside.** Exterior blinds, awnings, lattices or plants on the balcony perform more than any curtain.
4. **Improves glass.** Solar control films or low-e glass reduce heat gain, but also some light gain.
5. **Ventilate during cool hours.** Opening at night and early in the morning and closing during the sunnier hours helps a lot in hot climates.

## Tools to study the sun

You don't have to be an architect to get an idea of sunlight:

- **Solar path simulators and applications** show where the sun rises and sets each day of the year at your location.
- **Solar charts** printed for your latitude indicate the height of the sun in each month.
- **Direct observation:** marking on a map where the sun shines at 9, 12 and 16 o'clock in different seasons is one of the most reliable ways to understand a terrain.

For a new project or a major expansion, an architect can carry out precise sunlight studies and size eaves and protections for your latitude.

## Frequent errors

- **Copy recommendations from another hemisphere.** What is valid for Madrid or Monterrey is not valid for Santiago or Quito.
- **Open large windows to the west** for the view, without any exterior protection.
- **Rely only on curtains** to stop the heat.
- **Forget the roof** in tropical climates, where it is the surface that receives the most sun.
- **Ignore the wind** and design only for the sun.
- **Use evergreen trees** in front of the facade that needs sun in winter in cold climates.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it better for the living room to face north or south?

It depends on the hemisphere and the climate. In the Southern Cone, a room to the north receives winter sun and is easy to shade in summer. In the north of Mexico the same thing happens with the south orientation. In the tropical zone, the most important thing is to avoid the west and protect the roof well.

### How tall should an eaves be?

It depends on the latitude, the height of the window and the season you want to shade. As a general rule, the closer to the equator, the higher the sun passes and the shorter the eaves can be on the north or south facades, but the east and west facades still need vertical protections. For exact measurements, a sunlight study is advisable.

### Is it useful to paint the walls facing west white?

Yes, it helps. Light colors reflect more radiation and the wall heats up less. Even so, the most effective protection is still shade.

## In summary

The sun moves differently in each part of Latin America: southward in northern Mexico, northward in the Southern Cone and almost overhead in the tropics. Open the house towards the equator outside the tropics, always protect the west façade with vertical elements, use eaves on the façade that faces the equator and don't forget the roof in hot climates. Locate the rooms according to their use, use bathrooms and tanks as a thermal mattress and take into account the wind. If the batch is already given, exterior protections and good ventilation make the difference.
