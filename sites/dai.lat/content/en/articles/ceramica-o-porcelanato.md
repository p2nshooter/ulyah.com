In the store, a ceramic and a porcelain tile can look almost the same. Both imitate wood, stone, cement or marble; Both come in similar colors and formats. But inside they are different materials, with different behaviors against water, wear, frost and shocks. Choosing one or the other without knowing the difference can lead to spending more on a wall that does not need it or placing a piece in a high-traffic kitchen that will be scratched in a few years.

Furthermore, the success of a floor does not depend only on the piece, but on how it is installed: the base, the adhesive, the joints and the manufacturing lot count as much as the material. In this guide you will see what distinguishes ceramic from porcelain, how to read the box classifications, what is suitable in each environment and what to check when purchasing and installing.

## The big difference: how much water they absorb

Ceramic pieces are classified according to their **water absorption**, which depends on how compact their mass is. International standards group tiles into several groups:

| Group | water absorption | Usual type |
|---|---|---|
| Very low | 0.5% or less | porcelain tile |
| Low | between 0.5 and 3% | stoneware and compact body ceramics |
| Medium | between 3 and 10% | floor ceramics |
| High | more than 10% | wall coverings |

**Porcelain** is made with selected clays and minerals, pressed with great pressure and fired at very high temperatures. The result is a very dense piece, which hardly absorbs water, and is more resistant to wear, stains and frost.

**Ceramic** absorbs more water, is somewhat softer and is usually cheaper and easier to cut. High-absorption ceramics are ideal for interior walls, where there is no traffic.

## How to read the box

### Wear resistance: PEI

In enameled parts, the **PEI** classification indicates how much the surface resists wear and tear due to passing:

| PEI class | Recommended use |
|---|---|
| I | only walls |
| II | light traffic floors, such as bedrooms and bathrooms |
| III | floors of the entire house |
| IV | heavy traffic, entrances, small businesses |
| V | very heavy traffic |

In a kitchen, an entrance or a heavily used hallway, at least PEI III is recommended, and preferably IV.

### Other useful information

- **Grounded:** Ground parts have precision cut edges, allowing for thinner joints.
- **Tone and size:** two lots of the same reference may have slightly different shades or measurements. The box indicates the lot.
- **Anti-slip:** essential for outdoors and bathrooms, as we explain in the guide to [non-slip exterior floors] (/articulos/pisos-exteriores-antiderrapantes/).
- **Frost resistance:** necessary outdoors in cold, high altitude areas.

## Glazed and colored mass porcelain

- **Glazed porcelain:** has a decorative layer on the surface. It allows very varied designs, such as imitations of wood or marble. If the surface is chipped, the dough is seen in a different color.
- **Colored or technical mass porcelain:** the color runs through the entire piece. A blow is less noticeable. It is common in high traffic spaces.
- **Polished porcelain:** very shiny. In some varieties it is advisable to seal the surface, because polishing opens microscopic pores that can stain. It is slippery with water.

## What suits each environment?

| Environment | Usual recommendation |
|---|---|
| Bathroom and kitchen walls | wall ceramic or thin porcelain tile |
| bathroom floor | Porcelain or ceramic floor with non-slip finish |
| kitchen floor | porcelain or ceramic PEI III or IV |
| Living room, dining room and bedrooms | ceramic or porcelain floor, depending on budget and traffic |
| Entrance and hallways | colored mass porcelain or PEI IV |
| Terraces and patios | porcelain tile for exterior, non-slip |
| Cold areas with frost | very low absorption porcelain |

In small bathrooms, large pieces with few joints help the space appear larger, as we tell in the [small bathroom](/articulos/bano-pequeno-medidas-y-distribucion/) guide.

## Large formats: beautiful, but demanding

Pieces measuring 60 by 120 centimeters or more reduce joints and give a continuous image. They demand:

- **A very flat base.** Any irregularities are noticeable as small steps between pieces.
- **Quality adhesive and double gluing,** that is, adhesive on the base and on the back of the piece, so that there are no gaps.
- **Leveling systems** that avoid unevenness between neighboring pieces.
- **Experienced labor** and proper tools to cut and manipulate large pieces without breaking them.

## Installation: what is not seen

- **Firm, clean and flat base.**
- **Adhesive suitable for the material and location:** Porcelain tile, which hardly absorbs water, requires adhesives with better adhesion than a traditional mortar. Flexible adhesives are used outdoors, on underfloor heating or in large formats.
- **Placement joints:** even if the pieces are ground, it is advisable to leave a minimum joint of a few millimeters. Placing pieces "butt", without a joint, encourages them to lift or break with movements.
- **Movement joints** on the perimeter and on large surfaces, which absorb expansion.
- **Suitable Gasket Material:** In showers and kitchens, epoxy gaskets resist water and stains better. We see it in the guide to [waterproofing the shower](/articulos/impermeabilizar-la-ducha/).

## Placement patterns

The way the pieces are placed changes the result completely:

- **Straight or flush joint:** the simplest and most economical, with few cuts.
- **Locked:** each row displaced from the previous one, typical of pieces that imitate wood. In long pieces, it is advisable that the displacement is not exactly half, because a slight factory curvature can create steps between pieces; many manufacturers recommend a third or less.
- **Diagonal:** visually expands small spaces, but generates more cuts and waste.
- **Herringbone:** very decorative, requires specific pieces or a lot of precision.

The more cuts, the more installation time and the more reserve material you have to purchase.

## Traditional ceramics from the region

Latin America has a rich ceramic tradition that is still valid in today's homes:

- **Talavera Puebla:** enameled ceramics handmade in Mexico, with designation of origin. Ideal for details, borders, kitchens and fountains. Their handmade pieces have small variations that are part of their charm.
- **Hydraulic tile:** pieces of pressed colored cement, very present in old houses in Cuba, Colombia, Argentina and other countries. They are porous, need sealing and do not tolerate acidic cleaners.
- **Cooked clay or terracotta:** like the Saltillo tiles in Mexico, warm and rustic. They absorb a lot of water and stains, so they tend to seal and require maintenance.

These pieces require different care than porcelain tile, but they add character and memory to the house.

## When purchasing

1. **Calculate the area** and add a margin for cuts and replacements, about 10 percent in straight placements and more on diagonal or with many cuts.
2. **Buy everything from the same lot,** checking tone and gauge in the boxes.
3. **Open several boxes** and compare parts before starting to install.
4. **Save some leftover parts** for future repairs. In a few years it may be impossible to find the same model.

## Cleaning and maintenance

- **After installing,** a film of cement may remain on the pieces. It is removed with specific products for that purpose, following the manufacturer's instructions.
- **On a day-to-day basis,** water and a neutral cleaner are sufficient.
- **Avoid products with wax or oils** on porcelain, which leave a slippery film.
- **Take care of cement joints:** Frequent acidic cleaners wear them down.

## Frequent errors

- **Use wall ceramic on floors.**
- **Choose a low PEI** for the kitchen or entryway.
- **Polished porcelain** in bathrooms, terraces or wet entrances.
- **Place large formats** on irregular bases.
- **Mix different lots.**
- **Install without perimeter or movement joints**.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is porcelain always better?

It is more resistant and absorbs less water, but it is not always necessary. On interior walls, a good wall ceramic works perfectly and is usually cheaper and easier to cut.

### Can porcelain tile be placed over old ceramic?

In some cases, yes, if the antique ceramic is firm, flat and clean, and a suitable adhesive is used. You have to consider the added height, which can affect doors and unevenness.

### What joint width is best?

It depends on the type of part and the manufacturer's recommendation. In ground interior parts, thin joints of about 2 or 3 millimeters are usually used; in unrectified, rustic or outdoor pieces, wider joints to compensate for differences in measurements and movements of the material.

### Why do pieces break or lift?

The most common causes are an unstable base, insufficient adhesive with gaps under the piece, lack of movement joints, and placement without a joint between pieces.

## In summary

Ceramic absorbs more water and is ideal for walls and floors with moderate traffic; Porcelain tile is denser, more resistant and suitable for demanding floors, exteriors and cold areas. Check the PEI class, grinding, anti-slip and batch in the box. For large formats, a flat base, double gluing and leveling is required. Use appropriate adhesives, leave installation and movement joints, buy with a margin and from the same batch, keep spare parts and clean with neutral products.
