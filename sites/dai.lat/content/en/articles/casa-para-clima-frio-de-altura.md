In the high-altitude cities of Latin America, the cold has a particular character. In Bogotá, Quito, Cusco, La Paz, Toluca or Puno the midday sun can burn the skin, but as soon as it sets the temperature plummets and the mornings are frosty. Many houses do not have heating, the walls and floors are cold for months and families stay warm indoors with several layers of clothing. Added to this is the humidity that condenses on glass and corners, and the mold that appears in closets and bedrooms.

The good news is that the high altitude climate offers a huge and free resource: **a very intense sun**. Well used and combined with a house that does not lose heat, it can completely change interior comfort. In this guide you will see what makes this climate different, how to capture and store the sun's heat, how to prevent it from escaping, how to ventilate without cooling the house and how to heat safely.

## What makes the high altitude climate different?

| Feature | Consequence for the house |
|---|---|
| Very intense solar radiation | the sun can greatly heat well-oriented spaces |
| Big difference between day and night | thermal mass is very useful for storing the heat of the day |
| Cold nights and early mornings, sometimes with frost | the house should lose heat as slowly as possible |
| Dry air in the highlands, humid in other cities | Condensation and mold vary by location |
| Few homes with heating | passive design is the main tool |

## 1. Capture the sun

The first strategy is to let the sun in during the hours of greatest radiation:

- **Windows on the façade that receives the most sun.** Outside the tropics, in the south of Peru, Bolivia, Chile or Argentina, that façade is the northern one. Near the equator, as in Bogotá or Quito, the sun passes almost overhead and it is advisable to take advantage of the morning sun from the east and the well-designed skylights. The guide on [house solar orientation](/articulos/orientacion-solar-de-la-casa/) explains how to identify each facade.
- **Attached solar spaces or greenhouses:** a glazed gallery on the sunny facade is heated during the day and delivers that heat to neighboring rooms. At night, communication with the house is closed so that it does not steal heat.
- **Walls that receive sun through glass:** in Andean rural housing projects, dark walls have been used behind glass, which accumulate the heat of the day and release it at night.

## 2. Keep warm: thermal mass

In climates with a big difference between day and night, heavy materials are allies:

- **Stone, brick or concrete floors** and interior adobe or brick walls that receive direct sun during the day store heat and slowly return it at dusk.
- **The mass must be inside the insulated envelope.** A heavy wall exposed to the cold outside without insulation loses heat to the outside.
- **The sun must reach the mass.** A thick rug on a sunny stone floor reduces its ability to store heat during the day. You can roll it up during sunny hours and spread it out at night.

The traditional adobe houses of the highlands take advantage of this principle, as we explain in the guide on [adobe and construction with earth] (/articulos/adobe-y-construccion-con-tierra/).

## 3. Don't let the heat escape

No matter how much sun comes in, if the house loses heat quickly, the night will be cold.

### The roof first

The hot air rises, and a large part of the heat is lost through the ceiling. An insulated ceiling or above-slab insulation makes a big difference. We see it in detail in the [roof thermal insulation] guide (/articulos/aislamiento-termico-del-techo/).

### Windows and doors

- **Double hermetic glass**, known in different countries as thermopanel or DVH, greatly reduces losses and condensation on the glass.
- **Frames with good closure:** Aluminum frames without thermal bridge break cool down a lot and condense water.
- **Seals** on doors and windows to cut off cold air currents.
- **Thick curtains** that close at dusk and open on sunny mornings. More ideas in the [curtains, textiles and rugs](/articulos/cortinas-textiles-y-alfombras/) guide.

### Walls and floors

- **Insulation in exterior walls,** ideally on the outside, which also protects the interior thermal mass.
- **Floors isolated from the terrain** or, at a minimum, carpets and rugs in bedrooms.
- **Compact shape:** The less exterior surface area the house has in relation to its volume, the less heat it loses.

## 4. Intermediate spaces

A closed vestibule at the entrance, a glazed gallery or a storage room on the coldest façade function as **thermal mattresses** between the interior and exterior. At the entrance, a double door prevents hot air from escaping every time someone enters or leaves.

## 5. Ventilate without cooling the house

Cold, closed houses accumulate moisture: from people, the kitchen, showers, and drying clothes. That moisture condenses on the glass and in the coldest corners, and mold appears. To avoid it:

- **Ventilate briefly and intensely:** open wide for a few minutes, in the warmest hours, renew the air without cooling walls and furniture.
- **Extracts steam where it is produced:** hood in the kitchen and extractor fan in the bathroom.
- **Do not dry clothes inside** without ventilation.
- **Maintain a stable temperature,** even if it is moderate, so that the walls do not get so cold.

The guide on [damp in walls](/articulos/humedad-en-paredes-causas-y-soluciones/) explains how to distinguish condensation from other causes.

## 6. Warm up safely

When passive design is not enough, heating must be **safe**:

- **Never use gas, kerosene or charcoal stoves without exhaust gases to the outside** in bedrooms or closed spaces. They produce carbon monoxide, an odorless, colorless gas that can be deadly, as well as a lot of water vapor.
- **Fireplaces and wood-burning stoves** require a well-installed, inspected and clean flue, and safe distances from combustible materials.
- **Install carbon monoxide detectors** if there is any fuel-burning appliance in your home.
- **Electric heat pumps** are an efficient option for heating rooms.
- **Warms people, not just the air:** Blankets, warm bedding, and hot water bottles used carefully help you get a good night's sleep without heating up the entire house.

More ideas to reduce spending in the [energy saving at home] guide (/articulos/ahorro-de-energia-en-casa/), and about the risks of gas in the [extractor hood and gas in the kitchen] guide (/articulos/campana-extractora-y-gas-en-la-cocina/).

## 7. The high sun also burns

The same radiation that heats the house is very intense in ultraviolet rays. It discolors fabrics, wood and paints and damages the skin. Protect furniture and textiles with curtains or blinds during hours of direct sunlight when the room is already hot, and choose radiation-resistant materials and paints outdoors.

## 8. Frost and installations

In places where it freezes, exposed water pipes can freeze and break. Protect them with insulation, bury them at the appropriate depth, and choose frost-resistant exterior flooring.

## Frequent errors

- **Large windows on the cold façade** and small ones on the sunny façade.
- **Ceiling without insulation,** where heat escapes.
- **Simple frames and glasses** that condense water every morning.
- **Heaters without gas outlet** in bedrooms.
- **Close the house completely** without ever ventilating.
- **Cover floors that receive sun during the day with thick rugs**.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why is my house in Bogotá so cold if it's sunny outside?

Probably because the windows don't catch the sun at the right times, the roof and windows lose a lot of heat and there is no thermal mass to store it. Insulating the roof, sealing the windows and opening the curtains to the sun usually give good results.

### Is double glazing worth it?

In cold climates, yes: it reduces heat loss, condensation on the glass and noise. It is one of the most noticeable improvements in bedrooms and living rooms.

### What heating is the safest?

Electrical ones in good condition, such as heat pumps or electric radiators, and combustion appliances with gas outlet to the outside, installed by authorized personnel and with carbon monoxide detectors.

## In summary

In the cold high altitude climate, take advantage of the intense sun with well-oriented windows, solar spaces and thermal mass inside the house. Prevent heat from escaping by first insulating the ceiling, using double glazing, weather stripping and thick curtains, and creating spaces in between. Ventilate briefly and intensely to avoid condensation, extract steam from the kitchen and bathroom and never use heaters without exhaust gases in closed spaces. Protect materials from strong sun and pipes from frost.
