In much of Latin America, building a house means building brick or block walls. It is the material that is seen in the neighborhoods of Lima, in the extensions of Mexico City, in the houses of Medellín and in the chalets of Buenos Aires. Precisely because it is so common, it is often chosen out of habit or price, without thinking about the differences between one brick and another.

Those differences matter. There are pieces that can be part of a weight-bearing wall and others that only serve to divide rooms. Some keep heat for hours and others let in less noise. And on a continent where earthquakes are a part of life, the way the wall is built matters as much as the brick. In this guide you will see the main types, their uses, how to recognize a good quality piece and what care a wall needs to last.

## Clay brick: solid, perforated and hollow

Traditional brick is made from molded and kiln-fired clay. According to their internal shape, three large groups are distinguished:

- **Solid or solid:** without gaps or with minimal perforations. It is heavy, resistant and has good thermal mass. It can be artisanal or industrial.
- **Perforated:** with vertical holes that reduce weight and improve mortar adhesion. Many industrial models are designed for load-bearing walls.
- **Hollow or tubular:** with large holes, usually horizontal. It is light and economical, but is generally used for non-weight-bearing partitions.

The names change a lot from one country to another and between manufacturers:

| Country | Frequent names |
|---|---|
| Mexico | annealed red partition, extruded partition, concrete block, partition |
| Colombia | tolete brick, clay block, lantern brick |
| Peru | King Kong brick, tambourine brick, concrete block |
| Chile | tax brick, princess brick, concrete block |
| Argentina and Uruguay | common brick, hollow brick, concrete block |

More than the name, what matters is the **technical sheet**: compression resistance, percentage of voids, water absorption and what type of wall it is approved for. For example, in Peru the tambourine brick, with horizontal holes, is not considered suitable for load-bearing walls in seismic zones, although it has been used this way in many homes.

## concrete block

The concrete block, also called block or concrete block, is a piece of cement, sand and fine gravel, normally with two or three vertical cells. It has several advantages:

- **Regular measurements** that allow straight walls that are quick to erect.
- **Cells that can be filled** with concrete and steel rods to reinforce the wall.
- **Good resistance** when it is of quality and well cured.

Its weak point is quality variation. Blocks made uncontrollably, with little cement or poorly cured, crumble in the corners and have little strength. Always ask for the technical sheet and be wary of parts that break easily when handled.

## Other alternatives

- **Ceramic concrete block:** very light and with better thermal insulation than the common block, although it requires specific mortars and anchors.
- **Compressed earth block:** earth stabilized with a little cement or lime, pressed into molds. It has low manufacturing energy and good thermal mass. We see it in the guide on [adobe and earth construction](/articulos/adobe-y-construccion-con-tierra/).
- **Facing or façade brick:** designed to be visible, with regular faces and less water absorption.

## The wall in seismic zones

Much of Latin America is in zones of seismic activity, from Mexico and Central America to the Pacific coast of South America. In these areas, an **unreinforced** brick or block wall is fragile: it can crack and collapse during an earthquake.

Masonry homes are therefore built with systems such as:

- **Confined masonry:** the walls are framed by reinforced concrete columns and beams, called castles and dalas in Mexico, columns and beams in Peru or chained in Argentina.
- **Reinforced masonry:** Block cells are filled with concrete and rebar, and horizontal reinforcement is placed at the joints.

Each country has its earthquake-resistant construction standard, with requirements for thickness, reinforcement, distances between columns and quality of materials. Any weight-bearing wall, addition or second story must be calculated by a civil or structural engineer and have the appropriate permits. ## Heat, cold and noise

No brick or block is a good thermal insulator on its own, but they behave differently:

| material | thermal mass | Isolation | Typical behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solid brick | high | low | stores heat and returns it hours later |
| Hollow or perforated brick | average | something better | lighter, the air in the gaps insulates a little |
| concrete block | medium to high | low | similar to solid brick, more so if filled |
| cellular concrete | low | good | heats up and cools down faster, insulates better |

**Thermal mass** is useful in climates with hot days and cold nights, such as the highlands, Andean valleys or deserts: the wall absorbs heat during the day and releases it at night. In hot, humid climates, where nights are also hot, very massive walls exposed to the sun can continue to radiate heat during the night. There it is more important to shade the walls and ventilate well. More ideas in the guide to [cool the house without air conditioning](/articulos/enfriar-la-casa-sin-aire-acondicionado/).

As for **noise**, heavy walls without cracks insulate better than light ones. A well-plastered solid brick wall stops more noise than a hollow brick wall of the same thickness.

## How to recognize a good brick

Some simple tests are used on site, which do not replace laboratory tests but help rule out bad parts:

- **Uniform color** throughout the lot. Very light or stained pieces may be undercooked.
- **Sound:** When you hit two good quality fired bricks, they sound clear and almost metallic. A dull sound indicates undercooking or internal cracks.
- **Regular edges and faces,** without cracks, deformations or crumbling.
- **Even measurements** between pieces, so that the mortar joints are regular.
- **Moderate absorption:** a brick that absorbs excessive water is usually not very resistant and more prone to humidity.

## Good practices when building a wall

1. **Well-made foundation and foundation,** with a barrier against moisture rising from the ground.
2. **Moisten clay bricks** before setting them, so they do not absorb water from the mortar. Concrete blocks, on the other hand, are usually placed dry. Follow the manufacturer's instructions and local regulations.
3. **Complete and even mortar joints,** usually around one centimeter.
4. **Coursed level and plumb,** checked with level and plumb line.
5. **Correct lacing,** without vertical joints aligned from one course to the next.
6. **Curing the mortar and concrete,** keeping them moist for the first few days.

## Humidity and efflorescence

The brick is porous. Without protection, soil moisture can rise by capillarity, and rain can pass through exposed walls.

- **Moisture rising from the ground:** is prevented with an impermeable barrier on the foundation. When it already exists, the treatment is more complex. We see it in the guide on [humidity in walls](/articulos/humedad-en-paredes-causas-y-soluciones/).
- **Exposed brick:** you need good quality pieces, well-made joints and, in areas with a lot of rain, a breathable water-repellent that reduces absorption without completely sealing the wall.
- **Efflorescences:** are white spots of salts that water drags to the surface. They are dry brushed. Washing them with a lot of water can make them worse. If they return, the cause is humidity, which must be resolved.

## Frequent errors

- **Use hollow brick in load-bearing walls** in seismic zones.
- **Build without columns or confinement beams** to save.
- **Buy by price** without asking for the technical sheet.
- **Do not moisten clay bricks** in hot, dry climates.
- **Bypassing the moisture barrier** on the subfloor.
- **Wash efflorescence with water** without resolving its origin.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which is better, brick or concrete block?

Both work well if they are of quality and used in the correct construction system. Clay brick usually has better behavior against humidity and a visually appreciated aesthetic. The block is faster to place and is easily reinforced by its cells.

### Can I leave the brick unplastered?

Yes, if it is a brick suitable for being exposed and the joints are well executed. On facades exposed to rain it is advisable to protect it with a breathable water repellent.

### Does a brick wall keep the house cool?

It depends on the weather. In places with cool nights, thermal mass helps stabilize the temperature. In hot, humid climates, a wall exposed to the sun can accumulate heat. Shade and ventilation are key.

## In summary

Solid brick is heavy and has good thermal mass, the perforated brick can be load-bearing and the opening is usually limited to partitions. Concrete block is fast and easily reinforced, but its quality varies greatly. Always ask for the technical sheet, check color, sound and measurements, and respect good placement practices. In seismic zones, all load-bearing walls must be confined or reinforced and calculated by a professional. Protect the walls from moisture from the foundation and choose the material thinking about your climate.
