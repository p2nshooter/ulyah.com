Wood gives warmth to any house. A solid table, a plank floor, a carved door or a pergola in the patio completely change an environment. In Latin America, in addition, there is an enormous tradition of carpentry and a great diversity of species, from the plantation pines and eucalyptus of the Southern Cone to the tropical hardwoods of Central America, Colombia, Brazil or Paraguay.

But wood is a living material: it moves with moisture, can be attacked by termites and fungi, and discolors in the sun. A good choice and good care make it last for generations; Poorly dried or poorly protected wood can warp or rot in a few years. In this guide you will see how to choose wood according to use, why its moisture content matters so much, how to prevent pests and rot, what finishes should be used and how to care for it on a daily basis.

## Choose the species according to the use

Each species has different hardness, stability and natural resistance to insects and fungi. Common names change a lot between countries, so it is a good idea to always ask about the scientific name and origin.

| Wood type | Common examples | Common uses | To take into account |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plantation conifers | pines of different species | furniture, light structures, interior coverings | soft, need treatment for outdoors and against insects |
| plantation eucalyptus | several species | structures, floors, furniture, treated exteriors | can crack if not dry properly |
| plantation teak | cultivated in Central America and other areas | outdoor furniture, decks, carpentry | good natural resistance to moisture |
| Tropical hardwoods | lapachos or guayacanes, carob trees and others | floors, decks, beams, furniture | very resistant, but verify its legal origin |
| Cedars and mahoganies | cedar, mahogany | fine carpentry, doors, furniture | trade regulated by international agreements |
| Bamboo and guadua | guadua in Colombia and Ecuador | structures, coverings, furniture | It is a grass, it needs treatment against insects |

**Legal and sustainable wood:** some species, such as mahogany and cedar, are protected by international conventions and their trade is regulated. Ask for documentation of origin and, when possible, forest-certified wood. Plantation species, such as pines, eucalyptus or teak, are an alternative with less pressure on native forests.

## Wood humidity: the detail that matters most

Freshly cut wood contains a lot of water. As it dries, it **shrinks, twists and cracks**. Therefore, wood for furniture, floors and carpentry must be **dry** before being worked, ideally dried in a chamber or oven and with a moisture content close to that of the environment where it will be used.

A piece of furniture made from damp wood can open joints, warp or crack in its first few months at home. When purchasing:

- **Ask how the wood was dried** and, if possible, its moisture content.
- **Allow the wood to acclimate** a few days in the place where it will be installed, especially on floors.
- **Be wary of very low prices** on "ready to use" wood that feels cold and heavy to the touch.

Even dry, wood continues to absorb and release moisture with the seasons. That is why floors need expansion joints and well-made furniture allows small movements.

## Termites and other insects

Termites, known as termites in many countries, and wood-boring beetles, called woodworms, are a common problem in warm climates.

**Warning signs:**

- **Mud tunnels** on walls, foundations or frames, typical of subterranean termites.
- **Small piles of sawdust or pellets** under furniture or frames.
- **Wood that sounds hollow** when hit or that sinks when pressed.
- **Loose wings** near windows or lights, after a nuptial flight.

**Prevention:**

- **Treated wood** with preservatives by impregnation for structures and exteriors.
- **Avoid direct contact of the wood with the ground** and with wet walls.
- **Periodically check** frames, baseboards, beams and furniture leaning against walls.
- **Remove leftover wood, cardboard boxes and firewood** next to the house.

If you detect an active infestation, contact a pest control company. Termites can seriously damage structures before the damage is visible.

## Rot: when wood gets wet over and over again

Wood-rotting fungi need constant moisture. Wood that gets wet and dries quickly lasts a long time; what remains wet rots. Therefore, outdoors and in humid areas:

- **Separate the wood from the ground** with stone, concrete or metal bases.
- **Design for water to drain:** sloping surfaces, rounded edges, drips.
- **Seals the testas,** the cut ends, which absorb water like a straw.
- **Ventilate** spaces under wooden floors and ceilings.

## Finishes: oil, varnish or protector

| Finish | How it protects | Where convenient | Maintenance |
|---|---|---|---|
| penetrating oil | penetrates the wood, natural appearance | furniture, covers, oiled floors | Renew frequently, easy to touch up |
| Wax | soft shine, light protection | little exposed interior furniture | renew often |
| Varnish or polyurethane | forms a resistant film | high traffic floors, tables | very durable, difficult to touch up in areas |
| Open pore sunscreen protector | protects from sun and water without forming a thick film | exteriors, pergolas, decks | renew every one to three years depending on exposure |
| Wood paint | opaque color and protection | exterior carpentry, doors | repaint according to wear |

Outdoors, the sun degrades the surface of the wood and turns it gray. Products with ultraviolet filters delay this process. More about pergolas and outdoor structures in the [pergolas, awnings and shade] guide (/articulos/pergolas-toldos-y-sombra/).

**Oil Safety:** Rags soaked in linseed oil and other drying oils can **ignite on their own** when drying in a pile. Spread them outdoors to dry, or store them in a closed metal container with water, and dispose of them according to local regulations.

## Daily furniture and floor care

- **Clean with a dry or slightly damp cloth.** Excess water damages joints and finishes.
- **Protect from direct sun** with curtains or blinds: light discolors the wood.
- **Use felts** under the legs of chairs and tables.
- **Dry up immediately** water, wine or coffee spills.
- **Avoid heaters and heat sources** near furniture.
- **Refinish** before the wood is exposed.

## Wood, engineered and laminate flooring

- **Solid wood:** one-piece boards. They can be sanded and renewed several times.
- **Engineered Flooring:** A hardwood top layer over a cross-ply base, more moisture stable.
- **Laminate:** is not wood, but an image printed on a board. It is inexpensive, but it fears water in the joints and cannot be sanded.

In very humid climates or on the ground floor with risk of soil moisture, engineered flooring usually performs better than solid flooring. In kitchens and bathrooms, ceramics and porcelain tend to be more practical.

## Renovate old furniture

Many homes have solid wood furniture from parents or grandparents, which is more solid than much of the new furniture. Renewing them is usually worth it:

1. **Check the structure:** loose legs, open joints and signs of insects.
2. **Clean thoroughly** with a slightly damp cloth and mild soap.
3. **Decide the finish:** If the varnish is healthy, sometimes a cleaning and a coat of wax or oil is enough. If it is very damaged, it will have to be sanded or stripped.
4. **Sand following the grain,** from coarse to fine grains, with a mask and good ventilation.
5. **Apply thin layers** of the new finish, respecting the drying times.

Keep in mind that very old furniture may have lead paint. If in doubt, consult a restorer before sanding.

## Treated wood: what not to do

- **Do not burn wood treated** with preservatives: it may release toxic fumes.
- **Use a mask when sanding** any wood, and even more so if it is treated or is an irritating species.
- **Do not use treated wood for cutting boards** or food contact surfaces unless expressly permitted by the product.

## Frequent errors

- **Buy wet wood** for furniture or floors.
- **Support wood directly on the ground** outdoors.
- **Do not seal the ends** of beams and boards.
- **Ignore the sawdust** that appears under a piece of furniture.
- **Leave oily rags in a pile.**
- **Use indoor products outdoors.**

## Frequently asked questions

### What wood is best for outdoor furniture?

Those with high natural resistance, such as teak or some hardwoods of legal origin, or plantation woods treated by impregnation. They all need sunscreen and periodic maintenance.

### How do I know if wood is dry?

The most reliable is a moisture meter for wood. Without it, be wary of heavy pieces, cold to the touch or with an intense smell of fresh wood.

### Does varnish prevent termites?

Not by itself. A finish protects the surface, but insect prevention requires treated wood, good design and periodic checks.

## In summary

Choose the species according to the use, verify its legal origin and prefer plantation or certified wood. Buy well-dried wood and let it acclimate before installing it. Prevent termites and rot with treated wood by separating it from the ground, sealing the ends, and checking frequently. Use oils, waxes, varnishes or sunscreens depending on the location and renew them in time. Clean with little water, protect from the sun and never leave oily rags in a pile.
