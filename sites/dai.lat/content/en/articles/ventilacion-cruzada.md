There are rooms with large windows where the air does not move, and modest houses where a pleasant breeze always blows. The difference is usually not the size of the windows, but their **location**. Air needs a path: a place to enter and another to leave. When this path exists and runs through the rooms, we speak of cross ventilation.

In the hot climates of Latin America, cross ventilation is one of the most effective and cheapest tools for comfortable living. It renews the air, removes accumulated heat, reduces humidity and makes the temperature feel lower. In this guide you will see how it works, how to locate and choose windows and doors, how deep the rooms can be and what to do if you live in an apartment with windows on a single facade.

## How air moves in a house

Air moves due to differences in pressure and temperature.

- **Due to the wind:** when the wind hits a facade, it creates an area of ​​higher pressure on that side and one of lower pressure on the opposite side. If there are openings on both sides, the air enters through the exposed facade and leaves through the opposite.
- **Due to temperature difference:** hot air is lighter and rises. If there are high openings, it comes out from above and cooler air enters through the low openings. It's the chimney effect.

Cross ventilation primarily takes advantage of the first mechanism, but works best when combined with the second.

## The basic rules

1. **One entrance and one exit.** A room with a single window barely ventilates, even if it is large. You need at least two openings, ideally on opposite walls.
2. **The entrance faces the breeze.** Observe where the wind blows from in the hot season, for example the sea breeze on the coast in the afternoon, and orient the entrance openings there.
3. **Outlet equal to or greater than the inlet.** When the outlet is somewhat larger than the inlet, the air enters faster and feels cooler.
4. **Free path.** Tall furniture, interior walls or heavy curtains can block the path.
5. **Openings at different heights.** Entrances at people's height cool the body. The high vents remove the hot air that accumulates under the ceiling.

## How deep can a room be?

For reference, many design guides state that:

| Situation | Depth that ventilates well |
|---|---|
| Cross ventilation, openings in opposite walls | up to about five times the height of the ceiling |
| Single side ventilation | up to about two and a half times the height of the ceiling |

With a 2.5 meter ceiling, a room with cross ventilation can be about 12 meters deep and still ventilate, while with windows on one side only the air is renewed well in the first 6 meters. That is why traditional warm climate houses are narrow, with rooms in a row and openings on both sides, or they are organized around a patio.

## Choose the type of window

Not all windows allow the same amount of air to pass through.

| Window type | Useful surface for ventilation | Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sliding | about half the span | economical, does not take up space when opening | ventilate only half |
| Folding or swing | almost all the space | captures and guides the breeze | the blade takes up space when opened |
| Projecting or awning | partial | can be left open in the rain | ventilates less than the folding one |
| Glass or slat lattice | almost all the space | Ventilates with privacy and some protection from rain | closes less tightly |
| Upright or vent over doors | small but tall | let the hot air out | requires hardware to open and close |

In hot and humid climates, blinds and casement windows are very effective. The **pillars** over the interior doors, traditional in old mansions, allow air to pass from one room to another even if the doors are closed.

## The interior layout also ventilates

A house with good windows can ventilate poorly if its interior is compartmentalized. Some ideas:

- **Align the openings** throughout the house, so that a current can pass through several rooms.
- **Avoid closed hallways** without windows at the ends.
- **Use interior doors with mullion, grille or louvre** where you need privacy without cutting off the air.
- **Separate rooms with low furniture** instead of full walls when possible.

## When the wind blows parallel to the facade

Sometimes the breeze does not hit head-on, but rather runs along the façade. In that case, a single window receives little air. Some resources help:

- **Fins or short perpendicular walls** next to the windows, which deflect the wind inward.
- **Folding windows** whose open leaf acts as a screen and directs the breeze inward.
- **Vegetation** strategically located to channel the wind.

## Mosquitoes, rain and safety

In the tropics, ventilating involves solving other problems at the same time:

- **Mosquito nets:** are essential in many regions to protect yourself from mosquito-borne diseases. They reduce air flow somewhat, so it is advisable to compensate with slightly larger openings.
- **Rain:** Generous eaves, lattices and projecting windows allow ventilation even if it rains.
- **Security:** Well-designed bars or fixed lattices allow windows to be left open at night.
- **Noise and dust:** On busy streets, ventilate during quieter hours and direct the main openings towards the interior of the lot or a patio.

## If you live in an apartment

Many apartments have windows on a single façade. Still, it can be improved:

1. **Open two windows far from each other** on the same façade. The air enters through one and exits through the other.
2. **Open a high window and a low one,** if there are any, to take advantage of the chimney effect.
3. **Take advantage of light wells or kitchen and bathroom windows** as an air outlet.
4. **Use a fan** facing the window to expel hot air and force in fresh air.
5. **Ventilate during cool hours,** especially in dry climates.

## Ventilate at the correct time

Opening the windows does not always cool you down. If the air outside is hotter than the inside, ventilating brings in heat. In dry climates, it is effective to open at night and early in the morning and close when the outside temperature begins to rise. In hot and humid climates, where the difference between day and night is small, it is advisable to maintain the current almost all day, with the windows shaded. A simple thermometer inside and another outside the house helps decide when to open and when to close. ## How to check if your house ventilates

A simple test is to light an incense stick and observe the smoke near each window and door. If the smoke stays still or rises slowly, air does not circulate. If it moves clearly inward at one opening and outward in another, there is cross ventilation. Repeat the test at different times, because the breezes change during the day.

## Ventilation, humidity and health

In addition to cooling, ventilation renews indoor air, reduces odors and humidity, and helps prevent mold. In kitchens and bathrooms, localized extraction is recommended so that steam and odors are not distributed throughout the house. ## Frequent errors

- **Put large windows on a single wall** and hope they ventilate.
- **Choose only sliding windows** in very hot climates.
- **Close the air path** with tall furniture or thick curtains.
- **Forget about high openings** where hot air can escape.
- **Forgo ventilation due to mosquitoes** instead of installing mosquito nets.
- **Ventilate during the hottest hours** in dry climates.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is one large window or two small ones better?

For ventilation, two openings in opposing walls work much better than a single large window. The ideal is to combine an entrance facing the breeze and an equal or slightly larger exit.

### Does cross ventilation work in cold climates?

Yes, to renew the air and control humidity, although in winter it is advisable to do it in short and intense periods, opening wide for a few minutes and closing afterwards.

### Can I ventilate if my house faces a noisy street?

You can, but it is advisable that the main openings face the interior of the lot or a patio. Towards the street, windows with good closure and ventilation in the quietest hours.

## In summary

Cross ventilation needs a path: an entrance facing the breeze and an equal or greater exit on the opposite side, with no obstacles between them. The rooms should not be too deep, folding windows and lattices ventilate more than sliding ones, and the mullions allow air to pass between rooms. Combine low and high openings, protect with mosquito nets and eaves and, in apartments, open windows far from each other and use a fan. Check the result with a simple incense stick.
