Many gardens in Latin America are full of plants that come from other continents. Lawns that need daily watering in dry climates, shrubs that suffer from the tropical sun, species that get sick from humidity or that, on the contrary, escape from the garden and become invasive. Meanwhile, a few kilometers away, native plants perfectly adapted to the climate, soil and fauna of the place grow without help.

A garden with native plants is not a neglected garden or a vacant lot. It can be as tidy and flowery as any other, with the advantage that it consumes less water, needs fewer chemicals and attracts hummingbirds, butterflies, bees and birds. In this guide you will see why it is convenient, how to plan it according to your climate, what techniques save water and how to avoid invasive species.

## Why choose native plants

- **They are adapted** to the rain, drought, heat, cold and soil of your region.
- **They need less watering** once established.
- **They resist local pests better,** with which they have coexisted for thousands of years.
- **They feed wildlife:** Many birds, butterflies and insects depend on specific native plants. Hummingbirds, which only exist in America, look for the flowers they evolved with.
- **They give identity:** a garden with local flora feels part of the landscape and not a copy of another place.

## Know your climate and your microclimates

Latin America brings together very different climates, and what is native in one place may not be native two hundred kilometers away. Before choosing plants, observe:

- **Rain:** how much it rains and in what months. Many regions have a marked dry season.
- **Altitude:** in the Andes or in central Mexico, altitude changes the temperature a lot.
- **Sun and shade:** each corner of the garden has its own microclimate. Note which areas receive sun all day, only in the morning, or almost not at all.
- **The wind:** exposed areas dry out the plants.
- **The Soil:** Dig a hole, fill it with water and see how long it takes to drain. A clay soil that retains water needs different plants than a sandy soil that lets it pass through immediately.

## Some examples by region

These are just ideas to get you started. Always consult nurseries specialized in native flora, botanical gardens or environmental programs in your area.

| Region | Examples of native plants |
|---|---|
| Arid north of Mexico | agaves, nopales, yuccas, native sages |
| Central Mexico | dahlias, marigolds, salvias, cacti and local succulents |
| Humid tropics of Central America, the Caribbean and Colombia | heliconias, bromeliads, coralillo, guayacanes |
| Andes | cantuta, quishuar, queñua, chilca |
| Central Chile | quillay, peumo, boldo, maitén |
| Pampa and coast of Argentina and Uruguay | ceibo, jacaranda in the northwest, verbenas and native grasses |

The same common name can refer to different species depending on the country. When purchasing, ask for the scientific name and origin of the plant.

## Group plants according to their need for water

One of the keys to saving water is **hydrozoning**: placing plants together that need similar amounts of watering.

- **High irrigation area:** near the house or the water tap, with the most demanding plants and pots.
- **Moderate irrigation zone:** shrubs and flowers that need water in the dry season.
- **Low or no irrigation zone:** plants adapted to drought, in the most remote or sunny places.

This way you don't overwater resistant plants or underwater sensitive ones.

## Less grass, more life

Traditional grass is one of the largest consumers of water in the garden. It is not necessary to eliminate it completely, but reduce it to the areas where it is really used, such as a play area. The rest can become:

- **Native soil covers,** that cover the soil with little irrigation.
- **Shrub and flower beds** with mulch.
- **Roads made of gravel, stone or permeable pavement,** that allow rainwater to pass through. We see it in the guide on [non-slip exterior floors](/articulos/pisos-exteriores-antiderrapantes/).

## Padding: the best ally

Covering the soil with a layer of **mulch**, also called mulch, such as bark, dry leaves, straw or crushed pruning remains, has great benefits:

- reduces evaporation of water from the soil,
- keeps the earth cooler,
- stops the appearance of weeds,
- It decomposes slowly and improves the soil.

A layer of several centimeters around the plants, without touching the stems, makes a big difference in the dry season.

## Water better

- **Water in the early morning or evening,** when less water evaporates.
- **Water deeply and less frequently** so the roots grow downward.
- **Use drip irrigation** in beds and bushes: bring the water directly to the roots.
- **Take advantage of stored rainwater**, as we explain in the guide on [rainwater harvesting](/articulos/captacion-de-agua-de-lluvia/).
- **Reduce watering** when native plants are established, usually after the first year.

## Gardens that absorb rain

In areas with heavy rainfall, a **rain garden** is a gentle depression in the soil, planted with species that tolerate both brief flooding and drought. It receives water from roofs or pavements and lets it infiltrate slowly, reducing runoff towards the street. It should be located away from the foundation of the house. More on water management in the guide to [rainwater, gutters and drainage](/articulos/agua-de-lluvia-canaletas-y-drenaje/).

## Compost: food for the soil

Native plants do not need large doses of fertilizer, but they appreciate living soil. Homemade compost, made with kitchen scraps of plant origin, dry leaves and pruning remains, improves the structure of the soil, helps retain water and provides nutrients slowly. To make it work well:

- **Alternate wet and dry layers,** such as vegetable peels and dried leaves.
- **Keep it moist like a wrung sponge,** neither puddled nor dry.
- **Stir it from time to time** to let air in.
- **Avoid meat, dairy and fats,** which attract animals and produce bad odors.

In a few months you will have a dark, loose material that you can spread at the base of the plants or mix with the soil when planting.

## Beware of invasive species

Some exotic plants escape from the gardens and displace the local flora. In different countries in the region, species such as thorny broom in areas of Colombia and Chile or kikuyu grass in Andean areas, among many others, are considered invasive. Before planting, check your country's lists of invasive species and avoid those that spread easily by seeds, roots or stems.

## Attracts pollinators and birds

- **Choose flowers of different colors and shapes** that bloom at different times of the year.
- **Includes plants with red or orange tubular flowers**, which attract hummingbirds.
- **Avoid broad-spectrum pesticides**, which also kill bees and butterflies.
- **Leave some less tidy corners** with leaves and branches, where useful insects take refuge.
- **Offer clean water in a shallow dish,** changing it every few days so it doesn't become a breeding ground for mosquitoes.

## Trees: shadow with criteria

A well-placed native tree provides shade, coolness and a home for birds. Before planting it, find out its adult size and root behavior, and keep it a safe distance from foundations, pipes, walls and cables. A tree to the west of the house can greatly reduce the afternoon heat, as we explain in the guide to [cooling the house without air conditioning](/articulos/enfriar-la-casa-sin-aire-acondicionado/).

## Safety with children and pets

Some plants, native or not, can be toxic if ingested or irritate the skin. Others have thorns. If you have small children or pets at home, learn about each species before planting it and place the thorny plants away from play areas.

## Frequent errors

- **Choose plants only for the photo,** without thinking about the climate of the place.
- **Mix high and low watering plants** in the same bed.
- **Water a little every day** instead of watering deeply.
- **Leave the floor bare** without padding.
- **Plant invasive species** because they grow fast.
- **Use pesticides** that eliminate pollinators.

## Frequently asked questions

### Don't native plants need any care?

They need less, but not none. During the first year they require regular watering until their roots are established. Then, occasional pruning, weed control and irrigation in the driest season.

### Can I mix native plants with others?

Yes. Many people combine native species with non-invasive ornamental plants. The important thing is that the base of the garden is adapted to the climate and that species that escape are not planted.

### Where do I get native plants?

In specialized nurseries, botanical gardens, municipal or environmental programs and plant fairs. Always ask for the scientific name.

## In summary

A garden with native plants consumes less water, resists pests better and attracts hummingbirds, butterflies and birds. Know your climate, your soil and the microclimates of each corner, group the plants according to their need for irrigation and reduce the grass to what is essential. Use mulch, water early and deeply, take advantage of rain, and avoid invasive species and pesticides. Thus, the garden takes care of itself almost and becomes a living part of the landscape of your region.
