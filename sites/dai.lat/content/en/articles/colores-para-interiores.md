Choosing a wall color seems like a simple decision until the shade that looked perfect in the store appears in the room as something completely different. Elegant gray turns bluish, warm white appears yellow, and soft green ends up dark and heavy. It's not a mistake in the painting: it's the light. The same color changes depending on the orientation of the room, the time of day, the type of light bulb and even the furniture that surrounds it.

In Latin America this is even more noticeable. The light of the tropics is intense and vertical, that of a mountain house can be cold and gray for months, and that of an apartment in the Southern Cone changes a lot between winter and summer. In this guide you will see how light influences color, how to put together a coherent palette for the entire house, what finishes should be used in each environment and how to test paint before buying extra gallons.

## First look at the light

Before opening a color catalog, observe the room at different times.

- **Rooms that face the equator** (to the south in northern Mexico, to the north in the Southern Cone) receive direct, warm light for much of the day. They tolerate cold colors well, which balance the warmth of the light.
- **Rooms that face the poles** receive indirect, colder and more even light. Warm colors, such as off-whites, sands or soft terracottas, prevent them from looking dull.
- **Rooms to the east** are bright in the morning and grayer in the afternoon. **Western rooms** are the opposite and become very warm at dusk.
- **In the tropical zone**, where the sun passes high, the outside light is very intense and the difference between sunny and shaded areas is marked. Highly saturated colors can be tiring in brightly lit spaces, while in shaded interiors they help bring life.

If you want to understand why each facade receives different light, the guide on [solar orientation of the house](/articulos/orientacion-solar-de-la-casa/) explains it in detail.

## Artificial light also paints

At night, the color of your walls depends on the lights. LED lamps are sold with different **color temperatures**, expressed in kelvin:

| Temperature | How it looks | Effect on paint |
|---|---|---|
| 2700 to 3000K | warm, yellowish light | enhances warm tones, can turn whites yellow |
| 3500 to 4000K | neutral light | shows colors more faithfully |
| 5000 to 6500K | cold, bluish light | cools tones, can turn beiges gray |

Choose the paint with the light that you are going to use at night. More about bulbs and color temperature in the [house lighting] guide (/articulos/iluminacion-de-la-casa/).

## Warm, cold and neutral

All colors have an **undertone**. Two whites may look the same on the sample and, on the wall, one looks cream and the other almost light blue. The same goes for grays, beiges and greens.

- **Warm:** reds, oranges, yellows, terracottas, ochers and whites with a yellow or pink base. They give a feeling of closeness and shelter.
- **Cold:** blues, teals, violets and grays with a blue base. They feel fresher and calmer.
- **Neutrals:** white, gray, beige and "greige", the mixture of gray and beige. They work as a background, but they also have an undertone.

An easy way to see the undertone is to place the sample next to a white sheet of paper. When compared, the hidden tone appears.

## The 60-30-10 rule

To make a room feel balanced, many decorators distribute colors in three proportions:

- **60 percent:** the main color, usually the walls.
- **30 percent:** a secondary color on large furniture, curtains or a wall.
- **10 percent:** an accent color on cushions, objects, pictures or a door.

It's not a law, but it helps avoid two common mistakes: the room where everything competes and the room so neutral that it seems unfinished.

## A palette for the whole house

Houses look more organized when colors relate to each other. They don't have to be the same, but they should share something.

1. **Choose a base color** for hallways and common areas, usually a white or soft neutral.
2. **Define two or three main colors** that are repeated in different rooms, with more or less intensity.
3. **Use accents with intention:** a front door, the wall behind the bed, or a painted piece of furniture.
4. **Keep in mind what is not painted:** floors, wood, countertops, exposed brick or stone. A reddish ceramic floor or orange parquet influences everything around it.

In small houses or apartments, using the same base color in all rooms gives a feeling of continuity and spaciousness. We also explain it in the guide to [gain space in a small apartment](/articulos/departamento-pequeno-ganar-espacio/).

## Colors for each environment

| Environment | Ideas that usually work | Recommended finish |
|---|---|---|
| Living room and dining room | warm neutrals, soft greens, terracottas on a wall | matte or satin |
| Bedrooms | muted blues and greens, beiges, earthy pinks | matte |
| Kitchen | whites, creams, sage greens, furniture accents | satin or semi-gloss, washable |
| Bathrooms | white, light blue, green, light gray | satin with anti-mold protection |
| Hallways and entrance | light colors that reflect light | satin, resists friction |
| Roofs | matte white | matte |

Bedrooms usually appreciate calm, unsaturated tones. In the kitchen and bathroom, more than the color, it is important that the paint can be **washed** and resist humidity. In bathrooms without good ventilation, a paint with an anti-mold additive is recommended, although the most important thing is to ventilate, as explained in the guide on [ventilation and mold in the bathroom] (/articulos/ventilacion-y-moho-en-el-bano/).

## Matte, satin or glossy

The finish changes as much as the color:

- **Matte:** does not reflect light, hides wall imperfections and looks elegant. It is less resistant to washing, although today there are washable mattes.
- **Satin or eggshell:** a soft shine, easier to clean. Widely used in living rooms, hallways and children's bedrooms.
- **Semibrillo:** resists humidity and frequent cleaning. Useful in kitchens, bathrooms, doors and frames.
- **Shiny:** Very durable, but shows every imperfection. It is reserved for carpentry and details.

The more shiny it is, the more the irregularities of the wall are noticeable. On walls with imperfect repairs or plastering, matte is more forgiving.

## How to test a color before painting

1. **Buy small samples** instead of gallons.
2. **Paint large pictures,** at least 50 by 50 centimeters, or use painted cardboard that you can move.
3. **Place two hands,** same as on the final wall.
4. **Look at them at different times,** with natural light in the morning and afternoon and with artificial light at night.
5. **Try them on more than one wall,** because the one in the sun and the one in the shade will look different.
6. **Wait at least a day** to decide. The wet color is darker than the dry one.

## The color outside

Latin American facades have a very strong tradition of color, from the towns of Mexico to the hills of Valparaíso or the streets of Cartagena. If you are going to paint the exterior, keep three things in mind:

- **The sun degrades pigments.** Very intense colors lose strength faster. Use paints formulated for exteriors.
- **Dark colors make walls exposed to the sun more hot**. In warm climates, light tones help keep the house cooler.
- **Some historic centers and condominiums regulate the façade colors**. Check local regulations before purchasing paint.

The types of paint for each surface are explained in the [interior and exterior paints] guide (/articulos/pinturas-interior-y-exterior/).

## Frequent errors

- **Choose the color under the light of the store** and not under the light of your home.
- **Decide with a tiny sample.** On a large wall the color looks more intense.
- **Ignore the undertone** of whites, grays and beiges.
- **Paint everything cold white** in a room that faces the poles, which ends up looking gray and sad.
- **Use matte in areas with a lot of friction** without checking that it is washable.
- **Forget floors and wood** when assembling the palette.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do dark colors make a room smaller?

Not always. A well-lit dark color can give depth and a cozy feeling. In small rooms with little natural light, however, light tones reflect more light and make the space feel larger.

### What white do I choose for the entire house?

One that works with your light and your floors. In cold and dimly lit rooms, a white with a warm base. In very sunny spaces, a neutral or slightly cold white. Try two or three on the wall before deciding.

### Can I paint the ceiling in color?

Yes. A ceiling in the same tone as the walls, lighter, creates an enveloping atmosphere. A dark ceiling makes it appear lower, something that can work in very high rooms.

## In summary

The color of a wall depends on the light it receives. Observe the orientation of each room, choose the paint with the artificial light that you will use at night and look at the undertone of each sample. Use the 60-30-10 rule to distribute colors, create a palette that connects the entire house and take into account floors and wood. Prefer matte finishes in bedrooms and living rooms, and satin or semi-gloss washable finishes in the kitchen and bathroom. Before purchasing, paint large samples and look at them for at least a day.
