The bigger pack is not always the better deal, and a pack that shrinks while its price stays the same is easy to miss. The unit price — the price per kilo, per litre or per item — is the fair way to compare. Many shops print it on the shelf label in small type; the comparer above works it out when they do not, or when two shelves use different units.

## How it works

For each pack the comparer divides the price by the amount and scales it to a common unit: per kilo for weights, per litre for volumes, per item for counts. Packs of the same kind are then sorted from cheapest to dearest, and each one shows how much more it costs per unit than the cheapest.

| Example | Price | Size | Unit price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ketchup, small bottle | 3.19 | 14 oz (397 g) | about 8.04 per kg |
| Ketchup, large bottle | 4.99 | 32 oz (907 g) | about 5.50 per kg |

The large bottle costs about 32 % less per kilo here. The NIST unit pricing guide uses the same example to show how unit prices make the comparison quick.

## Units the comparer understands

| Kind | Units | Compared |
|---|---|---|
| Weight | g, kg, oz, lb | per kg |
| Volume | ml, litres, fl oz | per litre |
| Count | items | per item |

A weight and a volume are never compared with each other: a litre of one product is not a kilo of another.

## When the cheapest is not a saving

A lower unit price only saves money if you use the whole pack before it spoils or before you lose interest in it. For fresh food, large packs can mean more waste. For cleaning products and toiletries, concentrates can be cheaper per wash than they look per litre. And a multi-buy is only a deal if you would have bought that many anyway.
