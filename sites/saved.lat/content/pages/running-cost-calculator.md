Every electrical appliance has a power rating in watts (W), printed on its label, rating plate or charger. Your electricity bill shows what you pay for each kilowatt-hour (kWh). With those two numbers and the hours you use the appliance, the cost is simple arithmetic, and the calculator above does it for you.

## The sum

**watts × hours ÷ 1,000 = kWh**, and **kWh × price per kWh = cost**.

A 2,000 W heater used for 3 hours uses 6 kWh. At 0.25 per kWh that is 1.50 a day. Used every day for a 20-week winter, it comes to about 210 — which is why a single heater can matter more than every light in the house.

| Appliance (typical rating) | Hours a day | kWh a day | Cost a day at 0.25 per kWh |
|---|---|---|---|
| LED bulb, 8 W | 5 | 0.04 | 0.01 |
| Laptop charging, 50 W | 6 | 0.3 | 0.08 |
| LED TV, 80 W | 4 | 0.32 | 0.08 |
| Fan heater, 2,000 W | 3 | 6 | 1.50 |
| Tumble dryer, 2,500 W | 1 | 2.5 | 0.63 |

The ratings are typical examples only. Look at your own appliance: two models of the same kind can differ several times over.

## Standby

Many devices draw a little power even when they look off: TVs, games consoles, chargers, set-top boxes. A device drawing 1 W around the clock uses about 8.8 kWh a year. Enter the standby watts if you know them; the calculator counts every hour the device is not in use as standby. A plug-in energy monitor shows the real figure for any device.

## Why the estimate can be high

Appliances with a thermostat or a motor do not draw their full rating all the time. A fridge's compressor switches on and off; a heater cycles once the room is warm; a dryer's heater runs only part of the cycle. For those, the calculator gives an upper estimate. The energy label on large appliances shows an estimated yearly consumption that already allows for this.
