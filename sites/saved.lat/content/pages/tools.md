Our guides explain how to spend less. These four tools do the arithmetic for your own household, with your own numbers. They run entirely in your browser: nothing you type is sent to us, there is no sign-up, and the only thing remembered is your last entry, by your own browser, so you do not have to start again next time.

## Running-cost calculator

Enter an appliance's power in watts, how long you use it and your price per kWh, and see what it costs per day, month and year, including what it uses on standby. It is the quickest way to find out whether the heater, the tumble dryer or the games console is the one worth changing your habits for. [Open the running-cost calculator](/running-cost-calculator/)

## Unit-price comparer

Type the price and size of up to five packs and see the price per kilo, per litre or per item side by side, with the cheapest marked and how much more each of the others costs. It works for weights, volumes and counts, in metric or imperial units. [Open the unit-price comparer](/unit-price-comparer/)

## Budget planner

Start from your take-home pay, list the month's costs as needs, wants or savings, and see what is left. The planner shows the familiar 50/30/20 split next to your own figures as a reference, and a sinking-fund line works out how much to set aside each month for a cost you know is coming. [Open the budget planner](/budget-planner/)

## Debt payoff calculator

Enter a balance, its APR and a monthly payment to see how long the debt takes to clear and how much interest that costs, and how the picture changes if you pay a quarter, a half or twice as much. [Open the debt payoff calculator](/debt-payoff-calculator/)

## Where the numbers come from

The tools use the same plain arithmetic our guides explain, and each page says exactly how it calculates. The running-cost sum is the one the U.S. Department of Energy describes; unit prices follow the NIST unit pricing guide; the budget split and the way card interest works follow the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. The sources are listed on each tool's page. A tool is an estimate, not advice: your bill, your statement and your contract have the final say, and for money worries a free, independent debt-advice service is the right next step.
