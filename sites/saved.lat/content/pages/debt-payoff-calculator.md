Interest is the price of borrowing. On a credit card it is stated as an annual percentage rate (APR), and on a balance that is not paid in full each month it adds up quickly. The calculator above shows how long a balance takes to clear at a fixed monthly payment, how much interest you pay on the way, and how that changes if you pay more.

## How it calculates

Each month the calculator adds one month of interest — the APR divided by 12 — to the balance and takes off your payment, until nothing is left. Many card issuers actually charge interest daily, at a daily periodic rate (the APR divided by 360 or 365), so your statement can differ slightly. The calculator assumes no new spending on the account, no fees and a rate that does not change.

| Balance 3,000 at 24 % APR | Monthly payment | Time to clear | Interest paid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lowest example | 100 | 3 years 11 months | about 1,630 |
| Half as much again | 150 | 2 years 2 months | about 870 |
| Double | 200 | 1 year 7 months | about 600 |

## When the payment does not cover the interest

If your monthly payment is less than one month's interest, the balance never goes down. The calculator says so plainly. Paying only the minimum on a card can keep a debt alive for many years; even a small amount above the minimum shortens it a lot.

## Ways to bring the interest down

- Pay more than the minimum whenever you can, and pay on time to avoid fees and penalty rates.
- If you have several debts, the one with the highest APR costs most for each unit you owe.
- On most cards, paying the full statement balance by the due date avoids interest on purchases altogether.
- Promotional "no interest" offers can be useful, but read how deferred interest works: if the balance is not cleared in time, interest can be charged back to the start.

This is general information, not advice for your situation. If you are struggling with repayments, contact the lender early and speak to a free, independent debt-advice service. Read more in our guide to [understanding interest on debt](/guides/understanding-interest-on-debt/).
