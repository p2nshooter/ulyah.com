Every home produces a steady stream of small jobs: a wobbly chair, a loose cupboard hinge, a picture to hang, a flat-pack bookcase, a dripping tap, a sticking door. Without tools, each job becomes a choice between living with the problem, paying someone, or buying a replacement. With a modest toolkit, most of them take ten minutes and cost almost nothing.

You do not need a garage full of power tools. A dozen well-chosen hand tools, stored together where you can find them, will handle the majority of household repairs. This guide explains what to buy, what each tool is for, and how to avoid spending money on the wrong things.

## How a toolkit saves money

The savings come in several ways:

- **Small repairs instead of replacements.** Tightening a hinge or re-gluing a chair leg extends the life of furniture by years.
- **Fewer call-out charges.** Tradespeople often charge a minimum fee even for tiny jobs; simple tasks are easy to do yourself.
- **Second-hand bargains become usable.** Furniture and bikes that need a little adjustment are often sold cheaply.
- **Maintenance that prevents bigger problems.** Bleeding a radiator or cleaning a washing machine filter avoids costly breakdowns.

## The twelve essential tools

### 1. Claw hammer

For driving and removing nails, light demolition and gentle persuasion of stuck parts. A medium-weight hammer is the most versatile; very heavy hammers are tiring to use for small jobs.

### 2. Screwdriver set

Screws come with different heads. At minimum you need flat-head and cross-head (Phillips and Pozidriv, which look similar but are different) in a few sizes. A set with a handle and interchangeable bits is compact and inexpensive. Using the correct bit type and size prevents "cam-out", where the screwdriver slips and damages the screw head.

### 3. Tape measure

A 5-metre tape with a lock covers almost every household measuring job, from fitting curtains to checking whether a sofa will fit through a door. Measure twice before cutting or buying anything.

### 4. Spirit level

For hanging pictures, shelves and curtain rails straight. A short level of around 40–60 cm is enough for most homes; some tape measures and phone apps include a basic level, but a proper one is more reliable for shelves.

### 5. Adjustable spanner (wrench)

One adjustable spanner fits a range of nuts and bolts on furniture, bikes and plumbing fittings. A medium size is most useful. Tighten it firmly on the nut before turning to avoid rounding the corners.

### 6. Pliers

Combination pliers grip, twist and cut wire; long-nose (needle-nose) pliers reach into tight spaces. Between them they handle countless small tasks, from straightening bent metal to retrieving dropped screws.

### 7. Utility knife

A retractable knife with replaceable blades cuts cardboard, carpet, sealant, plastic and string. Always cut away from your body, retract the blade after use and replace blunt blades — blunt knives slip and cause more injuries.

### 8. Hex keys (Allen keys)

Flat-pack furniture is usually assembled with these, and loose furniture can often be fixed by tightening them. A small set in metric sizes covers most needs (imperial sizes may be needed for some products).

### 9. Cordless drill-driver

The one power tool worth owning. It drills holes for wall plugs and shelves, drives screws far faster than by hand, and makes assembling furniture much easier. A modest cordless model with two batteries is enough for household use; choose a clutch setting to avoid over-tightening screws. If you rarely need one, borrowing from a neighbour or a tool library is a good alternative.

### 10. Junior hacksaw or small handsaw

For cutting small pieces of wood, plastic pipe and metal rod. A junior hacksaw is compact and inexpensive; a small general-purpose handsaw is useful if you do more woodwork.

### 11. Stud and cable detector

Before drilling into a wall, it is important to know what is behind it. A detector helps locate wooden or metal studs and warns of live electrical cables and some pipes. It is not infallible, so also avoid drilling directly above, below or beside sockets and switches, where cables commonly run.

### 12. Torch (flashlight) or head torch

Most repairs involve looking into dark cupboards, under sinks or behind appliances. A head torch keeps both hands free.

## Useful extras

Once you have the essentials, these inexpensive items make many jobs easier:

- **Wall plugs and an assortment of screws**, nails and picture hooks.
- **Wood glue** for loose joints.
- **Sandpaper** in a few grades.
- **Duct tape and electrical insulating tape.**
- **Sealant and a sealant gun** for baths, sinks and gaps.
- **A radiator key** for bleeding radiators.
- **A plunger** for blocked sinks and toilets.
- **Cable ties.**
- **A small set of clamps** for holding glued parts together.
- **A stepladder** — safer than standing on chairs.

## Safety equipment

Safety gear is part of the toolkit, not an optional extra:

- **Safety glasses** whenever drilling, cutting, hammering or working overhead.
- **Work gloves** for handling rough materials and sharp edges.
- **A dust mask** for sanding and drilling into plaster or masonry.
- **Ear protection** for noisy power tools.
- **A first-aid kit** within reach.

Know your limits. Gas work, mains electrical work beyond very simple tasks, structural alterations and anything involving asbestos-containing materials should be left to qualified professionals; in many countries, some of this work is legally restricted.

## Choosing quality without overspending

Cheap tools can be a false economy: screwdrivers with soft tips strip screws, and pliers with loose joints slip. On the other hand, professional-grade tools are unnecessary for occasional household use.

- **Mid-range brands** sold by hardware shops usually offer good value for household use.
- **Feel the tool.** A comfortable grip and solid construction matter more than extra features.
- **Avoid giant "200-piece" kits** full of items you will never use; a smaller set of decent tools is better.
- **Buy second-hand.** Hand tools last for decades; good-quality used hammers, spanners and saws are often available cheaply at car-boot sales, flea markets and online marketplaces.
- **Borrow rarely used tools.** Tile cutters, wallpaper steamers and specialist drills can be borrowed from friends, tool libraries or community sharing schemes, or rented by the day.

## Storing tools so you use them

A toolkit you cannot find is a toolkit you do not use.

- **Keep the essentials in one box or bag** in an accessible cupboard.
- **Return tools after every job.**
- **Keep screws and fixings sorted** in a small organiser or labelled jars.
- **Store tools dry** to prevent rust; a sachet of silica gel in the toolbox helps.
- **Charge the drill batteries** before putting them away.

## Ten jobs the basic toolkit handles

1. Tightening loose cupboard hinges and door handles.
2. Assembling and repairing flat-pack furniture.
3. Hanging pictures, mirrors and shelves securely.
4. Fixing a wobbly table or chair.
5. Bleeding radiators.
6. Replacing a tap washer (with the water supply isolated).
7. Unblocking a sink trap.
8. Fitting draught excluders and door seals.
9. Adjusting bike brakes and saddles.
10. Replacing batteries in toys and smoke alarms that need screwdrivers.

## Building the kit in stages

If buying everything at once is not realistic, spread the cost over a few months and buy in the order you are most likely to need things:

1. **Stage one:** screwdriver set, tape measure, adjustable spanner, pliers and a torch. These handle the most common small fixes — loose handles, wobbly furniture, batteries and simple adjustments.
2. **Stage two:** claw hammer, spirit level, picture hooks, wall plugs and a selection of screws. Now you can hang pictures, mirrors and light shelves.
3. **Stage three:** cordless drill-driver, stud and cable detector, and safety glasses. This opens up heavier shelving, curtain rails and furniture assembly.
4. **Stage four:** saw, utility knife, clamps, glue and sealant, added as real projects come along.

Buying gradually also gives you time to watch for sales and second-hand bargains, and to learn which tools you really use before spending on extras.

## Looking after your tools

Good tools last a lifetime if they are treated reasonably well:

- **Wipe tools clean** after messy jobs, especially after contact with water, glue or sealant.
- **Oil moving parts** such as pliers' joints and adjustable spanner jaws occasionally with a drop of light oil.
- **Remove surface rust** with fine wire wool and a little oil before it spreads.
- **Keep blades sharp or replace them**; a sharp saw or knife is safer and easier to control than a blunt one.
- **Check handles** for cracks or looseness, and replace damaged tools rather than risking injury.
- **Store batteries correctly.** Lithium-ion drill batteries prefer cool, dry storage and should not be left completely flat for long periods; follow the manufacturer's advice and never use a damaged or swollen battery.

## Learning the skills

Tools are only half of the equation; confidence comes with practice. Start with simple jobs, follow manufacturers' instructions, and use reputable how-to resources. Many community centres, libraries and repair cafés run workshops where volunteers teach basic repair skills — a good way to learn alongside others.

## The payoff

A basic toolkit is one of the few household purchases that genuinely pays for itself. The first loose hinge you tighten, the first shelf you put up and the first chair you save from the tip begin to repay the cost. Buy the twelve essentials gradually if you need to, choose sensible mid-range quality, store them together, and add extras as real jobs demand them. Before long, you will wonder how you ever managed without them.
