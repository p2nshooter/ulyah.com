Water streaming down the windows on a cold morning, black spots creeping across a bathroom ceiling, a musty smell in a bedroom cupboard: damp is one of the most common problems in homes, and one of the most frustrating. It damages paint, plaster, furniture and clothes, it makes rooms feel colder and harder to heat, and mould can affect the health of the people living there. The good news is that much of the damp in homes comes from condensation, which can usually be reduced with simple, low-cost habits.

This guide explains where condensation comes from, how to tell it apart from other types of damp that need a builder, what you can do every day to keep moisture down, when a dehumidifier makes sense, how to clean small areas of mould safely and what to do if you rent your home.

## Where condensation comes from

Air always holds some water vapour. Warm air can hold more than cold air. When warm, moist air touches a cold surface, such as a window, an outside wall or the back of a wardrobe, it cools and releases water as droplets. That is condensation.

Everyday life produces a surprising amount of moisture:

- **cooking**, especially boiling and steaming;
- **showers and baths**;
- **drying clothes indoors**;
- **breathing**, particularly in bedrooms overnight;
- **kettles, unvented tumble dryers and gas heaters without flues**.

Condensation is worst in cold weather, in rooms that are poorly heated or ventilated, and on the coldest surfaces in the home.

## Three types of damp

It is important to know which type of damp you have, because the solutions are very different.

| Type | What causes it | Typical signs | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Condensation | moisture from daily life meeting cold surfaces | wet windows, mould in corners, behind furniture and on ceilings, worse in winter | ventilation, heating, everyday habits |
| Rising damp | water from the ground moving up through walls | a tide mark low on ground-floor walls, crumbling plaster, salts | a qualified professional |
| Penetrating damp | water coming in from outside through leaks or defects | damp patches that grow after rain, often near gutters, roofs or window frames | find and fix the leak |

Many homes have condensation and nothing else. But if damp appears after rain, sits low on ground-floor walls or keeps growing despite good habits, ask a qualified surveyor or builder to look at it. Fixing a leaking gutter or a cracked roof tile early is far cheaper than repairing the damage later.

## Measure the humidity

A cheap **hygrometer**, a small device that shows relative humidity, takes the guesswork out of damp. Many guidelines suggest keeping indoor relative humidity roughly between 40 and 60 per cent. Readings that stay above that range for long periods make condensation and mould much more likely. Put the hygrometer in the room where damp is worst and check it at different times of day.

## Everyday habits that keep moisture down

### In the kitchen

- **Put lids on pans**, which also saves energy, as our guide to [energy-efficient cooking](/guides/energy-efficient-cooking/) explains.
- **Use the extractor fan** while cooking and for a while afterwards.
- **Close the kitchen door** when cooking, so the moisture does not spread through the home.

### In the bathroom

- **Run the extractor fan** during and after showers and baths, or open the window.
- **Close the bathroom door** while you shower.
- **Wipe down** tiles, the shower screen and the windows with a squeegee or cloth.
- **Keep the bath or shower area clean**, because soap residue feeds mould.

### Drying clothes

Drying a load of washing indoors releases a lot of water into the air.

- **Dry clothes outdoors** whenever the weather allows.
- **If you must dry indoors**, use one room with the door closed and a window slightly open or a dehumidifier running.
- **Avoid drying clothes on radiators**, which makes the heating work harder and fills the room with moisture.
- **Vent tumble dryers** to the outside, or use a condenser or heat-pump model and empty the water tank.

Our guide to [doing laundry for less](/guides/laundry-costs/) covers the cost side of drying.

### Ventilation

- **Open windows briefly**, for ten or fifteen minutes, rather than leaving them slightly open all day in winter. This exchanges moist air for drier air without cooling the walls too much.
- **Keep trickle vents open** in modern windows if you have them.
- **Do not block air bricks or vents** in walls.
- **Leave a small gap** between large furniture and outside walls so that air can circulate.
- **Avoid overfilling wardrobes and cupboards** on outside walls.

### Heating

Cold surfaces attract condensation. Keeping the home at a steady, moderate temperature, rather than letting it become very cold and then heating it quickly, reduces condensation. Draught-proofing helps keep surfaces warmer, but must never block the ventilation a home needs. See our guides to [draught-proofing](/guides/draught-proofing-your-home/) and [staying warm for less](/guides/lower-heating-bills-in-winter/).

### Morning routine

Wipe condensation off windows and sills first thing in the morning with a cloth or a window vacuum, and wring the cloth out in the sink rather than drying it on a radiator.

## Is a dehumidifier worth it?

A dehumidifier pulls water out of the air and collects it in a tank. It can make a real difference in homes with persistent condensation, especially when clothes are dried indoors. Before buying one:

- **Check the running cost.** Look at its power rating and estimate how many hours a day you would use it, then multiply by your electricity price, as explained in our guide to [reading your energy bill](/guides/reading-your-energy-bill/).
- **Choose the right type.** Compressor models work well in warmer rooms; desiccant models often perform better in cold spaces such as unheated rooms.
- **Look for a humidistat**, which switches the machine off when the air reaches a set humidity, saving energy.
- **Consider buying second-hand** if it is in good condition, following our guide to [buying second-hand safely](/guides/buying-secondhand-safely/).

A dehumidifier treats the symptom; good habits and ventilation tackle the cause. The best results usually come from both.

## Dealing with mould safely

Small patches of mould caused by condensation can often be cleaned by the household. Larger areas, mould that keeps returning quickly, or mould caused by leaks or rising damp need a professional.

To clean a small area:

1. **Protect yourself.** Wear rubber gloves, eye protection and a mask, and open a window.
2. **Do not brush mould dry.** Brushing releases spores into the air.
3. **Use a mould cleaner** suitable for the surface, following the instructions on the label exactly. Never mix cleaning products, especially those containing bleach and ammonia or acids, because they can release dangerous gases.
4. **Wipe with a damp cloth**, then dry the area thoroughly.
5. **Throw away the cloth** in a sealed bag.
6. **Tackle the cause**, or the mould will return.

Soft materials such as clothes, curtains and mattresses with mould may need professional cleaning or replacing. Mould-resistant paints can help in bathrooms and kitchens once the surface is clean and dry, but they are not a cure for a moisture problem.

### Health

Damp and mould can affect anyone, but babies, older people and people with asthma, allergies or other health conditions are especially vulnerable. If you or someone in your household has symptoms that may be linked to damp, such as breathing problems, speak to a doctor.

## If you rent

- **Report damp and mould to your landlord in writing**, with photographs and dates.
- **Keep copies** of all messages.
- **Follow good ventilation and heating habits**, because the landlord may ask what has been done.
- **Find out your rights.** In many countries, landlords are responsible for repairing leaks, rising damp and problems with the building, and there may be local rules on housing standards. A tenants' advice service or local authority can explain the rules where you live.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do my windows run with water every morning?

Overnight, moisture from breathing builds up in bedrooms and condenses on the cold glass. Open the window briefly in the morning, keep the bedroom door open during the day, and wipe the glass.

### Do salt crystal or chemical moisture traps work?

They absorb a small amount of moisture in enclosed spaces such as wardrobes, but they cannot dry a whole room.

### Does double glazing stop condensation?

It reduces condensation on the glass because the inner pane stays warmer, but moisture can still condense elsewhere, such as on cold walls, if ventilation is poor.

## The bottom line

Most damp in homes is condensation: moisture from cooking, washing, drying clothes and breathing meeting cold surfaces. Measure the humidity, use lids and extractor fans, dry clothes outdoors or in a ventilated room, open windows briefly, leave space behind furniture and keep the home steadily warm. A dehumidifier can help, especially with a humidistat. Clean small patches of mould with protection and the right product, never mixing chemicals, and call a professional for large areas or for damp caused by leaks or rising damp. If you rent, report problems in writing and find out your rights.
