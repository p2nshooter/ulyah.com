Freshly painted walls change how a room feels more than almost any other job, and they are one of the most affordable improvements a household can make itself. A professional decorator brings skill and speed, but filling a few holes and repainting a bedroom are well within reach of most people with a free weekend and a little patience. The difference between a finish that looks professional and one that looks patchy usually comes down to preparation, not talent.

This guide covers how to repair common wall damage, from nail holes to hairline cracks and small dents, how to prepare surfaces so paint sticks and looks smooth, how to choose paint and estimate how much to buy, the tools that are worth having, a step-by-step method for painting a room, and the jobs that are better left to a professional.

## Before you start: safety and checks

- **Check for hidden hazards.** Before drilling, filling deep holes or sanding, think about what may be inside or behind the wall, such as electrical cables and pipes. Use a detector if you are unsure, and avoid areas directly above, below and beside sockets and switches.
- **Old paint may contain lead.** In older homes, some layers of paint may contain lead, which is harmful when sanded or scraped into dust. If your home is old and you plan heavy sanding or stripping, check your local guidance and consider testing first.
- **Ventilate the room** while filling, painting and drying.
- **Protect your eyes and lungs** when sanding, with safety glasses and a dust mask.
- **If you rent, ask first.** Many tenancy agreements require permission before repainting or making repairs.

## Repairing common damage

### Nail and screw holes

1. Remove any loose plaster or paint around the hole.
2. Press a small amount of **ready-mixed filler** into the hole with a filling knife, slightly overfilling it.
3. Scrape off the excess so it is nearly flush with the wall.
4. Let it dry completely, following the time on the tub.
5. Sand gently with fine sandpaper until smooth, and wipe off the dust.

Small holes often need only one application. Deeper holes may shrink as they dry and need a second thin layer.

### Hairline cracks

Fine cracks often appear where walls meet ceilings or around door frames, as buildings move slightly with temperature and humidity.

1. Use the corner of a filling knife to open the crack slightly, so the filler has something to grip.
2. Brush out the dust.
3. Fill with a **flexible filler** or decorator's caulk, which copes better with small movements.
4. Smooth it with a damp finger or a cloth, and let it dry before painting.

Large cracks, cracks that grow quickly, cracks wider than a coin's edge or diagonal cracks above doors and windows may indicate structural movement. These should be checked by a qualified surveyor or builder before any cosmetic repair.

### Dents and gouges

For larger dents, apply filler in thin layers, letting each dry before adding the next. Thick single layers can crack or sag. Sand smooth once the final layer is dry.

### Bigger holes in plasterboard

Holes larger than a few centimetres in plasterboard, for example from a door handle, usually need a **repair patch** or a piece of plasterboard fixed behind the hole before filling. Kits are widely available and come with instructions. If the damage is extensive, a professional may give a better result.

## Preparing the walls

Good preparation makes paint go on more easily, last longer and look smoother.

1. **Clear the room** or move furniture to the centre and cover it.
2. **Protect the floor** with dust sheets. Old bed sheets work, but fabric dust sheets absorb drips better than thin plastic.
3. **Remove fittings** such as picture hooks and, where safe and permitted, switch plates and socket covers. Only remove electrical covers if you know how to do it safely with the power off; otherwise mask around them.
4. **Wash the walls** with a mild detergent solution, especially in kitchens and hallways, where grease and fingerprints stop paint from sticking. Rinse and let them dry.
5. **Fill and sand** all holes and cracks.
6. **Mask edges** with decorator's tape where needed: skirting boards, window frames and ceilings.
7. **Prime** bare plaster, filled areas or stains with a suitable primer or a mist coat, as recommended by the paint manufacturer. Filler patches can show through the topcoat as dull spots if they are not primed.

Stains from water or smoke may bleed through new paint. Use a stain-blocking primer, and fix the cause of any water stain first; our guide to [damp, condensation and mould](/guides/damp-and-condensation/) explains how to tell where moisture is coming from.

## Choosing paint

### Finish

| Finish | Look | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Matt | flat, hides imperfections | ceilings, bedrooms, living rooms |
| Eggshell or satin | soft sheen, easier to wipe | hallways, children's rooms |
| Silk | more sheen, shows imperfections | areas that need frequent cleaning |
| Kitchen and bathroom paint | resists moisture and mould | kitchens, bathrooms |

### Quality

Cheaper paints often need more coats to cover well, which can make them more expensive in practice. Mid-range paints frequently give the best balance between price and coverage. Compare the **coverage** printed on the tin, usually given in square metres per litre, not just the price per tin. Our guide to [unit pricing](/guides/unit-pricing-at-the-supermarket/) uses the same idea for groceries.

### How much to buy

1. Measure the **height and width** of each wall and multiply them to get the area.
2. Add the walls together.
3. **Subtract** large windows and doors.
4. **Multiply by the number of coats** you expect to need, usually two.
5. **Divide by the coverage** on the tin.

Buy a little extra for touch-ups, and keep the leftover paint, labelled with the room and the date, for future repairs.

## Tools worth having

- **A good-quality roller** and a tray, with a medium-pile sleeve for most walls.
- **An extension pole** for ceilings and high walls, which saves a lot of ladder climbing.
- **A cutting-in brush**, angled, for edges and corners.
- **Filling knives**, filler and fine sandpaper.
- **Decorator's tape**, dust sheets and a stepladder.
- **A bucket, sponge and cloths** for washing walls and cleaning up.

Good brushes and rollers last for years if cleaned properly. Consider borrowing or buying second-hand ladders and poles; our guide to [the basic home toolkit](/guides/basic-home-toolkit/) lists the tools that earn their keep.

## Painting a room step by step

1. **Start with the ceiling**, if you are painting it, so drips do not land on finished walls.
2. **Cut in** around the edges of each wall with a brush: along the ceiling, corners, skirting boards and around windows and doors.
3. **Roll the main areas** while the cut-in edges are still wet, to avoid visible lines. Work in sections, rolling in a W or M pattern and then filling in, without pressing too hard.
4. **Keep a wet edge**: finish one wall before taking a break, so that drying lines do not appear.
5. **Let the first coat dry** fully, as stated on the tin.
6. **Apply the second coat** in the same way.
7. **Remove masking tape** while the final coat is still slightly soft, pulling it slowly at an angle.
8. **Paint woodwork last**, such as skirting boards and doors, usually with a different paint.

### Avoiding common problems

- **Patchy finish:** usually caused by too little paint on the roller or uneven drying; apply a second coat evenly.
- **Visible roller marks:** use a better roller sleeve and do not overwork drying paint.
- **Drips:** load less paint and check for runs as you go.
- **Paint bleeding under tape:** press the tape edges firmly and do not overload the brush near them.

## Cleaning up and storing

- **Water-based paints** clean up with water and a little washing-up liquid.
- **Wrap rollers and brushes** tightly in plastic between coats on the same day to stop them drying out.
- **Store leftover paint** with the lid firmly closed, in a place that will not freeze.
- **Dispose of old paint responsibly**, following local rules; many areas do not allow liquid paint in household waste.

## When to call a professional

- large or growing cracks, or signs of structural movement;
- damp patches that keep returning;
- suspected lead paint that needs removing;
- high stairwells or ceilings that need scaffolding;
- extensive replastering.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I really need two coats?

Usually yes, especially when changing colour or painting over filler. One coat often looks fine wet but patchy when dry.

### Can I paint over wallpaper?

Sometimes, if it is firmly attached, but any loose seams may lift. Removing it is usually better in the long run.

### How long should I wait between coats?

Follow the time on the tin, which depends on the paint, the temperature and the humidity.

## The bottom line

Patching and painting walls yourself is one of the most rewarding ways to refresh a home for little money. Fill nail holes and small cracks carefully, using flexible filler for hairline cracks, and have large or growing cracks checked by a professional. Prepare thoroughly: clear the room, protect the floor, wash the walls, fill, sand, mask and prime. Choose paint by its finish and coverage, not just its price, and estimate how much you need from the wall area. Cut in first, roll while the edges are wet, apply two coats, and clean and store your tools so they are ready for the next job.
