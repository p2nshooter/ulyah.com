Life has a way of producing expenses nobody planned for: a car that will not start, a washing machine that floods the kitchen, a sudden drop in income, an urgent trip to see a relative, or a vet bill for a much-loved pet. Without savings to fall back on, these moments often end up on credit cards, overdrafts or high-cost loans — turning a single bad week into months of repayments.

An **emergency fund** is money set aside specifically for these situations. It is one of the most important foundations of a stable household budget, and it can be built gradually, even on a modest income. This guide explains what an emergency fund is for, how much to aim for, where to keep it and how to get started.

This article provides general information, not personal financial advice. If you are struggling with debt, a free and independent debt advice service can help you decide on priorities.

## What an emergency fund is for

An emergency fund covers costs that are:

- **Unexpected** — you did not know they were coming.
- **Necessary** — they cannot reasonably be avoided or delayed.
- **Urgent** — they need to be paid soon.

Typical examples include:

- Loss of income through redundancy, illness or reduced hours.
- Urgent home repairs, such as a broken boiler in winter or a leaking roof.
- Essential car repairs if you depend on the car for work.
- Replacing an essential appliance, such as a fridge or cooker.
- Unexpected medical, dental or veterinary costs.
- Emergency travel for family reasons.

It is not for planned costs like holidays, birthdays, Christmas or annual insurance renewals. Those belong in **sinking funds**, which are explained in our separate guide. Keeping the two separate stops your emergency fund being spent on predictable things.

## Why it matters

- **It avoids expensive borrowing.** Paying for emergencies with cash saves interest and fees.
- **It reduces stress.** Knowing you have a cushion makes unexpected events less frightening.
- **It protects your budget.** A single surprise does not derail months of careful planning.
- **It gives you choices.** If you lose your job, savings give you time to find the right next role rather than taking the first offer out of necessity.

## How much should you save?

There is no single right figure. A common rule of thumb is to aim for **three to six months of essential expenses** — not three to six months of income, but the costs you would need to cover if your income stopped: housing, utilities, food, transport, insurance and minimum debt payments.

Your own target depends on your circumstances. You may want a larger fund if:

- your income is irregular, seasonal or self-employed;
- you are the only earner in the household;
- you have dependants;
- your job is in an industry with frequent redundancies;
- you own an older car or home that may need repairs;
- you have health conditions that could affect your ability to work.

You may be comfortable with a smaller fund if:

- there are two or more stable incomes in the household;
- you have good sick pay or income protection insurance;
- your essential costs are low.

### Start with a smaller goal

Three to six months of expenses can feel overwhelming. Break it into stages:

1. **Starter fund:** a small amount that covers a minor emergency, such as a car repair or a broken appliance. Many people aim for an amount roughly equivalent to a few weeks of essential spending, or a round figure that feels achievable.
2. **One month of essential expenses.**
3. **Three months of essential expenses.**
4. **Your full target.**

Each stage provides real protection, and reaching the first milestone builds momentum.

## Working out your target

1. **List your essential monthly costs**: rent or mortgage, utilities, council or property tax, groceries, transport, insurance, phone and broadband, childcare and minimum debt payments.
2. **Add them up** to find your monthly essential spending.
3. **Multiply by the number of months** you want to cover.

For example, if your essential costs are 1,800 a month, a three-month fund would be 5,400 and a six-month fund 10,800. These figures are only illustrations; use your own numbers.

## Where to keep your emergency fund

An emergency fund should be:

- **Safe.** Keep it with a regulated bank or building society. In many countries, deposits up to a certain limit are protected by a government-backed deposit guarantee scheme; check the rules where you live.
- **Accessible.** You need to reach it quickly, ideally within a day or two.
- **Separate from your everyday account**, so you do not spend it without noticing.
- **Earning some interest**, if possible, so inflation does not erode it as quickly.

An **easy-access savings account** is a common choice. Some people keep a small part in their current account for immediate needs and the rest in savings.

Emergency funds are generally not suited to investments such as shares, because their value can fall just when you need the money. Fixed-term accounts and accounts with long notice periods may also be unsuitable for the whole fund, though some people keep part of a larger fund in a short notice account for a slightly better rate.

## How to build your fund

### 1. Make it automatic

Set up a standing order or automatic transfer to your emergency savings on payday. Treat it as a bill. Even a small amount each month adds up over time.

### 2. Find money in your budget

- **Review subscriptions** and cancel those you rarely use (see our subscription audit guide).
- **Compare bills** at renewal time: energy, insurance, phone and broadband.
- **Plan meals** and reduce food waste.
- **Set a limit** for flexible spending, such as eating out.

Direct the savings straight into your emergency fund before they are absorbed by other spending.

### 3. Use windfalls

Tax refunds, bonuses, gifts, cashback and money from selling unwanted items can give your fund a significant boost. Consider saving all or part of any unexpected money.

### 4. Round up

Some banks offer round-up features that move spare change from each card payment into savings. The amounts are small but painless.

### 5. Save pay rises

When your income rises, save part or all of the increase before you get used to spending it.

## Emergency fund or paying off debt?

If you have debts, it can feel wrong to save while you owe money. But without any savings, a single emergency can push you further into debt. Many people choose to:

1. **Build a small starter emergency fund** first.
2. **Focus on paying down high-interest debt**, while continuing to make at least the minimum payments on all debts.
3. **Build the full emergency fund** once high-interest debts are under control.

The right balance depends on your situation, the interest rates on your debts and your job security. A free debt advice service can help you decide.

## Using your emergency fund

When an emergency happens:

1. **Ask whether it is a genuine emergency** — unexpected, necessary and urgent.
2. **Check other options**: insurance, warranties, consumer rights or employer support may cover some costs.
3. **Use the fund without guilt.** That is what it is for.
4. **Make a plan to rebuild it**, by restarting or temporarily increasing your monthly contributions.

## Keeping it on track

- **Review your target yearly**, or when your circumstances change, such as moving home, having a child or changing jobs.
- **Increase it as costs rise**, since essential expenses grow over time.
- **Keep it separate and clearly labelled**, for example as "Emergency only".
- **Avoid dipping in** for non-emergencies. If you are tempted, a sinking fund for that purpose may be the answer.

## Insurance and other safety nets

An emergency fund works alongside other forms of protection, not instead of them:

- **Home and contents insurance** can cover major damage, theft and some accidents.
- **Income protection or sick pay** may replace part of your income if you cannot work through illness. Check what your employer provides.
- **Car breakdown cover** can be cheaper than paying for a single tow.
- **Pet insurance** may help with large vet bills, though it has exclusions and excesses.
- **Warranties and consumer rights** may cover faulty goods (see our guide to warranties and returns).

Insurance excesses — the amount you pay towards each claim — are a good use of your emergency fund. Knowing that you can afford the excess also lets you choose a policy with a higher excess and a lower premium, if that suits you.

## Talking about emergency savings in a household

If you share finances, agree together on what counts as an emergency, how much the fund should hold, and who can use it. Clear rules prevent disagreements and stop the fund being drained by non-urgent spending. Some couples keep one joint emergency fund for household costs and small personal buffers of their own.

## When you cannot save yet

If your income barely covers essentials, saving may not be possible right now. In that case:

- **Check you are receiving all the support you are entitled to**, such as benefits, discounts and social tariffs for energy, water and broadband.
- **Contact a free debt advice service** if you are behind on bills.
- **Talk to your creditors** early if you are struggling to pay; many have hardship arrangements.
- **Start with tiny amounts** when you can. Even a very small buffer can prevent bank charges.

## A quick action plan

1. Calculate your essential monthly costs.
2. Choose a starter target and a full target.
3. Open a separate easy-access savings account.
4. Set up an automatic transfer on payday.
5. Redirect savings from bills and subscriptions into the fund.
6. Add windfalls and pay rises.
7. Use it only for genuine emergencies, and rebuild it afterwards.

## The bottom line

An emergency fund is a financial safety net that turns crises into inconveniences. Start with a small, achievable goal, save automatically, keep the money safe and accessible in a separate account, and build towards several months of essential expenses over time. When the unexpected happens — as it eventually will — you will be ready.
