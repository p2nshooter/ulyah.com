Every time edible food goes in the bin, the money spent on it goes with it — along with the energy used to grow, transport, store and cook it. Waste-reduction organisations in many countries have found that households are responsible for a large share of all food that is wasted, and that much of what we throw away could have been eaten. The encouraging part is that most household waste comes from a handful of predictable habits, and each of them has a simple fix.

This guide looks at where food waste actually happens, then sets out practical ways to store food better, use leftovers well and build routines that keep good food on plates instead of in bins.

## Where household food waste comes from

It helps to know the main causes, because each one needs a different solution.

1. **Buying more than we use.** Large packs, multi-buy offers and shopping without a plan all lead to more food than the week requires.
2. **Poor storage.** Fruit and vegetables stored in the wrong place spoil days earlier than they need to.
3. **Forgetting what we have.** Food hidden at the back of the fridge or the bottom of the freezer is easily forgotten until it is past saving.
4. **Cooking too much.** Generous portions are kind, but rice, pasta and potatoes are among the foods most commonly thrown away after cooking.
5. **Misreading date labels.** Food is sometimes thrown away while still perfectly good because "best before" is confused with "use by".
6. **Not liking the leftovers.** Plain leftovers that are not reinvented often sit untouched until they spoil.

You do not need to fix everything at once. Start with whichever of these feels most familiar.

## Storage: making fresh food last

Correct storage can add days, sometimes weeks, to the life of fresh food. The general principles are simple: keep perishable food cold, keep certain foods apart, and control moisture.

### Your fridge

- **Keep it cold enough.** Many food-safety agencies recommend a fridge temperature of around 5°C or below. An inexpensive fridge thermometer tells you whether your appliance is really achieving this.
- **Do not overfill it.** Cold air needs to circulate. A packed fridge has warm spots.
- **Use the zones.** Ready-to-eat foods and leftovers go on the upper shelves, raw meat and fish in sealed containers on the bottom shelf where they cannot drip, and vegetables in the salad drawer.
- **Store leftovers promptly.** Cool cooked food quickly — dividing it into smaller, shallow containers helps — and refrigerate it within the time recommended by your national food-safety guidance, typically within a couple of hours.

### Fruit and vegetables

Not everything belongs in the fridge, and some produce should be kept apart.

| Food | Best place | Tip |
|---|---|---|
| Leafy herbs (parsley, coriander) | Fridge, stems in a glass of water | Cover loosely with a bag |
| Lettuce and salad leaves | Fridge, salad drawer | A dry paper towel absorbs moisture |
| Carrots, celery | Fridge | Stand celery in water to revive it |
| Apples | Fridge or cool place | Keep apart from other produce; they release ethylene |
| Bananas | Room temperature | Separate from other fruit to slow ripening of the rest |
| Tomatoes | Room temperature until ripe | Refrigerate only if very ripe and you need more time |
| Potatoes | Cool, dark, dry place | Not next to onions |
| Onions, garlic | Cool, dry, ventilated place | Away from potatoes |
| Berries | Fridge | Wash just before eating, not before storing |
| Bread | Cool place or freezer | The fridge makes bread go stale faster |

**Ethylene** is a natural gas given off by some fruits, such as apples, bananas and pears, as they ripen. It speeds up ripening in nearby produce. Keeping high-ethylene fruits apart from sensitive vegetables like leafy greens and broccoli helps both last longer.

### Reviving tired produce

Slightly limp vegetables are not finished:

- Soak limp carrots, celery or lettuce in cold water for half an hour to crisp them up.
- Roast soft peppers, courgettes and tomatoes into a sauce or soup.
- Blend overripe bananas into smoothies, or mash them into pancakes or banana bread.
- Use wrinkled apples and pears in crumbles, compotes or porridge.

## The freezer: your waste-prevention tool

The freezer pauses the clock on food. Almost anything you are not going to eat in time can be frozen, provided it is done before the food starts to spoil.

Freeze:

- **Bread**, sliced, to toast from frozen.
- **Ripe bananas**, peeled and chopped, for smoothies and baking.
- **Grated cheese**, which can go straight onto pasta bakes.
- **Herbs**, chopped and frozen in a little oil in ice-cube trays.
- **Leftover sauces, soups, stews and curries**, in labelled portions.
- **Cooked rice**, cooled quickly and frozen promptly — follow your local food-safety advice on cooling and reheating rice, which needs particular care.
- **Milk**, which can be frozen and defrosted in the fridge (shake well once thawed).

Two habits make the freezer work: **label everything** with what it is and the date, and **keep a simple list** of what is inside on the freezer door. Food you cannot identify in three months is food that will be thrown away.

## The "use first" shelf

One of the most effective habits is also one of the simplest. Choose one shelf or one clear box in the fridge and label it "use first". Everything that needs eating soon goes there: the half-tin of coconut milk, the last two sausages, the open yoghurt, the leftover pasta.

Before cooking or snacking, everyone in the household checks that shelf first. It turns forgotten food into the first choice instead of the last, and it makes leftovers visible rather than hidden behind newer purchases.

## Understanding date labels

Misunderstood date labels cause a large amount of unnecessary waste. In many countries:

- **Use by** relates to safety. It appears on highly perishable foods such as fresh meat, fish and some dairy. Do not eat food after this date, even if it looks and smells fine. You can, however, freeze many such foods before the date, which stops the clock.
- **Best before** relates to quality. After this date, food may be past its best in taste or texture but is often still safe to eat if it has been stored correctly and the packaging is undamaged. Dry goods, tins and frozen foods commonly carry best-before dates.
- **Display until** or **sell by** dates are for shop staff and are not a guide for consumers.

Rules vary between countries, so check your national food-safety agency's guidance. Eggs, for example, have specific advice in some places. When in doubt about a use-by food, throw it away; when a best-before food is a little past its date, check it carefully before deciding.

## Cooking the right amount

Cooking too much is one of the biggest sources of waste, especially with staples.

- **Measure rice and pasta.** A common guide for adults is around 75 grams of dried rice or 80 to 100 grams of dried pasta per portion, but appetites vary. Measuring for a few weeks shows you what your household really eats.
- **Plan for leftovers deliberately.** Cooking extra is efficient if you intend to use it. Cooking extra by accident is how food ends up in the bin.
- **Serve smaller portions first.** People can always take seconds. Food left on plates is almost always thrown away.

## Making leftovers people want to eat

Plain leftovers are easy to ignore. Transformed leftovers get eaten. A few reliable transformations:

- **Rice** becomes egg fried rice, rice pudding or stuffed peppers.
- **Pasta** becomes a frittata or a baked pasta with a little cheese on top.
- **Roast vegetables** become soup, a pasta sauce or a grain salad.
- **Roast meat** becomes sandwiches, a curry, a pie filling or tacos.
- **Mashed potato** becomes potato cakes or the topping for a pie.
- **Stale bread** becomes breadcrumbs, croutons, bread pudding or French toast.
- **Odds and ends of vegetables** become a stir-fry, a soup or a vegetable stock.

A "leftovers night" once a week, where the fridge is cleared and turned into a meal, is a good way to catch everything else.

## Shopping to reduce waste

Waste starts in the shop. Habits that help:

- **Check the fridge before shopping** and take a photo of its contents if that helps you remember.
- **Shop with a list** based on planned meals.
- **Buy loose fruit and vegetables** where possible, so you can buy exactly the quantity you need.
- **Think twice about multi-buy offers on perishables.** A deal is only a saving if you eat it all.
- **Consider "wonky" or reduced produce** if you will use it promptly — it is often perfectly good and sold more cheaply.

## Composting what you cannot eat

Some waste is unavoidable: peelings, cores, eggshells, coffee grounds and tea leaves. Rather than sending these to landfill, compost them if you can. Many local councils offer food-waste collections or discounted compost bins. Composting reduces the greenhouse gases produced when food rots in landfill and gives gardeners a free soil improver.

## Measuring your progress

If you want to see the difference, keep a simple waste diary for two weeks. Each time you throw away food, write down what it was and why. Patterns appear quickly — perhaps it is always bagged salad, or always bread, or always leftovers from a particular meal. Fixing your top two or three items often removes most of your waste.

## The bottom line

Reducing food waste does not require dramatic lifestyle changes. Store fresh food in the right place, use the freezer before food spoils, create a "use first" shelf, understand date labels, cook realistic amounts and turn leftovers into new meals. Each habit on its own makes a modest difference. Together, they keep a surprising amount of money in your pocket — and a surprising amount of good food out of the bin.
