Most advice about saving energy at home focuses on heating, lighting and laundry. Cooking gets less attention, yet in many households it accounts for a noticeable share of the electricity or gas bill, especially when the oven is used every day. The good news is that cooking efficiently rarely means eating differently. It usually means choosing the right appliance for the job, using it well and planning a little.

This guide explains how to work out what your cooking appliances cost to run, when each one is the most efficient choice, simple habits that reduce energy use on the hob and in the oven, and how to decide whether a new appliance such as a slow cooker or air fryer would really save money in your kitchen.

## How to work out what an appliance costs to run

You do not need a meter or an expert to estimate running costs. For an electric appliance:

1. **Find its power rating** in watts (W) or kilowatts (kW). It is usually printed on a label underneath or on the back, or in the manual. 1,000 W equals 1 kW.
2. **Estimate how long it runs** at full power, in hours. Many appliances, such as ovens and slow cookers, switch on and off to hold their temperature, so they draw full power only part of the time.
3. **Multiply** the power in kW by the hours to get kilowatt-hours (kWh).
4. **Multiply the kWh by your electricity price** per kWh, which you will find on your bill. Our guide to [reading your energy bill](/guides/reading-your-energy-bill/) explains where to look.

For example, an appliance rated at 2 kW running for half an hour at full power uses about 1 kWh. Multiply that by your own unit price and you have the cost.

For gas hobs and ovens, the calculation is harder at home, but the same principle applies: the less time a burner or oven runs, and the less heat is wasted, the less you pay.

## The big principle: heat only what you need

Most cooking waste comes from heating more than the food: a whole oven for a single potato, a full kettle for one cup, a large pan of water for a handful of pasta. Matching the appliance and the quantity to what you are cooking is the single most effective habit.

## Appliance by appliance

### The oven

The oven is often the most energy-hungry cooking appliance, because it heats a large insulated box of air before it heats the food. It makes sense when:

- you are cooking **large quantities**, such as a roast or several trays at once;
- you can **fill it**: bake bread, roast vegetables and cook a casserole in one session;
- the dish genuinely needs an oven, such as many baked goods.

To use it more efficiently:

- **Batch cook.** Plan oven sessions so several dishes cook together. Our guide to [batch cooking and freezing](/guides/batch-cooking-and-freezing/) shows how.
- **Use a fan oven setting** if you have one; many recipes suggest a lower temperature for fan ovens because they circulate heat more efficiently.
- **Only preheat when needed.** Cakes and bread usually need a hot oven from the start; casseroles and long roasts often do not.
- **Keep the door closed.** Every time you open it, heat escapes and the oven works to recover.
- **Use residual heat.** Switch off a few minutes before the end of long cooking times; the oven stays hot.
- **Choose the right dishes.** Glass and ceramic dishes hold heat well; dark metal trays heat up quickly.

### The hob

- **Put a lid on the pan.** Water boils faster and simmers with less energy.
- **Match the pan to the ring.** A small pan on a large burner wastes heat around the sides.
- **Use only as much water as you need** for vegetables, pasta and rice.
- **Turn down once boiling.** A gentle simmer cooks just as well as a rolling boil.
- **Cut food into smaller pieces** so it cooks faster.
- **Use one pot or a steamer stack** to cook several things over one burner.
- **Keep burners and rings clean**; burnt-on residue reduces efficiency on some hobs.

Induction hobs heat the pan directly and are generally more efficient than older electric hobs, but replacing a working hob purely to save energy rarely pays off quickly.

### The kettle

Boiling water in an electric kettle is usually quicker and more efficient than heating it in a pan on most hobs. Boil only the amount you need, and descale the kettle regularly in hard-water areas, because limescale makes it work harder.

### The microwave

For reheating, defrosting small amounts, cooking vegetables and making single portions, a microwave is often the most efficient option because it heats the food rather than a large space, and it runs for a short time. It is less suited to browning, crisping or large quantities.

### The slow cooker

A slow cooker runs for many hours but at a low power, so its total energy use for a stew or casserole can be modest compared with keeping an oven on for hours. It suits:

- cheaper cuts of meat that need long, gentle cooking;
- beans, lentils and soups;
- households that like a meal ready at the end of the day.

### The pressure cooker

Pressure cookers, whether stovetop or electric, cook food much faster than normal boiling or stewing because the pressure raises the boiling point of water. Shorter cooking times mean less energy, especially for dried beans, pulses, tough cuts of meat and stocks. Always follow the manufacturer's safety instructions.

### The air fryer

An air fryer is essentially a small fan oven. For small quantities, such as a portion of chips, vegetables or reheated leftovers, it can use less energy than heating a full-size oven, because it is smaller and heats up quickly. For large batches, you may need several rounds, and the full oven may be just as efficient.

### Toasters and sandwich makers

For toast, a toaster is usually more efficient than the grill, which heats a large area and wastes much of it.

## Comparing the options

| Task | Often the most efficient choice |
|---|---|
| Boiling water | electric kettle, only the amount needed |
| Reheating a single portion | microwave |
| Cooking vegetables | microwave or steamer over one pan with a lid |
| Dried beans and tough cuts | pressure cooker, or slow cooker |
| Stews and casseroles | slow cooker or pressure cooker |
| Small portions of crispy food | air fryer |
| Large roasts and batch baking | oven, filled with several dishes |
| Toast | toaster |

This is a general guide. The real answer depends on your appliances, your energy prices and how much you cook at once.

## Should you buy a new appliance?

Before buying a slow cooker, pressure cooker or air fryer to save energy, ask:

1. **How often would I really use it?** An appliance that sits in the cupboard saves nothing.
2. **What would it replace?** If it replaces daily oven use for small meals, the saving may be real. If it replaces the microwave, probably not.
3. **How much does it cost compared with the saving?** Estimate the energy saved per use, multiply by the number of uses per year and compare with the price. Our guide to [cost per use](/guides/cost-per-use/) explains the idea.
4. **Could I buy it second-hand?** Small appliances are often available second-hand in good condition; check safety, cables and plugs. Our guide to [buying second-hand safely](/guides/buying-secondhand-safely/) explains what to look for.

## Planning: the hidden energy saver

The most efficient kitchens plan ahead:

- **Plan the week's meals** so oven days are grouped together. Our guide to [meal planning a week of dinners](/guides/how-to-meal-plan-a-week-of-dinners/) helps.
- **Defrost food in the fridge overnight** instead of using the microwave or hot water.
- **Cook double** and freeze or refrigerate the second portion for another day.
- **Let hot food cool** before putting it in the fridge, but do not leave it out longer than food safety guidance allows.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it cheaper to cook with gas or electricity?

It depends on the prices of gas and electricity where you live and on your appliances. Compare the unit prices on your bill, and remember that efficient habits matter more than the fuel.

### Does a microwave use a lot of electricity?

It draws a high power, but for a short time. For small portions and reheating, it is usually one of the most efficient options.

### Is it worth turning off the oven early?

Yes, for long cooking times. The oven stays hot for several minutes after switching off, and the food keeps cooking.

## The bottom line

Cooking efficiently means heating only what you need, for as short a time as possible. Work out what your appliances cost to run from their power rating, the time they run and your unit price. Use the oven for big batches and fill it when you do; on the hob, use lids, the right-sized pan and only as much water as needed; choose the kettle, microwave, pressure cooker, slow cooker or air fryer when they suit the task better. Before buying a new appliance, check how often you would use it and whether the saving would cover its price, and remember that planning meals and cooking in batches saves energy as well as time.
