Batch cooking is the practice of making several meals' worth of food in one session and storing the extra portions for later. It is one of the most reliable ways to spend less on food, because it tackles the moment when good intentions usually fail: the evening when you are too tired to cook and a takeaway seems like the only option. With a stocked freezer, the alternative is a home-cooked meal that only needs reheating.

Many people try batch cooking once, spend an entire Sunday in the kitchen, and never do it again. The method below is designed to be sustainable: shorter sessions, a small set of reliable recipes, and a simple system for storing and using what you make.

## Why batch cooking saves money

The savings come from several directions at once:

- **Bulk buying makes sense.** A large pack of mince or a big bag of onions is usually cheaper per kilogram, and batch cooking means you can use it all.
- **Less energy per portion.** Cooking one large pot of stew uses far less energy per serving than cooking the same dish four separate times.
- **Fewer takeaways.** A freezer full of your own meals competes directly with delivery apps on convenience.
- **Less waste.** Ingredients are used while fresh, and portions are frozen before they can spoil.
- **Predictable lunches.** Portions for work or school replace bought sandwiches and meal deals.

## What freezes well — and what does not

Before planning a session, it helps to know which dishes survive the freezer.

| Freezes well | Freezes less well |
|---|---|
| Soups, especially blended ones | Dishes based on raw salad vegetables |
| Stews, casseroles and curries | Boiled eggs (whites turn rubbery) |
| Chilli and bolognese-style sauces | Sauces thickened only with cream or yoghurt, which can split |
| Lasagne and pasta bakes | Fried foods meant to be crisp |
| Cooked beans and lentils | Raw potatoes; boiled potatoes in chunks can turn watery |
| Meatballs, burgers, patties | Cucumber, lettuce, watercress |
| Pancakes, muffins and bread | Custard and mayonnaise-based dishes |
| Cooked grains (frozen promptly) | Jelly and gelatine desserts |

Mashed potato freezes reasonably well when it contains some butter or milk, which is why it works as a pie topping. Cream-based sauces can sometimes be rescued after thawing by whisking them gently while reheating, but it is safer to add cream after defrosting.

## The equipment you need

You do not need special gadgets. The essentials are:

- **One or two large pans** — a big saucepan or stockpot and a large roasting tin or casserole dish.
- **Freezer-safe containers** in a few sizes, ideally rectangular so they stack efficiently. Reused takeaway containers work if they are freezer-safe.
- **Freezer bags** for soups and sauces; frozen flat, they take up far less space.
- **A marker pen and labels** or masking tape.
- **A large baking tray** for freezing individual items (meatballs, cookie dough, chopped fruit) before bagging them, so they do not freeze in a lump.

## Planning a session

A good batch-cooking session produces two or three different dishes, not six. Choose recipes that:

1. **Share ingredients**, so one shopping trip covers everything. Onions, garlic, carrots, celery, tinned tomatoes and pulses appear in many dishes.
2. **Use different cooking methods**, so you are not waiting for one hob ring. A stew can simmer while a tray bake roasts and a soup cooks on another ring.
3. **Suit your household.** There is no point freezing a dish nobody really enjoys.

A typical beginner's combination:

- A large pot of **vegetable and lentil soup** (lunches)
- A big batch of **bolognese-style sauce** (pasta, lasagne, stuffed peppers, baked potatoes)
- A tray of **chicken or chickpea curry** (dinners with rice)

These three share onions, garlic, carrots and tinned tomatoes, and between them they can produce twelve to twenty portions.

## A two-hour session, step by step

1. **Start with the longest-cooking dish.** Chop the onions and garlic for all three recipes at once, then get the bolognese sauce simmering, since it improves with long, gentle cooking.
2. **Prepare the second dish while the first cooks.** Start the curry base in another pan or in the oven.
3. **Make the quickest dish last.** Lentil soup needs about thirty minutes once the vegetables are softened.
4. **Wash up as you go.** A clean kitchen at the end makes you far more likely to repeat the session.
5. **Cool everything quickly.** Divide food into shallow containers to speed cooling. Many food-safety agencies advise getting cooked food into the fridge or freezer within about two hours.
6. **Portion, label and freeze.** Write the dish, the number of portions and the date on every container.

## Freezing safely

Safe freezing is mostly about speed, cleanliness and temperature.

- **Cool food quickly before freezing.** Large pots of hot food cool slowly and spend too long in the temperature range where bacteria multiply. Spreading food into shallow containers or standing the pot in a sink of cold water helps.
- **Keep the freezer at about -18°C or colder**, the temperature commonly recommended for home freezers.
- **Leave a little space at the top** of containers, because liquids expand as they freeze.
- **Squeeze air out of bags.** Air contact causes freezer burn — dry, discoloured patches that are safe but unappetising.
- **Freeze in meal-sized portions.** It is much easier to defrost two portions than to chisel them off a large block.

## Labelling and rotation

The freezer is where good intentions go to be forgotten unless you label and rotate.

- **Label every item** with the contents, the date and the number of portions.
- **Keep an inventory** on a sheet stuck to the freezer door or in a note on your phone. Cross items off as you use them.
- **Use the oldest food first.** Put new portions at the back or bottom.
- **Aim to eat most dishes within about three months.** Frozen food stays safe for long periods at the right temperature, but quality — flavour and texture — declines over time. Your national food-safety agency may give specific guidance for different foods.

## Defrosting and reheating safely

How you defrost matters as much as how you freeze.

- **The safest method is in the fridge**, usually overnight. Place containers on a plate to catch drips.
- **Microwave defrosting** is fine if you cook or reheat the food immediately afterwards.
- **Avoid defrosting at room temperature**, especially for meat, fish and rice dishes.
- **Reheat until steaming hot all the way through.** Stir soups and stews during reheating to avoid cold spots.
- **Reheat only once.** Defrost and reheat the portion you need rather than reheating a large batch repeatedly.

Cooked rice deserves particular care. It can carry bacterial spores that survive cooking, so it should be cooled quickly, refrigerated or frozen promptly, and reheated thoroughly. Many cooks prefer to cook rice fresh, which takes little time, and freeze only the sauces.

## Building a rolling freezer stock

The most sustainable approach is not one enormous session but a rolling stock. Each time you cook a dish that freezes well during the week, simply make double and freeze half. Over a few weeks you build up a variety of dishes without ever spending a whole day cooking.

A healthy freezer stock for a household of two to four might hold:

- Four to six portions of soup for lunches
- Two or three family-sized dinners (a lasagne, a stew, a curry)
- A bag of homemade meatballs or burgers
- Cooked beans or lentils in recipe-sized portions
- A loaf of sliced bread and a bag of frozen vegetables

With this in place, you always have a home-cooked option, even in the busiest week.

## Batch cooking components, not just meals

Some cooks prefer to batch-prepare components rather than complete dishes, giving more flexibility at mealtimes:

- **A base sauce**, such as a plain tomato and onion sauce, which can become pasta sauce, a pizza topping, shakshuka or curry.
- **Cooked pulses**, ready to add to salads, stews and wraps.
- **Browned mince or shredded cooked chicken**, ready to become tacos, pies or pasta dishes.
- **Chopped onions, peppers and herbs**, frozen flat in bags so you can break off what you need.

Components work particularly well for households where people like different meals, since the same base can be finished in several ways.

## Making it a habit

Batch cooking only saves money if it becomes routine. A few tips that help it stick:

- **Pick a regular slot**, even if it is only every other weekend.
- **Keep a short list of "freezer favourites"** — five or six recipes your household loves — and rotate them.
- **Cook with someone else.** Sessions go faster and feel less like a chore.
- **Start small.** Doubling one dinner a week is a perfectly good beginning.
- **Eat from the freezer on purpose.** Plan one or two freezer meals into each week so the stock keeps turning over.

## The payoff

A modest freezer stock changes how a household eats. The tired evening, the unexpected late meeting and the week before payday all become easier, because there is always a decent, inexpensive meal waiting. Start with two or three recipes that share ingredients, freeze them safely in labelled portions, and keep a simple inventory. Within a month you will wonder how you managed without it.
