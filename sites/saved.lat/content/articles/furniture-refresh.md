Good furniture is expensive to buy new, yet charity shops, online marketplaces, garage sales and family attics are full of solid pieces that only need some attention. A scratched table, a wobbly chair or a dated chest of drawers can often be transformed with cleaning, simple repairs and a careful finish. Refreshing furniture saves money, keeps usable pieces out of landfill and gives you something that suits your home exactly.

This guide walks through the process from assessing a piece to the final finish. It focuses on simple, low-cost techniques suitable for beginners.

## Why refresh rather than replace?

- **Solid wood lasts.** Many older pieces are made from solid wood or quality veneers that outlast cheaper modern furniture.
- **Second-hand prices are low.** Well-made pieces are often available for a fraction of the cost of new equivalents.
- **You choose the look.** Paint colours, finishes and handles let you match your décor.
- **It reduces waste.** Furniture is bulky and often difficult to recycle, so keeping it in use is worthwhile.

## Step one: assess the piece

Before buying or starting work, check:

- **Structure.** Is it sturdy? Push gently on corners and tops. Loose joints can usually be fixed; broken or split structural parts are more work.
- **Material.** Is it solid wood, veneer (a thin layer of wood over another material), laminate or chipboard? Each needs different treatment. Look at edges, the underside and the backs of drawers.
- **Drawers and doors.** Do they open and close smoothly? Are runners and hinges intact?
- **Smell.** Strong musty smells can be hard to remove; smoke odours may need repeated cleaning.
- **Pests.** Look for small round holes and fine powdery dust, which can indicate woodworm. Avoid pieces with active infestation unless you are prepared to treat them, and keep them away from other wooden furniture.
- **Upholstery.** Stained or worn fabric can be cleaned or replaced, but check frames and springs for damage.

### Fire safety for upholstered furniture

In some countries, upholstered furniture must meet fire-safety standards and carry a label. When buying second-hand upholstered pieces, check for any required labels; in some places, charities and shops are not allowed to sell unlabelled upholstered furniture. Older foam may not meet modern standards.

### Old paint

Paint on very old furniture may contain lead. Avoid sanding or burning off old paint unless you know it is lead-free; lead test kits are available in many areas, and official guidance explains how to deal with it safely. When in doubt, seal over the old paint or choose a different piece.

## Step two: deep cleaning

Many pieces look dramatically better after a thorough clean.

1. **Vacuum dust** from corners, drawers and crevices.
2. **Wash wooden surfaces** with a cloth dampened in warm water with a few drops of washing-up liquid. Avoid soaking wood; wring the cloth out well and dry the surface immediately.
3. **Remove sticky residue** and old polish build-up with a little white spirit or a specialist wood cleaner on a cloth, testing in a hidden spot first and working in a ventilated area.
4. **Clean hardware** separately. Handles and knobs can be removed and soaked in warm soapy water; brass can be polished with a suitable cleaner.
5. **Air out drawers.** For musty smells, leave drawers open in a well-ventilated space, wipe the insides, and place an open bowl of bicarbonate of soda or a sachet of activated charcoal inside for a few days. Lining paper or a coat of shellac inside drawers can help seal persistent odours.

## Step three: structural repairs

### Wobbly chairs and tables

Wobbles usually come from loose joints or uneven legs.

- **Tighten screws and bolts** where they exist. Many tables and chairs have corner brackets underneath that can loosen.
- **Re-glue loose joints.** For wooden chairs, a loose joint may need to be carefully separated, the old glue scraped away, and fresh wood glue applied before clamping the joint while it dries. Follow the glue manufacturer's drying times.
- **Level uneven legs** with adjustable felt pads or glides, or very carefully trim a long leg after measuring.

### Drawers that stick

- **Rub candle wax or soap** along wooden runners to make drawers glide.
- **Check for loose drawer bottoms** or joints, and re-glue or add small screws.
- **Replace broken metal runners** with matching new ones from a hardware shop.

### Loose hinges and doors

- **Tighten hinge screws.** If the screw holes are worn, fill them with wooden matchsticks or toothpicks dipped in wood glue, trim them flush once dry, and screw back into the new wood.
- **Adjust cabinet hinges.** Many modern concealed hinges have adjustment screws for aligning doors.

## Step four: surface repairs

### Scratches and scuffs on wood

- **Light scratches** often disappear with furniture polish or a wax finish, or with a specialist scratch-cover product in a matching colour.
- **A walnut trick:** rubbing the meat of a walnut into light scratches on some wooden finishes can darken them so they blend in. Test first.
- **Deeper scratches** can be filled with wax filler sticks in matching colours.

### White water rings

White marks from wet glasses are usually moisture trapped in the finish rather than damage to the wood. Gently warming the area with a hairdryer on a low setting, or carefully using an iron on low heat over a clean cotton cloth (no steam), can sometimes draw the moisture out. Test carefully and stop if the finish changes.

### Dents

Small dents in bare or oil-finished solid wood can sometimes be raised by placing a damp cloth over them and briefly applying a hot iron, which swells the compressed fibres. This does not work well on veneer or heavily finished surfaces.

### Damaged veneer

Lifting veneer can often be re-glued: slide wood glue underneath with a thin blade, press it flat, wipe off excess and clamp or weight it while it dries. Missing pieces can be patched with matching veneer.

## Step five: choosing a finish

### Keeping the wood

If the wood is attractive, a gentle approach often works best:

- **Clean and wax** for a soft sheen.
- **Re-oil** oiled furniture, such as many teak and oak pieces, with an appropriate furniture oil.
- **Sand and refinish** for more damaged surfaces. Sanding back to bare wood is a bigger job, and veneers are thin, so sand very carefully or avoid sanding them at all.

### Painting

Paint is a popular way to transform dated or damaged furniture.

1. **Clean thoroughly** and let the piece dry.
2. **Lightly sand** glossy surfaces to help the paint grip, or use a suitable primer designed for difficult surfaces such as laminate.
3. **Remove or mask hardware.**
4. **Prime** where needed, especially on bare wood, laminate or dark wood that might bleed through light paint.
5. **Apply thin coats** of furniture or multi-surface paint, allowing each to dry fully. Several thin coats look better and last longer than one thick coat.
6. **Seal** with a clear topcoat if the paint requires it, especially on tabletops and other heavily used surfaces.

Chalk-style paints are popular for their matt finish and ease of use, while hard-wearing furniture enamels suit kitchen chairs and tables. Always work in a ventilated space and follow the product instructions.

### Safety when sanding and painting

- **Wear a dust mask** and safety glasses when sanding.
- **Work in a well-ventilated area**, especially with oil-based products or solvents.
- **Dispose of oily rags safely.** Rags soaked in some oils, such as linseed-based finishes, can self-heat and catch fire. Lay them flat outside to dry or store them in a sealed metal container with water, following the product label.

## Step six: new hardware

Replacing handles and knobs is one of the quickest, cheapest ways to update furniture.

- **Measure the distance between screw holes** on existing handles so new ones fit without drilling.
- **Change the style** to match your home — simple wooden knobs, brass handles or matt black pulls.
- **Fill old holes** with wood filler if you choose different-sized handles, then sand and paint.

## Refreshing upholstery

- **Clean fabric** according to its care code, often found on a label underneath. Many fabrics can be spot-cleaned; test any cleaner in a hidden area first.
- **Re-cover drop-in chair seats**, a simple beginner project: unscrew the seat, remove the old fabric, wrap new fabric over the padding, staple it underneath, and screw the seat back in.
- **Use throws and cushions** to update sofas that are structurally sound but worn.
- **Replace flattened cushion foam** where the covers are in good condition.

## Where to find pieces

- Charity and second-hand furniture shops.
- Online marketplaces and community sharing groups.
- Garage sales, car-boot sales and flea markets.
- Family members downsizing.
- Reuse centres run by local authorities or charities.

Measure your space before you go, bring a tape measure, and plan how you will transport larger pieces.

## The bottom line

Refreshing furniture is a satisfying way to furnish a home for less. Check structure and materials first, clean thoroughly, fix wobbles, sticky drawers and loose hinges, then choose a finish that suits the piece — wax, oil or paint — and update it with new hardware. With patience and basic tools, tired furniture can become something you are proud to use for years to come.
