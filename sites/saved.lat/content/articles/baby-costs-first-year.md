Preparing for a new baby is exciting, and also a little overwhelming. Shops, apps and social media present long lists of products that every new parent supposedly needs, from special gadgets to entire nurseries in matching colours. Many of these items are used for a few weeks, or not at all. At the same time, a new baby often arrives at a moment when household income may drop for a while because of parental leave. Spending wisely in the first year makes a real difference.

This guide separates the genuine essentials from the nice-to-haves, explains what can safely be bought second-hand and what should be bought new, looks at the biggest ongoing costs such as nappies and feeding, offers tips for clothes and gifts, and suggests how to plan the household budget for the first year. For anything to do with your baby's health, feeding or development, the right people to ask are your midwife, health visitor, nurse or doctor.

## Start with the true essentials

Babies need less than marketing suggests. Most families find that the first weeks require:

- **a safe place to sleep**, such as a cot, crib or Moses basket that meets current safety standards, with a firm, flat, well-fitting mattress;
- **a car seat**, if you will travel by car, suitable for a newborn and fitted correctly;
- **a way to carry or move the baby**, such as a pram, pushchair or carrier;
- **clothes** in small sizes, for a few days at a time;
- **nappies and changing supplies**;
- **feeding supplies**, depending on how you feed your baby;
- **a few blankets and muslin cloths**;
- **basic bath and care items**.

Everything else can be added later, if and when you find you need it. Follow your country's official safe sleep advice when choosing bedding and setting up the baby's sleeping space.

## What can wait or be skipped

Many families find they used these items little or not at all:

- matching nursery furniture sets;
- special baby bath tubs, when a sink or a regular bath with care can work;
- wipe warmers, bottle gadgets and single-use novelty items;
- large quantities of newborn-size clothes, which are quickly outgrown;
- many toys, which babies do not need in the first months;
- expensive changing tables, when a changing mat on a safe, low surface or the floor can work.

Wait a few weeks, see how your baby and your household settle, and then buy only what you really need.

## Second-hand: what is fine and what to avoid

Buying second-hand can save a great deal, and many baby items are used only briefly. Our guide to [buying second-hand safely](/guides/buying-secondhand-safely/) covers the basics. For baby items in particular:

| Usually fine second-hand | Buy new or check very carefully |
|---|---|
| Clothes, in good condition and washed | Car seats, unless you know their full history; they may have hidden damage from an accident and have an expiry date |
| Prams and pushchairs, checked for brakes, folding and wear | Cot mattresses; a new, firm, well-fitting mattress is generally recommended |
| Cots, if they meet current safety standards, are complete and undamaged, with all instructions | Breast pumps intended for single users, unless designed for multiple users |
| Baby carriers, checked for wear and with instructions | Products that have been recalled; check national product safety websites |
| Toys and books, cleaned and checked for small parts | Anything with missing parts, cracks or broken mechanisms |

Always check the model against **recall lists** before buying second-hand.

## Nappies

Nappies are one of the largest ongoing costs of the first years.

- **Compare unit prices** per nappy rather than per pack; our guide to [unit pricing](/guides/unit-pricing-at-the-supermarket/) explains how.
- **Try store brands**, which many families find work as well as big brands.
- **Do not stock up on one size** too early; babies grow quickly.
- **Consider reusable nappies.** They cost more at the start, but can work out cheaper over time, especially if used for more than one child. Factor in the cost of washing and drying. Some areas offer schemes or discounts for reusable nappies.

## Feeding

How you feed your baby is a personal and medical decision; your midwife, health visitor or doctor can help you with it.

- If you **breastfeed**, the main costs may be comfortable clothing, a few accessories and possibly a pump, which you may be able to borrow or rent.
- If you use **formula**, follow the preparation instructions exactly and ask a health professional which type is suitable. Formula brands often cost very different amounts while meeting the same legal standards for composition in many countries; ask your health professional about cheaper options.
- When your baby starts on **solid food**, usually at the age your health professional advises, homemade purées and family food mashed or cut appropriately can cost much less than ready-made baby food. ## Clothes

- **Buy a few items in each size**, rather than many newborn items.
- **Choose simple, practical clothes** that are easy to wash and change.
- **Accept hand-me-downs** from family and friends.
- **Use clothing swaps** and second-hand shops.
- **Buy the next size up** in sales, if you know you will need it.

## Gifts and lists

Friends and family often want to help. You can guide them:

- create a **simple list** of things you actually need, including larger items people could share;
- suggest **vouchers** for nappies or groceries;
- welcome **practical help**, such as meals, laundry or babysitting, which can be more valuable than another outfit;
- **keep receipts** and duplicates for exchange.

## Planning the household budget

The first year often brings both new costs and changes in income.

1. **Estimate new costs**: nappies, feeding, clothes, any childcare, extra laundry and energy.
2. **Check your income during leave**, including any statutory or employer pay, and when it changes.
3. **Find out what support exists**: in many countries there are benefits, tax credits or allowances for families with young children. Check official government sources.
4. **Build a small buffer** before the birth, if possible. Our guide to [emergency fund basics](/guides/emergency-fund-basics/) explains how.
5. **Review regular bills and subscriptions**; our guide to [the subscription audit](/guides/subscription-audit/) helps you cut what you no longer use.
6. **Plan for childcare** if you are returning to work, including waiting lists and costs.

Our guide to [building a simple household budget](/guides/how-to-build-a-simple-budget/) gives you a framework.

## Childcare: plan early

For many families, childcare becomes the largest cost of all once parents return to work, and it is easy to underestimate.

- **Start researching early**, ideally during pregnancy. Popular nurseries and childminders can have waiting lists.
- **Compare types of care**: nurseries, registered childminders, nannies, nanny-shares and care by relatives each have different costs and flexibility.
- **Ask what is included**: meals, nappies, outings and extra hours may or may not be in the basic fee.
- **Check for help with costs.** Many countries offer free hours, tax relief, vouchers or subsidies for childcare, often with eligibility rules and deadlines. Official government websites explain what is available.
- **Think about working patterns**: flexible hours, part-time work or different days for each parent can reduce the number of paid hours needed.
- **Build the cost into your budget** before the return to work, so it is not a shock when the first invoice arrives.

## Looking after yourselves

New parents often spend money in moments of exhaustion: late-night online shopping, takeaway meals, gadgets that promise better sleep. Being aware of this helps. Batch-cooked meals in the freezer, a few healthy snacks in the cupboard and a rule of waiting a day before any non-essential purchase can save money and stress. ## Frequently asked questions

### How much should we spend before the baby arrives?

Only on the essentials. Many things can be bought or borrowed later, once you know what your baby and your household really need.

### Is it safe to use a second-hand cot?

It can be, if it meets current safety standards, is complete and undamaged, comes with instructions and has not been recalled. Use a new mattress that fits properly.

### Are expensive baby products safer?

Not necessarily. Products that meet the required safety standards protect your baby; price and brand do not guarantee extra safety.

## The bottom line

A new baby needs less than marketing suggests: a safe place to sleep, a correctly fitted car seat if you drive, a way to move the baby, a few clothes, nappies, feeding supplies and basic care items. Buy clothes, prams, cots and toys second-hand where they are safe and not recalled, but be very careful with car seats and buy a new cot mattress. Compare nappy prices or consider reusables, ask health professionals about feeding, guide friends and family towards useful gifts and plan the household budget around both new costs and changes in income. For health and feeding questions, always ask your midwife, health visitor or doctor.
