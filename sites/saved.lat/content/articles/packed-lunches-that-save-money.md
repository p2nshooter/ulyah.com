Buying lunch every day is one of those costs that hides in plain sight. A sandwich here, a coffee and a snack there, a meal deal on the way back to the office: each purchase feels small, but over a working week, a month or a school year, they add up to a surprisingly large sum. Packed lunches are one of the most reliable ways to bring that number down, and they often mean eating better too. The trouble is that many people start with enthusiasm on a Monday and give up by Thursday, because preparing a lunch every morning feels like one more chore.

This guide sets out a simple system for packed lunches that actually lasts: how to work out what you are spending now, a basic formula for a filling lunch, how to prepare in batches, how to keep food safe, what kit is worth buying and how to keep the menu interesting for adults and children alike.

## Start by knowing what you spend now

Before changing anything, track your lunch spending for two weeks. Write down every bought lunch, drink and snack eaten during the working or school day. Then work out:

- the **average cost per day**;
- the **total for a month**;
- an **estimate for a year**, allowing for holidays and days off.

Most people are surprised by the yearly figure. That number becomes your motivation, and it also gives you a realistic target: even replacing three bought lunches a week with packed ones makes a real difference. Our guide to [tracking your spending for a month](/guides/tracking-spending-for-a-month/) explains how to do this without it becoming a burden.

## The packed lunch formula

A lunch that keeps you going through the afternoon usually has four parts:

| Part | Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| A base | energy | bread, wraps, rice, pasta, couscous, potatoes, oats |
| Protein | fullness | eggs, beans, lentils, chickpeas, cheese, tinned fish, leftover meat, tofu |
| Vegetables or fruit | fibre, freshness, colour | salad leaves, carrots, cucumber, peppers, apples, bananas, frozen berries |
| Something extra | flavour and satisfaction | a dressing, pickles, hummus, a handful of nuts, a homemade biscuit |

If every lunch has something from each column, it will be filling and balanced without much thought. Our guide to [affordable protein](/guides/cheap-protein-sources/) has more ideas for the most expensive part of most meals.

## Prepare in batches, not every morning

The secret to sticking with packed lunches is to do most of the work once or twice a week, not every day.

### On a weekend or evening session

- **Cook a big batch of a grain**: rice, pasta, barley or couscous, enough for several lunches.
- **Roast a tray of vegetables**: they keep well in the fridge for a few days and work in salads, wraps and grain bowls.
- **Boil a few eggs**, keeping them in their shells until needed.
- **Make a dressing or sauce** in a jar.
- **Wash and chop salad vegetables** and store them in a container lined with a paper towel.
- **Portion snacks** such as nuts, crackers or homemade flapjack into small boxes.

### Each evening, five minutes

Assemble the next day's lunch from what is already prepared and put it in the fridge. In the morning, you simply take it with you.

### Cook once, eat twice

The easiest packed lunch of all is last night's dinner. When you cook in the evening, make one or two extra portions and pack them straight away. Soups, stews, curries, pasta bakes and chilli all work well, hot from a flask or reheated at work. Our guide to [batch cooking and freezing](/guides/batch-cooking-and-freezing/) shows how to build a small stock of frozen lunch portions too.

## Keeping food safe

Food that sits in a bag for several hours needs some care, especially in warm weather.

- **Keep cold foods cold.** Use an insulated lunch bag with an ice pack, or freeze a bottle of water or a carton of drink to act as one. Put the lunch in a fridge at work or school if there is one.
- **Keep hot foods hot.** A vacuum flask keeps soup or stew hot for hours if you fill it with boiling water first, empty it and then add the hot food.
- **Cool cooked food quickly** before refrigerating it, and use leftovers within the time recommended by your local food safety guidance.
- **Reheat properly.** If you reheat food at work, make sure it is steaming hot all the way through.
- **Wash containers every day** with hot soapy water.
- **Be careful with high-risk foods** such as rice, cooked meat, fish and dairy: keep them cold and do not leave them out for long.

If you are unsure how long something keeps, follow the advice of your national food safety authority.

## Kit worth having

You do not need much, but a few good items make the habit easier:

- **Leak-proof containers** in a few sizes, ideally ones that can go in the microwave and the dishwasher.
- **An insulated lunch bag** and one or two **ice packs**.
- **A vacuum flask** for soups and hot meals.
- **A reusable water bottle**, which also saves the cost of bought drinks.
- **A small jar** for dressings, so salads do not go soggy.
- **Reusable cutlery** kept in your bag or at your desk.

Buy them once and look after them; good containers last for years. If you are trying to judge whether something is worth the price, our guide to [cost per use](/guides/cost-per-use/) is a handy tool.

## Ideas that do not get boring

Rotating a few formats keeps lunches interesting:

- **Grain bowls:** a base of rice or couscous, roasted vegetables, a protein and a dressing.
- **Wraps and sandwiches:** with different fillings each day; keep wet ingredients separate until lunchtime.
- **Big salads:** with beans, lentils or eggs to make them filling.
- **Soup in a flask:** with bread or crackers on the side.
- **Leftovers:** last night's curry, pasta or stew.
- **Picnic boxes:** crackers, cheese, vegetable sticks, hummus, fruit and a boiled egg.
- **Breakfast for lunch:** overnight oats or yoghurt with fruit, for lighter days.

Keep a list of lunches the household enjoys on the fridge. When you plan your weekly shop, choose three or four from the list.

## Packed lunches for children

Children's lunches have their own challenges: they need to be appealing, quick to eat and often comply with school rules.

- **Check the school's policy** on nuts, sweets and drinks.
- **Involve children in choosing and packing** their lunches; they are more likely to eat food they helped prepare.
- **Offer small portions of several things** rather than one large item.
- **Cut fruit and vegetables into easy shapes**, and add a little lemon juice to apple slices to stop them browning.
- **Keep it simple on busy days**: a sandwich, a piece of fruit, a yoghurt and some vegetable sticks is a perfectly good lunch.

Our guide to [cutting back-to-school costs](/guides/back-to-school-costs/) covers the other expenses of the school year.

## Drinks and snacks: the hidden costs

Often the biggest saving is not the lunch itself but the drinks and snacks around it:

- **Take water** in a reusable bottle.
- **Make coffee or tea at home** and carry it in a travel mug.
- **Pack your own snacks**: fruit, a handful of nuts, homemade biscuits or crackers.
- **Keep a small emergency stash** at work, such as oatcakes or a tin of soup, for days when you forget your lunch.

## Making it stick

- **Start with three days a week**, not five. Build up when it feels easy.
- **Shop for lunches as part of your weekly plan**, so the ingredients are always there. Our guide to [meal planning a week of dinners](/guides/how-to-meal-plan-a-week-of-dinners/) shows how to plan lunches at the same time.
- **Allow a treat.** Buying lunch with colleagues once a week is fine if the rest of the week is packed.
- **Track the saving.** Seeing the money you have kept is the best motivation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a packed lunch always cheaper?

Almost always, as long as the ingredients are used and not wasted. Lunches made from leftovers or batch-cooked staples are usually the cheapest of all.

### How long can a packed lunch stay out of the fridge?

It depends on the food and the temperature. Keep perishable food cold with an ice pack, use a fridge where possible and follow your national food safety advice.

### What if there is no microwave at work?

Use a vacuum flask for hot meals, or choose lunches that are good cold, such as salads, wraps and grain bowls.

You will find more ideas for eating well on less in the [Kitchen & Groceries](/topics/kitchen-and-groceries/) section.

## The bottom line

Packed lunches save money when they become a simple routine rather than a daily chore. Track what you spend now, use a basic formula of a base, a protein, vegetables or fruit and something extra, prepare components once or twice a week, and turn dinner leftovers into lunches. Keep food safe with insulated bags, ice packs and flasks, invest in a few good containers and rotate a handful of formats so lunches stay interesting. Start with three days a week, include drinks and snacks in the plan, and watch the saving grow.
