Lighting is one of the easiest places in the home to save energy permanently. LED bulbs use a fraction of the electricity of old incandescent and halogen bulbs for the same amount of light, and they last many times longer. If your home still has older bulbs, replacing them — starting with the lights you use most — is a straightforward improvement that pays for itself.

The difficulty for many people is the shopping itself. Bulb packaging is full of unfamiliar numbers: lumens, kelvins, colour rendering, fittings and dimmer compatibility. This guide explains what those numbers mean so you can buy the right bulb the first time.

## Why LEDs save money

Old incandescent bulbs produce light by heating a filament until it glows, which wastes most of their energy as heat. Halogen bulbs are a slightly more efficient variant of the same idea. LEDs (light-emitting diodes) produce light electronically and convert far more of their energy into light.

The practical result:

- **Lower running costs.** An LED producing the same brightness as an old incandescent bulb typically uses a small fraction of the electricity — often around a sixth to a tenth, depending on the bulbs compared.
- **Longer life.** Good-quality LEDs are often rated for many thousands of hours, far longer than incandescent or halogen bulbs, so you replace them much less often.
- **Less heat.** LEDs produce much less waste heat, which is useful in summer and safer around fabrics.

Many countries have phased out or restricted the sale of most incandescent and halogen bulbs for this reason, so LEDs are now the default choice in many shops.

## Lumens, not watts

The biggest change in buying LEDs is learning to judge brightness by **lumens** instead of watts.

- **Watts** measure how much electricity a bulb uses.
- **Lumens** measure how much light it gives out.

With old bulbs, wattage was a rough guide to brightness because all bulbs were similarly inefficient. With LEDs, a low-wattage bulb can be very bright, so lumens are the number to look at. Approximate equivalents commonly given for standard bulb shapes are:

| Old incandescent | Approximate light output | Typical LED wattage |
|---|---|---|
| 25 W | about 220–250 lumens | about 3–4 W |
| 40 W | about 400–470 lumens | about 4–6 W |
| 60 W | about 700–810 lumens | about 6–9 W |
| 75 W | about 1,000–1,100 lumens | about 9–12 W |
| 100 W | about 1,400–1,600 lumens | about 13–17 W |

These are approximate. Check the lumens figure on the packaging and match it to the brightness you want.

## Colour temperature: warm or cool light

LEDs come in different shades of white, described by **colour temperature** in kelvin (K). Confusingly, lower numbers look warmer.

- **Warm white (about 2,700–3,000 K)** gives the cosy, yellowish light similar to old incandescent bulbs. It suits living rooms, bedrooms and dining areas.
- **Neutral or natural white (about 3,500–4,500 K)** is crisper and suits kitchens, bathrooms and work areas.
- **Cool white or daylight (about 5,000–6,500 K)** is bluish-white, useful for garages, workshops and some offices, but can feel harsh in living spaces.

If you want your rooms to look the same as before, choose warm white. Mixing very different colour temperatures in one room can look odd.

## Colour rendering

The **colour rendering index (CRI)** describes how naturally a light source shows colours, on a scale up to 100. A higher CRI makes food, skin tones and fabrics look more natural. For general home use, many people find bulbs with a CRI of 80 or above perfectly good; for kitchens, dressing areas or craft work, a higher figure such as 90 can be worthwhile.

## Choosing the right fitting

Bulbs must match your light fittings. Common types include:

- **Screw fittings**, such as E27 (standard) and E14 (small).
- **Bayonet fittings**, such as B22 (standard) and B15 (small), common in some countries.
- **GU10** — twist-and-lock spotlights used in many ceilings and kitchens.
- **MR16 / GU5.3** — small spotlights that often run on low voltage via a transformer.
- **G9 and G4** — small capsule bulbs used in some decorative fittings.

The easiest approach is to take the old bulb to the shop, or photograph its base and the markings printed on it. Also check the bulb's shape and size will fit inside the lampshade or fitting.

### Low-voltage spotlights

Older MR16 halogen spotlights run on a transformer. Some LED replacements are not compatible with older transformers and may flicker or fail. If you have many such lights, it may be worth asking an electrician whether to replace the transformers or convert the fittings to mains-voltage GU10.

## Dimmers

Not all LEDs are dimmable, and not all dimmer switches work well with LEDs.

- **Buy bulbs labelled "dimmable"** for any light on a dimmer.
- **Older dimmers designed for incandescent bulbs** may cause LEDs to flicker, buzz or not dim smoothly. LED-compatible dimmer switches solve this; replacing a switch is electrical work that should be done safely, by a competent person or a qualified electrician.
- **Check minimum loads.** Some dimmers need a minimum wattage that a few low-wattage LEDs may not reach.

## Working out the savings

You can estimate the savings for any light:

1. **Find the difference in wattage** between the old bulb and the LED (for example, 60 W − 8 W = 52 W).
2. **Multiply by the hours the light is on per year** (for example, 4 hours a day × 365 = 1,460 hours).
3. **Divide by 1,000 to get kilowatt-hours**: 52 × 1,460 ÷ 1,000 ≈ 76 kWh per year.
4. **Multiply by your electricity price per kWh** to get the yearly saving for that one bulb.

The calculation shows why it is best to **start with the lights you use most** — kitchens, living rooms, hallways and outdoor lights on timers. A bulb in a rarely used cupboard saves very little and can be replaced when it fails.

## Quality matters

Cheap, poor-quality LEDs may flicker, fail early or produce unpleasant light. To choose well:

- Buy from reputable brands and retailers.
- Check the rated lifetime and warranty.
- Look for safety and conformity markings required in your country.
- Read the lumens, colour temperature and CRI on the packaging rather than relying on wattage.
- Be cautious with extremely cheap bulbs from unknown sellers online.

## Make the most of your lighting

Efficient bulbs are only part of the picture. Good habits matter too:

- **Switch off lights in empty rooms.**
- **Use task lighting**, such as a desk lamp, rather than lighting a whole room to read.
- **Use daylight** by keeping windows clean and curtains open during the day.
- **Choose light-coloured walls and ceilings**, which reflect light and make rooms feel brighter.
- **Use motion sensors or timers** for outdoor lights, hallways and garages.
- **Consider smart bulbs** only if their features genuinely help you; they draw a little standby power and cost more.

## Integrated LED fittings

Some modern light fittings have built-in LEDs that cannot be replaced individually. They are efficient and long-lasting, but when the LEDs eventually fail, the whole fitting may need replacing. Bear this in mind when choosing new fittings, and keep the receipt and warranty details.

## Disposing of old bulbs safely

- **Incandescent and halogen bulbs** are not usually recyclable through household collections in many areas; check local guidance.
- **Compact fluorescent lamps (CFLs) and fluorescent tubes** contain a small amount of mercury and should be taken to an appropriate recycling point, not put in household waste. If one breaks, follow official clean-up guidance, which usually involves ventilating the room and avoiding vacuum cleaners.
- **LED bulbs** are electronic items and are often accepted at electrical recycling points or retailers' take-back schemes.

## A room-by-room plan

1. List the lights in your home and estimate how many hours each is on per day.
2. Replace the most-used bulbs first.
3. Choose warm white for living spaces and neutral white for kitchens and work areas.
4. Match fittings and check dimmer compatibility.
5. Replace remaining bulbs as they fail.
6. Recycle old bulbs properly.

## The bottom line

Switching to LEDs is one of the simplest, most reliable energy savings available. Buy by lumens, choose the colour temperature that suits each room, match the fitting, check dimmer compatibility and start with the lights you use most. Each bulb you change keeps saving money for years — and you will be climbing a stepladder to replace bulbs far less often.
