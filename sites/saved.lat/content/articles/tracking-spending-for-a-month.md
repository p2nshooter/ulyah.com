Ask most people how much they spend each month on food, transport or entertainment, and the answer is usually a guess — and usually too low. Small purchases, card payments that feel invisible and irregular costs all blur together. Without an accurate picture, it is hard to build a realistic budget or know where to cut back.

Tracking every penny you spend for one month is one of the most useful money exercises there is. It takes a few minutes a day, requires no special skills, and almost always reveals something surprising. This guide explains how to do it, how to make sense of the results and how to turn them into lasting changes.

## Why track spending?

- **Accuracy.** You discover what you really spend, rather than what you think you spend.
- **Awareness.** Simply recording purchases makes you more mindful, and many people naturally spend less while tracking.
- **Spotting leaks.** Small, frequent purchases — snacks, coffees, app purchases, delivery fees — become visible.
- **Better budgets.** A budget based on real spending is far more likely to work. See our guide to building a simple budget.
- **Conversations.** For couples and families, shared data replaces arguments based on impressions.

## Before you start

### Choose a method

There is no single right way to track spending. Pick the one you are most likely to stick with:

- **A notebook or small diary** carried with you. Simple and satisfying for people who like writing things down.
- **A notes app on your phone**, with each purchase added as you go.
- **A spreadsheet**, updated daily or every few days from receipts and bank transactions.
- **A budgeting app** that connects to your bank account and categorises transactions automatically. These save time but still need checking, as automatic categories are not always accurate.
- **Your banking app.** Many banks now categorise spending and show monthly summaries.

Many people combine methods: a banking app or spreadsheet for card spending, plus a quick note of any cash purchases.

### Decide what to record

For each purchase, note:

- **Date.**
- **Amount.**
- **Where or what** (shop or item).
- **Category** (see below).
- **Optional notes**: whether it was planned, how you were feeling, or whether it was a need or a want.

### Choose your categories

Use categories that make sense for your household. A practical set might be:

- Housing (rent, mortgage, maintenance).
- Utilities (energy, water, phone, broadband).
- Groceries.
- Eating out and takeaways.
- Transport (fuel, fares, parking).
- Health and personal care.
- Clothing.
- Children.
- Pets.
- Entertainment and hobbies.
- Subscriptions.
- Gifts and celebrations.
- Household items.
- Savings and debt repayments.
- Miscellaneous.

Keep the list short enough to manage. You can always split categories later if one turns out to be very large.

## During the month

### Record everything

The key to useful results is completeness. Record every purchase, however small: the bus fare, the newspaper, the vending machine snack, the in-app purchase, the parking charge. Small, frequent spending is exactly what tends to be underestimated.

Remember to include:

- **Cash spending**, which leaves no digital trail.
- **Direct debits and standing orders** that leave your account automatically.
- **Purchases made with different cards** or accounts.
- **Online purchases and digital wallets.**
- **Spending by other household members** if you share finances.

### Make it a habit

- **Record purchases immediately**, or set a daily reminder to update your records each evening.
- **Keep receipts** in one place to check later.
- **Review your bank transactions** every few days to catch anything you missed.

### Do not change your behaviour — at first

It is tempting to spend less while tracking because you know you are watching. Some change is natural and helpful, but try to keep your spending reasonably normal for the first month so the results reflect your usual habits.

## Include irregular costs

A single month will not capture costs that happen less often, such as car insurance, annual subscriptions, holidays and festivals. After your month of tracking:

1. **Look back at the last twelve months** of bank and card statements.
2. **List all irregular costs** and their amounts.
3. **Divide the annual total by twelve** to get a monthly average.

These monthly averages can then be added to your budget and saved for through sinking funds, as explained in our separate guide.

## Analysing the results

At the end of the month, total each category.

### Questions to ask

- **Which categories are largest?** Housing, transport and food usually dominate. These offer the biggest opportunities for savings.
- **What surprised you?** Most people find at least one category much higher than expected.
- **Where are the small leaks?** Look for frequent small purchases that add up.
- **What was planned versus unplanned?** If you noted this, see how much was spontaneous.
- **Which purchases brought real value?** Some spending is worth every penny; some feels wasted in hindsight.
- **Are there patterns?** Spending may rise at weekends, on payday, when tired or stressed, or when shopping with others.

### Look at annual totals

Multiply monthly figures by twelve to see the annual cost. A small daily purchase can look very different as a yearly total. This is not a reason to give up everything you enjoy, but it helps you decide what is really worth it.

## Turning insights into action

### Identify three changes

Rather than trying to change everything at once, choose three specific actions based on your results. For example:

1. **Cut takeaway spending** by planning two simple "fakeaway" meals at home each week.
2. **Cancel two unused subscriptions** (see our subscription audit guide).
3. **Take a packed lunch** three days a week.

Specific, realistic changes are much more likely to stick than vague goals.

### Set category limits

Use your tracking results to set realistic monthly limits for each category in your budget. Start close to your current spending and reduce gradually, rather than setting a target you cannot reach.

### Redirect savings

Decide where the money you save will go — an emergency fund, a savings goal or debt repayment — and move it there automatically, so it does not get absorbed into other spending.

## Needs, wants and values

Once your spending is sorted into categories, a second useful exercise is to mark each purchase as a **need** or a **want**:

- **Needs** are costs you cannot reasonably avoid: housing, basic food, essential transport, utilities, medicine and minimum debt payments.
- **Wants** are everything else: eating out, entertainment, upgrades, treats and non-essential shopping.

The line between the two is not always sharp. A car may be a need for someone in a rural area and a want for someone in a city with good public transport. A phone is a need; the latest model usually is not. The point is not to feel guilty about wants — they are part of a good life — but to see clearly how much of your spending is truly fixed and how much is a choice.

A third question can be even more revealing: **did this purchase reflect what matters to me?** Spending on a hobby you love, a meal with friends or a trip to see family may feel completely worthwhile. Spending on things you barely remember a week later probably does not. Many people find that once they see the difference, cutting back on the second kind is easy — and they can spend more freely on the first.

## What a typical month can reveal

People who track their spending for the first time often discover similar things:

- **Food costs are higher than expected**, especially when supermarket shops, top-up shops, lunches, snacks and takeaways are added together.
- **Delivery and convenience fees** — for food delivery, express postage and small online orders — add up quickly.
- **Subscriptions and memberships** include services nobody in the household uses any more.
- **Small transport costs**, such as parking and short taxi trips, are larger than imagined.
- **Spending clusters around payday** and falls away towards the end of the month.

None of these discoveries means you have been careless. They simply show where the easiest opportunities are.

## Making tracking easier in future

After your first month, you may decide to continue tracking in a lighter form:

- **Weekly check-ins** with your banking app or spreadsheet.
- **Tracking only flexible categories**, such as groceries, eating out and entertainment, where spending varies most.
- **Using separate accounts or pots** for different types of spending, so balances show progress at a glance.
- **Repeating a full tracking month** once or twice a year, or when your circumstances change.

## Tracking as a household

If you share finances:

- **Agree to track together** for the same month.
- **Use a shared spreadsheet or app** where everyone can add purchases.
- **Discuss the results without blame.** The aim is to understand, not to criticise.
- **Agree on priorities** for changes together.
- **Involve older children**, who can learn a lot from seeing how household money is spent.

## Common pitfalls

- **Giving up after a few days.** If you miss a day, catch up from your bank statement and carry on.
- **Forgetting cash and small purchases.**
- **Being too hard on yourself.** Tracking is about information, not judgement.
- **Too many categories**, which makes tracking tedious.
- **Not acting on the results.** Choose a few changes and follow through.

## The bottom line

Tracking every purchase for one month is a simple, eye-opening exercise. It shows exactly where your money goes, highlights small leaks and patterns, and provides the real numbers you need to build a budget that works. You do not have to track in such detail forever — but one honest month can change the way you spend for years.
