A bicycle is one of the cheapest ways to get around: no fuel, no parking charges, and exercise built into the journey. But a bike that is neglected soon becomes slow, noisy and unsafe, and the repair bill for worn-out chains, cassettes and brake parts can come as a surprise. Most of that wear is preventable with a few minutes of care each week and some simple skills anyone can learn.

This guide covers the essential checks, the most common repairs and the basic kit you need. It is aimed at everyday riders with ordinary town, hybrid, road or mountain bikes. Electric bikes need the same basic care, but their batteries, motors and electrics should be handled according to the manufacturer's instructions and serviced by specialists.

## Why basic maintenance pays

- **Safety.** Working brakes, sound tyres and secure wheels prevent accidents.
- **Lower costs.** A clean, lubricated chain lasts longer and wears the more expensive gears more slowly.
- **Easier riding.** Correctly inflated tyres and a smooth drivetrain make every journey faster and less tiring.
- **Fewer breakdowns.** Spotting worn parts early stops small problems becoming roadside emergencies.

## The basic bike toolkit

- **A pump** with a pressure gauge, ideally a floor (track) pump for home and a small portable pump for rides.
- **Tyre levers**, usually plastic, for removing tyres.
- **A puncture repair kit** with patches and glue, or self-adhesive patches.
- **A spare inner tube** matching your tyre size and valve type.
- **A multi-tool** with hex keys and screwdrivers in the sizes commonly used on bikes.
- **Chain lubricant** suitable for your riding conditions.
- **Rags and a brush** for cleaning.
- **A bucket and mild soapy water** or a bike-specific cleaner.

You will find the tyre size printed on the sidewall of the tyre, and the recommended pressure range printed there too.

## The quick pre-ride check

Before riding, especially after the bike has been stored for a while, run through a simple check. Many cyclists use the mnemonic **"ABC"**:

- **A for air.** Squeeze the tyres. Are they firm? Check for cuts, bulges or embedded glass and flints.
- **B for brakes.** Squeeze each brake lever. The brakes should engage firmly before the lever touches the handlebar, and the bike should not roll forward when you push it with the front brake applied.
- **C for chain.** Is it clean and lubricated, not rusty or squeaking? Do the gears shift smoothly?

Add a **quick-release check**: make sure wheel quick-release levers or thru-axles are fully closed and tight, and that the seat post and handlebars are secure.

## Tyre pressure

Under-inflated tyres are the most common problem on everyday bikes. They make riding harder, wear out faster and are more prone to "pinch flat" punctures, where the tube is trapped against the rim by a bump.

1. **Find the recommended range** on the tyre sidewall.
2. **Identify your valve type.** Common types are Schrader (like a car valve), Presta (thin, with a small locknut on top that must be unscrewed before pumping) and Dunlop/Woods valves found on some town bikes. Many pumps fit more than one type.
3. **Pump to within the recommended range**, using a gauge rather than guesswork. Lighter riders and rough surfaces may suit the lower end; heavier loads and smooth roads may suit the upper end.
4. **Check pressure regularly** — weekly for frequently used bikes — because tyres slowly lose air even without a puncture.

## Fixing a puncture

Punctures happen to every cyclist. Fixing one at home is a useful skill that also prepares you for the roadside.

### Removing the wheel and tube

1. **Shift into the smallest rear sprocket** if you are removing the rear wheel; it makes refitting easier.
2. **Release the brake** if you have rim brakes with a quick-release mechanism, so the tyre can pass between the brake pads.
3. **Open the quick-release or undo the axle nuts** and remove the wheel.
4. **Let out any remaining air** from the tube.
5. **Insert tyre levers** under the tyre bead opposite the valve, hook them onto the spokes, and lever one side of the tyre off the rim. Work around the wheel until one side is free.
6. **Pull out the inner tube**, pushing the valve back through the rim last.

### Finding the cause

This step matters: if you do not find and remove whatever caused the puncture, the new tube may puncture immediately.

1. **Inflate the tube slightly** and listen or feel for escaping air. Passing the tube through a bowl of water and watching for bubbles shows small holes.
2. **Line the tube up with the tyre** to find the matching spot, then carefully run your fingers around the inside of the tyre to find thorns, glass or wire. Go slowly and gently to avoid cutting yourself.
3. **Check the rim tape** that covers spoke holes inside the rim; a displaced or damaged rim tape can cause repeated punctures.

### Patching or replacing

- **To patch:** roughen the area around the hole with the sandpaper in the kit, apply a thin layer of glue, wait until it is touch-dry, then press the patch firmly in place. Self-adhesive patches are quicker but may be less durable.
- **To replace:** use a new tube of the correct size and valve type. Keep the punctured tube to patch later as a spare.

### Refitting

1. **Inflate the tube just enough** to give it shape.
2. **Insert the valve** through the rim and tuck the tube inside the tyre evenly.
3. **Push the tyre bead back onto the rim** with your thumbs, working from the valve outwards in both directions. Avoid using levers to refit if possible, as they can pinch the new tube.
4. **Check that the tube is not trapped** between the tyre bead and rim all the way round.
5. **Inflate to the correct pressure**, refit the wheel, close the brake release and check the brakes before riding.

## Cleaning and lubricating the chain

The chain is the part that most benefits from regular care. A dirty, dry chain wears quickly and also wears the chainrings and rear sprockets, which are more expensive to replace.

1. **Wipe the chain** with a rag while turning the pedals backwards.
2. **Clean off heavy grime** with a brush and a bike-specific degreaser or soapy water, especially after wet or muddy rides. Rinse gently, avoiding high-pressure water near bearings.
3. **Let it dry.**
4. **Apply lubricant** sparingly to each link, on the inside of the chain, while turning the pedals backwards.
5. **Wait a few minutes**, then **wipe off the excess**. Oil on the outside of the chain attracts dirt.

Use a "wet" lubricant for rainy, muddy conditions and a "dry" lubricant for dry, dusty conditions, following the product instructions. How often to lubricate depends on riding: every week or two for regular commuters, and after every wet ride.

### Checking chain wear

Chains stretch as they wear. An inexpensive **chain checker** tool shows when a chain needs replacing. Replacing a chain before it is badly worn can protect the cassette and chainrings, saving money overall. If you are unsure, a bike shop can check it quickly.

## Checking and adjusting brakes

### Rim brakes

- **Check the pads** for wear; many have grooves that disappear when they need replacing.
- **Make sure the pads hit the rim squarely**, not the tyre, and not below the rim.
- **Clean the rims** of grime that reduces braking performance.
- **Adjust cable tension** with the barrel adjuster near the lever if the lever pulls too close to the handlebar; turning it out a little tightens the cable.

### Disc brakes

- **Check pad thickness** through the caliper, following the manufacturer's guidance on minimum thickness.
- **Keep rotors clean** and free of oil; contaminated pads and rotors lose braking power. Never spray lubricant near disc brakes.
- **Listen for rubbing** and look for warped rotors.
- **Hydraulic disc brakes** that feel spongy may need bleeding — a job best done by a bike shop unless you have the correct kit and instructions.

If brakes still feel weak after adjustment, do not ride the bike until it has been checked.

## Gears

If the gears jump, skip or hesitate, small adjustments to cable tension using the barrel adjuster on the rear derailleur or shifter often fix the problem. Make small turns, a quarter turn at a time, and test after each. Bent derailleur hangers, worn cables and worn chains also cause poor shifting; a bike shop can diagnose and fix these.

## Cleaning the whole bike

A clean bike is easier to inspect and lasts longer.

- **Use a bucket of warm soapy water**, a soft brush and a sponge.
- **Avoid pressure washers** close up, which can force water into bearings.
- **Dry the bike** and re-lubricate the chain after washing.
- **Inspect as you clean**: look for cracks in the frame, loose bolts, worn tyres and damaged cables.

## Storage

- **Store the bike indoors or under cover** if possible, to prevent rust and protect it from theft.
- **Lock it securely** with a good-quality lock to a fixed object, and consider registering its frame number with a bike-marking scheme where available.
- **Hang it or stand it** so the tyres are not resting flat for long periods if it is stored for months.

## When to visit a bike shop

Some jobs need specialist tools or experience:

- Wheel truing and spoke replacement.
- Bottom bracket, headset and hub bearing servicing.
- Hydraulic brake bleeding.
- Suspension servicing.
- Frame damage or cracks.
- Electric bike motor and battery issues.

An annual service can be good value for frequently used bikes, and many shops and community workshops run basic maintenance classes.

## A maintenance routine

- **Every ride:** quick ABC check.
- **Weekly:** check tyre pressure; wipe and lubricate the chain if used regularly.
- **Monthly:** clean the bike; check brake pads, tyres and bolts.
- **Every few months:** check chain wear and cable condition.
- **Yearly:** consider a professional service.

## The bottom line

Basic bike maintenance is simple, quick and saves money. Keep the tyres properly inflated, clean and lubricate the chain, check the brakes before riding, and learn to fix a puncture. These habits keep you safe, make riding easier and prevent expensive wear — and they turn a bicycle into an even cheaper, more reliable way to get around.
