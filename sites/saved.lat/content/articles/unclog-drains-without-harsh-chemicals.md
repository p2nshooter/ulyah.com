A slow or blocked drain is one of those problems that always seems to happen at the worst moment: the sink fills with dirty water just before guests arrive, or the shower turns into a paddling pool on a busy morning. The temptation is to pour in a bottle of strong chemical drain cleaner, or to call a plumber straight away. Yet most household blockages are close to the plughole and can be cleared with simple tools, a little patience and no harsh chemicals at all.

This guide explains what causes blockages, how to clear them step by step — from the gentlest methods to the more hands-on ones — and how to stop them coming back.

## Why avoid harsh chemical cleaners?

Strong chemical drain cleaners can work, but they have drawbacks:

- **Safety risks.** Many contain highly corrosive substances that can burn skin and eyes and give off fumes. If they do not clear the blockage, you are left with a sink full of dangerous water to deal with.
- **Damage to pipes and fittings.** Repeated use can harm some older pipes, seals and finishes.
- **Mixing hazards.** Combining different cleaning products can create dangerous reactions and fumes. Never mix drain cleaners with other products, including bleach.
- **Cost.** Mechanical tools such as a plunger and a drain snake are cheap, reusable and often more effective.

If you do decide to use a commercial product, read and follow the label exactly, wear gloves and eye protection, ventilate the room, and never plunge or use other tools straight after adding a chemical cleaner, because splashes are dangerous. Tell anyone who later works on the drain what you have used.

## What causes blockages

Most blockages are made of a few familiar materials:

- **Kitchen sinks:** fats, oils and grease that solidify as they cool, combined with food scraps and coffee grounds.
- **Bathroom basins:** hair, soap scum, toothpaste and shaving residue.
- **Showers and baths:** hair and soap scum, often trapped just below the plughole.
- **Toilets:** too much toilet paper, wet wipes (including those labelled "flushable"), cotton buds, sanitary products and other items that should never be flushed.
- **Hard-water areas:** limescale, which narrows pipes over time and gives debris more to cling to.

## Step one: check how bad it is

- **Is only one drain affected?** If a single sink or shower is slow, the blockage is probably local and easy to reach.
- **Are several drains slow,** or does water back up into a different sink or shower when you use another fitting? The problem may be further down the system.
- **Do toilets gurgle, or does water rise in a shower when the toilet is flushed?** That suggests a blockage in the main drain or soil pipe.
- **Is there a bad smell from an outside drain or manhole?** The blockage may be in shared or external drains.

Local blockages are usually DIY jobs. Problems affecting the whole house or external drains may need professional help, and in some places the water company or local authority is responsible for shared sewers.

## Step two: clear the plughole

Start with the simplest fix:

1. **Remove the plug or pop-up stopper.** Many basin stoppers lift out or unscrew; some are connected to a rod behind the tap that needs loosening.
2. **Pull out visible hair and debris** with gloved fingers, tweezers or a cheap plastic drain-cleaning strip with barbs (sometimes called a hair snake).
3. **Clean the stopper** before refitting it.

In showers and baths, a surprising number of slow drains are solved this way in a couple of minutes.

## Step three: hot water and washing-up liquid (kitchen sinks)

For a kitchen sink slowed by grease, try a squeeze of washing-up liquid followed by a kettle of hot water poured slowly into the plughole. The detergent helps break up the grease and the heat softens it.

A word of caution: very hot or boiling water should not be poured into some plastic pipes, toilet bowls (which can crack) or sinks made of materials that may be damaged by sudden heat. Hot water from the tap, or water that has cooled a little after boiling, is the safer choice in those cases.

## The baking soda and vinegar method

Many people pour bicarbonate of soda (baking soda) into the drain followed by vinegar, which fizzes as it reacts. It can help freshen drains and loosen minor build-up near the plughole, but it is not a powerful cleaner and rarely clears a solid blockage. Treat it as a maintenance method rather than a cure, and flush with hot water afterwards.

## Step four: use a plunger

A plunger is the most useful single tool for blocked drains.

### Sinks, basins and baths

1. **Use a cup plunger** — the flat-bottomed kind — for sinks and baths.
2. **Block the overflow** with a damp cloth. Otherwise the pressure escapes through the overflow instead of pushing on the blockage. In a double sink, block the other plughole too.
3. **Make sure there is enough water** in the sink to cover the plunger cup.
4. **Seal the plunger over the plughole** and push down and up firmly and rhythmically for twenty to thirty seconds.
5. **Lift the plunger** and see whether the water drains. Repeat a few times if needed.

### Toilets

1. **Use a flange plunger**, which has an extra rubber sleeve that fits into the toilet outlet.
2. **Make sure there is enough water** in the bowl to cover the plunger.
3. **Insert the plunger slowly** to push out air, then plunge firmly up and down, keeping the seal.
4. **Do not keep flushing** a blocked toilet; it can overflow. Wait until the water level drops before testing with a single flush.

Wear rubber gloves, keep children and pets away, and clean the plunger and area afterwards.

## Step five: clean the trap

Under every sink and basin is a **trap** — a bend in the pipe (U-bend or bottle trap) that holds water to stop drain smells coming up. Debris often collects here, and cleaning it is a straightforward job.

1. **Place a bucket or bowl** underneath, and lay down an old towel.
2. **Unscrew the trap** by hand. Most modern plastic traps have large nuts that loosen anticlockwise without tools. Bottle traps often have a removable base.
3. **Empty and clean** the trap and the short pipes either side, removing hair, grease and debris. An old toothbrush is ideal.
4. **Check the rubber seals** are in place and undamaged.
5. **Reassemble**, tightening by hand until firm, then run water and check for leaks.

If the trap was clean, the blockage is further along the pipe.

## Step six: use a drain snake (auger)

A **drain snake** is a flexible coiled cable that reaches further into a pipe. Basic hand-operated versions are inexpensive.

1. **Feed the cable into the drain** (or into the pipe after removing the trap) while turning the handle.
2. **When you feel resistance**, keep turning to break up or hook the blockage.
3. **Pull the cable back slowly**, bringing debris with it.
4. **Flush with hot water** and test.

Toilet augers, designed with a protective sleeve to avoid scratching the bowl, are available for stubborn toilet blockages.

## When to call a professional

Call a plumber or drainage specialist if:

- Several drains are blocked, or water backs up in other fittings.
- Sewage is coming up through drains or overflowing from an outside manhole.
- You have tried plunging, trap cleaning and a drain snake without success.
- Pipes are leaking, cracked or badly corroded.
- You suspect tree roots or a collapsed pipe.

Check your home insurance and your local water company's responsibilities before paying for external drain work; in some areas, shared drains and sewers are maintained by the utility.

## Preventing blockages

Prevention is far cheaper and easier than clearing blockages.

### In the kitchen

- **Never pour fats, oils or grease down the sink.** Let them cool and solidify, then scrape them into the bin, or wipe greasy pans with kitchen paper before washing.
- **Scrape plates into the bin or food waste caddy** before washing.
- **Use a sink strainer** to catch food scraps.
- **Keep coffee grounds out of the sink.**

### In the bathroom

- **Fit hair catchers** over shower and bath plugholes, and empty them regularly.
- **Clean plugholes and stoppers** every couple of weeks.
- **Only flush the three Ps**: pee, poo and (toilet) paper. Wet wipes, cotton buds, dental floss, sanitary products and nappies belong in the bin, even if packaging says "flushable".
- **Keep a small bin in the bathroom** to make this easy.

### General maintenance

- **Run hot water** through kitchen drains after washing up.
- **Pour a kettle of warm water** down rarely used drains occasionally to keep the trap topped up and clear.
- **Descale** plugholes and fittings in hard-water areas.

## A quick toolkit for drains

- Rubber gloves.
- A cup plunger for sinks and a flange plunger for toilets.
- A plastic hair-removal strip.
- A hand-operated drain snake.
- A bucket and old towels.
- Sink strainers and shower hair catchers.

All of these together cost less than a single plumber's call-out in many areas, and they last for years.

## The bottom line

Most household blockages are close to the plughole and are caused by grease, hair, soap and things that should never go down a drain. Before reaching for harsh chemicals, clear the plughole, try hot water and detergent, use the right plunger with the overflow blocked, clean the trap and, if needed, use a drain snake. Simple prevention — no grease down the sink, hair catchers in the shower and only toilet paper in the toilet — keeps drains flowing and saves money on call-outs and cleaners.
