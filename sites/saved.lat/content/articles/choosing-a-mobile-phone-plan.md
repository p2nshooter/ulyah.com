For many households, mobile phone plans are one of those monthly payments that quietly grow over time. A contract taken out years ago keeps rolling, the phone was paid off long ago but the bill stayed the same, and nobody has checked whether the data allowance matches how the family actually uses their phones. Because phone plans are complicated, with bundles, extras and different contract lengths, it is easy to pay for far more than you need.

This guide explains how to work out what you really use, the main types of plan, how to compare handset deals with buying the phone separately, what to watch for in the small print, and how to switch providers without losing your number.

## Step 1: find out what you actually use

Before looking at plans, look at your own usage for the last few months:

- **Data:** your phone's settings usually show how much mobile data you have used per month, and which apps used the most. Your provider's app or bill often shows it too.
- **Calls and texts:** check whether you ever come close to your allowance. Many people now use messaging apps over Wi-Fi far more than traditional calls and texts.
- **Roaming:** note how often you use your phone abroad.
- **Extras:** look for charges for premium numbers, international calls or add-ons.

Write down the **highest month** as well as the average, so your plan covers the busy months without paying for a huge allowance you never use.

## Step 2: understand how data is used

| Activity | Data use |
|---|---|
| Messages and email | low |
| Browsing and social media without video | moderate |
| Music streaming | moderate to high, depending on quality |
| Video streaming and video calls | high |
| Using the phone as a hotspot for a laptop | very high |

Using **Wi-Fi at home, at work and at school** dramatically reduces how much mobile data you need. Check that your phone connects automatically to trusted Wi-Fi networks, and consider:

- downloading music, podcasts and videos over Wi-Fi before travelling;
- setting apps to update only on Wi-Fi;
- lowering video quality on mobile data in streaming apps.

## The main types of plan

### SIM-only

You keep or buy your own phone and pay only for the service. SIM-only plans are often much cheaper than plans that include a handset, and many have short contracts or none at all. They are a good choice if:

- your current phone still works well;
- you are happy to buy a phone outright, new or refurbished;
- you want flexibility to switch when a better deal appears.

### Handset contracts

You get a new phone and pay for it, along with the service, over a fixed term. They spread the cost of an expensive phone, but:

- the total cost over the contract can be higher than buying the phone and a SIM-only plan separately;
- you are usually tied in for the full term;
- if the phone cost is not shown separately, the monthly price may not fall when the phone is paid off.

### Prepay or pay-as-you-go

You top up in advance. There is no contract and no risk of bill shock, which suits light users, children and anyone who wants tight control. Check how long credit lasts and whether bundles renew automatically.

### Family or shared plans

Some providers offer discounts for several lines on one account, or plans where data is shared. They can save money, but check whether one heavy user could use up everyone's data, and what happens if someone wants to leave.

## Comparing a handset deal fairly

To compare a handset contract with buying the phone separately:

1. **Total cost of the contract:** multiply the monthly price by the number of months and add any upfront payment.
2. **Total cost of buying separately:** the price of the same phone, new or refurbished, plus the cost of a SIM-only plan with similar allowances for the same period.
3. **Compare the two totals.**

If the separate option is cheaper, or only slightly more expensive but more flexible, it is usually the better deal. Our guide to [buying refurbished electronics](/guides/buying-refurbished-electronics/) explains how to buy a good phone for less.

## The small print to check

- **Contract length and minimum term.**
- **Price rises during the contract.** Some contracts include annual increases, sometimes linked to inflation or set amounts. Work out what you will pay in later years, not just the first month.
- **Early exit fees.**
- **What happens at the end of the contract.** Many plans move to a higher rolling price automatically. Set a reminder a month before the end date.
- **Data caps and speeds.** "Unlimited" plans may have fair-use limits or reduced speeds after a certain amount.
- **Roaming rules and charges** in the countries you visit.
- **Charges for extras**, such as premium numbers or international calls.
- **Coverage.** Check the provider's coverage map for your home, work and travel routes, and ask neighbours about real-world signal.

## Roaming without bill shock

- **Check the rules before you travel**: some plans include roaming in certain countries; others charge by the day or by the megabyte.
- **Turn off data roaming** if your plan does not include it, and rely on Wi-Fi.
- **Consider a local or travel SIM** or an eSIM for longer trips, if your phone is unlocked.
- **Set a spending cap** if your provider offers one.

## Switching providers

Switching is usually easier than people expect, and in many countries you can **keep your number**.

1. **Check your contract end date** and any exit fees.
2. **Choose a new plan** based on your usage.
3. **Follow the number transfer process** in your country. This often involves requesting a transfer code from your current provider or asking the new one to arrange it.
4. **Check that your phone is unlocked** if you are keeping it, or ask your current provider to unlock it.
5. **Keep both SIMs** until the transfer is complete.
6. **Cancel any extras** on the old account and check the final bill.

Some providers offer better deals to keep you when you say you are leaving. It is worth asking, but compare the offer with what you found elsewhere.

## Questions to ask before you sign

1. What is the **total cost** over the whole contract, including any upfront payment?
2. Are there **price rises** written into the terms, and how are they calculated?
3. What happens to the price **when the contract ends**?
4. What are the **exit fees** if I leave early?
5. Is "unlimited" data subject to a **fair-use policy** or reduced speeds?
6. What are the **roaming rules** in the countries I visit?
7. Can I set a **spending cap** to prevent bill shock?
8. Is the phone **locked** to this network?

Ask for the answers in writing, or check them in the contract summary before you agree.

## Older and second-hand phones

Keeping your current phone, or buying an older model, is usually the cheapest option, but check that it still receives **security updates** from the manufacturer. A phone without updates can be risky for banking and email. Also check that an older phone supports the networks your new provider uses, since some providers switch off older network technologies over time.

## Children's phones

- **Start with prepay or a low-limit plan.**
- **Use spending caps and parental controls**, and block premium services.
- **Talk about data, in-app purchases and scams**, so children understand the costs.

## A yearly phone check

Once a year, or when a contract ends:

1. check your actual usage;
2. compare it with your allowance;
3. look at SIM-only and prepay options;
4. check for price rises;
5. switch, renegotiate or stay, based on the numbers.

This fits neatly into the yearly review described in our guide to [the subscription audit](/guides/subscription-audit/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is unlimited data worth it?

Only if you regularly use a lot of mobile data, for example streaming video away from Wi-Fi or using your phone as a hotspot. Many people use far less than they think.

### Can I keep my old phone when I switch?

Usually yes, if it is unlocked and compatible with the new network.

### Why did my bill go up in the middle of my contract?

Many contracts include price rises written into the terms. Check your contract for the rules and the dates.

## The bottom line

The best phone plan is the one that matches how you actually use your phone. Check your data, calls and roaming over several months, use Wi-Fi wherever possible, and consider SIM-only or prepay plans if your phone still works well. Compare handset contracts by their total cost against buying the phone separately, read the small print on price rises, exit fees and end-of-contract prices, check coverage, and set a reminder before the contract ends. Switching is usually simple and lets you keep your number, so review your plan at least once a year.
