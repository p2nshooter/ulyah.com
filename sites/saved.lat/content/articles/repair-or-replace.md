When the washing machine starts making a grinding noise, the laptop battery barely lasts an hour or a favourite pair of shoes splits at the sole, the same question arises: is it worth repairing, or is it time to buy a new one? Many people replace things by default, because a new item feels simpler and the repair process is unfamiliar. Others hold on to broken things for years, intending to fix them one day. Neither approach saves money reliably. What helps is a clear, quick way to weigh up the options.

This guide offers a simple framework for deciding whether to repair or replace, explains the questions to ask about age, cost, safety, energy use and parts, covers where to find affordable repairs, including community repair cafés, and suggests what to do with items you decide not to keep.

## Start with three quick questions

1. **Is it safe?** If a repair would leave the item unsafe, or the fault is a safety risk in itself, that decides it.
2. **Is it under warranty or guarantee?** If so, contact the seller or manufacturer before doing anything else. Opening an item yourself may affect the warranty. Our guide to [warranties, guarantees and returns](/guides/warranties-and-returns/) explains your options.
3. **Can it be repaired at all?** Some faults cannot be fixed economically, and some parts are no longer made.

If the item is safe to repair, out of warranty and repairable, move on to the cost.

## Comparing the cost

### Estimate both sides

- **Repair:** the price of parts, labour, any diagnosis or call-out fee, and how long you will be without the item.
- **Replacement:** the price of a comparable new or second-hand item, delivery, installation, disposal of the old one and any accessories you would need to buy again.

### A common rule of thumb

Many repairers and consumer advisers use a rough guide: if the repair costs more than about half the price of a comparable replacement, and the item is already past the middle of its expected life, replacement is often the better choice. It is only a rule of thumb, but it is a useful starting point. A repair that costs a small fraction of a new item is usually worth it, even on an older product.

### Think about what you get

- A repaired item may have **other parts nearing the end of their life**. Ask the repairer about the overall condition.
- A replacement may be **better, or worse**. Some new products are more efficient or have useful features; others are less durable than the one you have.
- Consider the **cost per use** over the remaining life of each option. Our guide to [cost per use](/guides/cost-per-use/) explains how to compare.

## Age and condition

Check the **expected lifespan** of the type of item, which manufacturers, consumer organisations and repairers can give a rough idea of. Then look at its condition:

- Is this the **first fault** or one of several recently?
- Is the fault in a **minor part**, such as a door seal or a belt, or in a **major one**, such as a motor, compressor or main circuit board?
- Has the item been **looked after**? Regular maintenance extends life; our guide to [making appliances last](/guides/extend-the-life-of-appliances/) explains what helps.

A first, minor fault on a well-maintained item is a strong case for repair.

## Energy and running costs

For appliances that run every day, such as fridges and freezers, running costs matter as well as the purchase price. A very old appliance may use considerably more energy than a modern efficient one. To compare:

- look at the **energy label** or the power rating of a new model;
- estimate the **energy use of the old one**, from its label or with a plug-in energy monitor;
- work out the **difference per year** using your electricity price, as explained in our guide to [reading your energy bill](/guides/reading-your-energy-bill/);
- compare that saving with the **price of the new appliance**.

If the saving is small, repairing the old appliance may still make sense. If it is large and the old appliance is near the end of its life, replacement may pay back over time.

## Safety comes first

Some situations should always lead to a professional repair or a replacement:

- **damaged cables, scorch marks, burning smells or sparks** from electrical items;
- **gas appliances**, which must be repaired only by qualified, registered engineers;
- **products subject to a safety recall**: check the manufacturer's or your national product safety website, and follow the instructions;
- **swollen or damaged batteries** in phones, laptops and other devices, which can be a fire risk;
- **child safety items**, such as car seats and cots, after an accident or damage.

Never attempt repairs on mains electrical equipment unless you are competent and it is safe to do so with the power disconnected.

## Parts and repairability

- **Are spare parts available?** Search the model number with the word "parts" or ask the manufacturer.
- **How easy is it to open?** Some products are designed to be repaired; others are glued shut.
- **Are repair guides available?** Many manufacturers and independent websites publish step-by-step instructions.
- **Is there a right to repair?** In some places, rules require manufacturers to make spare parts and repair information available for certain products for a number of years. Check what applies where you live.

## Where to get affordable repairs

- **Do it yourself** for simple jobs, with the right part and a guide. Our guide to [the basic home toolkit](/guides/basic-home-toolkit/) lists the tools that cover most small repairs.
- **Repair cafés:** community events where volunteers help people fix electrical items, clothes, bicycles, furniture and more, often free or for a donation. They are a great way to learn.
- **Independent repairers:** often cheaper than manufacturers for phones, computers and appliances. Ask for a quote, a warranty on the work and the use of quality parts.
- **Specialist trades:** cobblers for shoes and bags, tailors for clothes, upholsterers for furniture.
- **Manufacturer services:** sometimes the best option for newer products, especially within warranty.

Always ask for a **written quote** before work begins, and ask whether there is a fee if the item turns out not to be repairable.

## Examples

| Item and fault | Usually worth repairing? |
|---|---|
| Washing machine with a worn door seal or blocked pump | often yes |
| Fridge with a failed compressor on an old model | often replace |
| Phone with a cracked screen or tired battery | often yes, if the phone is otherwise good |
| Laptop with a failed main board on an old model | often replace |
| Good leather shoes with worn soles | usually yes |
| Clothes with a broken zip or small hole | usually yes; see our guide to [mending clothes](/guides/mending-clothes-basics/) |
| Solid wood furniture with loose joints | usually yes; see our guide to [refreshing old furniture](/guides/furniture-refresh/) |
| Bicycle with worn brakes or chain | usually yes; see our guide to [basic bike maintenance](/guides/bike-maintenance-basics/) |

These are general patterns, not rules. The age, condition and cost of each item decide the answer.

## If you replace: what to do with the old item

- **Sell it for parts or as "spares or repair"**, clearly described.
- **Donate it** if it still works, to a charity shop or community group that accepts it.
- **Return it to the retailer**, as many are required to take back old appliances when you buy a new one.
- **Recycle it properly**: electrical items, batteries and some materials need special recycling, not household waste.
- **Wipe personal data** from phones, computers and tablets before passing them on.

## If you repair: protect your investment

- keep the **receipt and warranty** for the repair;
- follow any **maintenance advice** the repairer gives;
- note the **date** of the repair in your household records; our guide to a [household paperwork system](/guides/household-paperwork-system/) suggests where to keep it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is repairing always better for the environment?

Usually, because making a new product uses materials and energy. The main exception is some very old, inefficient appliances that use far more energy than modern equivalents.

### How do I find a trustworthy repairer?

Ask for recommendations, check independent reviews, ask for a written quote and a warranty on the work, and be wary of anyone who asks for full payment before diagnosing the fault.

### Should I pay for a diagnosis?

It is often worth it for expensive items, as long as you know the fee in advance and whether it is deducted from the repair cost.

## The bottom line

To decide between repairing and replacing, check safety first, then the warranty, then whether a repair is possible. Compare the full cost of each option, using the rough rule that a repair costing more than half the price of a replacement, on an item past the middle of its life, often tips the balance towards replacing. Consider the item's age and condition, its energy use, the availability of parts and the long-term cost per use. Look for affordable repairs through do-it-yourself guides, repair cafés and independent repairers, always with a written quote. And if you replace, sell, donate or recycle the old item responsibly.
