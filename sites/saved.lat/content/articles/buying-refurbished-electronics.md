A new phone, laptop or tablet is one of the biggest purchases many households make, and the price keeps rising. Refurbished devices offer a way to get a good machine for considerably less, often with a warranty, and they keep perfectly usable electronics out of landfill. But the word "refurbished" covers everything from a device that was opened and returned unused to one that has had a hard life and a quick clean. Knowing what to look for makes the difference between a bargain and a headache.

This guide explains the difference between refurbished and used, what condition grades mean, how to check batteries, screens, storage and accounts, which kinds of seller and warranty offer the most protection, and the warning signs that should make you walk away.

## Refurbished, used and "open box": the differences

| Term | Usually means |
|---|---|
| Refurbished by the manufacturer | inspected, repaired if needed and tested by the brand, often with a new battery or casing, and a manufacturer's warranty |
| Refurbished by a specialist | inspected, repaired and tested by an independent business, with that seller's warranty |
| Used or second-hand | sold as it is, usually by a private seller, with little or no testing or warranty |
| Open box or returned | returned to the shop, often hardly used, resold at a discount |
| "Spares or repair" | faulty, sold for parts |

These terms are not regulated in the same way everywhere, so always read exactly what the seller promises. Our guide to [buying second-hand safely](/guides/buying-secondhand-safely/) covers private purchases in more detail.

## Condition grades

Many refurbishers grade their devices by appearance. The names vary, but a common pattern is:

- **Like new or Grade A:** minimal or no visible signs of use.
- **Very good or Grade B:** light scratches or marks, not noticeable in normal use.
- **Good or Grade C:** visible wear, such as scratches or small dents, but fully working.

Grades usually describe the **cosmetic condition only**. A lower grade can be a smart way to save money if you plan to use a case or do not mind a few marks, as long as everything works. Check the seller's own definitions, and look for photos of the actual device where possible.

## What to check before buying

### The battery

Batteries wear with use, and a tired battery is the most common disappointment with refurbished devices.

- **Ask about battery health.** Many phones and laptops can report the battery's capacity compared with when it was new. Some refurbishers guarantee a minimum level or fit new batteries.
- **Check the warranty on the battery.** Some warranties exclude it.
- **Factor in replacement.** If the battery is weak, find out what a replacement would cost for that model.

### The screen and the body

- look for **dead pixels, burn-in, discoloured areas or touch problems**;
- check for **cracks** under the edges of cases or screen protectors;
- check **buttons, ports, speakers and cameras**.

### Storage and memory

Make sure the device has enough storage and memory for how you use it. Older models with little storage can feel slow and full very quickly.

### Software and updates

This is one of the most important checks:

- **Is the device still receiving security updates?** Manufacturers support devices for a limited number of years. A device that no longer receives updates may be unsafe for banking, email and other sensitive tasks.
- Check the manufacturer's information on how long the model is supported.

### Accounts and locks

A device that is still linked to the previous owner's account may be impossible to use.

- Make sure **activation locks and previous accounts have been removed**.
- For phones, check that it is **unlocked** for your network, or compatible with it.
- For private sales, consider checking whether the device is **reported lost or stolen**, where a service for that exists in your country.

### The model and specification

Confirm the **exact model, year, storage and colour**, and compare the price with the same model from other sellers and with a newer budget model. Sometimes a new mid-range device offers better value than an old flagship.

## Choosing a seller

| Seller | Pros | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Manufacturer refurbished | strong testing, often new battery and case, manufacturer warranty | limited stock; price closer to new |
| Specialist refurbisher | wide choice, clear grades, warranty | check reviews and warranty terms |
| Large retailers and networks | easy returns, local support | check whether the warranty is theirs or a third party's |
| Marketplaces | lots of choice, competitive prices | quality varies by seller; read seller ratings and terms |
| Private sellers | lowest prices | little protection; check everything in person |

Look for:

- a **clear warranty**, ideally of at least a year, from a business you can contact;
- a **return period** that gives you time to test the device;
- **transparent grading** and photos;
- **independent reviews** of the seller.

Our guide to [warranties, guarantees and returns](/guides/warranties-and-returns/) explains your rights, and our guide to [avoiding online shopping scams](/guides/avoiding-online-shopping-scams/) explains how to spot fake shops.

## What to check by type of device

### Phones

- **Network compatibility:** check that the model supports the mobile networks and frequencies used in your country, especially if it was originally sold abroad.
- **Water resistance:** a phone that has been opened for repair may no longer be as water-resistant as when it was new, even if the original model was rated. Ask the seller.
- **Cameras and biometrics:** test fingerprint or face recognition, and both front and rear cameras.
- **Charging:** check that it charges with a cable and, if the model supports it, wirelessly.

### Laptops

- **Keyboard and trackpad:** test every key and the trackpad's clicks and gestures.
- **Hinges:** open and close the lid; loose or creaking hinges can fail.
- **Ports:** try each port with a device or cable.
- **Fans and heat:** a laptop that gets very hot or loud with light use may have a cooling problem.
- **Operating system and licence:** make sure the system is legitimately installed and activated, and that it can be updated.
- **Upgrade options:** some laptops allow more memory or storage to be added cheaply; others do not.

### Tablets

- **Screen and touch response** across the whole surface.
- **Accessories:** check whether keyboards or pens are compatible with that model.
- **Storage:** tablets often cannot be expanded, so choose enough from the start.

## Questions to ask the seller

1. Who refurbished the device, and what was tested or replaced?
2. What is the battery health, and is the battery covered by the warranty?
3. Is the device free of previous accounts and locks?
4. How long is the warranty, and who provides it?
5. How long is the return period, and who pays for return postage?
6. Are the charger and cable included, and are they original or compatible parts?

A reputable seller answers these questions clearly and in writing.

## Red flags

- prices far below other refurbishers for the same model and grade;
- no warranty, or a warranty with vague terms;
- no company name, address or contact details;
- requests for payment by bank transfer or methods without buyer protection;
- devices described as "unlocked" without proof, or still linked to an account;
- no information about battery condition;
- models that no longer receive security updates sold without mention of it.

## When the device arrives

1. **Check it against the description**: model, storage, grade and accessories.
2. **Test everything** in the first days: battery life, screen, cameras, speakers, microphone, charging, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and mobile data.
3. **Check for updates** and install them.
4. **Confirm accounts are clear** and that you can set it up with your own.
5. **Keep the packaging and paperwork** until the return period ends.
6. **Register the warranty** if required.

If anything is wrong, contact the seller in writing within the return period.

## Is refurbished right for you?

Refurbished devices suit most everyday uses: browsing, email, video calls, streaming, office work and school. They are less suitable if you need the very latest features, or if you plan to keep the device for many years and the model is already old, because software support will end sooner.

Before buying, think about the **cost per year of use**: a slightly more expensive, newer refurbished model that will receive updates for longer may be better value than the cheapest option. Our guide to [cost per use](/guides/cost-per-use/) explains the idea.

## Selling or passing on your old device

- **back up your data**;
- **sign out of all accounts** and remove activation locks;
- **reset to factory settings**;
- **remove SIM and memory cards**;
- sell, trade in, donate or recycle it responsibly.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is refurbished the same as second-hand?

Not exactly. Refurbished devices should have been tested and, where needed, repaired, and usually come with a warranty. Second-hand devices are usually sold as they are.

### Does a lower grade mean a worse device?

Usually it means more cosmetic wear, not worse performance. Check the seller's grading and warranty.

### How long will a refurbished device last?

It depends on its age, condition, battery and software support. A device that still has several years of updates ahead and a healthy battery can serve well for a long time.

## The bottom line

Refurbished electronics can offer excellent value, as long as you know what you are buying. Understand the difference between refurbished, used and open box, read the seller's grading carefully and remember that grades usually describe appearance. Check battery health, screen, storage, software updates and account locks, choose a seller with a clear warranty and return period, and avoid deals with no contact details, no warranty or suspiciously low prices. Test everything as soon as the device arrives, and when you pass on your old one, wipe it properly first.
