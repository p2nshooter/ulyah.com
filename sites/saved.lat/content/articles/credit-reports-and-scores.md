Whenever you apply for a loan, a credit card, a mobile phone contract or sometimes even a rental property, the company you apply to may look at your credit history. For many people, this process is a mystery. They know that a "good credit score" matters but are not sure what it is, who calculates it or how to improve it. Myths spread easily, and some companies take advantage of that confusion to sell expensive services that promise quick fixes.

This guide explains in plain language what credit reports and scores are, what information they usually contain, how lenders use them, the everyday habits that tend to affect them, how to check your report and correct mistakes, and how to protect yourself from identity fraud and credit repair scams. Systems differ from country to country, so check the official guidance where you live. This is general information, not personal financial advice.

## Credit report and credit score: two different things

- A **credit report** is a record of how you have managed credit and certain bills. It is held by credit reference agencies, also called credit bureaus, in many countries.
- A **credit score** is a number calculated from the information in your report, intended to summarise how risky it might be to lend to you.

There is often **no single score**. Different agencies and lenders use different scoring methods and scales, and lenders also use their own information, such as your income and outgoings, when deciding. A score shown in an app is a useful guide, but it is not exactly what every lender sees.

## What a credit report usually contains

| Section | Typical information |
|---|---|
| Personal details | name, date of birth, current and previous addresses |
| Credit accounts | loans, credit cards, overdrafts, store cards, some phone contracts, with balances and limits |
| Payment history | whether payments were made on time, late or missed |
| Defaults and arrears | accounts that were seriously behind or closed because of non-payment |
| Public records | in some countries, court judgments, insolvency or bankruptcy |
| Searches | lenders who have looked at your report when you applied for credit |
| Financial links | in some countries, people you share joint accounts with |

What is included, and for how long information stays on the report, depends on the country and the agency.

## How lenders use your report

When you apply for credit, a lender typically checks:

- whether you have paid previous debts on time;
- how much credit you already have and how much of it you use;
- whether you have applied for a lot of credit recently;
- whether the details you give match the report.

They combine this with their own rules and information. Being turned down by one lender does not mean every lender will refuse you.

## Hard and soft searches

- A **hard search** happens when you formally apply for credit. It is usually recorded on your report and visible to other lenders. Many hard searches in a short time can suggest that you are under financial pressure.
- A **soft search** happens when you check your own report, or when some lenders run an eligibility check before you apply. It is generally not visible to other lenders and does not affect applications.

Using **eligibility checkers** that rely on soft searches can help you avoid unnecessary hard searches and refusals.

## Habits that tend to affect credit history

Scoring methods vary, but these factors are widely recognised:

1. **Paying on time.** Payment history is usually one of the most important parts of a credit report. Setting up automatic payments for at least the minimum amount helps avoid accidental late payments.
2. **Not using all your available credit.** Using a high proportion of your credit limits can be seen as a sign of stress. Keeping balances well below limits is generally viewed more positively.
3. **Length of history.** A longer record of well-managed accounts tends to help.
4. **Not applying for too much credit at once.** Spread out applications and only apply when you need to.
5. **Keeping details up to date and consistent.** Make sure your address and other details are correct with your lenders and, where relevant, on official registers used for identity checks.
6. **Dealing with problems early.** If you are struggling, contact the lender before you miss payments. Arrangements to pay can sometimes be recorded differently from missed payments.

## Myths

- **"Checking my own report harms my score."** In most systems, checking your own report is a soft search and does not affect lenders' decisions.
- **"Everyone has one universal score."** Different agencies and lenders use different scores.
- **"Earning more money raises my score."** Income is usually not part of the credit report itself, although lenders consider it separately.
- **"Having no credit at all is perfect."** With no history, lenders have little information, which can make approval harder.
- **"Paying for credit repair fixes everything."** Accurate negative information usually cannot be removed simply by paying a company.

## How to check your report

In many countries, you have a legal right to see the information held about you, often free of charge, and some agencies provide free access to your report and score online.

1. **Find out which credit reference agencies operate in your country.** Official consumer or financial regulator websites usually list them.
2. **Request your report** from each one, as they may hold slightly different information.
3. **Read it carefully**: personal details, accounts, payment history and searches.
4. **Note anything you do not recognise.**

Checking once a year, and before a major application such as a mortgage or car loan, is a sensible habit. Keep copies with your financial papers; our guide to a [household paperwork system](/guides/household-paperwork-system/) explains how long to keep different documents.

## Correcting mistakes

Errors happen: an old address, an account that was paid off but still shows a balance, a missed payment that was actually made on time.

1. **Gather evidence**, such as statements or letters.
2. **Contact the agency and the lender**, explaining the error in writing.
3. **Keep copies** of everything you send and receive.
4. **Follow up** if the correction is not made within the stated time.
5. **Use the complaints process** or an independent ombudsman if the problem is not resolved.

In some systems, you can also add a short **explanatory note** to your report about circumstances such as illness or redundancy.

## Identity fraud: warning signs

Check your report for:

- accounts or applications you did not make;
- addresses where you have never lived;
- searches by companies you have not contacted.

If you see these, contact the agency and the lender immediately, change passwords for important accounts and report it to the police or the national fraud reporting service in your country. Protect your personal information, shred documents with financial details and be wary of messages asking for personal data. ## Credit repair and "guaranteed approval" scams

Be very cautious with companies that:

- promise to remove accurate negative information from your report;
- charge fees in advance for things you can do yourself for free, such as requesting your report or correcting errors;
- offer "guaranteed" loans regardless of your history, often with high fees;
- ask you to create a new identity or use a different address to hide your history, which can be illegal.

Free, impartial **debt advice services** in many countries can help if you are struggling with debt, without charging fees.

## Building credit history from scratch

If you have little or no credit history:

- check whether regular bills, such as phone contracts, are reported in your country;
- consider a basic credit product designed for building history, used for small purchases and paid off in full each month;
- avoid high-cost borrowing just to build history;
- be patient: a good record takes time to build.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does negative information stay on my report?

It depends on the country and the type of information. Official guidance from your national regulator or the credit reference agencies explains the rules.

### Does a joint account link me to my partner's credit history?

In some countries, yes. If you separate, you may be able to ask the agencies to remove the financial link.

### Will paying off a debt remove it from my report?

The account usually remains, showing that it was paid. A history of debts repaid as agreed is generally positive.

## The bottom line

A credit report records how you have managed credit, and a credit score is a number calculated from it; different agencies and lenders use different scores. Paying on time, keeping balances well below limits, avoiding many applications at once and keeping your details correct are widely recognised good habits. Check your report with each agency in your country at least once a year and before big applications, correct mistakes in writing, watch for signs of identity fraud and avoid companies that charge to "repair" credit or promise guaranteed approval.
