There is a popular idea that making everything from scratch is always cheaper. Sometimes it is dramatically cheaper. Sometimes it costs about the same as the shop-bought version once you count the ingredients, the energy and the time. And occasionally, homemade is more expensive, especially when it involves specialist ingredients or a long time in the oven. The trick is knowing which staples are worth making yourself and which are better bought.

This guide looks at the everyday basics that many households buy ready-made, explains how to work out whether making them at home saves money, and offers simple methods for the ones that usually do. It also covers food safety, because some homemade foods need more care than others.

## How to tell whether homemade is cheaper

For any staple, compare the cost of making a batch with the cost of buying the same amount:

1. **Add up the ingredients** you would use for one batch. Use the unit prices on shelf labels; our guide to [unit pricing](/guides/unit-pricing-at-the-supermarket/) shows how.
2. **Consider the energy.** An hour in a full-size oven costs more than a few minutes on the hob. Batch baking, or cooking several things at once, spreads that cost.
3. **Think about the time.** Your time has value too. A staple that takes five minutes of hands-on work is easy to fit in; one that needs constant attention is harder.
4. **Count the waste.** Homemade foods often keep for less time than shop-bought ones with preservatives. If half a batch goes off, the saving disappears.
5. **Compare like with like.** Homemade bread is cheaper than an artisan loaf, but may not be cheaper than the most basic supermarket sliced bread.

Doing this once for each staple tells you where your effort pays off.

## Staples that usually save money

### Stock

Vegetable peelings, onion ends, herb stalks and the bones from a roast chicken make excellent stock for very little extra cost. Keep a bag in the freezer and add scraps as you cook. When it is full, cover the contents with water, simmer gently for an hour or two, strain, cool quickly and freeze in portions. Homemade stock is often far cheaper than cubes or cartons of good quality, and it uses food that would otherwise be thrown away. It fits naturally with the ideas in our guide to [cutting food waste at home](/guides/reduce-food-waste-at-home/).

### Salad dressings

A basic dressing is three parts oil to one part vinegar or lemon juice, with a little mustard, salt and pepper, shaken in a jar. It costs a fraction of most bottled dressings and keeps in the fridge for a week or more.

### Pasta and tomato sauces

A sauce made from tinned tomatoes, an onion, garlic and dried herbs is usually much cheaper than a jar of ready-made sauce, and it freezes well. Make a large batch and freeze it in portions for quick dinners.

### Hummus and bean dips

Cooked or tinned chickpeas, a spoonful of tahini or oil, lemon juice, garlic and water, blended together, make a large bowl of hummus for less than a small tub. Other beans work too.

### Granola and muesli

Oats, a few nuts or seeds and a little dried fruit, mixed yourself, are often cheaper than branded cereals. For granola, toast the oats with a little oil and honey in the oven, ideally when the oven is already on for something else.

### Flatbreads and pizza bases

Simple flatbreads made from flour, water, salt and a little oil cook in a dry pan in minutes, with no oven needed. Pizza dough made at home is usually much cheaper than ready-made bases.

## Staples that may or may not save money

### Bread

Homemade bread can be cheaper than bakery bread, but often costs about the same as the cheapest supermarket loaves once you add the energy of the oven. It is worth making if:

- you bake several loaves at once and freeze some;
- you value the taste, texture and lack of additives;
- you already have the oven on for other dishes.

### Yoghurt

Yoghurt can be made from milk and a spoonful of live yoghurt as a starter, kept warm for several hours. Whether it saves money depends on the price of milk compared with yoghurt in your area, and on whether you have a way to keep it warm without using much energy, such as a vacuum flask or an insulated box. It also needs careful hygiene: clean equipment, fresh milk and a reliable starter.

### Jam and preserves

Jam is worth making when you have free or cheap fruit, from a garden, a glut or a reduced-price box. With bought fruit at full price and the cost of sugar and jars, it is often more expensive than shop-bought jam.

## Staples that rarely save money

- **Specialist breads and pastries** that need expensive flours, butter and long baking times.
- **Nut butters**, unless you can buy nuts cheaply in bulk; nuts are expensive, and a strong blender is needed.
- **Plant milks**, which can work out cheaper or more expensive depending on the ingredient, and are often not fortified with the vitamins and minerals added to shop-bought versions.
- **Anything needing equipment you do not already own**, unless you will use it often.

## Food safety: where to be careful

Homemade staples are safe when a few rules are followed:

- **Cool cooked foods quickly** and refrigerate or freeze them within the time recommended by your local food safety authority.
- **Label and date everything** in the fridge and freezer.
- **Keep equipment clean**, especially for yoghurt and fermented foods.
- **Be very cautious with home canning and bottling.** Low-acid foods such as vegetables, meat and beans can carry serious risks if they are bottled at home without proper equipment and tested methods. If you want to preserve food this way, follow tested recipes from a reputable food safety source exactly.
- **Garlic or herbs in oil** stored at room temperature can be risky; keep such mixtures in the fridge and use them quickly, or freeze them.

If in doubt, freezing is the simplest and safest way to keep homemade staples.

## A simple cost comparison you can do yourself

You do not need a spreadsheet to compare homemade and shop-bought. Take one staple, for example tomato sauce, and write down:

1. **The shop-bought price** for the amount you normally use in a week, worked out from the unit price on the label.
2. **The ingredients for a homemade batch** of the same size: the tins of tomatoes, the onion, the garlic, the oil and the herbs, each costed by the share you actually use.
3. **The energy**, roughly: how long the hob or oven is on.
4. **Your time**: how many minutes of hands-on work it takes.

Repeat the comparison for two or three staples you buy often. Most households quickly find one or two items where the saving is large and the effort small, and those are the ones worth making a habit. Write them on a list inside a cupboard door, so the decision is made once rather than every week.

## Fitting it into a busy week

Making staples does not have to take over your weekend. A few strategies help:

- **Pair tasks.** While a sauce simmers, make a dressing and blend some hummus.
- **Use the oven fully.** When the oven is on for dinner, add a tray of granola or a batch of flatbreads.
- **Cook once, freeze many times.** A large pot of sauce or stock can supply weeks of meals. Our guide to [batch cooking and freezing](/guides/batch-cooking-and-freezing/) explains how to organise the freezer.
- **Choose two or three staples** to make regularly rather than trying to make everything.
- **Keep basic ingredients in stock**: oats, flour, tinned tomatoes, dried beans, oil and vinegar. Our guide to [pantry staples that save money](/guides/pantry-staples-that-save-money/) lists the most useful ones.

## Equipment worth having

Most staples need only ordinary kitchen equipment. A few items help:

- a **large pot** for stock and sauces;
- a **blender or hand blender** for dips and soups;
- **jars and freezer containers** for storage;
- **labels and a marker pen**.

Buy second-hand where possible; our guide to [buying second-hand safely](/guides/buying-secondhand-safely/) explains what to check.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is homemade always healthier?

Not automatically, but it lets you control the salt, sugar and fat. A homemade sauce with lots of oil and salt is not healthier than a carefully chosen bought one.

### How long do homemade staples keep?

It varies. Dressings and dips usually keep a few days in the fridge, sauces and stock a few days chilled or several months frozen. Follow your local food safety advice and label everything with the date.

### Is making bread worth buying a bread machine?

Only if you will use it often. Compare the price of the machine with the saving per loaf, and remember the cost of the electricity it uses.

## The bottom line

Making staples at home saves money when the ingredients are cheap, the energy and time needed are modest and nothing goes to waste. Stock from scraps, dressings, tomato sauces, hummus, granola and flatbreads are usually clear winners. Bread, yoghurt and jam can go either way, depending on local prices and how you make them. Specialist bakes, nut butters and anything requiring new equipment rarely pay off. Compare the cost of a batch with the shop-bought equivalent, cook in batches, use the oven fully, follow food safety rules carefully, especially for preserving, and choose the two or three staples that suit your household best.
