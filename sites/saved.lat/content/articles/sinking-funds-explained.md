Many of the expenses that knock a budget off course are not really emergencies at all. The car insurance renews every year. Birthdays and festivals come round on the same dates. Children need new school shoes each autumn. The boiler needs servicing, the car needs tyres eventually, and glasses or dental treatment are needed from time to time. Because these costs do not arrive every month, they are easy to forget — until they appear all at once and land on a credit card.

A **sinking fund** is a simple solution: you save a small amount every month for a specific future expense, so the money is ready when the bill arrives. This guide explains how sinking funds work, how to set them up, and how they can make budgeting much smoother.

This article provides general information about saving and budgeting, not personal financial advice.

## What is a sinking fund?

A sinking fund is money set aside regularly for a planned or predictable expense. The term originally described funds that businesses and governments used to repay debts gradually, but in household finance it simply means saving ahead.

For example, if your car insurance costs 600 a year and renews in twelve months, putting aside 50 a month means the full amount is ready on time. When the bill comes, you pay it from the sinking fund — and your monthly budget is untouched.

## Sinking funds versus an emergency fund

The two are related but different:

- **An emergency fund** covers the unexpected: job loss, urgent repairs, sudden medical costs or other genuine emergencies. You do not know when or whether you will need it. See our guide to emergency funds.
- **A sinking fund** covers expected costs: bills, purchases and events you know are coming, even if the exact amount varies.

Keeping them separate matters. If you use your emergency fund for predictable costs like holidays or Christmas presents, it may be empty when a real emergency happens.

## What to use sinking funds for

Common sinking funds include:

### Annual and periodic bills

- Car insurance, vehicle tax and servicing.
- Home and contents insurance, if paid annually.
- Annual subscriptions and memberships.
- Professional fees, licences and renewals.
- TV licences or similar annual charges where they apply.
- Property maintenance charges.

### Predictable but irregular costs

- Car repairs and tyres.
- Home maintenance: boiler servicing, gutter cleaning, decorating.
- Replacing appliances and electronics.
- Dental check-ups, glasses and contact lenses.
- Pet costs such as vaccinations, check-ups and grooming.
- Clothing and shoes.
- Haircuts if you have them infrequently.

### Events and occasions

- Birthdays and anniversaries.
- Festivals and religious holidays.
- Weddings, christenings and celebrations you are invited to.
- School trips, uniforms and activities.
- Holidays and travel.

### Larger planned purchases

- A new car or bike.
- New furniture.
- A new phone or laptop.
- Home improvements.

## How to calculate your sinking fund amounts

1. **List your irregular expenses.** Go through the last twelve months of bank statements and note every cost that does not happen monthly. Add anything you know is coming.
2. **Estimate the cost of each.** Use last year's figure and adjust for expected price increases. For variable costs like car repairs, use an average of recent years or a realistic estimate.
3. **Note when each is due.** Some are annual; others may be due in a few months or a few years.
4. **Divide the cost by the number of months until it is due.**

**Monthly saving = expected cost ÷ months until due**

### Example

| Expense | Cost | Due in | Monthly saving |
|---|---|---|---|
| Car insurance | 600 | 12 months | 50 |
| Car service and tyres | 420 | 12 months | 35 |
| Birthdays and festivals | 720 | 12 months | 60 |
| Holiday | 1,200 | 10 months | 120 |
| New laptop | 900 | 24 months | 37.50 |
| **Total** | | | **302.50** |

The figures are illustrative; your own list will be different. The total may look large, but these costs exist whether or not you plan for them. Sinking funds simply spread them evenly across the year.

### If a bill is due soon

If an expense is due in only a few months, you will need to save more each month to catch up, or accept that you will cover part of it from your monthly budget this time. Next year, the fund will be on track from the start.

## Organising your sinking funds

You can manage sinking funds in several ways:

### Separate savings pots or accounts

Many banks and savings apps let you create multiple named pots within one account — "Car", "Christmas", "Holiday", "Home repairs". You can see exactly how much is available for each purpose. This is the simplest method for most people.

### One savings account with a tracker

Keep all sinking fund money in one savings account and use a spreadsheet or budgeting app to track how much belongs to each fund. This works well if your bank does not offer pots, but requires careful record-keeping.

### Envelopes

Some people keep cash envelopes for certain categories. This can work for small funds, but holding large amounts of cash at home carries risks, and the money earns no interest.

### Choosing where to keep the money

Sinking fund money should be:

- **Easy to access** when the bill arrives.
- **Separate from your everyday account**, so it is not spent accidentally.
- **Safe**, held with a regulated bank or savings provider.

An easy-access savings account that pays some interest is often a sensible choice. Avoid tying sinking fund money up in investments or accounts with notice periods if you might need it soon.

## Automating your sinking funds

Automation makes sinking funds work:

- **Set up a standing order or automatic transfer** from your current account to your sinking fund account on payday.
- **Treat it as a bill**, not optional saving.
- **Review the amounts yearly**, or whenever prices change.

## Using the money

When an expense arrives:

1. **Pay it from the relevant sinking fund.**
2. **If there is money left over**, either leave it in the fund for next time, move it to another fund that is short, or put it towards a savings goal.
3. **If the fund is short**, cover the difference from your monthly budget if possible, and increase the monthly saving for next time.

## Starting when money is tight

If your budget is tight, you may not be able to fund every sinking fund at once. Prioritise:

1. **Essential bills** that would cause problems if unpaid, such as car insurance or vehicle tax if you depend on a car.
2. **Costs that would otherwise go on credit**, such as car repairs or festival spending.
3. **Planned purchases and holidays**, which can be delayed if needed.

Even small amounts help. Saving a little towards Christmas from January onwards makes December much less stressful than trying to find everything at once.

## Common questions

**How many sinking funds should I have?** As many as are useful, but not so many that they become hard to track. Grouping small items — for example, a single "annual subscriptions" fund — keeps things manageable.

**What if I overestimate a cost?** The surplus can be rolled into next year's fund or moved to savings or debt repayment.

**Should I use sinking funds if I am in debt?** Sinking funds can actually help with debt, because they stop predictable costs from adding to credit card balances. Many people balance modest sinking fund contributions with debt repayments. If you have serious debt, a free debt advice service can help you prioritise.

**Is this the same as saving for a goal?** Saving for a goal, such as a house deposit, is a long-term version of the same idea. The main difference is that sinking funds are usually for regular, recurring costs.

## A sinking fund calendar

It helps to map irregular costs across the year, so you can see when the heavy months fall. Many households find that a few months are particularly expensive — for example, late summer with school uniforms and holidays, and the end of the year with festivals and winter bills. A simple calendar might look like this:

- **January–March:** winter energy bills, annual subscriptions renewing after the new year, birthdays.
- **April–June:** car tax or servicing, garden and home maintenance, early holiday payments.
- **July–September:** summer holidays, school uniforms and shoes, back-to-school costs.
- **October–December:** heating, festival gifts and food, insurance renewals.

Your own calendar will differ. Once you can see it, you can check that your sinking funds will be large enough by each peak, and increase contributions earlier in the year if one month looks especially heavy.

## Sinking funds and peace of mind

Perhaps the biggest benefit of sinking funds is emotional. When the car insurance renewal arrives, or the children need new shoes, there is no panic and no need to borrow. The money is already there, waiting. That predictability makes the rest of your budget easier to manage, because each month looks much more like the last.

## A quick start plan

1. Go through twelve months of statements and list every irregular cost.
2. Estimate the cost and due date of each.
3. Calculate the monthly amount for each fund.
4. Set up savings pots or a tracking spreadsheet.
5. Automate transfers on payday.
6. Pay irregular bills from the funds when they arrive.
7. Review the amounts every year.

## The bottom line

Sinking funds turn irregular, predictable expenses from budget-breaking surprises into planned, manageable costs. By saving a little each month for car bills, birthdays, holidays and repairs, you protect your emergency fund, avoid unnecessary borrowing and make your monthly budget far more stable. It is one of the simplest budgeting tools — and one of the most effective.
