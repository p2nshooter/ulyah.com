Energy bills are some of the most important documents a household receives, and some of the least read. Many people glance at the total, check that it is roughly what they expected and file it away. Yet the bill contains everything you need to understand your energy use, compare tariffs, spot mistakes and plan savings. Once you know where to look, it takes only a few minutes.

This guide explains the sections you will find on most electricity and gas bills, what the key terms mean, how to tell whether your bill is based on a real meter reading, how different tariffs work, how to check for errors and how to turn the information into practical savings. Bills differ from country to country and supplier to supplier, but the basic elements are very similar.

## The main parts of a bill

Most bills include:

- **Your details:** name, supply address and account number.
- **The billing period:** the dates the bill covers.
- **Meter readings:** the previous and current readings, and whether each is actual or estimated.
- **Your usage:** how many units you used in the period, usually in kilowatt-hours (kWh).
- **The charges:** the unit rate, any fixed or standing charge, taxes and other items.
- **Your balance:** whether you owe money or are in credit.
- **Your tariff:** the name of your plan, its prices and, if it is fixed, when it ends.
- **Payment details:** how and when you pay.

Keep at least the last year of bills, or make sure you can access them online. They are useful for comparing tariffs and checking seasonal patterns. Our guide to a [household paperwork system](/guides/household-paperwork-system/) explains how long to keep different documents.

## Units: what a kilowatt-hour is

Electricity is usually charged by the **kilowatt-hour (kWh)**. One kWh is the energy used by an appliance with a power of 1,000 watts running for one hour. A 100-watt device running for ten hours also uses 1 kWh.

Gas is often measured by volume at the meter, in cubic metres or cubic feet, and then converted into kWh on the bill using standard conversion figures. You do not need to do the conversion yourself, but it explains why the number on your gas meter does not match the kWh on the bill.

Knowing your kWh helps you:

- compare your use with previous months and years;
- estimate the cost of running a particular appliance;
- compare tariffs fairly, because prices are quoted per kWh.

Our guide to [energy-efficient cooking](/guides/energy-efficient-cooking/) shows how to estimate the running cost of an appliance from its power rating.

## The charges

### Unit rate

The price you pay for each kWh. On some tariffs it changes by time of day or season.

### Standing charge or fixed charge

Many suppliers add a **fixed daily or monthly charge** that you pay regardless of how much energy you use. It covers costs such as maintaining the network and meters. Because it is fixed, a household that uses very little energy may find that the standing charge makes up a large part of its bill. When comparing tariffs, always look at both the unit rate and the standing charge.

### Taxes and levies

Bills often include taxes, such as value-added tax, and other government or regulatory charges. These are usually set by law, not by your supplier.

### Discounts and credits

Some bills show discounts for paying by a particular method, receiving paperless bills or being part of a support scheme.

## Actual or estimated readings

This is one of the most important things to check. Next to each reading, bills usually show whether it is:

- **Actual (A):** taken from the meter by you, a meter reader or a smart meter.
- **Estimated (E):** calculated by the supplier from your past usage.

Estimated bills can be too high or too low. If they are too low for months, you may face a large catch-up bill later; if they are too high, you are lending money to your supplier. To avoid this:

- **submit your own meter readings** regularly, as often as your supplier allows;
- **photograph the meter** when you read it, with the date visible, in case of disputes;
- **check that your reading appears** on the next bill.

Smart meters send readings automatically, but it is still worth checking that the bill shows actual readings.

## Types of tariff

The names vary by country and supplier, but most tariffs fall into a few types:

| Tariff type | How it works | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed | the unit rate and standing charge stay the same for a set period | households that want predictable bills |
| Variable | prices can rise or fall, usually with notice | those who expect prices to fall, or want flexibility |
| Time-of-use | cheaper at certain times, such as overnight | households that can shift use, such as laundry or electric car charging |
| Prepayment | you pay in advance, by card, key or app | those who want tight control; sometimes more expensive |

Check whether your tariff has an **end date** and whether there are **exit fees** for leaving early. Many people are moved to a more expensive default tariff when a fixed deal ends without realising it.

## How to check your bill for mistakes

Billing errors are not common, but they happen. Each time a bill arrives:

1. **Check the name, address and account number.**
2. **Compare the meter readings** with the meter itself, or with your own photographs.
3. **Check whether the readings are actual or estimated.**
4. **Check the dates** of the billing period, to make sure there is no overlap or gap with the previous bill.
5. **Check the unit rate and standing charge** against your tariff.
6. **Compare the usage** with the same period last year. A sudden jump may mean an estimate, a faulty appliance or a meter problem.
7. **Check that your payments** have been recorded.

If something looks wrong, contact your supplier in writing, keep a copy of your complaint and note the date. Many countries have an independent energy ombudsman or regulator that handles complaints if the supplier does not resolve them.

## Using your bill to save money

### Understand your pattern

Look at your usage over a year. Most households use more energy in winter for heating and lighting, or in summer for cooling. Knowing your peaks helps you target savings. Our guides to [staying warm for less](/guides/lower-heating-bills-in-winter/) and [keeping cool without a huge electricity bill](/guides/keep-cool-without-air-conditioning-costs/) cover the biggest seasonal costs.

### Find the background load

Read your meter late at night, when almost everything is off, and again an hour later. The difference shows your **background use**: fridges, freezers, routers and devices on standby. Our guide to [standby power](/guides/standby-power-and-phantom-loads/) explains how to find and cut it.

### Compare tariffs once a year

Use your annual kWh, not just your monthly bill, to compare offers. A tariff with a lower unit rate but a higher standing charge may suit a large household but not a small one.

### Spread the cost

If bills vary a lot through the year, a regular monthly payment based on your annual use can make budgeting easier. Check it regularly so that you do not build up a large debt or a large credit. Our guide to [sinking funds](/guides/sinking-funds-explained/) offers another way to prepare for high-bill months.

## If you are struggling to pay

- **Contact your supplier early.** Many have payment plans, hardship funds or support schemes.
- **Ask about support** from government or local programmes for energy costs.
- **Seek free, independent advice** from a consumer or debt advice service in your country.
- **Do not ignore the bill.** Problems are easier to solve when tackled early.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why did my bill go up when I used less energy?

Possible reasons include a price rise, the end of a fixed tariff, a previous estimated bill that was too low, or a higher standing charge. Check the readings, the dates and the prices.

### Should I pay a bill I think is wrong?

Contact your supplier straight away and explain why you think it is wrong. Ask how to handle payment while the query is resolved, and keep records of every contact.

### Do smart meters save money?

They do not save money by themselves, but they make it easier to see your usage and avoid estimated bills, which can help you change habits.

You will find more ways to bring household bills down in the [Home & Energy](/topics/home-and-energy/) section.

## The bottom line

Your energy bill tells you how much energy you used, what you pay per unit, what you pay regardless of use, which tariff you are on and whether the readings are real or estimated. Read it every time: check the readings, the dates, the prices and your payments, submit your own meter readings to avoid estimates, and note when a fixed tariff ends. Use your annual usage to compare tariffs fairly, look at your seasonal pattern and background load to target savings, and contact your supplier early if anything is wrong or if paying becomes difficult.
