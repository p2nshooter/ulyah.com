Big family gatherings are some of the moments people remember most: a holiday meal around a crowded table, a reunion with cousins who live far away, a long lunch for a grandparent's birthday. They are also some of the most expensive occasions in the household calendar, and the cost often falls on whoever offers to host. Food, drinks, extra chairs, decorations and the pressure to make everything perfect can turn a happy occasion into a stressful bill.

This guide explains how to host a family gathering that feels generous and welcoming without overspending: setting a budget, sharing the cost and the work, planning a menu that feeds many people well, shopping smartly, handling drinks and decorations, making the most of leftovers and keeping the atmosphere relaxed.

## Start with a budget and a guest list

Before planning food or invitations, decide two things:

1. **How much you can comfortably spend.** Include food, drinks, any rented or borrowed items, decorations and extra energy for cooking.
2. **How many people will come.** The guest list drives almost every cost.

If the budget is tight, a smaller gathering, a lunch rather than a dinner or a different format, such as an afternoon tea or a picnic, can be just as memorable. Our guide to [sinking funds](/guides/sinking-funds-explained/) explains how to save ahead for occasions you know are coming every year.

## Share the load

The most effective way to keep costs down is to stop expecting one household to pay for everything.

### Shared dishes

Ask each household to bring something:

- assign **categories**, such as a starter, a side dish, a dessert or drinks, so you do not end up with five desserts and no vegetables;
- keep a **simple shared list**, on paper or in a group message, of who is bringing what;
- the host provides the **main dish** and the space.

Shared dishes also spread the cooking work and let people show off family recipes.

### Shared costs

For larger events, such as reunions, consider:

- asking each household to contribute an agreed amount to a shared fund;
- assigning someone to collect contributions and keep receipts;
- being clear and fair, so no one feels taken advantage of.

### Shared work

Ask people to help with setting up, serving, washing up and tidying. Many guests are happy to help when asked, and it makes the gathering feel more like a shared event than a performance.

## Plan the menu

### Choose dishes that feed many

- **Stews, curries, chilli, bakes and roasts** stretch well and can be prepared in advance.
- **Grains and potatoes** make filling, inexpensive side dishes.
- **Seasonal vegetables** are usually cheaper and taste better.
- **Big salads** with beans, lentils or grains are colourful and filling.
- **Home-baked desserts**, such as a tray bake or a fruit crumble, cost less than shop-bought cakes for many people.

Our guide to [affordable protein](/guides/cheap-protein-sources/) has ideas for the most expensive part of most meals.

### Plan quantities carefully

Over-catering is one of the biggest sources of waste. Think about:

- how many adults and children are coming;
- how many dishes there will be in total, including what guests bring;
- whether people will eat a full meal or graze over the afternoon.

When there are many dishes, people take smaller portions of each. Plan for that.

### Cater for different needs

Ask in advance about allergies, dietary requirements and religious or cultural food rules. It is easier and cheaper to plan one or two dishes that suit everyone than to make separate meals at the last minute.

## Shop smartly

- **Make a detailed list** from the menu and check what you already have.
- **Buy staples in advance**, when they are on offer, and fresh items a day or two before.
- **Compare unit prices** on large packs; our guide to [unit pricing](/guides/unit-pricing-at-the-supermarket/) explains how.
- **Consider bulk buying** for items you will use up, such as rice or drinks, but avoid large packs of things that will go to waste. Our guide to [bulk buying without the waste](/guides/bulk-buying-without-waste/) helps.
- **Use frozen vegetables and fruit**, which are often cheaper and cause less waste.

## Cook ahead

- **Prepare dishes that freeze well** a week or two before, such as sauces, stews and some desserts.
- **Chop vegetables** and prepare cold dishes the day before.
- **Plan oven time** carefully if you have only one oven; our guide to [energy-efficient cooking](/guides/energy-efficient-cooking/) has tips on using the oven fully.
- **Keep food safe**: cool cooked dishes quickly, keep cold food in the fridge, and reheat thoroughly.

## Drinks

Drinks can quietly become one of the biggest costs.

- **Offer a simple selection** rather than a full bar.
- **Make a large jug** of a homemade drink, such as lemonade, iced tea or a fruit punch.
- **Serve tap water** in jugs with lemon or mint.
- **Ask guests to bring drinks** as their contribution, if that suits your family's customs.

## Seating, tableware and decorations

- **Borrow chairs, tables and serving dishes** from family and neighbours instead of renting.
- **Use what you have**: mismatched plates and glasses can look charming.
- **Avoid single-use plates and cutlery** where possible; they cost money every time and create waste. If you do use them, choose recyclable or compostable options.
- **Decorate simply**: seasonal flowers or branches from the garden, candles, fabric you already own, or children's drawings.
- **Reuse decorations** each year, stored carefully.

## Activities

Gatherings are about the people, not just the food. Low-cost ideas include:

- old family photos to look through;
- a walk after the meal;
- board games and card games;
- a family quiz;
- children's games in the garden or a nearby park.

Our guide to [free and low-cost activities with kids](/guides/free-and-low-cost-activities-with-kids/) has more ideas.

## Leftovers

- **Provide containers** or ask guests to bring their own, so leftovers can be shared rather than wasted.
- **Refrigerate leftovers promptly**, within the time recommended by your local food safety guidance.
- **Plan a leftover meal** for the next day.
- **Freeze** what you cannot eat in the next couple of days. Our guide to [cutting food waste at home](/guides/reduce-food-waste-at-home/) explains how.

## A sample timeline for a shared holiday meal

Spreading the work over a few weeks keeps both the cost and the stress down.

**Three to four weeks before**

- confirm the date, the guest list and the budget;
- agree who is bringing which dish, and write it down in a shared list;
- check what you need to borrow, such as chairs, tables, large pans and serving dishes.

**Two weeks before**

- buy long-life items when they are on offer: rice, flour, tins, drinks;
- cook and freeze dishes that keep well, such as sauces and some desserts.

**A few days before**

- buy fresh ingredients;
- collect borrowed items;
- plan the cooking order and oven times on paper.

**The day before**

- prepare vegetables, cold dishes and desserts;
- set the table and arrange seating;
- clear space in the fridge for dishes guests will bring.

**On the day**

- cook the main dish;
- put out a few simple snacks so no one is waiting hungry;
- ask for help with serving and washing up;
- send leftovers home with guests in containers.

## Gatherings away from home

If no one has a home big enough, consider:

- **a picnic in a park**, where everyone brings food and blankets;
- **a community hall** or a room at a place of worship, often available at modest cost; check booking rules, cleaning requirements and whether you can bring your own food;
- **a beach or riverside gathering**, following local rules on fires and barbecues.

Outdoor gatherings need a backup plan for bad weather, but can be among the most relaxed and affordable.

## Keeping it relaxed

- **Let go of perfection.** People remember the company and the atmosphere, not whether every dish matched a magazine photo.
- **Prepare as much as possible in advance**, so you can enjoy the day.
- **Accept help.**
- **Rotate hosting** between households each year, so the cost and the work are shared over time.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it rude to ask guests to bring food?

In many families it is completely normal and appreciated. Explain the plan warmly and give people a choice of what to bring.

### How do I handle relatives who expect a lavish event?

Be honest and positive: explain that you would love to host and that sharing dishes means everyone can enjoy the day without anyone carrying the whole cost.

### What is the cheapest way to feed a large group?

Dishes based on grains, pulses, potatoes and seasonal vegetables, with a main dish that stretches well, such as a stew, curry or bake, are usually the most economical.

## The bottom line

A family gathering can be generous and memorable without a huge bill. Set a budget and guest list first, share the cost and the work with shared dishes and clear lists, and plan a menu built on dishes that feed many people well. Shop with a list, cook ahead, keep drinks and decorations simple, borrow what you need, plan for leftovers and keep food safe. Above all, relax: the company is what people remember.
