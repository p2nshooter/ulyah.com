For many households, a car is the second-largest purchase after a home. Buying a used one can save a great deal compared with buying new, because cars tend to lose a large part of their value in their first years. But a used car can also hide expensive problems, an unclear history or outright fraud. The difference between a good buy and a costly mistake usually comes down to doing the checks in the right order and not being rushed.

This guide walks through the whole process: setting a realistic budget that includes running costs, choosing the right type of car, deciding between a dealer and a private seller, checking paperwork and history, inspecting the car, taking a test drive, getting an independent inspection, negotiating and paying safely. Rules and documents vary from country to country, so check the official guidance where you live as you go.

## Step 1: set a budget that includes running costs

The purchase price is only part of the cost. Before you look at cars, estimate the yearly cost of:

- **insurance**, which can vary a lot by model, engine size, your age and where you live;
- **road tax or registration fees**;
- **fuel or charging**, based on your typical mileage;
- **servicing and maintenance**, including tyres, brakes and regular inspections;
- **parking**, tolls and any charges in your area;
- **depreciation**, the value the car loses each year.

Get insurance quotes for the models you are considering **before** you buy; it can change your choice. Keep some money aside for unexpected repairs after purchase. Our guide to [emergency fund basics](/guides/emergency-fund-basics/) explains how to build a buffer, and our guide to [sinking funds](/guides/sinking-funds-explained/) shows how to save for regular car costs.

If you plan to borrow to buy, compare the **total cost of credit**, not just the monthly payment. Our guide to [understanding interest on debt](/guides/understanding-interest-on-debt/) explains how.

## Step 2: choose the right type of car

- **What do you really need?** Number of seats, boot space, typical journeys, parking at home.
- **Fuel type:** petrol, diesel, hybrid or electric, depending on your mileage, local fuel and electricity prices, charging access and any rules on older vehicles in your area.
- **Reliability:** look at independent reliability surveys and owners' forums for the models you like. Some models are known for specific problems at certain ages.
- **Parts and servicing:** common models are usually cheaper to repair.

Make a shortlist of two or three models rather than one.

## Step 3: dealer, private seller or auction

| Where | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Franchised or large dealer | inspected cars, warranties, easier complaints | higher prices |
| Independent dealer | wider range, lower prices than franchised dealers | quality varies; check reviews |
| Private seller | often the lowest prices | fewer legal protections; more risk |
| Auction | potential bargains | little time to inspect; for experienced buyers |
| Online platforms | wide choice | check the seller carefully; beware of scams |

When you buy from a business, you usually have more legal rights than when you buy privately. Find out what applies where you live.

## Step 4: check the paperwork and history

Before travelling to see a car, ask the seller for the registration number and key details, and check:

- **Registration and ownership documents:** the name and address should match the seller and the place where you view the car.
- **Identification numbers:** the vehicle identification number on the documents should match the number on the car, usually found on the windscreen and in the door frame or engine bay.
- **Service history:** stamps, invoices and receipts showing regular servicing.
- **Inspection or roadworthiness certificates**, where your country requires them, and their history if it is available online.
- **Mileage:** check that it is consistent with the age of the car and with past records. Unusually low mileage with heavy wear is a warning sign.
- **Recalls:** check the manufacturer's or your national authority's recall information for the model.
- **Outstanding finance, theft or write-off records:** in many countries, a paid vehicle history check can show whether the car is still owed on a loan, has been reported stolen or has been written off after an accident.

If the seller cannot or will not provide documents, walk away.

## Step 5: inspect the car

View the car **in daylight and in dry weather**, ideally at the seller's home address rather than a car park. Bring someone with you if possible.

### Outside

- **Rust**, especially around wheel arches, sills and underneath.
- **Panel gaps and paint**: uneven gaps or slight colour differences can indicate accident repairs.
- **Tyres**: tread depth, uneven wear and whether all four match. Uneven wear can suggest alignment or suspension problems.
- **Glass and lights**: cracks, chips and working lights.

### Inside

- **Wear** on the seats, steering wheel and pedals consistent with the mileage.
- **Damp** or musty smells, which can mean leaks.
- **Electrics**: windows, mirrors, locks, air conditioning, heating, lights, wipers and dashboard.
- **Warning lights**: all should come on briefly when you start the car and then go out.

### Under the bonnet

- **Oil level and colour**, and signs of leaks.
- **Coolant level** and any residue.
- **Belts and hoses** for cracks.
- **Ask to start the engine from cold.** Some problems are hidden once the engine is warm. Be cautious if the engine has been run just before you arrive.

You do not need to be a mechanic to notice obvious problems. If anything worries you, ask an expert.

## Step 6: take a test drive

Check that you are **insured** to drive the car first. During the drive:

- listen for **unusual noises** from the engine, suspension or brakes;
- check that the car **drives straight** and the steering wheel is centred;
- test the **brakes** firmly in a safe place;
- check the **gears or clutch** for smooth changes, or a smooth automatic transmission;
- drive at **different speeds**, including on a faster road if possible;
- watch for **smoke** from the exhaust and for warning lights.

## Step 7: get an independent inspection

For any car that represents a large sum of money to you, an **independent inspection** by a qualified mechanic or a vehicle inspection service is worth considering. It can reveal problems that a buyer would not see, and the report can help you negotiate or decide to walk away.

## Extra checks for hybrid and electric cars

- **Battery health:** ask for a battery health report, which some manufacturers and garages can produce, and check whether any battery warranty is still valid and transferable.
- **Range:** compare the range shown on a full charge with what the model offered when new, bearing in mind that cold weather and driving style affect it.
- **Charging:** check that the charging cables are included and that the car charges correctly. Make sure you will have somewhere practical and affordable to charge it.
- **Software:** ask whether the car's software has been updated, and whether any connected services require a subscription.

## Step 8: negotiate

- **Research prices** for the same model, age, mileage and condition.
- **Use what you found**: worn tyres, an upcoming service or a missing document are reasonable grounds for a lower price.
- **Stay calm and polite**, and be ready to walk away.
- **Do not be rushed** by claims that other buyers are waiting.

## Step 9: pay safely and complete the paperwork

- **Avoid carrying large amounts of cash.** Use a traceable payment method, and do not pay until you have the keys, the documents and the car.
- **Never pay a deposit** for a car you have not seen, and be very wary of sellers who are "abroad" or who insist on an unusual payment or delivery service. These are common scams. Our guide to [avoiding online shopping scams](/guides/avoiding-online-shopping-scams/) explains the warning signs.
- **Get a written receipt** with the date, price, vehicle details and both parties' names and signatures.
- **Complete the transfer of ownership** according to your country's rules, and arrange insurance before driving away.
- **Keep all documents** together. Our guide to a [household paperwork system](/guides/household-paperwork-system/) explains how long to keep them.

## After you buy

- **Book a service** if the history is incomplete.
- **Check the tyre pressures, fluids and lights.**
- **Read the handbook** for service intervals.
- **Start saving** for the next big maintenance item.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a dealer always safer than a private seller?

Usually you have more legal protection with a business, but not every dealer is trustworthy. Check reviews and do the same checks you would do with a private seller.

### What mileage is too high?

It depends on the model, how the car has been maintained and the type of driving. A well-serviced car with higher mileage can be a better buy than a neglected one with low mileage.

### Should I buy an extended warranty?

Read exactly what it covers, the exclusions and the claim limits, and compare the cost with setting aside the same money for repairs. Our guide to [warranties, guarantees and returns](/guides/warranties-and-returns/) explains the questions to ask.

You will find more ways to get real value for your money in the [Smart Shopping](/topics/smart-shopping/) section.

## The bottom line

Buying a used car well is about patience and order. Set a budget that includes insurance, tax, fuel, maintenance and depreciation; choose reliable models that fit your needs; check the documents, identification numbers, service history, recalls and, where available, a history report for finance, theft or write-offs. Inspect the car in daylight, start it from cold, take a proper test drive and consider an independent inspection. Negotiate with facts, never pay before seeing the car and receiving the keys and documents, use a traceable payment method and complete the ownership transfer according to local rules.
