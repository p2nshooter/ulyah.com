A toilet that keeps running after a flush, refills by itself in the middle of the night or trickles a thin stream into the bowl can waste a surprising amount of water. On a metered water supply, that waste shows up on the bill; even without a meter, it wastes treated water and, in some homes, the energy used to pump it. The good news is that most running toilets are caused by a handful of simple problems inside the cistern, and many can be fixed in less than an hour with basic tools and an inexpensive spare part.

This guide explains how a typical cistern works, how to find out what is wrong, and how to fix the most common faults: a float set too high, a worn flapper or seal, a faulty fill valve and a sticking push-button mechanism. It also explains when it is better to call a plumber.

## How a cistern works

Designs vary from country to country, but almost all cisterns have the same two main parts:

- **The fill valve** lets water in from the supply pipe. A **float**, either a ball on an arm or a cup that slides up and down a vertical tube, rises with the water level and shuts the valve when the cistern is full.
- **The flush valve** lets water out into the bowl when you flush. In many homes it is a rubber **flapper** lifted by a chain; in others, especially with push-button and dual-flush toilets, it is a **drop valve** or a siphon that lifts and then seals with a rubber washer.

There is also an **overflow**, a pipe or tube that carries excess water away safely if the level gets too high, either into the bowl or outside the building.

When a toilet keeps running, the problem is almost always either water entering when it should not (the fill valve) or water escaping when it should not (the flush valve).

## Find the problem: two simple tests

### The overflow check

Take the lid off the cistern and look at the water level.

- If water is running into the **overflow tube or pipe**, the **fill valve** is letting in too much water. The float may be set too high, or the valve may be worn.
- If the level is below the overflow but the cistern keeps refilling, water is probably **leaking through the flush valve** into the bowl.

### The dye test for silent leaks

Some leaks are so slow that you cannot see or hear them.

1. Add a few drops of food colouring to the water in the cistern.
2. Do not flush for at least half an hour.
3. Look in the bowl. If coloured water appears, the flush valve is leaking.
4. Flush afterwards to clear the colour.

This test is worth doing once in a while even if the toilet seems fine, because silent leaks are common.

## Before you start

- **Turn off the water** to the toilet. Most have an **isolating valve** on the supply pipe, often a small screw that turns with a flat screwdriver or a coin. If there is none, turn off the main stopcock.
- **Flush** to empty the cistern, and soak up the remaining water with a sponge or cloth.
- **Lay a towel** on the floor.
- **Take photos** of the inside before you remove anything, so you remember how it goes back together.
- **Note the make and model** printed inside the cistern or on the parts. It makes buying the right replacement much easier.

## Fix 1: adjust the float

If water is running into the overflow, the float is probably set too high.

- **Ball on an arm:** many have an adjustment screw where the arm meets the valve. Turning it lowers the float. On older types, the arm can sometimes be bent very gently downwards, but adjusting the screw is better if there is one.
- **Cup float on a vertical tube:** there is usually a clip, a screw or a threaded rod that lets you slide the float lower.

Set the water level a little below the top of the overflow, often marked by a line inside the cistern. Turn the water back on, let the cistern fill and check that the valve shuts off cleanly.

Also check that the float is not rubbing against the side of the cistern or catching on another part, and that a ball float is not cracked and filling with water.

## Fix 2: clean or replace the fill valve

If adjusting the float does not stop the water, the fill valve itself may be dirty or worn.

1. With the water off, remove the top of the valve following the manufacturer's design; many twist or clip off.
2. Look for **grit, limescale or a worn washer**.
3. Hold a cup over the open valve and briefly turn the water on to flush out debris, then turn it off again.
4. Replace the washer or diaphragm if it is cracked or hardened. Replacement kits are often available for common models.
5. If the valve is old or badly worn, replacing the whole fill valve is usually straightforward: disconnect the supply, undo the locking nut under the cistern, fit the new valve and reconnect. Follow the instructions with the new part.

## Fix 3: replace the flapper or the flush valve seal

If the dye test shows a leak, the seal between the cistern and the bowl is not closing properly.

### Flapper with a chain

- **Check the chain.** If it is too short, it holds the flapper open slightly; if it is too long, it can get trapped underneath. Adjust it so there is a little slack when the flapper is closed.
- **Check the flapper.** Rubber hardens, warps and wears over time. If it is stiff, cracked or covered in deposits, replace it. Take the old one to the shop to match the size and type.
- **Clean the seat**, the ring the flapper sits on, with a soft scourer to remove limescale or grit.

### Push-button or drop valve

Many modern toilets use a drop valve with a rubber washer at the bottom.

1. With the cistern empty, release the valve according to its design; many twist out or unclip from a frame.
2. Inspect the **rubber washer** at the base. Replace it if it is worn, cracked or deformed.
3. Clean the seat it sits on.
4. Refit the valve and test.

Washers for common models are inexpensive. A whole replacement valve is also an option if the mechanism is worn.

## Fix 4: a sticking flush button or handle

If the toilet runs only after you flush, and stops when you jiggle the handle or press the button again, the mechanism may be sticking:

- check that the **handle or button** returns fully;
- make sure the **linkage** is not catching on the cistern lid;
- for dual-flush buttons, check that the **rods or cables** connecting the button to the valve are the right length.

## When to call a plumber

- the cistern or bowl is **cracked** or leaking onto the floor;
- the leak is at the **connection between the cistern and the bowl** or at the supply pipe and does not stop when nuts are gently tightened;
- the **isolating valve or stopcock** will not turn or is leaking;
- you cannot find replacement parts for an unusual model;
- the job involves **concealed cisterns** behind walls that are difficult to reach.

A plumber may also be the better choice if you are not comfortable working with water connections. Our guide to [repair or replace](/guides/repair-or-replace/) helps you weigh the cost of a call-out against a new fitting.

## Prevention

- **Do the dye test** a few times a year.
- **Avoid tablet cleaners** that sit in the cistern; some can degrade rubber parts over time. Check the toilet manufacturer's advice.
- **Descale** in hard-water areas, following the product instructions.
- **Keep a spare washer or flapper** for your model in the toolbox.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a running toilet expensive?

It can be, especially on a metered water supply, because a steady trickle runs day and night. Fixing it is usually cheap compared with the water wasted over months.

### Why does my toilet refill every few minutes without anyone flushing?

Water is slowly leaking from the cistern into the bowl through the flush valve, and the fill valve tops it up. Replacing the flapper or the valve washer usually solves it.

### Do I need special tools?

Usually not. A flat screwdriver, an adjustable spanner, a sponge, a towel and the right replacement part are normally enough. Our guide to [the basic home toolkit](/guides/basic-home-toolkit/) lists other useful tools.

## The bottom line

A running toilet usually has one of two causes: too much water coming in through the fill valve, or water escaping through the flush valve into the bowl. Check whether water is reaching the overflow, and use the dye test to find silent leaks. Then lower the float, clean or replace the fill valve, adjust the chain or replace the flapper, or change the washer on a push-button drop valve. Turn off the water first, photograph the parts and note the model before you start. Call a plumber for cracks, leaking connections, stuck valves or concealed cisterns, and do a dye test a few times a year to catch leaks early.
