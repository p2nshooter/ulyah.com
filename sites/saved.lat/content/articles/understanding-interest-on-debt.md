Interest is the price of borrowing money. Every time you use a credit card, take out a loan, use an overdraft, buy something on finance or take on a mortgage, interest decides how much more you will pay back than you borrowed. Yet the way interest works — percentages, APRs, compounding and minimum payments — is often confusing, and that confusion can make borrowing far more expensive than it first appears.

This guide explains the key ideas in plain language, with simple examples. It is general educational information, not personal financial advice. If you are worried about debt, a free and independent debt advice service can help you understand your options.

## What is interest?

When you borrow money, the lender charges you for the use of it. That charge is usually expressed as a percentage of the amount you owe, over a period of time — typically a year.

- **The principal** is the amount you borrowed.
- **The interest rate** is the percentage charged.
- **The term** is how long you have to repay.

For example, if you borrow 1,000 at a simple interest rate of 10% a year, you would pay 100 in interest over one year if you repaid everything at the end. In practice, most consumer borrowing is more complicated, as the sections below explain.

## Simple versus compound interest

### Simple interest

Simple interest is calculated only on the original amount borrowed. It is easy to understand but uncommon for consumer borrowing.

### Compound interest

Compound interest is calculated on the amount owed **including any interest already added**. In other words, you pay interest on interest. Over time, this can make debts grow faster than people expect.

Imagine you owe 1,000 on a credit card and make no payments, with interest added each month at a rate equivalent to about 2% per month:

- After the first month, interest of about 20 is added, so you owe 1,020.
- The next month, interest is calculated on 1,020, so slightly more is added.
- After a year, you would owe noticeably more than 1,240 because of compounding.

The example is illustrative; actual card interest calculations vary. The principle is what matters: when interest compounds, the cost of borrowing grows over time, especially when balances are not reduced.

Compounding also works in your favour with savings, where interest is earned on interest.

## Understanding APR

The **APR (annual percentage rate)** is a standardised way of showing the yearly cost of borrowing. In many countries, lenders must show it so that consumers can compare products.

The APR typically includes:

- the interest rate;
- some compulsory fees, such as arrangement fees.

Because it includes fees and reflects compounding, the APR can be higher than the headline interest rate.

### Representative APR

Adverts often show a **representative APR**. This is the rate that a proportion of successful applicants — often a majority — will receive. You may be offered a higher rate depending on your credit history and circumstances. Always check the APR you are actually offered before agreeing.

### Comparing APRs

When comparing similar products, a lower APR generally means cheaper borrowing. But also compare:

- **The total amount repayable**, which shows the full cost in money.
- **The term.** A longer term may mean lower monthly payments but more interest overall.
- **Fees** for late payments, early repayment or other charges that may not be included in the APR.

## Common types of borrowing

### Credit cards

- Interest is usually charged on balances not paid in full by the due date.
- Many cards offer an interest-free period on purchases if you pay the full balance each month.
- **Cash withdrawals** on credit cards often have higher interest rates, fees and no interest-free period.
- **0% promotional deals** on purchases or balance transfers can save money if used carefully, but rates usually rise when the promotion ends, and balance transfers often have a fee.

### Personal loans

- You borrow a fixed amount and repay in fixed monthly instalments over a set term.
- The interest rate is often fixed, making payments predictable.
- Rates are often lower for larger loans and borrowers with good credit histories.

### Overdrafts

- An arranged overdraft lets you borrow up to a limit on your current account.
- Overdraft interest rates can be high, so they are best suited to short-term borrowing.
- Unarranged overdrafts or exceeding your limit can lead to declined payments and charges.

### Buy now, pay later and store finance

- These split purchases into instalments, sometimes interest-free.
- Late payments may lead to fees, and some agreements charge interest if not repaid within a promotional period.
- They can make it easy to borrow without noticing how much you owe in total.

### Mortgages

- Mortgages are long-term loans secured on your home.
- Even small differences in the interest rate make a large difference over the full term.
- Your home may be at risk if you do not keep up repayments.

### High-cost short-term credit

- Payday loans and similar products can have very high APRs.
- They are designed for short periods but can become very expensive if rolled over or repaid late.
- Free debt advice services can help if you are relying on this type of borrowing.

## The minimum payment trap

Credit cards usually require only a small **minimum payment** each month — often a small percentage of the balance, or a minimum amount.

Paying only the minimum keeps the account in good standing, but:

- **Most of the payment may go on interest**, with only a little reducing the balance.
- **It can take many years** to clear the debt.
- **You may pay back much more than you borrowed.**

Many card statements now show how long it would take to repay the balance by paying only the minimum, and how much interest you would pay. Reading this can be eye-opening.

### Paying a little more

Paying even a modest fixed amount above the minimum each month can:

- shorten the time to repay significantly;
- reduce the total interest paid.

Setting a fixed monthly payment by direct debit, rather than the minimum, is a simple way to make progress.

## How to reduce the cost of interest

### Pay on time

Late payments often lead to fees, higher interest rates and damage to your credit record. Direct debits help avoid missed payments.

### Pay more than the minimum

Every extra payment reduces the balance on which interest is charged.

### Prioritise high-interest debts

If you have several debts and can afford more than the minimum payments, putting extra money towards the debt with the highest interest rate first (sometimes called the "avalanche" method) usually minimises total interest. Another approach is to clear the smallest balance first (the "snowball" method), which some people find motivating. Always keep up minimum payments on all debts.

### Consider lower-cost options carefully

- **0% balance transfer cards** may reduce interest if you can clear the balance during the promotional period. Check the transfer fee and what happens when the promotion ends.
- **Consolidation loans** combine several debts into one, sometimes at a lower rate. Make sure the total cost is genuinely lower, and avoid building up new debts on cleared cards.

### Avoid cash withdrawals on credit cards

These usually cost more than purchases.

### Talk to lenders early

If you are struggling, contact your lenders before you miss payments. Many have hardship arrangements and may be able to reduce payments, freeze interest temporarily or agree a plan.

## Questions to ask before you borrow

Before taking on any new borrowing, it helps to pause and ask:

1. **Do I need to borrow at all?** Could I wait and save for the purchase, use a sinking fund, or buy second-hand instead?
2. **What is the total amount repayable**, not just the monthly payment?
3. **Can I afford the repayments** if my income falls or other costs rise?
4. **What happens if I miss a payment?** Check fees, interest changes and any effect on your credit record.
5. **Is the rate fixed or variable?** Variable rates can rise during the term.
6. **Are there early repayment charges** if I want to clear the debt sooner?
7. **Have I compared at least a few options?**

Taking a day or two to consider these questions, rather than deciding at the checkout or on the spot, often leads to a better decision — or to deciding not to borrow at all.

## Your credit record

How you manage borrowing is recorded by credit reference agencies in many countries. Paying on time, keeping balances well below limits and avoiding too many applications in a short period generally help. A better credit record can mean access to lower interest rates in future. You can usually check your credit report free of charge through official or approved services.

## When to seek help

Consider contacting a free, independent debt advice service if:

- you are using credit to pay for essentials or other debts;
- you are only able to make minimum payments;
- you are missing payments;
- debt is causing stress or affecting your health or relationships.

Be cautious of companies that charge high fees for debt help; free, regulated advice is often available.

## Key terms at a glance

- **Principal:** the amount borrowed.
- **Interest:** the cost of borrowing.
- **APR:** the annual cost of borrowing, including some fees.
- **Compound interest:** interest charged on interest already added.
- **Minimum payment:** the smallest amount you must pay each month.
- **Term:** the length of time to repay.
- **Total amount repayable:** everything you will pay back, including interest and fees.

## The bottom line

Interest determines the true cost of borrowing. Understanding APRs, compounding and minimum payments makes it easier to compare products, avoid expensive debt and clear what you owe faster. Pay on time, pay more than the minimum, focus on high-interest balances, and ask for help early if debt becomes difficult. Knowledge of how interest works is one of the best protections against paying far more than you need to.
