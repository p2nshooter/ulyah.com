Before shining in a ring, an emerald spent millions of years locked in a vein of black rock in the Colombian Andes. An aquamarine grew slowly in a pocket of hot liquids inside a granite from Minas Gerais. A diamond formed more than a hundred and fifty kilometers deep and rose to the surface in a violent volcanic eruption. Each gem is the result of a very rare combination of chemical elements, temperature, pressure, space and time.

Understanding how gems are formed is not just a geological curiosity: it helps understand why some are so rare, why certain regions produce stones with recognizable character, and why inclusions seen with a magnifying glass can tell where a stone comes from. In this guide you will see what exactly a gem is, what are the great environments where they are born, with examples from Latin America, where their color comes from and what their inclusions reveal.

## What is a gem

Strictly speaking, a gem is a natural material beautiful, durable, and rare enough to be used in jewelry or ornamentation. The vast majority are minerals, that is, natural substances with a defined chemical composition and an ordered crystalline structure. But not all:

- **Minerals:** diamond, corundum, which gives ruby and sapphire, beryl, which gives emerald and aquamarine, quartz, tourmaline or topaz.
- **Rocks:** some gems are mixtures of several minerals, such as lapis lazuli, made up mainly of lazurite, with calcite and pyrite.
- **Organic materials:** created by living beings, such as pearls, amber, which is fossil resin, coral or jet.

Of the thousands of known mineral species, only a few dozen are commonly used as gems. To be so, a mineral needs to have beauty, due to its color, shine or optical effects, sufficient durability to withstand use, and a certain rarity.

## The ingredients: elements, pressure, temperature and time

For a gem-quality crystal to grow, several conditions are needed at the same time:

- **The appropriate chemical elements,** sometimes very scarce in the Earth's crust, such as the beryllium of the emerald or the chromium that gives it its green.
- **A stable environment,** with the correct temperature and pressure for long enough.
- **Space to grow:** cavities, fractures or pockets where the crystal can develop without colliding with others.
- **Few interruptions:** Sudden changes produce fractures, inclusions and irregular growths.

That is why most of the crystals from a site are not suitable for carving. Only a small fraction has sufficient transparency, color and size.

## Six environments where gems are born

| Environment | How it works | Examples of gems | A Latin American example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Magmatic | crystallization from molten rock or transport from the mantle | diamond, peridot | Historical alluvial diamonds from Brazil |
| pegmatitic | last liquids of a granitic magma, rich in rare elements | aquamarine, topaz, tourmaline, morganite | pegmatites from Minas Gerais, Brazil |
| Hydrothermal | hot waters that circulate through fractures and deposit minerals | emerald, amethyst, rhodochrosite | emeralds from Colombia; rhodochrosite from Catamarca, Argentina |
| Metamorphic | transformation of rocks by heat and pressure | ruby, sapphire, garnet, lapis lazuli, jade | lapis lazuli from the Coquimbo region, Chile |
| Superficial or sedimentary | disturbance and precipitation near the surface | opal, turquoise, malachite | fire opal in volcanic rocks of Mexico |
| Alluvial or pleasure | erosion and concentration of resistant gems in rivers and gravels | diamond, sapphire, garnet | ancient diamond deposits of Minas Gerais |

## From magma: diamonds and peridots

Diamond is formed in the Earth's mantle, more than one hundred and fifty kilometers deep, where the pressure is so high that the carbon is organized into its most compact structure. It reaches the surface dragged by very rapid eruptions of special magmas, such as kimberlites, which form vertical chimneys. If the ascent were slow, the diamond would transform into graphite along the way.

Peridot, the gem variety of olivine, also comes from the mantle and usually appears in fragments carried by basaltic lavas. Its green color is due to iron, which is part of its own composition.

## Pegmatites: giant crystals

When a large body of granitic magma cools, the last remaining liquids concentrate water and rare elements, such as beryllium, lithium, boron or fluorine. When they solidify in cavities and dams, they form pegmatites, rocks with enormous crystals where aquamarines, topazes, tourmalines or morganites of large size and transparency can grow.

The Brazilian state of Minas Gerais is one of the great pegmatitic territories in the world. We see it in the guide on [Minas Gerais gems: topaz and aquamarine](/es/gemas-de-minas-gerais-topacio-y-aguamarina/).

## Hot water in fractures: the hydrothermal origin

Many gems are born from hot aqueous solutions that circulate through cracks in the rock and, upon cooling or reacting with it, deposit minerals. It is the origin of quartz veins, numerous amethysts and minerals such as rhodochrosite.

The Colombian emeralds are an exceptional case: unlike most of the world's emeralds, which are related to granites and pegmatites, the Colombian ones were formed from very saline hydrothermal fluids that circulated through black slates, without the intervention of magmas. That origin gives them characteristic inclusions that gemologists use to recognize them. We tell it in the guide on [Colombian emeralds, from mine to table](/es/esmeraldas-colombianas-de-la-mina-a-la-mesa/), and in the one on [Argentine rhodochrosite](/es/rodocrosita-argentina/).

## Heat and pressure: metamorphic gems

When rocks sink into the crust during mountain building, heat and pressure rearrange their minerals without melting them. This is how many rubies and sapphires are formed in marble, garnets in schist or jade under high pressure conditions. Lapis lazuli, for its part, is formed when limestone or marble reacts with fluids rich in sodium and sulfur in contact with magma intrusions. Chile has one of the few important deposits in the world, as we explain in the guide on [Chilean lapis lazuli](/es/lapislazuli-chileno/).

## On the surface: alteration, sediments and alluvium

Near the surface, rainwater and groundwater dissolve some minerals and deposit others. This is how materials such as turquoise or malachite are formed, associated with copper deposits in arid areas, and opal, which is silica with water in its structure.

In Mexico, fire opal formed in volcanic rocks when silica-laden waters filled cavities and fractures. We see it in the guide on [Mexican fire opal](/es/opalo-de-fuego-mexicano/). In southern Brazil and northern Uruguay, large amethyst geodes grew inside ancient gas bubbles from lava flows, as we explain in the guide on the [amethyst geodes of Uruguay and Brazil](/es/geodas-de-amatista-de-uruguay-y-brasil/).

When erosion destroys the original rock, the harder, denser gems resist and travel down rivers, which concentrate them into gravel. These deposits, called alluvial or placer deposits, were for centuries the main source of diamonds in Brazil.

## The organic gems

Some gems are not formed by geological processes, but biological ones:

- **Pearls:** layers of nacre that a mollusk deposits around an irritant.
- **Amber:** resin from ancient trees hardened over millions of years. In the region, for example, the amber from Chiapas, in Mexico, is known.
- **Coral:** skeletons of colonies of marine animals, today highly protected in many areas.
- **Azabache:** very compact fossilized wood, black in color.

## crystalline systems

The internal structure of each mineral is repeated in an orderly manner, and that organization determines the external shape of its crystals when they grow freely.

| System | Examples of gems |
|---|---|
| cubic | diamond, garnet, spinel |
| Tetragonal | zircon |
| Hexagonal | beryl: emerald and aquamarine |
| Trigonal | quartz, corundum, tourmaline |
| Orthorhombic | topaz, peridot, chrysoberyl |
| Monoclinic | jadeite, orthoclase |
| Triclinic | turquoise, labradorite |

Recognizing the typical shape of a crystal, its habit, is one of the first clues to identifying a rough gem.

## Why does a gem have color?

- **Due to their own composition:** some minerals have color because a coloring element is an essential part of their formula, such as iron in peridot, copper in malachite or manganese in rhodochrosite.
- **Due to traces of other elements:** many minerals would be colorless if they were pure. Small amounts of chromium turn corundum red, which we then call ruby, and beryl green, which we call emerald; iron and titanium give the blue of sapphire.
- **Due to defects in the structure:** natural radiation creates color centers, responsible for example for the color of smoky quartz or amethyst.
- **By physical phenomena:** the play of colors of the opal comes from the diffraction of light in microscopic spheres of silica, and the star of some sapphires, from needles of other minerals arranged inside.

## The inclusions, a travel diary

Almost all natural gems contain inclusions: crystals of other minerals, bubbles, liquids, or healed fractures that became trapped during their growth. Far from being just defects, they are invaluable clues:

- **They indicate that a stone is natural,** because many synthetic gems have different inclusions.
- **They may suggest their geographical origin:** Colombian emeralds, for example, are famous for their three-phase inclusions, with a liquid, a gas bubble, and a small salt crystal in the same cavity.
- **They reveal treatments,** such as heating, which alters certain inclusions.

That is why laboratories study inclusions under a microscope before issuing a report. We explain it in the guide on [gem treatments](/es/tratamientos-de-gemas/).

## Frequent errors

- **Believe that all gems are minerals:** there are rocks and organic materials.
- **Thinking that harder means unbreakable:** hardness is not the same as toughness, as we see in the guide on [hardness, toughness and stability] (/es/dureza-tenacidad-y-estabilidad/).
- **Consider any inclusion a defect:** many confirm that the stone is natural.
- **Assume that the color always depends on the mineral:** two stones of the same species can have very different colors due to traces of other elements.
- **Assume the origin by color:** only a laboratory can suggest the origin of a stone with substance.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take for a gem to form?

The geological processes that create the conditions for a gem to grow last millions of years, although the growth of a particular crystal can be faster. What is almost always very long is the complete path, from the formation until the rock that contains it emerges on the surface.

### Why does Latin America have so many gems?

For its varied geology: the Andes mountain range, with its history of volcanoes and hydrothermal fluids, the ancient shields of Brazil with large pegmatites and extensive lava flows where geodes grew.

### Does a synthetic gem form the same as a natural one?

It has the same composition and structure, but grows in a laboratory in days, weeks or months, under controlled conditions. Its inclusions and growth patterns often give it away to a gemologist.

### Can you tell the origin of a gem at a glance?

No. Determining geographic origin requires laboratory analysis of the inclusions and chemistry of the stone, and even then it is not always possible.

## In summary

Gems are beautiful, durable, and rare minerals, rocks, or organic materials. They are born in very different environments: from the mantle and magma, like diamond and peridot; of pegmatites, such as aquamarine and topaz; of hydrothermal fluids, such as Colombian emeralds; of metamorphism, like ruby ​​or lapis lazuli; from the surface, like opal, or from concentration in rivers. Its color comes from its composition, from traces of other elements, from defects or physical phenomena, and its inclusions are a diary that helps to recognize its nature, its treatments and, sometimes, its origin.
