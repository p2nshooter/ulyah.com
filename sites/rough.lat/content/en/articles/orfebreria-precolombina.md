Long before European ships reached their shores, the people of America were already working gold, silver and copper with astonishing mastery. Pectorals that shone like the sun, nose rings that transformed the face of a ruler, tiny figures of frogs, birds and jaguars cast with a detail that is difficult to imitate today, alloys capable of changing color on the surface and even platinum objects, a metal that in Europe would not be known to work until centuries later.

Much of that heritage was lost, melted into ingots after the conquest. But what survived in tombs, offerings and museums allows us to reconstruct one of the most sophisticated goldsmithing traditions of the ancient world. In this guide you will see where American metallurgy was born, what metals and alloys it used, what techniques it mastered, what metals meant for these cultures, what some of its most famous works are, where to see them today and why you should never buy archaeological pieces.

## A metallurgy that was born in the Andes

American metallurgy emerged in the central Andes, where the oldest known pieces of hammered gold are at least three and a half thousand years old. From there, knowledge spread and transformed:

| Region | Some cultures | Featured Features |
|---|---|---|
| Central Andes and coast of Peru | Chavín, Moche, Lambayeque, Chimú, Inca | hammered sheets, embossing, alloys and sophisticated gilding |
| Ecuador and southern Colombia | La Tolita and Tumaco | platinum work mixed with gold |
| Colombia | Calima, Quimbaya, Muisca, Tairona, Sinú | lost wax casting and votive figures |
| Panama and Costa Rica | Coclé, Diquís | cast pendants of animals and symbolic figures |
| Mesoamerica | Western Mexico, Mixtecs | later metallurgy; Mixtec jewelry of great finesse |

Metallurgy arrived in Mesoamerica relatively late, probably through contacts with South America, and reached its peak with the Mixtec goldsmiths of Oaxaca in the centuries before the conquest.

## Gold, silver, copper and tumbaga

American goldsmiths worked gold, silver and copper, and especially their alloys. The most characteristic is the tumbaga, a mixture of gold and copper, sometimes with silver. It had several advantages: it melts at a lower temperature than pure gold, it is harder and it saves gold. And, thanks to a special technique, a tumbaga piece could look as if it were made of solid gold.

## The great techniques

| Technique | What does it consist of? | Where stood out |
|---|---|---|
| Hammered and annealed | hitting the metal to make it sheet and heating it so it doesn't break | Central Andes |
| Embossed | give relief to a sheet by hitting it from the back | Andes, Colombia |
| Lost wax casting | model in wax, cover with a mold, melt the wax and pour the metal | Colombia, Panama, Costa Rica, Mesoamerica |
| Welding and joining | joining parts with heat, alloys or mechanical joints | the entire region |
| Gilding by oxidation | enrich the surface of an alloy in gold by eliminating copper | Andes, Colombia, isthmus |
| Gold and silver surfaces | covering copper with very thin layers of precious metal | northern coast of Peru |
| Platinum sintering | joining platinum grains with gold by heat and hammering | La Tolita and Tumaco |

## Gilding by oxidation

One of the most ingenious techniques consisted of heating a piece of tumbaga so that the copper on the surface oxidized and then removing that rust with acidic substances of plant or mineral origin. By repeating the process, the surface was enriched in gold and acquired a golden shine, while the interior remained an alloy with a lot of copper. For these cultures, this change in color also had a symbolic meaning: the metal was transformed.

On the northern coast of Peru, Moche goldsmiths also managed to cover copper objects with very thin layers of gold or silver, in processes that specialists still study.

## The lost wax in Colombia and the isthmus

In Colombia, Panama and Costa Rica, lost wax casting reached an extraordinary level. The goldsmith modeled the figure in wax, covered it with a mixture of clay and charcoal, left a duct, heated the mold so that the wax came out, and poured the molten metal into the hole. This is how the Muisca votive figures called tunjos were born, the pendants of frogs and birds from Costa Rica and Panama and highly complex pieces such as the Muisca raft, which represents a ceremony in the lagoon and is associated with the legend of El Dorado.

## The platinum of La Tolita and Tumaco

On the Pacific coast, between northern Ecuador and southern Colombia, the goldsmiths of the La Tolita and Tumaco culture achieved something remarkable: working platinum, a metal whose melting point was impossible to reach with their furnaces. They achieved this by mixing grains of platinum with gold and heating and hammering them together, in a technique that today is compared to powder metallurgy. In Europe, platinum would not be worked on a regular basis until many centuries later.

## Meanings: shine, sun and transformation

For these cultures, gold was not above all money, but a sacred and political material:

- **Brightness and light:** Gold was often associated with the sun, and in the Inca world silver was related to the moon.
- **Power:** pectorals, nose rings, earflaps and crowns identified rulers and priests.
- **Transformation:** many pieces represent beings that combine human and animal traits, such as birds, bats or jaguars, linked to ideas of spiritual transformation.
- **Offering:** numerous figures were deposited in lagoons, caves or tombs as offerings.

## emblematic works

- **The trousseau of the Lord of Sipán,** in Lambayeque, Peru: a Moche tomb found intact, with pectorals, earflaps and ornaments of gold, silver and stones.
- **The tumi,** ceremonial knife of the Lambayeque culture, converted into a symbol of Peru.
- **The Muisca raft,** in the Gold Museum of Bogotá.
- **The treasure of the quimbayas,** with tumbaga containers and figures, preserved in a museum in Madrid.
- **The treasure from tomb 7 of Monte Albán,** in Oaxaca, with Mixtec gold jewelry of extraordinary fineness.
- **The Diquís pendants,** in Costa Rica, with frogs, birds and shamanic figures.

## Conquest and molten gold

After the arrival of the Spanish, enormous quantities of gold and silver objects were melted down into ingots and sent to Europe. The ransom demanded by Atahualpa in Cajamarca is the best-known example. For this reason, most of the pieces we see today come from tombs and offerings found later, and many others were lost forever, some due to the looting of archaeological sites, which continues to be a problem in the region.

## Replicas yes, archaeological pieces no

- **Archaeological pieces are heritage:** in most countries in the region, their trade and export are prohibited, and buying them fuels the looting of sites.
- **Many are fakes:** "authentic" pieces offered in markets are almost always modern copies.
- **Legit replicas** are sold in many museum stores and in recognized workshops, identified as such. They are a beautiful and honest way to carry that legacy.

If they offer you an old piece, be suspicious and don't buy it. The guide on [collecting gems and minerals](/es/coleccionar-gemas-y-minerales/) expands on the legal aspects of collecting.

## An inspiring heritage

Many contemporary jewelers in the region are inspired by pre-Columbian shapes, symbols and techniques, and some workshops reproduce pieces using traditional techniques. When inspiration is done with respect and knowledge, and recognizing its origin, it helps keep that legacy alive. We also see it in the guide on [Latin American silverwork and filigree] (/es/plateria-y-filigrana-latinoamericana/).

## Frequent errors

- **Buy supposed archaeological pieces** in markets or online.
- **Thinking that everything is solid gold:** many pieces are made of gilded tumbaga on the surface.
- **Believe that metallurgy arrived with the Europeans:** in America it had existed for millennia.
- **Visiting a museum without context:** a guided tour completely changes the experience.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the tumbaga?

An alloy of gold and copper, sometimes with silver, widely used in pre-Columbian goldsmithing. With oxidation gilding techniques, its surface could look like pure gold.

### Where is the largest collection of pre-Columbian goldsmithing?

The Bogotá Gold Museum brings together one of the largest collections in the world, and there are very important collections in Peru, Costa Rica, Mexico and other countries.

### Is it legal to buy a replica?

Yes, if it is clearly sold as a replica. What is prohibited in many countries is trading in authentic archaeological pieces.

### Did the Incas work with platinum?

There is no evidence of this. Those who worked on it were the goldsmiths of the La Tolita and Tumaco culture, on the Pacific coast.

## In summary

American goldsmithing was born in the Andes millennia ago and spread to Colombia, the Central American isthmus and Mesoamerica. Its goldsmiths worked with gold, silver, copper and alloys such as tumbaga, and mastered techniques such as hammering, embossing, lost wax, oxidation gilding and even platinum work. For them, metals were symbols of light, power and transformation. Much of that heritage was melted down after the conquest, but what survived can be seen in museums throughout the region. Enjoy it there or in honest replicas, and never buy archaeological pieces.
