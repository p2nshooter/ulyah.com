Since jewelry has existed, imitations have existed. In the eighteenth century, a Paris jeweler became famous by making glass so brilliant that his surname, Strass, became synonymous with rhinestone. Today, the repertoire is much broader: glass of all colors, plastics, cubic zirconia, synthetic gems used to imitate other species and composite stones, made up of several layers glued together, which can deceive even buyers with some experience.

An imitation is not necessarily a fraud. Honest jewelry, sold as such, has its place and its charm. The problem appears when an imitation is presented as a natural gem. In this guide you will see what the most common imitations are, how doublets and triplets are made and recognized, what trade names are confusing, what limits diamond testers have and what to do if you suspect a piece.

## Imitation, synthetic and natural

An imitation, also called a simulant, is a different material that only resembles the gem it imitates: for example, a green glass that imitates an emerald. A synthetic gem, on the other hand, has the same composition and structure as a natural gem, although it is created in a laboratory. We explain it in the guide on [synthetic and laboratory gems](/es/gemas-sinteticas-y-de-laboratorio/).

## The most common imitations

| material | What imitates | Common signs |
|---|---|---|
| glass | almost any transparent gem | round bubbles, swirls, rounded edges, wear on facets |
| Plastics and resins | amber, pearls, turquoise, coral, opal | very light, warm to the touch, mold marks |
| cubic zirconia | diamond | very bright and with many flashes of color, but heavier and less hard |
| synthetic spinel | aquamarine, sapphire, other gems | properties different from those of the gem it imitates |
| Garnets and quartz | ruby, emerald, amethyst and others | other optical properties; a gemologist distinguishes them immediately |
| Dyed materials | turquoise, jade, lapis lazuli, coral | color concentrated in cracks and pores |

## Glass: the oldest imitation

Glass can be made in any color and faceted or shaped. Some clues to recognize it with a magnifying glass:

- **Round** or elongated gas bubbles inside.
- **Swirls** in color or transparency, like honey veins.
- **Rounded edges** in molded stones, instead of the sharp edges of a carving.
- **Wear:** With use, glass scratches and wears on the edges sooner than many gems, because it is softer.

Some very well-crafted antique glass is part of period jewelry pieces with historical and collectible value, as long as they are sold as what they are.

## Plastics and resins

Plastics mainly imitate organic or opaque materials, such as amber, pearls, coral or turquoise. They are usually very light, feel warm to the touch, and may show mold marks or bubbles. Some resins are loaded with stone dust to appear more authentic, as in certain reconstituted turquoises.

## Doublets and triplets

A composite stone is made up of two or more parts joined together, usually with glue or fusion, to appear as a single, more valuable gem.

| Type | How is it | What imitates |
|---|---|---|
| Garnet and glass doublet | a thin layer of garnet fused on colored glass | almost any gem; widely used in antique jewelry |
| sapphire doublet | a natural sapphire crown on a synthetic sapphire base | good color natural sapphire |
| opal doublet | a sheet of opal glued on a dark base | solid opal |
| Opal Triplet | opal sheet between a dark base and a transparent cover | solid opal with good color play |
| Emerald Triplet | two colorless pieces joined together with a green layer in between | emerald |

Composite stones set in a closed setting are especially difficult to recognize, because the setting hides the joining line.

## How to detect a composite stone

- **Look for the union line** on the edge of the stone, with a magnifying glass and good light. Sometimes you see a change in brightness, color or a fine line.
- **Look for flat bubbles** in the joint plane, where air was trapped when gluing.
- **Look at the stone from the side:** in some, the color is concentrated in one layer instead of spread throughout the stone.
- **The red ring test:** If a garnet and glass doublet is placed board side down on white paper, a reddish ring is often seen on the edge, coming from the garnet layer.
- **In opals,** observe the profile: a very thin sheet on a perfect black base reveals a doublet; a transparent domed cover, a triplet.

A gemologist can confirm this with instruments such as a microscope or refractometer.

## Names that confuse

Names circulate in the trade that suggest a valuable gem but designate something else:

| Trade name | What is it really |
|---|---|
| Herkimer diamond and similar names | quartz crystals, not diamonds |
| Bahia topaz or madeira topaz | citrine, which is quartz |
| Mexican jade | calcite or green-dyed marble |
| Mexican onyx | banded calcite, not the chalcedony called onyx |
| Bohemian Ruby | red garnet |
| Swiss lapis | blue dyed jasper |

None of these materials are necessarily ugly or worthless, but the name shouldn't make you pay as if they were the gem they evoke. We also see it in the guides on [Chilean lapis lazuli](/es/lapislazuli-chileno/) and the [Minas Gerais gems](/es/gemas-de-minas-gerais-topacio-y-aguamarina/).

## Imitations in antique and inherited jewelry

Antique jewelry deserves special attention. During the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, high-quality glass, garnet and glass doublets, and stones with metal plates on the back were very common, a technique that was used to give more shine and color to stones mounted in closed settings. Therefore:

- **A closed back setting** on an antique piece can hide a lamellar, doublet, or just a pale stone.
- **A large, intensely colored stone** in a modest piece of jewelry should make you suspicious before insuring or selling it.
- **Family jewels** do not always contain the gems that family tradition attributes to them.

Before insuring, selling or refurbishing an inherited piece, have your stones identified by a gemologist. We explain it in the guide on [inherited and second-hand jewelry](/es/joyas-heredadas-y-de-segunda-mano/).

## Diamond testers and their limits

Electronic testers that measure how a stone conducts heat serve to rule out imitations such as glass or cubic zirconia, but they have important limits:

- **Moissanite conducts heat much like diamond,** and simple thermal testers may mistake it for a diamond. There are devices that combine several measurements.
- **They do not distinguish a natural diamond from a laboratory diamond:** both are diamonds.
- **They require good technique:** a dirty stone or a poorly made measurement gives erroneous results.

For important parts, the only reliable result is that of a laboratory. We explain it in the guide on [diamonds: essential guide](/es/diamantes-guia-esencial/).

## Jewelry: when the imitation is honest

Quality jewelry, with cut glass, crystals or zircons, allows you to show off striking designs at an affordable price, travel without fear of losing an expensive piece of jewelry, or reproduce historical pieces. There is nothing wrong with it when it is sold and bought as costume jewelry. Some pieces of period jewelry even have collector's value today.

## What to do if you suspect a piece

1. **Do not do destructive tests,** such as scratching the stone or heating it.
2. **Check it with a magnifying glass** looking for bubbles, swirls, seam lines or wear.
3. **Consult a gemologist** or a recognized laboratory.
4. **Check the invoice:** If the piece was sold as natural and it is not, you have arguments to complain.
5. **Before purchasing,** ask in writing what exactly the stone is.

## Frequent errors

- **Trust a diamond tester** to rule out moissanite or detect a lab-grown diamond.
- **Do not look at the edge** of a stone for a joining line.
- **Buy opal mounted in closed setting** without knowing if it is solid.
- **Let yourself be carried away by commercial names** that evoke valuable gems.
- **Scratch a stone** to check its hardness.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I know if a gem is glass?

With a magnifying glass you can look for bubbles, swirls and rounded edges, but safe confirmation is given by a gemologist with instruments.

### Does a double have any value?

It has the value of what it is: a composite stone, much cheaper than the gem it imitates. Some antique doublets have historical or collector's interest.

### Is cubic zirconia an imitation diamond?

Yes. It is a synthetic material different from diamond, used to imitate it, heavier and less hard.

### Is Mexican onyx onyx?

In the strict gemological sense, no: it is usually banded calcite. Onyx itself is a variety of chalcedony.

## In summary

Imitations are materials that only look like a gem: glass, plastics, cubic zirconia, other cheaper synthetic or natural gems, and dyed materials. Composite stones, such as doublets and triplets, join layers together to look like a more valuable gem. With a magnifying glass you can look for bubbles, swirls, rounded edges and joining lines, but confirmation is given by a gemologist. Be wary of commercial names that evoke valuable gems, know the limits of diamond testers and always ask in writing what exactly the stone you buy is.
