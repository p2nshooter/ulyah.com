Turning a piece of agate into a sparkling cabochon or a fragment of quartz into a faceted gem is an engaging experience. Lapidary combines patience, precision, knowledge of materials and enormous satisfaction when seeing the shine appear after hours of work. More and more people in Latin America are approaching this profession as a hobby, through clubs, shared workshops or courses.

But lapidary also has risks that are not always explained at the beginning. The most serious is invisible: the fine silica dust that many stones release when cutting or dry polishing them, capable of irreversibly damaging the lungs. Added to this are machines with discs that rotate at high speed, water close to electricity and some toxic minerals. In this guide you will see where to start, what equipment is needed, what the main risks are and how to prevent them, how to make a first cabochon step by step and where to learn.

## Where to start: cabochon before faceting

Most lapidaries start with the cabochon, the cut with a domed top and no facets. It's simpler, more forgiving of mistakes, and teaches the fundamentals: cutting, shaping, sanding, and polishing. Faceting, which requires a specific machine and great precision in angles, usually comes later. We explain it in the guide on [gem cut types](/es/tipos-de-talla-de-gemas/).

## The basic equipment

| Team | What is it for? |
|---|---|
| Trimming saw with diamond blade | cut the stone into sheets or small blocks |
| cabochon machine | coarse to fine grit wheels for shaping, sanding and polishing |
| Rods and wax or adhesive | hold the stone during work |
| Templates | mark standard shapes and sizes |
| faceting machine | for later: cutting facets with precise angles |
| Magnifying glass and good lighting | review work closely |
| Protective equipment | glasses, appropriate mask, hearing protection |

Before purchasing machinery, it is a good idea to learn in a shared workshop or club, where you can use the equipment and receive advice.

## The great invisible risk: silica dust

Many of the stones most used in lapidary, such as agate, jasper, quartz and their varieties, are made of silica. When cut, sanded or dry polished, they release a very fine dust that can reach the depths of the lungs. Repeated exposure to such dust can cause silicosis, a serious and irreversible lung disease. To prevent it:

- **Always work wet:** Lapidary machines use water to cool and trap dust. Do not dry sand or polish.
- **Ventilate the work space**.
- **Use a high filtration mask** certified for fine dust when there is any risk of dust.
- **Clean with wet methods:** a wet cloth or a suitable vacuum cleaner, never dry sweeping.
- **Do not eat or drink** in the work area and wash your hands when finished.
- **Consult a doctor** if you notice a persistent cough or shortness of breath.

## Minerals requiring special caution

| Material type | Because |
|---|---|
| Copper minerals, such as malachite or chrysocolla | its dust should not be inhaled or ingested |
| Minerals with lead, arsenic or mercury | they are toxic; some should not be worked on in a home workshop |
| Silky fibrous materials | some fibrous minerals can be dangerous without their complete transformation |
| Unknown materials | Without knowing what they are, you cannot know what risk they have. |

Before working with a material, find out about its composition. If you don't know what it is, don't cut it.

## Machines, water and electricity

- **Read the instructions** for each machine and respect its protections.
- **Water and electricity:** use grounded plugs and installations protected with a differential switch, and keep cables away from water.
- **Discs and wheels:** check them before using them; A damaged disc may break.
- **Cutting Oils:** Some saws use oil instead of water; They require good ventilation and precautions against fire.
- **Never force the stone** against the disc; let the tool do the work.
- **Turn off and unplug** the machine before changing discs or cleaning it.

## Eyes, ears, hands and clothes

- **Protective glasses** or face shield whenever you cut or grind.
- **Hearing protection** with loud saws and machines.
- **Hair up** and no loose clothing, necklaces or bracelets near the rotating parts.
- **Be careful with gloves:** near moving discs and shafts they can catch and drag your hand. Use them only when the task allows it.
- **Closed footwear:** stones and tools are heavy.

## Heat, waxes and adhesives

To fix the stone to the rod, heated waxes or adhesives are used. Hot waxes and small alcohol lamps can cause burns or fires, and some adhesives give off fumes. Work with good ventilation, away from flammable materials and with a fire extinguisher on hand.

## Posture, light and breaks

- **Sit at the appropriate height** from the machine to avoid straining your back.
- **Use good lighting** and a magnifying glass instead of putting your face too close to the disc.
- **Take regular breaks:** Concentration drops with fatigue, and with it, confidence.

## A first cabochon step by step

1. **Choose a suitable stone:** an agate or a jasper without cracks, economical and resistant.
2. **Cut a sheet** a few millimeters thick with the saw, wet.
3. **Mark the shape** with a template and a foil or fine-tipped marker.
4. **Trim off the excess** with the saw, without putting your fingers near the disc.
5. **Shape the contour** on the thick wheel, always with water.
6. **Attach the stone to a rod** with wax or adhesive.
7. **Form the dome** by turning the rod on the wheel, with gentle movements.
8. **Sand with progressively finer grits,** removing marks from the previous step before moving on to the next.
9. **Buff** with final abrasive to an even shine.
10. **Remove the stone from the rod,** clean it and check the result with a magnifying glass.

## From cabochon to faceted

When you master cabochon, you can move on to faceting. It is common to start with quartz, which is inexpensive and well tolerant of errors, and follow simple faceting diagrams. Little by little you learn the precision of the angles, the polishing of facets and the orientation of the stone, which we explain in the guide on [from rough to gem: orientation and performance](/es/del-bruto-a-la-gema-orientacion-y-rendimiento/).

## Organize a small workshop at home

If you decide to set up your own space, some decisions from the beginning avoid problems:

- **Choose a ventilated place** that is easy to clean and away from bedrooms and kitchens.
- **Separate the wet area** from the electrical area, with the machines on stable surfaces and cables protected.
- **Do not pour sanding mud down the drain:** stone sludge can clog pipes. Let it decant into a container and discard it as solid waste.
- **Have a first aid kit** and a suitable fire extinguisher on hand.
- **Keep sharp tools and abrasives** out of the reach of children and pets.
- **Label materials,** especially those that require special precautions.

An orderly workshop is not only safer: it also allows you to work more calmly and with better results.

## Where to learn

- **Mineralogy and lapidary clubs** which often have shared workshops and experienced amateurs.
- **Courses in technical schools, cultural centers or gemology institutes** in large cities.
- **Artisan workshops** that accept apprentices or teach classes.
- **Mineral fairs,** where you meet other fans and suppliers.

If you are interested in learning about the centers in the region, the guide on [lapidary workshops in Latin America](/es/talleres-lapidarios-de-america-latina/) is a good starting point.

## Materials to practice

- **Start with cheap and resistant materials,** such as agates, jaspers and quartz.
- **Rough purchase of legal origin,** with invoice, as we explain in the guide on [buying raw gems without deception](/es/comprar-gemas-en-bruto-sin-enganos/).
- **Reserve the valuable stones** for when you have experience.
- **Keep a record** of your work: material, steps and results.

## Frequent errors

- **Sand or dry polish.**
- **Work without glasses** or a suitable mask.
- **Wear loose clothing,** jewelry or loose hair near the machines.
- **Cut unknown minerals** without knowing if they are toxic.
- **Start with expensive stones** and faceting.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is lapidary dangerous?

It has risks, like any job with machines and dust, but with wet work, ventilation, protective equipment and good practices they are greatly reduced.

### What stone is best to start with?

Agates and jaspers for cabochons, and quartz for faceting. They are economical, resistant and easy to obtain.

### Do I need to buy machines to learn?

Not at first. A club or a shared workshop allows you to learn and decide later if it is worth the investment.

### Why do you have to work with water?

To cool the stone and tools and, above all, to trap silica dust and prevent it from being inhaled.

## In summary

Lapidary is an exciting craft that is best learned starting with cabochon, with inexpensive stones such as agate or jasper, and then moving on to faceting with quartz. The big risk is silica dust: always work wet, ventilate, use a suitable and clean mask without raising dust. Educate yourself about toxic minerals, respect machine guards and electrical safety, protect eyes and ears, and avoid loose clothing. Learn in clubs, courses or shared workshops before investing in equipment, and enjoy the process without putting your health at risk.
