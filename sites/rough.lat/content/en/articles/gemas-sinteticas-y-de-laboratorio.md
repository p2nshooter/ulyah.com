A lab-created ruby ​​has the same chemical composition, crystal structure, and hardness as a natural ruby. At first glance, even an experienced jeweler cannot distinguish them with certainty. The same happens with emeralds, sapphires, alexandrines or laboratory diamonds, which in recent years have gained an important place in jewelry stores around the world, including in Latin America.

Synthetic gems are not a scam in and of themselves. They are a different product, with advantages and limitations, which becomes a problem only when it is sold as natural or when the buyer does not understand what they are paying for. In this guide you will see how synthetic gems differ from imitations and treated gems, how they are made, how they are recognized, what names are correct, what resale value they have and in which cases they can be a good choice.

## Synthetic, imitation and treated: three different things

| Term | What is it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| natural | formed by nature, without human intervention except the carving | ruby extracted from a mine |
| Treated | natural, but with its appearance improved through a process | heated natural sapphire |
| Synthetic or laboratory | with the same composition and structure as the natural one, but created by humans | synthetic ruby, laboratory diamond |
| Imitation or simulant | different material that only looks like the gem | red glass, cubic zirconia like imitation diamond |

A synthetic stone is a real gem in the mineralogical sense, but it is not natural. An imitation is not even the same substance. We see it in detail in the guide on [imitations and doublets](/es/imitaciones-y-dobletes/).

## A history of more than a hundred years

Gem making is not new. In the early twentieth century, French chemist Auguste Verneuil perfected a method of producing synthetic rubies and sapphires by melting aluminum oxide powder in a flame, and within a few years they were being manufactured in industrial quantities. In the mid-twentieth century, the first synthetic diamonds reproducible using high pressure and high temperature were achieved, and in the following decades emeralds, opals and many other laboratory-created gems appeared.

## How they are made

| Method | How it works | Common Gems |
|---|---|---|
| Flame fusion | molten powder that falls drop by drop and crystallizes in a cylinder | ruby, sapphire, spinel |
| Stretched from a cast | a seed crystal is slowly extracted from a molten material | corundum, alexandrite and technical materials |
| Flux | The components dissolve in a flux and crystallize upon slow cooling | emerald, ruby, alexandrite |
| Hydrothermal | crystals that grow in pressurized hot water, imitating natural processes | emerald, quartz, corundum |
| High pressure and high temperature | imitates the conditions of the earth's mantle | diamond |
| Chemical vapor deposition | carbon from a gas is deposited layer by layer on a seed | diamond |

Slower and more expensive methods produce stones with fewer traces of their artificial origin and are more difficult to identify.

## laboratory diamonds

Laboratory diamonds are manufactured today on a large scale using two methods: high pressure and high temperature, and chemical vapor deposition. They are real diamonds, made of pure carbon with the same structure and hardness, and are classified with similar criteria of color, purity, cut and weight.

- **Identification:** They cannot be distinguished with the naked eye. Laboratories recognize them with specialized instruments that detect growth patterns and fluorescence and spectroscopy characteristics.
- **Inscriptions:** Many have laser engraved on the cutting edge, the edge of the stone, a mention of its laboratory origin and the number of its report.
- **Price:** Its price has dropped a lot in recent years and is much lower than that of a similar natural diamond.

## Moissanite

Moissanite is lab-created silicon carbide. In nature it only exists in tiny and very rare grains, first discovered in the remains of a meteorite. It is used as an alternative to diamond: it is very hard and shiny, with more flashes of color than diamond. It is not a synthetic diamond, but another material, and it is distinguished because it shows double refraction, which is seen as a doubling of the edges of the facets when viewed with a magnifying glass in certain directions.

## How are they recognized?

Synthetic gems usually leave traces of their manufacture that a gemologist identifies with a microscope:

| Method | Typical signs |
|---|---|
| Flame fusion | curved growth lines and round gas bubbles |
| Flux | fingerprint-like veils formed by remains of the flux; sometimes metal plates from the crucible |
| Hydrothermal | zigzag or V-shaped growth patterns and sometimes remnants of the seed plate |
| laboratory diamonds | growth patterns and responses to UV light and spectroscopy other than those in nature |

Natural gems, on the other hand, have inclusions of minerals and fluids typical of their geological environment. With high-quality synthetic stones, signs can be minimal, which is why lab reports are so important. We see it in the guide on [gemological laboratory reports](/es/informes-de-laboratorio-gemologico/).

## The correct names

Many countries' regulations and industry codes require that synthetic gemstones be clearly identified. Terms such as "synthetic", "laboratory created" or "laboratory" are correct. Be wary of ambiguous expressions such as "authentic", "genuine" or "real" applied to a laboratory stone without further explanation, and of commercial names that sound like natural varieties. A synthetic stone should never be sold as natural.

## Are they worth it?

**In favor:**

- **Price:** They allow access to colors and sizes that would be unattainable in nature.
- **Purity and color:** They are usually very clean and uniform.
- **Traceability:** their origin is known and they do not depend on mining.

**Against:**

- **Resale value:** very low, because its production has no natural limit and prices tend to fall.
- **Rarity:** They lack the rarity and geological history that many buyers value.
- **Perception:** some people prefer the symbolic value of a natural stone.

No option is better in absolute terms: it depends on what each person is looking for. The important thing is to choose with information.

## The environmental argument

Lab-grown gems are often presented as the eco-friendly option. It is true that they do not require mining, but their manufacture consumes a lot of energy, and their impact depends largely on the source of that energy. Mining, for its part, has highly variable environmental and social impacts depending on the mine and its practices. If this aspect matters to you, ask the seller for specific information instead of sticking with a slogan. The guide on [responsible gold and traceability](/es/oro-responsable-y-trazabilidad/) expands on these ideas.

## Uses you don't see

Synthetic gems are not only used in jewelry. Synthetic sapphire protects the dial of many watches, due to its resistance to scratches; synthetic ruby ​​was used in early lasers and as a bearing in mechanical watches; synthetic quartz is essential in electronics; and laboratory diamond is used in cutting tools and in research.

## Synthetics in tourist markets

In some markets in the region, especially those aimed at visitors, synthetic stones circulate presented as natural. Two frequent cases:

- **Synthetic emeralds** with a very intense color and almost no inclusions, offered as Colombians at prices that seem like an opportunity.
- **False alexandrites:** synthetic corundum or spinel that change color with light, sold as natural alexandrite, a rare and expensive gem.

The rule is simple: a natural gem of exceptional color, great size and great purity is never cheap. If the price seems too good, ask for a lab report before paying or buy knowing it's synthetic. The guide on [buy raw gems without deception](/es/comprar-gemas-en-bruto-sin-enganos/) expands on these tips.

## How to buy a synthetic gem

1. **Request that it be stated in writing** that it is synthetic or laboratory-made.
2. **Compare prices** with other laboratory stones, not with natural ones.
3. **In laboratory diamonds,** ask for the report and check the inscription on the edge.
4. **Do not buy it as an investment:** its resale value is low.
5. **Choose for beauty and wear,** as you would with any other jewelry.

## Frequent errors

- **Confuse synthetic with imitation.**
- **Pay for a synthetic** a price close to that of a natural one.
- **Buy a synthetic as an investment.**
- **Accept ambiguous names** without written clarification.
- **Believe that every very clean gem** is necessarily synthetic.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a lab diamond a real diamond?

Yes, it has the same composition and structure as a natural one. The difference is in its origin, which must be declared, and its market value.

### Can you distinguish it with the naked eye?

Not for sure. Laboratory instruments are needed, especially for diamonds and high-quality synthetic gems.

### Do synthetic gems lose value?

Their resale value is usually low, and in the case of laboratory diamonds, prices have dropped significantly in recent years.

### Is moissanite a synthetic diamond?

No. It is silicon carbide, another material with different properties that is used as an alternative to diamond.

## In summary

A synthetic gem has the same composition and structure as a natural gem, but is created in a laboratory; It is not an imitation or a treated stone. Rubies, sapphires, emeralds, alexandrites and diamonds are manufactured using methods such as flame fusion, fluxing, hydrothermal growth, high pressure and vapor phase deposition. They are recognized by traces of their manufacture that a gemologist detects with instruments. They are a valid option if they are purchased knowing what they are, with their correct name in writing and at a corresponding price, but not as an investment.
