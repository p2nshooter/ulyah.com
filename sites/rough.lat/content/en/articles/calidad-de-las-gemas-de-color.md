For diamonds there is a widespread classification system, with letters for color and acronyms for purity. For colored gemstones, such as rubies, sapphires, emeralds, tourmalines or aquamarines, there is no single scale that everyone uses in the same way. Each laboratory, each merchant and each market has its own references, and that confuses many buyers.

Even so, professionals share a way of looking. They know that color weighs more than any other factor, that inclusions are judged differently depending on the species, that a bad cut can dull an excellent stone and that the origin and treatments greatly change the value. In this guide you will see how color is analyzed in its three dimensions, how light influences it, how purity and cut are valued, what role weight, origin and treatments play and how to look at a gem before buying it.

## Why is there no single scale?

Colored gems belong to dozens of species with different properties, and each one has its own range of colors, typical inclusions and treatments. A shade that is ideal in an aquamarine is not ideal in a sapphire, and an acceptable level of inclusions in an emerald would be a serious defect in a topaz. That's why valuing a colored gem requires comparison with stones of the same species and often with the help of a professional.

## Color: hue, tone and saturation

Color is almost always the most important factor. Gemologists describe it with three dimensions:

| Dimension | What does it describe? | What are you looking for in general? |
|---|---|---|
| Nuance | the color itself: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet | a pure and characteristic nuance of the species |
| Tone | how light or dark the color is | medium to medium dark tones, without looking black |
| Saturation | the intensity or purity of the color, compared to grayish or brown | intense, strong or vivid colors |

A stone that is too light appears faded; one that is too dark loses brightness and looks black in low light. Between both extremes is the most appreciated stripe, which changes depending on the species.

## Secondary colors and masks

Many colors have a secondary hue: a slightly violet red, a somewhat greenish blue, a green with a hint of yellow or blue. Depending on the species and the market, this secondary nuance can add or subtract value. What almost always remains are the so-called saturation masks: grayish or brownish components that dull the color. A ruby ​​with a brown veil or a sapphire with a gray tint is worth less than stones of a clean hue.

## How color changes with light

The color of a gem depends on the light that illuminates it:

- **Natural daylight:** the most common reference for judging color.
- **Warm light:** Favors red and orange stones, and can dull blues.
- **Cold light:** enhances blues and greens.
- **Jewelry spotlights:** very intense, they make any stone shine and can deceive.

Always look at a gem under several lights before deciding. Some gems, such as alexandrite, change color dramatically depending on the light, and in them this change is precisely what is valued.

## Purity: three types of gems

As each species usually forms under different conditions, some appear almost always clean and others almost always with inclusions. That is why there are three types of gems in terms of purity:

| Type | Examples | What is expected |
|---|---|---|
| Type I | aquamarine, topaz, morganite, blue zircon | They are usually clean at first glance; Visible inclusions detract a lot from value |
| Type II | ruby, sapphire, garnet, peridot, many tourmalines | They usually have some inclusions; are accepted if they do not affect the appearance |
| Type III | emerald, red tourmaline | they almost always have visible inclusions; are valued with more tolerance |

Thus, an emerald with a visible garden may be excellent, while a topaz with a similar inclusion would be of low quality. We see it in the guide on [Colombian emeralds](/es/esmeraldas-colombianas-de-la-mina-a-la-mesa/).

## Inclusions that subtract and those that add

- **Remaining:** cracks that reach the surface, because they affect the resistance; large, dark inclusions in visible areas; clouds that cloud the stone.
- **They can add:** fine needles that produce stars or cat eyes; a smooth silkiness that gives some sapphires a velvety appearance; inclusions that confirm a natural or geographical origin.

Position also matters: an inclusion under the board, the top facet, is much more visible than one near the edge, where it can be hidden by the setting.

## The carving of a colored gem

In colored gems, the cut seeks above all to show the color, but also to give life and shine to the stone. When you look at it, notice:

- **Windows:** central areas where you can see through the stone, like glass, because the bottom is too low and the light escapes.
- **Extinction:** dark areas that appear on the stone seen from the front, often due to a background that is too deep.
- **Proportions:** Many stones are originally cut with very deep bottoms to conserve weight; They appear smaller from the front than their weight suggests.
- **Symmetry and polishing:** Well-aligned facets and mark-free surfaces.

## Weight and size

In most species, large, good quality stones are much rarer than small ones, so the price per carat usually increases with size. But weight is not everything: two stones of the same weight can look different sizes depending on their density and cut. Always compare measurements in millimeters, in addition to weight.

## Transparency and shine

A good transparent gem is alive: light enters, bounces off the facets and returns to the eye. Stones that are milky, cloudy or dull in appearance lose their appeal, even if their color is good. For gemstones that are cabochon cut, such as some emeralds or star sapphires, surface translucency and brilliance are also important.

## Origin and treatment

- **Origin:** In some gems, geographic origin influences the market, such as Colombian emeralds or certain Asian rubies and sapphires. Only a laboratory can suggest the origin with substance.
- **Treatment:** Many gemstones are heated, filled, or treated in other ways to improve their appearance. Untreated stones, of comparable quality, tend to be valued more highly.

We see it in the guides on [gem treatments](/es/tratamientos-de-gemas/) and [gemological laboratory reports](/es/informes-de-laboratorio-gemologico/).

## Optical phenomena

Some gems are valued for special effects:

- **Asterism:** a four or six-armed star on the surface of a cabochon.
- **Cat's eye:** a luminous line that moves when the stone is moved.
- **Color change:** different color with natural light and warm light.
- **Play of colors:** flashes of spectral colors, as in precious opal.

The sharpness, intensity and position of the phenomenon are judged on these stones, in addition to the background color.

## How to look at a gem

1. **Use indirect natural light** or a neutral white light, and then compare with other lights.
2. **Place the stone on a white card** to see its color without interference.
3. **Look at it from the front,** how it will look assembled, and then from the side.
4. **Move it slowly** to see the brightness, windows and extinction.
5. **Use a 10x magnifying glass** for inclusions, but judge the overall appearance by eye.
6. **Compare with other stones** of the same species and size.

## Frequent errors

- **Judging the color under the spotlights** of a display case.
- **Apply to all gems** the diamond purity criteria.
- **Buy by weight** without comparing measurements.
- **Ignore windows** and dark areas of the carving.
- **Do not ask about treatments** or ask for information on important stones.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most important thing in a colored gem?

In the vast majority of species, the color: a pure hue, a medium to medium dark tone and an intense saturation. Next come purity, size, weight, origin and treatment.

### Is a gem with inclusions of poor quality?

Not necessarily. It depends on the species, the size and position of the inclusions and whether they affect appearance or strength.

### Why do two stones of the same weight appear to be different sizes?

Because each species has a different density and because the size can be deeper or flatter. That is why it is convenient to compare the measurements in millimeters.

### Is there an official scale for colored gems?

Not a single universal. Laboratories and retailers use proprietary descriptions and systems, which is why a detailed report is so useful.

## In summary

The quality of a colored gem is judged primarily by its color, described by its hue, hue, and saturation, and by how it behaves under different lights. Purity is valued based on the type of gem, because some species almost always have inclusions. The size should show the color without windows or excessive fading, and the weight is compared along with the measurements. The origin, treatments and optical phenomena complete the assessment. Look at each stone in neutral light, head on and in motion, and rely on a lab report for important pieces.
