When a gem costs enough that making a mistake hurts, a lab report stops being a formality and becomes the centerpiece of the purchase. It is the document that confirms what the stone is, whether it is natural or synthetic, whether it has received treatments and, in some cases, what region of the world it might come from. For diamonds, it also includes the color, purity and cut classification that determines a good part of their price.

But not all reports are worth the same. There are laboratories of international prestige, serious local laboratories and official-looking documents that have no support. Knowing how to read a report and check its authenticity is as important as looking at the stone. In this guide you will see what a gemological report contains, what types exist, how to verify it, what signs should put you on guard, what a report does not tell you and how it differs from an appraisal.

## What is a gemological report

A gemological report is a document issued by an independent laboratory that records the result of the analysis of a stone on a specific date. Many laboratories prefer the word "report" to "certificate", because what they issue is a technical opinion based on available analysis, not an absolute guarantee.

A good report is independent: the laboratory does not buy or sell the stone, and its prestige depends on the accuracy of its results.

## What's included

| Section | What does it say |
|---|---|
| Identification | the species and variety: corundum, ruby variety; beryl, emerald variety |
| Natural or synthetic | whether the stone is natural, synthetic or an imitation |
| Weight and measurements | weight in carats and dimensions in millimeters |
| Shape and size | the shape of the stone and the style of carving |
| Color | a description of the color, sometimes using laboratory terms |
| Treatments | if there are signs of heating, fillers, dyes, diffusion or other |
| Origin | in some reports, an opinion on the geographical origin |
| Photography | an image of the stone to identify it |
| Comments | additional laboratory observations |
| Number and date | the report identifier and the analysis date |

## Report Types

- **Identification report:** confirms what the stone is and if it is treated. It is the most common for colored gems.
- **Report with opinion of origin:** adds the probable geographical region, especially in rubies, sapphires, emeralds and some other valuable gems.
- **Diamond grading report:** includes color, purity, cut, proportions, fluorescence and a diagram of inclusions.
- **Brief reports:** summarized, cheaper versions for stones of lower value.

## Recognized laboratories

In the international market, some laboratories enjoy wide recognition, such as the GIA, based in the United States, or laboratories in Switzerland and other countries specialized in colored gems. There are also serious national and regional laboratories in Latin America, some very specialized, such as those dedicated to emeralds in Bogotá.

The prestige of a laboratory matters because the market trusts some more than others, especially for opinions of origin and for diamond classification. Before purchasing an important stone, ask which laboratory issued the report and see if it is known and accepted in the market where you may want to sell it one day.

## How to check a report

1. **See report number** on the lab page if it offers online verification.
2. **Check that the data matches** with the stone: weight, measurements, shape and color.
3. **Compare the photograph** of the report with the stone.
4. **On diamonds, look for the laser inscription** on the edge, if it has one, and verify that it matches the number on the report.
5. **Look at the date:** An old report may not reflect later changes, such as oil drying or a stone cutout.
6. **If you have doubts,** ask for a new verification from the laboratory or another prestigious one.

## Warning signs

| Signal | Why worry |
|---|---|
| Unknown laboratory, without address or verifiable website | may not be independent or may not exist |
| Report issued by the seller himself | is not independent |
| Data that does not match the stone | the report may correspond to another gem |
| Vague or advertising language | a technical report describes; does not promote |
| Market values written in the report | serious laboratories do not usually put prices on an identification report |
| Lack of information about treatments | in commonly treated gems, it is a suspicious omission |

## What a report doesn't say

- **This is not an appraisal:** it does not indicate the price of the stone.
- **Does not guarantee the future:** describes the stone on the date of analysis; Treatments like oils can change over time.
- **Does not describe the complete jewelry:** metal, setting or small stones are usually not included.
- **Not infallible:** some treatments or origins cannot be determined with certainty, and different laboratories may reach different conclusions.

## The origin: an expert opinion

Determining which country or region a gem comes from is based on the study of its inclusions and chemistry, compared to reference collections. It is a technical opinion, not a certainty. Some regions produce very similar stones, and sometimes the laboratory cannot pronounce. As the origin can greatly influence the price, for example in [Colombian emeralds](/es/esmeraldas-colombianas-de-la-mina-a-la-mesa/), it is advisable that this opinion comes from a recognized laboratory.

## How to read the treatments

The reports use formulas that are important to understand:

- **"No signs of heating":** The laboratory found no signs that the stone had been heated.
- **"Indications of heating":** the stone was heated; Sometimes it is added if there is residue in the cracks and in what quantity.
- **Grades of fill in emeralds:** from insignificant or minor to moderate or important.
- **"Synthetic" or "laboratory created":** the stone is not natural.
- **"Composite" or "assembled":** This is a doublet, triplet, or glass-filled material.

## Diamonds: the ranking report

In diamonds, the report is almost essential for stones of a certain size. It collects the color, purity and size according to the laboratory scale, weight, proportions, symmetry and polishing, fluorescence and a diagram with the position of the inclusions. It also indicates whether the diamond is natural or laboratory-made and whether it has received treatments. Grading criteria may vary slightly between laboratories, so two reports from different laboratories are not always equivalent. ## When is it worth ordering one?

- **Major purchases,** when the price justifies the cost of the analysis.
- **Stones that are sold as natural, untreated** or with a specific origin.
- **Diamonds** of a certain size.
- **Inherited jewelry** whose content is not known with certainty.
- **Before insuring or selling** a valuable item.

For stones of low value, the cost of a report may exceed that of the gem itself; In those cases, a trusted gemologist may be enough.

## How to send a stone to the laboratory

- **Dismantle the stone if possible:** Some analyzes cannot be done with the stone set, and the report may be incomplete.
- **Use insured shipping** or deliver in person.
- **Ask about deadlines and fees** before submitting, because they vary depending on the laboratory and service.
- **Keep receipt** and tracking number.

## Report and appraisal are not the same

The report identifies and describes the stone; The appraisal estimates its value for a specific purpose, such as insurance or sale. For a serious appraisal, the appraiser usually relies on a laboratory report. We explain it in the guide on [jewelry insurance and appraisal](/es/seguro-y-tasacion-de-joyas/).

## Frequent errors

- **Accept a report from the seller himself** as if he were independent.
- **Do not verify the number** of the report on the laboratory website.
- **Confuse report with appraisal.**
- **Confirm an opinion of origin** from a little-known laboratory.
- **Forget that the report is dated** and that some treatments change over time.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do all gems need a report?

No. For stones of low value, the cost of the report may not be worth it. It is recommended for large purchases, for diamonds of a certain size and when it is stated that a stone is not treated or comes from a specific origin.

### Does a report guarantee the value of the stone?

No. Describe what the stone is; The value depends on the market and is estimated by an appraisal.

### Why can two laboratories give different results?

Because some analyses, such as origin or certain treatments, are technical opinions, and because classification scales may vary slightly between laboratories.

### Can a report be made on a set stone?

Sometimes yes, but some analyzes require disassembling it, and the report may be more limited.

## In summary

A gemological report is the technical opinion of an independent laboratory on what a stone is, whether it is natural or synthetic, what treatments it has and, sometimes, where it comes from. Choose recognized laboratories, verify the number on their website, check that the data matches the stone and be wary of reports from the seller himself or from unknown laboratories. Remember that the report is dated, that the origin is an opinion and that it does not replace an appraisal. For important purchases, conditioning the purchase on the result of a good laboratory is the best protection.
