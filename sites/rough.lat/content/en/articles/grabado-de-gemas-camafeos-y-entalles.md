Long before faceted cutting existed, gems were works of art. Antique engravers carved tiny figures in hard stones to use as seals, amulets or ornaments, and some of these pieces, just a few centimeters in size, are today among the treasures of great museums. This art is called **glyptic**, and it is still alive in workshops that engrave cameos, signet rings and portraits in stone with techniques that mix ancient and modern tools.

In this guide you will see what differentiates a carving from a cameo, how this art has evolved, what stones and materials are used, how an engraver works, how to distinguish a hand-carved piece from one molded or machine-made, and how to care for these jewelry.

## Engraving and cameo: two ways to engrave

| | Fit | cameo |
|---|---|---|
| How to carve | the figure is excavated into the stone | the figure stands out in relief |
| What was it for? | seal documents on wax or clay | ornament, portrait, prestige object |
| How it looks | in hollow; wax printing gives relief | embossed, often two-color |
| Common materials | chalcedony, carnelian, jasper, rock crystal, hematite | layered stones such as onyx and sardonyx, shells, lava |

In a notch, the image is **inverted**, like in a mirror, so that the print comes out right side up. That is why signet rings with indented shields or initials can still be used for sealing today. If you are interested in signet rings, check out the guide on [men's jewelry](/es/joyeria-masculina/).

## A history of thousands of years

- **Mesopotamia:** Cylindrical seals**, small engraved stone cylinders that were rolled on wet clay, were used to sign contracts and mark properties.
- **Egypt:** the **scarabs** carved in stone or glazed ceramic were both amulets and seals.
- **Greece and Rome:** engravers achieved extraordinary mastery in engravings and cameos of chalcedony, sardonyx and other stones. Some imperial Roman cameos, large in size and carved from layered stones, are preserved today in European museums.
- **Renaissance and Neoclassicism:** Collectors once again sought out ancient gems, and European workshops produced copies, imitations and new works. In the 18th century, even **glass pastes** that reproduced famous carvings became popular.
- **19th century:** The shell cameo became a very popular piece of jewelry, with specialized workshops in southern Italy.

In the Hispanic world, another material with a glyptic tradition is **azabache**, a shiny black lignite that has been carved for centuries. The workshops in northern Spain worked on it for pilgrims on the Camino de Santiago, and the custom of the jet amulet traveled to America, where even today, in several countries in the Caribbean and the region, bracelets with a small jet are given to newborns.

## The materials

### Layered stones

The great secret of the classic cameo is to use stones with **layers of different colors**. The engraver carves the figure in a light layer and leaves a dark layer in the background, or the other way around. The most used are:

- **onyx**, with black and white layers;
- **sardonyx**, with white and reddish brown layers;
- **banded agate**, with layers of various colors.

Many of these stones are dyed to accentuate contrast, an ancient practice in Chalcedony. You can learn more about this family in the guide on [quartzes from Latin America](/es/cuarzos-de-america-latina/).

### Other stones

- **Carnelian, jasper and heliotrope**, classic in cuts and stamps.
- **Rock crystal**, for transparent cuts and pieces that play with light.
- **Lapis lazuli and malachite**, for intensely colored cameos, although malachite is soft.
- **Hematite**, widely used in signet rings for its metallic shine.

### Organic and other materials

- **Shells** of certain marine mollusks, with layers of colors that allow cameos of great delicacy.
- **Volcanic lava**, carved in reliefs of gray and brown tones.
- **Coral**, today subject to protection restrictions in many places.
- **Azabache**, black and light.

## How a recorder works

The work of a stone engraver is not similar to that of a metal engraver. Hard stone is not cut with a burin: it is worn down with abrasives.

1. **The design:** the figure is drawn on the stone, taking into account the layers, color and inclusions.
2. **Roughing:** with rotating tools that contain abrasive, traditionally metal points loaded with emery or diamond dust, and today diamond burs, the material is reduced.
3. **Modeling:** with increasingly finer tips, volumes, hair, folds, expressions are defined.
4. **Polishing:** The surfaces are polished with fine powders, with special care in the reliefs.
5. **The control:** in the notches, impressions are made in wax or plasticine to check the result.

A quality cameo or carving can take anywhere from days to months of work, depending on the size, material, and detail.

### Modern techniques

Today there are also:

- **ultrasound carving**, which reproduces the same design on many stones at the same time;
- **carving with numerical control and laser machines**, which copy digital models;
- **molding** of glass, resin or composite materials.

These techniques are not bad in themselves, but the value of a piece hand-carved by a good engraver is very different from that of a mass-produced piece.

## How to distinguish a hand-carved piece

With a 10x magnifying glass and some practice you can recognize many differences:

| Signal | hand carved | Molded or serial |
|---|---|---|
| Depth | variable, adapted to each volume | uniform |
| Background | with small natural irregularities | perfectly flat |
| Details | fine, with polished tool marks | rounded or schematic |
| Repetition | each piece is unique | identical pieces |
| Material | cold to the touch, stone hardness | glass and resin may have bubbles; the resin feels warm and light |
| Layers | the figure and the background belong to the same stone | sometimes they are two pieces glued together |

Some cameos are made by **gluing** a figure of one material on a background of another. Look at the outline from the side: a line of glue or a sudden change in material will give it away. The [imitations, doublets and triplets](/es/imitaciones-y-dobletes/) guide explains how to detect assembled parts.

## Order an engraving today

There are still engravers who accept commissions: family crests, initials for a signet ring, portraits or personal motifs. If you are interested:

- **Carry clear references:** a drawing, a well-defined shield or photos of the motif. The simpler the design, the better it reads on a small surface.
- **Choose the stone well:** Chalcedonies, such as carnelian or onyx, are the most appreciated for their hardness and tenacity. Stones with fractures or cleavage break easily during work.
- **Decide if it will be a cut or cameo:** in hollow if you want to seal with it, in relief if it is just an ornament.
- **Ask about the technique:** by hand, mechanically or with laser. Change the price, the term and the result.
- **Approve the design before starting**, and in a detail check that the text is inverted so that the print is legible.

## Buy and inherit cameos and engravings

Many cameos come to us as inheritance. Before deciding what to do with them:

- **identifies material**: stone, shell, lava, glass or resin;
- **check condition**: cracks in the shells, wear on the reliefs, loose mount;
- **look for markings** on the metal frame, which can help date it;
- **ask for an appraisal** if you think the piece may be antique or from a good engraver.

The guide on [inherited and second-hand jewelry](/es/joyas-heredadas-y-de-segunda-mano/) explains how to value, restore and distribute these types of pieces.

To buy:

- ask if the piece is **hand carved** and by whom;
- be wary of "old" cameos at very low prices and in identical series;
- requests that the invoice indicate the **material**, the **technique** and, if stated, the **age**.

## Care

- **Shells, coral, jet and lava** are soft: store them separately, away from hard stones.
- **Do not use ultrasound, steam or chemicals.**
- Clean with a **soft brush** and a barely damp cloth, without leaving water on the reliefs.
- **Avoid sudden changes in temperature**, which can crack the shells.
- Check the **mount**: many cameos are held on with small claws that come loose.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I still use a signet ring to seal letters?

Yes, if the engraving is indented and inverted. It is pressed on hot sealing wax and the relief image is obtained.

### Does a shell cameo have little value?

Not necessarily. A hand-carved shell cameo by a good artist can be a valuable piece. The value depends on the quality of the carving, the condition, and sometimes the age and setting.

### How do I know if my cameo is made of stone or shell?

The shell is usually somewhat translucent when backlit, lighter and with a slightly curved texture. The stone is colder, heavier and harder. A gemologist can confirm this without damaging the piece.

## In summary

Glypticism, the art of engraving gems, distinguishes two forms: the engraving, excavated and designed to seal, and the cameo, carved in relief and often in two colors thanks to layered stones such as onyx or sardonyx. It was born in Mesopotamia, reached its splendor in Greece and Rome, returned with force in the Renaissance and in the 19th century, and is still alive in workshops that combine ancient and modern tools. To buy or inherit wisely, it is advisable to identify the material, distinguish what is hand-carved from what is molded or mechanical, check the condition and the setting, and take special care of the shell, coral, lava or jet pieces.
