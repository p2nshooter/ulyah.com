Few things are as discouraging as wearing beautiful earrings for the first time and, the next day, having a red, swollen and itchy earlobe. Or discovering a greenish mark on your finger after a weekend with a new ring. Skin reactions to jewelry are very common, and almost always have a simple explanation. Knowing it helps you make better choices, avoid discomfort, and know when it is best to consult a doctor.

In this guide you will see what types of reactions exist, why nickel is the main protagonist, which metals are usually best tolerated, what the "hypoallergenic" label means, why some jewelry stains the skin and what is good to know about piercings. This guide is informative: if you have a skin reaction, the best person to diagnose and treat it is a dermatologist.

## Three different things that are confused

When a piece of jewelry "gives an allergy," one of three things may actually be happening:

1. **A contact allergy**: the immune system reacts to a specific metal. It causes redness, itching, pimples or peeling right where the jewelry touches the skin, sometimes hours or days after contact.
2. **An irritation**: it is not an allergy, but a skin reaction due to friction, humidity, sweat or soap residue trapped under the jewel.
3. **A stain or discoloration**: The skin turns green, gray or black due to the reaction of the metal with sweat or cosmetics. It is not usually an allergy and disappears when you wash the skin.

Distinguishing them helps you find the right solution.

## Nickel, the great protagonist

**Nickel** is a metal widely used in alloys because it provides hardness, shine and white color at a low cost. It is found in a lot of jewelry, in some white gold, in some steel and in brooches, buckles and metal buttons on clothing.

Nickel allergy is one of the most common contact allergies. Some features that should be known:

- Once a person has been sensitized to nickel, the sensitivity usually remains for years;
- **piercings**, such as those in the ears, are a common route of sensitization, because the metal is in contact with a wound;
- Sweat and humidity increase the amount of nickel a piece releases.

In the European Union, for example, there is a legal limit on the amount of nickel that can be released by objects that come into prolonged contact with the skin, such as jewelry. In other countries the standards are different or do not exist, so it is advisable to ask the seller about the composition of the metal.

## Which metals are usually best tolerated?

No metal is suitable for one hundred percent of people, but some are well tolerated in the vast majority of cases:

| metal | Why is it usually tolerated? | To take into account |
|---|---|---|
| Implant Grade Titanium | very inert and light | Not all commercial titanium is the same grade |
| Niobium | inert; it is colored by anodizing | less common in fine jewelry |
| Platinum | very stable; jewelry alloys contain little more | high price |
| High purity gold (18 to 22 carats) | less added metals | the 24 carat is very soft |
| Nickel-free white gold | alloyed with palladium or other metals | asks the composition; many white golds have rhodium plating |
| Sterling silver | tolerated by many people | contains copper; can darken and stain the skin |

Some important clarifications:

- **White gold is not always nickel-free.** There are white gold alloys with nickel and others with palladium. If you have an allergy, ask for confirmation of the composition in writing.
- **Rhodium plating protects while it lasts.** Many white gold jewelry has a layer of rhodium that acts as a barrier. When it wears out, the metal underneath touches the skin again.
- **"Surgical steel" is not a guarantee.** There are steels that release very little nickel and others that release more. Some very sensitive people react even to the former.

## What does "hypoallergenic" mean?

In many countries, **"hypoallergenic" is not a regulated term**. It indicates that the manufacturer believes the part causes fewer reactions, but does not say what metal it contains or guarantee anything. It is much more useful to ask:

- What metal exactly is the piece, including the part that touches the skin?
- Does it have nickel in the alloy or only in the bath?
- Is the earring post the same metal as the rest?

A "gold" piece of jewelry may have a post of another metal. Check it out, especially on earrings.

## Green fingers and other spots

If a ring leaves a green mark on you, it most likely contains **copper**, either in an alloy like brass or bronze, in sterling silver, or under a worn-out plating. Sweat, creams and seawater react with copper and form greenish compounds that stain the skin.

- **It is not usually an allergy.** The stain disappears when you wash the skin with soap and water.
- **To avoid this**, keep the jewelry and skin dry, remove the ring to wash your hands and apply creams, and ask a jeweler to re-dip the piece if the dip has worn out.
- **Gray or black spots** are usually due to silver or metals that react with certain cosmetics.

## "Ring dermatitis"

Many people who wear gold or platinum rings for years without problems suddenly develop a red, itchy area under the band. Often it is not a metal allergy, but **irritation from soap, water and dirt trapped under the ring**. Some guidelines:

- Take off the ring to wash your hands, scrub, apply creams or use hydroalcoholic gel;
- dry your skin well before putting it back on;
- clean the inside of the ring regularly;
- If the skin does not improve, consult a dermatologist.

## Perforations: a special case

A fresh piercing is a wound, and the metal placed in it is in direct contact with healing tissue. That's why:

- It is advisable to go to **professionals** who work with sterile material and with jewelry made of **materials suitable for implants**, such as implant-grade titanium, niobium, high-purity nickel-free gold or platinum;
- It is preferable to wait for the piercing to heal before switching to costume jewelry earrings;
- If signs of infection appear, such as increasing pain, heat, pus or fever, you should see a doctor.

## How to choose jewelry if you have sensitive skin

1. **Always ask about the composition**, including that of posts, closures and welds.
2. **Prioritize the most tolerated metals**: implant grade titanium, niobium, platinum, high purity gold or nickel-free alloys.
3. **Be wary of the "hypoallergenic" label** if it is not accompanied by the name of the metal.
4. **Please note baths**: they protect while they last; He asks what's underneath.
5. **Try small pieces** and for a short time before wearing a new piece of jewelry all day.
6. **Keep the skin and jewelry dry and clean**, especially in summer and when exercising.
7. **If you are allergic to nickel, tell your jeweler** before ordering a piece or repair: some solders may contain it.

## When to see a doctor

It is advisable to consult a dermatologist if:

- The reaction is repeated with different jewelry;
- It also appears with buckles, buttons or other metal objects;
- the skin cracks, oozes or does not improve when the jewelry is removed;
- you want to know exactly what metal you react to.

The dermatologist can do **specific tests**, such as patch or patch tests, to identify the responsible substance. It is not advisable to self-medicate or apply home remedies on irritated skin.

## Care that helps

- Clean the jewelry with warm water and mild soap, and dry well.
- Store the pieces in a dry place.
- Renew rhodium or gold plating when it wears out.
- Do not share earrings, for hygiene.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I be allergic to gold?

Reactions to gold itself are rare. The most common thing is to react to the metals with which gold is alloyed, especially in low carats.

### Does clear nail polish serve as a barrier?

Some people use it as a temporary solution on the inside of rings or posts, but it wears out quickly and is not a substitute for jewelry made of a well-tolerated metal.

### Is it safe to give earrings to someone with sensitive skin?

Yes, if you choose well-tolerated metals and indicate it on the card. The guide on [gifting jewelry](/es/regalar-joyas/) has more ideas.

## In summary

Reactions to jewelry can be contact allergies, irritations from moisture and soap, or simple stains from the reaction of the metal with sweat. Nickel is the most common cause of allergy, and it is in many jewelry, in some white gold and in some steel. Implant-grade titanium, niobium, platinum, and high-purity nickel-free gold are typically better tolerated, and the "hypoallergenic" label says little if it doesn't name the metal. Green fingers are almost always due to copper and are not an allergy. If a reaction recurs or does not improve, it is best to consult a dermatologist, who can identify the cause with specific tests.
