Some of the most striking jewels in the region do not have a large faceted stone in the center, but rather dozens of small stone pieces fitted together like a puzzle: turquoise next to coral, lapis lazuli next to mother-of-pearl, malachite next to onyx. It is the art of **inlay**, one of the oldest techniques in goldsmithing and stone carving, which requires millimeter precision, patience and a good eye for color.

In this guide you will see the great inlay techniques, their history in America and other cultures, what materials are used, how to make a piece step by step, how to distinguish a real stone inlay from a mixture of powder and resin, and how to care for these jewelry so that the pieces do not come loose.

## What is embedding

Inlaying is **fitting pieces of stone, shell or other materials** into a support, metal, wood or other stone, so that the surface is continuous. Unlike traditional setting, where the metal holds the stone with claws or bevels, in inlay pieces are custom-cut to fill a gap and are almost always secured with an adhesive in addition to mechanical adjustment.

## The great techniques

| Technique | How is it | Where to see |
|---|---|---|
| Mosaic | many small parts on a stand, with no metal walls between them | masks, ceremonial objects, pendants |
| Channel embedding | pieces carved to fit into cells separated by thin metal walls | silver rings, bracelets and pendants |
| Intarsia | pieces of stone joined together to form a continuous surface, sometimes faceted or embossed | signature jewelry, pendants, boxes |
| hard stone | High precision hard stone mosaic that forms realistic images | tables, paintings, boxes and some jewelry |
| One-piece inlay | a sheet of stone fitted into a metal hole | signet rings, cufflinks, bangles |

## A story that is also American

### Mesoamerica and the Andes

The turquoise mosaics of the Mixtec and Mexica peoples are among the most famous in the world: masks, shields, ceremonial knives and figures covered with thousands of tesserae of turquoise, shell, coral and other stones, glued with natural resins on wood or bone. In the Andes, cultures such as the Moche combined gold with mosaics of turquoise, shell and other stones in earflaps and ornaments of great refinement. You can learn more about these traditions in the guide on [pre-Columbian goldsmithing](/es/orfebreria-precolombina/) and about turquoise in the guide on [copper stones](/es/piedras-del-cobre/).

### Other traditions

- Florentine **hard stone**, developed in the Medici workshops, created authentic stone paintings with marble, jaspers, lapis lazuli and other stones cut with astonishing accuracy.
- In India, the technique known as **pietra dura** or **parchin kari** decorated palaces and mausoleums with semi-precious stone flowers embedded in marble.
- In the American Southwest, silversmiths from several Native peoples developed highly recognizable styles of channel inlay and mosaic using turquoise, coral, jet, and shell.

### Latin American silverware

In the silver workshops of Mexico, Peru and other countries in the region it is common to see pieces that combine silver with **inlays of mother-of-pearl, abalone, malachite, lapis lazuli, obsidian or turquoise**. The guide on [Latin American silversmithing and filigree](/es/plateria-y-filigrana-latinoamericana/) covers these craft centers.

## The materials

- **Turquoise, chrysocolla and malachite**, due to their color, although they are soft and porous.
- **Lapis lazuli**, one of the most intense blues; In the region, Chile stands out, as we tell in the guide on [Chilean lapis lazuli] (/es/lapislazuli-chileno/).
- **Coral, mother-of-pearl and abalone**, for its shine and its red, white and iridescent tones.
- **Onyx, jet and obsidian**, for deep blacks.
- **Opal**, sometimes in sheets and sometimes crushed and mixed with resin.
- **Jaspers, agates and ornamental stones**, for hard stone and intarsia.

The choice not only depends on the color, but also on **hardness**: combining a very hard stone with a very soft one on the same surface makes polishing difficult, because the soft one wears out sooner and remains sunken.

## How to make a channel inlay

1. **The metal:** the silversmith builds the piece with cells or channels, welding thin walls onto a base.
2. **The selection of the stone:** sheets with the appropriate color and pattern are chosen for each cell.
3. **The Cut:** Each piece is cut a little larger than the cell and worn away until it fits precisely.
4. **Gluing:** the pieces are fixed with an adhesive, today almost always an epoxy resin.
5. **Leveling:** once the adhesive is dry, the entire surface is sanded at the same time, stone and metal, until it is smooth.
6. **Polishing:** The set is carefully polished so that the soft stones do not sink and the metal shines.

In **mosaic**, the process is similar, but the pieces are placed next to each other on a support, without metal walls, and the fit between them is what defines the quality.

## Authentic inlay or "reconstituted stone"

A very widespread practice, especially in inexpensive pieces, is to fill the gaps with **stone powder or granules mixed with resin**, or directly with **dyed resin**. The result may be beautiful and durable, but it is not the same as a carved inlay, and should be sold as such.

| Signal | carved pieces | Powder and resin |
|---|---|---|
| Surface | You can see the grain and natural texture of each piece | grainy or uniform appearance |
| Boundaries between pieces | fine and precise lines | diffuse mixture or without clear boundaries |
| Color | natural variations within the same stone | very homogeneous color |
| Touch and temperature | cold and hard | somewhat warmer; the resin scratches more |
| Price | reflects work hours | much lower |

**Plastics and resins** that imitate turquoise, coral or onyx are also used. The [imitations, doublets and triplets](/es/imitaciones-y-dobletes/) guide explains how to recognize them.

## Design an embedded part

If you order a piece with an inlay from a workshop, these decisions greatly change the result:

- **The palette:** two or three well-chosen colors usually work better than many. The contrast between a dark stone and a light one, such as onyx and mother-of-pearl, gives a very legible drawing.
- **The hardness of the stones:** Combining stones of similar hardness facilitates uniform polishing and a surface that ages equally.
- **The size of the pieces:** the very small tesserae look like meticulous work, but each joint is a weak point; In rings for daily use, it is advisable for the pieces to be somewhat larger.
- **The surface:** flat, domed or embossed. Domed surfaces take more hits in the center.
- **Use:** For an everyday ring, the channel inlay is better, protected by metal walls; For a pendant or earrings, open mosaic is a good option.

If you want to commission your own design, the guide on [bespoke jewelry](/es/joyas-a-medida/) explains how to work with a workshop step by step.

## What to check when buying

1. **That the pieces fit together without gaps** or visible glue fillings.
2. **That the surface is level**, without sunken or protruding pieces.
3. **That the metal is well welded** and without adhesive residue.
4. **What materials are**: ask that the invoice indicate whether they are carved natural stones, stabilized, reconstituted or imitation stone.
5. **How ​​has the turquoise or chrysocolla been stabilized, if applicable.
6. **If the part is old**, it must not have loose or replaced parts.

## Care

Inlays combine different materials held together by an adhesive, so they are more delicate than a metal piece of jewelry with a single stone.

- **Avoid prolonged water**: showers, swimming pools, the sea and washing dishes can weaken the adhesive over time.
- **Do not use ultrasound or steam.**
- **Protect them from heat**: intense sun from the car or a heat source can loosen the parts.
- **Avoid perfumes, creams and chemical products**, especially in turquoise, coral and mother-of-pearl.
- **Clean only with a soft, barely damp cloth** and dry immediately.
- **Store them separately**, in a padded case.
- **If a piece comes loose, save it** and take it to a workshop: a good craftsman can reattach it or replace it with the same one.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does the resin make the piece fake?

No. Almost all modern inlays use resin as an adhesive. What must be declared is whether the pieces are made of carved stone or whether the filling is stone powder with resin or an imitation.

### Can an inlay be repaired?

Yes, in most cases. A craftsman can replace a damaged part, re-level the surface and polish it.

### Do embedded stones lose color?

Porous stones, like turquoise, can change shade with skin oils and cosmetic products. Hard stones, such as lapis lazuli or onyx, maintain their color if they are cared for.

## In summary

Inlay fits pieces of stone, shell, or other materials into a support to form a continuous surface. Its great techniques are mosaic, channel inlay, intarsia and hard stone, and it has a rich tradition in America, from Mesoamerican turquoise mosaics to current silverware. To buy wisely, you must check that the pieces fit together without gaps, that the surface is level and that the invoice indicates whether they are carved stones, reconstituted stone or imitations. For them to last, it is advisable to protect them from prolonged water, heat, chemicals and ultrasound, and take them to a workshop when the first loose part occurs.
