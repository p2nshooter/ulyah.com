Some gems are valued not just for their color or transparency, but for something that seems to move within them: a star that slides over the surface, a band of light that opens and closes like a cat's eye, a bluish glow that floats under the stone's skin, or a mosaic of colors that changes with each turn. They are **optical phenomena**, effects produced by the internal structure of the mineral, by tiny inclusions or by microscopic layers that interfere with light.

In this guide you will see what phenomena exist, what causes them, in which gems they appear, how a stone should be cut to show them and how to distinguish natural phenomena from imitations.

## Why do they appear?

Almost all optical phenomena have one of three causes:

1. **Oriented inclusions:** very fine needles, fibers or tubes aligned in one or more directions that reflect light.
2. **Microscopic layers or structures** that scatter or diffract light, such as feldspar sheets in moonstone or silica spheres in opal.
3. **Reflective plates** of other minerals that sparkle within the stone.

Many of these effects only look good if the stone is cut in **cabochon**, the rounded shape without facets that we explain in the [types of gem cut](/es/tipos-de-talla-de-gemas/) guide.

## Asterism: the star gems

### What is it

The asterism is the appearance of a **star** with four, six or, rarely, twelve arms on the surface of a cabochon. It occurs when the stone contains very fine needles oriented in various directions: each group of needles reflects a line of light, and the lines intersect forming the star.

### Where does it appear

- **Star Sapphires and Rubies:** Rutile needles align in three directions and give six-armed stars.
- **Star garnets:** They usually show four-armed stars.
- **Star rose quartz**, **star diopside** and others, less common.

### How it is valued

A good star has **straight, sharp and full** arms, centered on the cabochon, and moves smoothly as the light moves. The color of the background stone is still important: a good blue star sapphire is worth much more than a gray one. We explain it in detail in the guide on [rubies and sapphires](/es/rubies-y-zafiros-el-corindon/).

## Chatoyancy: the cat's eye

### What is it

Chatoyancy is a **band of light** that slides over a cabochon when the stone or light source is moved. It is due to parallel fibers, needles or tubes in **one direction**.

### Where does it appear

- **Chrysoberyl cat's eye:** the cat's eye par excellence. When in the trade they say only "cat's eye", it usually refers to this gem.
- **Tourmaline, aquamarine, scapolite, apatite** and others can show it.
- **Tiger's eye:** Quartz with fibers that give it a golden band and a silky appearance. Widely used in beads and cabochons.

A thin, straight, sharp and centered band is the most valued. When bringing two light sources close together, the eye of a good chrysoberyl appears to "open" in two bands, like a cat's pupil.

## Adularescence: the moonstone

**Moonstone** is a feldspar formed by alternating and very thin layers of two different minerals. These layers scatter light and produce a white or bluish glow that appears to float beneath the surface, called adularescence.

- The **intense blue** glow on a transparent background is the most appreciated.
- It is cut in a high cabochon so that the glow is centered.
- It is a **relatively soft** gem (around 6) and with exfoliation: it needs a protective setting and it is advisable to avoid knocks.

## Color play: opal

### What is it

**Color play** is the flashes of spectral colors that change as a precious opal is rotated. It is due to millions of **microscopic silica spheres** arranged like balls in a box. When the size of the spheres is appropriate, they diffract light and break down white into colors.

### In Latin America

Mexican fire opal, with its orange, red or yellow body, may or may not show color play; When you have it, its value goes up. We tell it in the guide about [Mexican fire opal](/es/opalo-de-fuego-mexicano/). Other countries in the region, such as Brazil, also produce opal.

### What to check

- The **intensity**, the **variety of colors** and the **drawing** of the sparkles.
- If the piece is a **doublet or triplet**, that is, a thin sheet of opal glued to a dark base and sometimes covered with quartz or glass. It should be sold as such. The guide to [imitations, doublets and triplets](/es/imitaciones-y-dobletes/) teaches how to recognize them.
- Opal contains water and can **crack** with sudden changes in temperature or humidity.

## Labradorescence

**labradorite**, another feldspar, shows blue, green, gold or orange metallic flashes that appear and disappear when you tilt the stone. It is due to internal layers that interfere with light. The widest variety of colors on a dark background is known in the trade as **spectrolite**, a name associated with deposits in Finland.

Labradorite is used in cabochons, beads and decorative carvings, and its effect is only seen from a specific angle, so the carver must orient the surface well.

## Adventurescence

**Adventurescence** is a glitter sheen produced by reflective plates within the stone.

- Green aventurine quartzite contains plates of a mineral from the mica group.
- The **stone of the sun** is a feldspar with plates of hematite or, in some deposits, copper, which give it golden or reddish sparkles.
- The **"gold stone"** or aventurine glass is an imitation of glass with copper crystals, very common in jewelry.

## Iridescence and the orient

- In **pearls**, the **orient** is the iridescent shine that arises from the superimposition of layers of nacre. It is one of the most important quality factors, as we explain in the guide on [natural and cultured pearls](/es/perlas-naturales-y-cultivadas/).
- **Fire agate**, present in northern Mexico and the southwestern United States, shows iridescent flashes in thin layers of iron oxide within the chalcedony. It is carved following those layers very carefully.

## Dispersion or "fire"

It is not a phenomenon of inclusions, but of the gem itself: the ability to **decompose white light into colors**, like a prism. It is seen in very dispersive faceted gems, such as diamond, demantoid, zircon or sphene. The size greatly influences how much fire is seen, as we explain in the guide on [brightness, windows and extinction](/es/brillo-ventanas-y-extincion/).

## How gems are cut with phenomena

1. **Precise orientation:** The needles or capes must be in the correct position relative to the base of the cabochon. An error of a few degrees decenters the star or twists the band.
2. **Suitable dome:** Tall cabochons concentrate the star or eye better; the bass disperses the effect.
3. **Impeccable polishing:** any mark on the surface cuts off the light.
4. **Irregular base sometimes:** Many carvers leave the base unpolished or somewhat thick to conserve weight.

## Frequent imitations

| phenomenon | Imitation | How to suspect |
|---|---|---|
| Asterism | synthetic star sapphires; stars etched or scratched on the surface | too perfect stars on a uniform color; arms that look painted |
| cat's eye | fiber optic glass | very bright band, honeycomb pattern visible with a magnifying glass on the sides |
| color game | synthetic opal, plastic, doublets and triplets | "lizard skin" or column pattern, glue layer on the side |
| Adventurescence | "gold stone" glass | very regular triangular or star-shaped crystals |
| Adularescence | opalescent glass | uniform glow that does not move with light |

A gemologist almost always distinguishes these imitations with a magnifying glass, microscope and a good light source.

## How to observe a phenomenon when buying

- Use **a single point light source**, like your phone's flashlight: stars and cat eyes look better this way than under several diffuse lights.
- **Move the stone slowly** to see if the effect moves smoothly and stays sharp.
- **Look sideways** to check if it is a single layer piece or a doublet.
- Ask about the **species**, if it is **natural or synthetic** and if there are **treatments**.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a star or cat's eye increase value?

Yes, when they are sharp and well centered. But the background color still rules: a weak effect on a stone of poor color does not make it a valuable gem.

### Can I facet a star stone?

You can, but you lose the star. Sometimes, if the stone is very transparent and has little silk, the cutter decides to facet it because it is worth more that way.

### Are the phenomena seen in photos?

Bad. Photos and videos rarely capture adularescence or play of color well. Whenever possible, see the stone in person or ask for a video of a single moving light.

## In summary

Optical phenomena arise from oriented inclusions, microscopic layers or reflective plates that play with light. The asterism gives stars in sapphires, rubies and garnets; chatoyancy, cat's eye in chrysoberyl and other gems; the adularescence, the glow of the moonstone; the play of color, the sparkles of opal; labradorescence and adventurescence, the brilliance of labradorite and the stone of the sun. To shine, almost everyone requires a well-oriented and polished cabochon. To buy well, you must observe them with only one light, move the stone slowly, look from the side to rule out doublets and always ask if it is natural or synthetic.
