Faced with raw glass, the carver has to make decisions that cannot be turned back. Every millimeter that the wheel removes is lost forever, and poor orientation can turn a precious colored crystal into a dull stone, or leave an inclusion right under the table, where it will be seen for a lifetime. That's why good carvers often spend more time looking at the stone than cutting it.

Understanding how a carving is planned helps to evaluate the work of the lapidary, to understand why a carved stone weighs much less than the rough from which it came, and to talk to a carver if a piece is commissioned from him. In this guide you will see the dilemmas that a cutter faces, how to read a rough stone, how color, pleochroism and inclusions influence orientation, what performance is, what the stages of cutting are and what to keep in mind when re-carving a stone or having your own rough cut.

## The carver's dilemma

| Objective | What does it collide with? |
|---|---|
| Maintain maximum weight | a deep cut conserves weight, but can darken the stone |
| Achieve maximum beauty | ideal proportions usually require losing more material |
| Hide inclusions | moving them off the table may force you to reduce the size |
| Show the best color | color orientation does not always coincide with the one with the greatest weight |
| Adjust to standard measurements | calibrating the stone for standard frames means losing material |

There is no perfect solution: each stone is a compromise between weight, beauty and value.

## Read the rough stone

1. **Clean it:** remove remains of rock and dirt.
2. **Wet or submerge it:** Water or a suitable liquid reduces surface reflections and reveals the interior.
3. **Illuminate it from various angles:** with intense light you can see fissures, inclusions and areas of color.
4. **Polish a window,** if necessary: ​​a small polished facet allows you to look inside clearly.
5. **Mark:** Many carvers draw the clean areas, fissures and intended cutting lines on the stone with a fine marker.

## The map of inclusions and fissures

- **Fissures that pass through the stone:** they often force it to be divided into two or more pieces.
- **Dark inclusions:** an attempt is made to eliminate them or move them towards the girdle, where the setting can hide them.
- **Light or small inclusions:** These are sometimes accepted if they do not affect the front appearance.
- **Cracks near the surface:** can open during carving if not taken into account.

## Color: zones and pleochroism

Many gemstones do not have their color distributed evenly, and some show different colors depending on the direction in which they are viewed, a phenomenon called pleochroism.

| Gem | What happens | How to orient yourself |
|---|---|---|
| ruby | depending on the direction, the red can be more purple or more orange | look for the direction that gives the most intense red from the front |
| Sapphire | It usually has bands of color and paler areas | The colored areas are placed near the butt so that they dye the entire stone |
| Tourmaline | along the glass the color can look much darker | You often avoid looking straight ahead in that direction so that it doesn't look almost black. |
| Tanzanite | shows blue, violet and brownish tones depending on the direction | is oriented to show off the most appreciated violet blue |
| Aquamarine | may look bluer in one direction than another | the most intense blue direction is used |

An instrument called a dichroscope helps see these colors separately. With good guidance, the cutter can make a stone of mediocre color in the rough look an excellent color once cut.

## Guide optical phenomena

In star or cat's eye cabochons, the orientation is decisive: the base of the cabochon must be parallel to the fine internal needles that produce the effect, and the dome must center the star or the luminous line. An orientation error moves the star to one side or makes it blurry. We see it in the guide on [types of gem cutting](/es/tipos-de-talla-de-gemas/).

## Performance: how much weight is preserved

The yield is the proportion of the weight of the rough that the cut stone retains. It is almost always much less than half, and in stones with many inclusions or irregular shapes it can be much less. It depends on:

- **The shape of the crystal:** an elongated crystal is best used with elongated cuts.
- **Purity:** each inclusion removed costs weight.
- **Color:** Guiding the color may force you to sacrifice material.
- **The size chosen:** Round brilliant sizes usually retain less weight than some elongated or mixed sizes.
- **Proportions:** A deep pavilion retains weight, but can produce dark areas.

Therefore, when buying raw, it is advisable to estimate the performance before paying, as we explain in the guide on [buy raw gems without deception](/es/comprar-gemas-en-bruto-sin-enganos/).

## From plan to machine: the stages

1. **Cutting:** If the stone must be split or defective areas removed, it is cut with a cooled diamond saw.
2. **Roughing or preshaping:** the stone is given its approximate shape on a thick abrasive wheel.
3. **Rod Mounting:** The stone is attached with wax or adhesive to a small metal rod for precision handling.
4. **Pavilion faceting:** With a faceting machine, which controls the angles and position of each facet, the lower facets are cut.
5. **Transfer:** the stone is changed to another rod to work the other side.
6. **Crown Faceting:** Table and top facets are cut.
7. **Polishing:** With increasingly finer discs and abrasives, each facet is polished to a shine.
8. **Cleaning and control:** The stone is removed from the rod, cleaned and the symmetry and polishing are checked.

## Diamonds: digital planning

In diamonds, whose value per carat is very high, planning today relies on scanners that create a three-dimensional model of the stone and its inclusions. The programs calculate which combination of sizes offers the best possible result, and the carver decides with that information. Still, human experience remains key in final decisions.

## Examples of decisions

- **A crystal with a crack in the center:** it is cut along the crack and two smaller stones are obtained, instead of one large one that would break.
- **A long tourmaline crystal:** It is cut into an elongated rectangular shape that takes advantage of its length.
- **A flat crystal:** a faceted cut would have a huge window; A cabochon or shallow fancy carving may be better.
- **A sapphire with the color concentrated in one corner:** it is oriented so that that area is close to the stock and dyes the entire stone.

## Recarving a stone

Recutting can greatly improve a gem with poor proportions, chips or poor polish, but it should be weighed:

- **You always lose weight.**
- **Some gems are sensitive to the heat** generated by polishing, or have exfoliation.
- **Ancient carvings** may have historical value that would be lost.
- **A good shaper** will first explain how much weight he expects to lose and what improvement he expects.

## Order the carving of your own rough

- **Ask the carver to study the stone** before committing.
- **Agree in writing** the shape, size and expected weight range.
- **Ask how he charges:** per piece, per carat or per hour of work.
- **Accept this is an estimate:** a hidden inclusion can change plans mid-job.
- **Ask to save the remains** if you are interested, for example for small pieces.

## Frequent errors

- **Judge a dry brute** and with bad light.
- **Expect the cut stone to weigh** almost the same as the rough one.
- **Ignore pleochroism** and misdirect the color.
- **Re-carving an ancient stone** without valuing what is lost.
- **Do not leave in writing** what was agreed with the carver.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much weight is lost when cutting a gem?

It depends on the stone, but almost always more than half the weight of the rough is lost, and sometimes much more.

### What is pleochroism?

The property of some gems to show different colors depending on the direction in which they are looked. Properly used, it greatly improves the color of the carved stone.

### Can any rough stone be carved?

In theory yes, but it is not always worth it: if the stone has many cracks or a poor color, the result may not justify the work.

### Why do some stones split in two?

Because a major fissure or inclusion in the center would make a single large stone weak or cloudy; two clean stones may be worth more.

## In summary

Cutting a gem is a compromise between weight, beauty and value. The cutter reads the wet, well-lit stone, marks its inclusions and fissures, and decides whether to split it. Guide the color taking into account the color zones and pleochroism, and in star or cat's eye cabochons, align the base with the internal needles. The yield is usually less than half the weight of the raw material. Carving progresses in stages, from cutting and roughing to faceting and polishing. If you carve a stone or order a rough carving, ask for a prior study and leave in writing the shape, size and expected weight.
