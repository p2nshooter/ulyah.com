Few gems have such a well-placed name. The Mexican fire opal shines with yellow, orange and red tones that are reminiscent of a flame, and some specimens also ignite green, blue or violet flashes inside when they move under the light. It is extracted from volcanic rocks in the states of Querétaro, Jalisco and Nayarit, and in towns in the region it has been carved and sold for generations.

Opal is, however, a delicate gem and very different from others: it contains water in its structure, has no crystalline form and can crack if it dries too quickly. Knowing its particularities is essential to choose it and take good care of it. In this guide you will see what opal is, what varieties Mexico produces, how it is formed, why some stones crack, how it is valued, how it is cut and what care it needs.

## What is opal

Opal is hydrated silica: the same component as quartz, but without an ordered crystalline structure and with a variable proportion of water. That is why mineralogists classify it as a mineraloid. It is softer than many gems, with a hardness of around 5.5 to 6.5 on the Mohs scale.

Two large groups are distinguished:

- **Precious opal:** shows color play, that is, flashes of spectral colors that change when the stone is moved.
- **Common opal:** it has no play of colors, although it can have very attractive background colors.

The play of colors is due to a microscopic structure: tiny silica spheres arranged in layers that diffract light and break it down into colors, like a network. The size of the spheres determines which colors appear.

## Mexican opal and its varieties

| Variety | How to recognize |
|---|---|
| fire opal | transparent or translucent, yellow, orange or red, with or without play of colors |
| Fire opal with play of colors | combines the warm background color with colorful flashes |
| water opal | transparent and almost colorless, with a play of colors that seems to float within the stone |
| backlit opal | shows its play of colors especially when the light passes through it from behind |
| Quarry or matrix opal | the opal is left in its original volcanic rock, which is part of the piece |

The word "fire" in this case refers to the background color, not the play of colors. A fire opal without sparkles is still a fire opal, and can be very beautiful if its color is intense and its transparency high.

## Where is it located

- **Querétaro:** the region around San Juan del Río and Tequisquiapan is the most associated with Mexican opal, exploited since the 19th century.
- **Jalisco:** in areas such as Magdalena and nearby municipalities there are opal mines and workshops.
- **Nayarit and other states** of the volcanic axis also have deposits.

Many mines are small open pit operations, sometimes family-owned, that work volcanic rock with simple tools and machinery.

## How it is formed in volcanic rock

Mexican opal formed in rhyolites, volcanic rocks rich in silica, which cooled, leaving cavities and fractures. Over time, water loaded with dissolved silica circulated through these openings and deposited silica gel that slowly hardened. Iron impurities gave the characteristic yellow, orange and red colors. We explain it with more context in the guide on [how gems are formed](/es/como-se-forman-las-gemas/).

## From rock to gem

1. **Extraction:** The rhyolite is broken in search of nodules and opal veins.
2. **Selection:** Miners separate fragments with quality opal, often wetting them to better see their color and sparkle.
3. **Rest:** Careful tradesmen let the material rest for a while before carving, to see if it cracks as it loses moisture.
4. **Size:** It is cut, shaped and polished, in facets, cabochon or preserving the rock.

## Cracking: the great risk

Cracking is the appearance of a network of fine cracks in opal, usually because it loses water too quickly or suffers sudden changes in temperature. Some stones crack shortly after leaving the mine; others never do.

- **Be wary of opal sold submerged in water** without explanation: water can hide cracks or temporarily improve its appearance.
- **Ask how long it has been cut:** A stone that has been unchanged for months offers more guarantees.
- **Look at the surface with a magnifying glass** before purchasing.

## Patterns of color play

When an opal has color play, it is not only important how many colors it shows, but also how they are distributed. Several patterns are described in trading:

- **Specific:** small flashes like points of light spread across the stone.
- **Flash:** Large areas of color that turn on and off when you turn the stone.
- **Harlequin:** spots of color in the form of a mosaic or board, very rare and appreciated.
- **Flame or ribbon:** elongated stripes of color that run through the stone.

Always look at the opal from different angles and in different lighting, such as indirect natural light, a spot lamp, and light passing through it from behind. An opal may appear dull in one light and come to life in another, and it's a good idea to know this before purchasing.

## How it is valued

| factor | What to watch |
|---|---|
| background color | intense oranges and reds are the most sought after; pale yellows, less |
| Transparency | A clean and luminous stone is worth more than a cloudy one. |
| Color game | Its presence, intensity, variety of colors and extension greatly increase the value |
| Purity | no cracks, sand or dull areas |
| Size | proportions that show color and sparkles |
| Stability | no signs of cracking |

## Faceted, cabochon and quarry

Unlike most of the world's opals, which are cut cabochon, clear fire opal can be faceted like any other gemstone, enhancing its color. Stones with play of colors are usually cut in cabochon to better show off the sparkles. Quarry pieces preserve the brown or reddish volcanic rock around the opal and are often free-shaped or sculpted, taking advantage of the contrast. We see it in the guide on [types of gem cutting](/es/tipos-de-talla-de-gemas/).

## Treatments, synthetics and imitations

- **Impregnation:** Some opals are impregnated with resins, oils or waxes to improve their appearance or stabilize them.
- **Smoked and Dyed:** Certain treatments darken the background of some opals to highlight the play of colors.
- **Synthetic opal:** Laboratory-created opal has existed for decades, with very regular color patterns.
- **Imitations:** glass, plastic and resins can imitate opal.
- **Doublets and triplets:** thin opal sheets glued on a dark base and, in the triplet, covered with a transparent layer.

We explain it in the guides on [gem treatments](/es/tratamientos-de-gemas/) and [imitations and doublets](/es/imitaciones-y-dobletes/).

## Buy Mexican opal

- **Look at the dry stone,** out of the water, and with different lights.
- **Ask about stability** and time since size.
- **Ask to be informed of any treatment.**
- **For important pieces,** request a laboratory report, as explained in the guide on [gemology laboratory reports](/es/informes-de-laboratorio-gemologico/).
- **Purchase from workshops and merchants with experience,** with invoice.

## Opal Care

- **Avoid heat and sudden changes in temperature:** do not leave it in the sun, near a heat source or inside a hot car.
- **No ultrasound or steam.**
- **Clean with a soft cloth** and, if necessary, warm water; dry it carefully.
- **Protect from shocks:** On rings, a bezel setting offers more protection.
- **Keep separate** from harder gems, which can scratch it.
- **Take it off** when playing sports, doing housework or using chemicals.

More ideas in the guide on [hardness, toughness and stability](/es/dureza-tenacidad-y-estabilidad/).

## Visit the region

In Querétaro, towns like Tequisquiapan and San Juan del Río have opal workshops and shops, and some nearby mines offer tours where you can see the volcanic rock and sometimes search for small fragments. Find out about the conditions of the visit, wear appropriate footwear and sun protection, and compare before buying.

## Frequent errors

- **Buy opal immersed in water** without seeing it dry.
- **Expose it to heat** or sudden changes in temperature.
- **Mount on rings with exposed settings** for heavy daily wear.
- **Confusing a triplet with solid opal.**
- **Ignore impregnation treatments**.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do all fire opals have color play?

No. The name refers to its warm background color. Some also have a play of colors, which makes them more rare and valuable.

### Is this a good stone for an everyday ring?

It is possible, but it requires care: it is softer and more sensitive than other gems. A protective bezel and removing it for demanding tasks extends its life.

### Why does opal crack?

Because it contains water in its structure and, if it loses it too quickly or suffers sudden changes in temperature, fine cracks may appear. Not all opals are equally sensitive.

### What is quarry opal?

A piece in which the opal is preserved within its original volcanic rock, which is part of the design.

## In summary

Mexican fire opal, from Querétaro, Jalisco and Nayarit, is hydrated silica formed in volcanic rocks, with yellow, orange and red background colors and sometimes play of colors. Mexico also produces water, backlight and quarry opal. Its value depends on color, transparency, play of colors, purity, cut and stability, because some stones crack when they lose water. Buy it dry and well observed, ask about treatments and stability, and protect it from heat, shock, chemicals and ultrasound.
