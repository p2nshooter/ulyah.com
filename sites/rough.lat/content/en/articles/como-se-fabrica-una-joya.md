Behind a simple ring there are more steps than we imagine. Metal is cast, shaped, filed, soldered, set and polished, and each stage leaves traces that a trained eye can read. Understanding how a piece of jewelry is made helps to better value the craftsmanship, to talk to a jeweler when ordering a piece, and to recognize, with a magnifying glass and some practice, whether a piece of jewelry is well made or if it hides shortcuts that will shorten its life.

In this guide you will see the main manufacturing techniques, from lost wax casting to hand forging, how the stones are set and finished, what the differences are between a handmade piece and a mass-produced one, and what signs indicate a good quality jewel.

## First stage: the design

Every jewel begins with an idea. Today there are two ways to put it on paper:

- **The hand sketch**, with measurements, views and often watercolor or gouache to show the color of the gems. Many jewelers still think with a pencil.
- **Computer-aided design**, with programs that allow you to model the piece in three dimensions, adjust the exact size of each stone, calculate the weight of the metal and generate realistic images before manufacturing.

In this phase, aspects are decided that are difficult to change later: the thickness of the walls, the height of the stone, the type of setting and the size of the ring. If you are ordering a piece, the guide on [bespoke jewelry](/es/joyas-a-medida/) explains how to work this stage with the jeweler.

## Second stage: the model

Before metalworking, many pieces are first made in a material that is easier to model.

### hand carved wax

The jeweler carves a block of special wax with files, burins and hot tools. It is a slow and very expressive technique, ideal for organic shapes, reliefs and unique pieces.

### 3D modeling and printing

The digital design is printed in **resins or special waxes** with high precision printers. It allows very fine details, repeating a part exactly and adjusting the model before casting. Today it is the dominant technique in many workshops.

## Third stage: lost wax casting

It is one of the oldest techniques in goldsmithing. The pre-Columbian cultures of the region already dominated it with astonishing mastery, as you can see in the guide on [pre-Columbian goldsmithing](/es/orfebreria-precolombina/). Its principle remains the same:

1. **The wax tree:** the wax or resin models are attached to a central trunk, forming something similar to a small tree.
2. **The coating:** the tree is placed in a cylinder and covered with a refractory plaster-like paste.
3. **The burning:** the cylinder enters a furnace; The wax melts and evaporates, leaving a hollow mold with the exact shape of the pieces.
4. **The pour:** The molten metal is poured into the mold, often with the help of vacuum or centrifugal force so that it fills every detail.
5. **Demolding:** when it cools, the coating breaks and the raw pieces appear, still attached to the tree.

### Defects to avoid

- **Porosity:** small holes in the metal, which weaken the piece and appear when polishing.
- **Incomplete fills:** details that were not filled with metal.
- **Walls too thin:** cheap in metal, but fragile with use.

A good shop monitors temperature, metal, and coating to minimize these defects, and checks each part before moving forward.

## Fourth stage: forging and bench work

Many jewels, or parts of them, are made without casting, working the metal directly on the **jeweler's bench**:

- **Laminated:** the metal is passed between rollers to obtain sheets of the desired thickness.
- **Drawing:** Stretching through increasingly smaller holes to make thread.
- **Forging:** It is shaped with hammers and tools. Wrought metal is denser and stronger than cast metal.
- **Openwork:** the sheet metal is cut with a fine saw to create drawings.
- **Welding:** the pieces are joined with specific alloy welds, using a torch. A good weld is barely noticeable and has the same color as the metal.
- **Filing and sanding:** marks are removed, shapes are adjusted and surfaces are prepared.

Traditional techniques such as **filigree**, **embossing** or **granulation** belong to this world of manual work. Several of them are still alive in workshops in the region, as the guide on [Latin American silversmithing and filigree] (/es/plateria-y-filigrana-latinoamericana/) tells us.

## Other manufacturing techniques

- **Stamping or die-cutting:** a press cuts and shapes the sheet metal with dies. Fast and accurate for mass production.
- **Electroforming:** metal is deposited on a mold using electricity. It allows large and very light pieces, but hollow and more delicate.
- **Chain Manufacturing:** Specialized machines make continuous chains that are then cut and finished by hand.
- **Direct Metal Additive Manufacturing:** Some pieces are printed directly in metal, although in jewelry it is still less common than casting.

## Fifth stage: crimping

With the metal piece finished, it is time to place the gems. The **setter** prepares the seats, fits each stone and bends or pushes the metal to hold it:

- **claws** that embrace the stone;
- **bevels** that completely surround it;
- **rails** that hold it between two walls;
- **grains** raised with a graver in the pavé.

It is precision work: a poorly seated stone can come loose, and excessive pressure can break it. The guide on [types of crimping](/es/tipos-de-engaste/) explains the advantages of each one.

## Sixth stage: finishing

- **Polishing:** with increasingly finer discs, brushes and polishing pastes, until obtaining the final shine.
- **Satin or matte:** with brushes, sand or special wheels.
- **Textures:** hammered, engraved, diamond-coated.
- **Oxidized:** in silver, the reliefs are darkened to give contrast.
- **Platings:** rhodium over white gold for a brilliant white; gold plating on silver or other metals.

Next comes the **final check**: checking welds, settings, clasps, size and weight, cleaning the piece and, when appropriate, marking the grade of the metal with a punch or engraving.

## Handmade, serial or mixed

| Type | How to do it | Advantages | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| handmade | forging and bench work | unique pieces, dense metal, character | more expensive and slower |
| Hand casting and finishing | model, lost wax, bench, setting | balance between precision and craftsmanship | It depends on the quality of the workshop |
| serial production | stamping, mass casting, machines | low price, regularity | less personality, sometimes thin walls |

Most of today's jewelry combines techniques. The important thing is not so much the method as the care with which it is applied.

## How to recognize a well-made piece of jewelry

With a 10x magnifying glass you can check:

1. **The welds:** that no lines of another color or pores are visible.
2. **The claws and bezels:** that all the claws have the same shape and height, and that the stone does not move.
3. **The inside of the ring:** that is polished, without file marks or burrs.
4. **The thickness of the metal:** that the ring is not so thin that it deforms when pressed gently.
5. **Clasps and hinges:** that fit firmly.
6. **Symmetry:** that the parts that should be equal are so.
7. **The marks:** the grade of the metal and, if applicable, the manufacturer's mark.

## What to ask the jeweler

- How will the part be manufactured: casting, forging or a combination?
- How thick will the ring or walls be?
- What type of setting will you use and why?
- Will it have a bathroom? How often should it be renewed?
- Can it be sized or repaired in the future?
- What warranty do you offer on manufacturing and setting?

The responses say a lot about the seriousness of the workshop. For the metal and its laws, see the guide on [gold, silver and platinum](/es/oro-plata-y-platino/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a cast jewel of worse quality than a forged one?

Not necessarily. A good foundry, well finished, can last for generations. Forging gives a denser metal, but the final quality depends above all on the workshop.

### Why does my ring have a small hole inside?

It may be cast iron porosity. If it is small and does not affect resistance, it is not serious; If several appear or grow, it is advisable to consult the jeweler.

### What is better, hand or 3D design?

None is better by itself. The 3D design provides precision and allows you to see the part before manufacturing it; Hand modeling offers a different expressiveness. Many jewelers combine the two.

## In summary

A piece of jewelry goes through several stages: design, by hand or on a computer; the model, in wax or resin; lost wax casting or bench forging; the setting of the stones; and the finish, with polishing, textures and bathrooms. Each technique has its advantages, and most pieces combine several. What distinguishes a well-made piece of jewelry is the care taken in each step: invisible welds, equal claws, a polished interior, walls with sufficient thickness and firm closures. Asking the jeweler how the piece will be made and reviewing it with a magnifying glass are the best ways to buy wisely.
