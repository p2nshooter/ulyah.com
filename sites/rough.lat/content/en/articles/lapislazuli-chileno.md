The deep blue of lapis lazuli has fascinated humanity for millennia. The ancient Egyptians embedded it in jewelry and funerary masks, Renaissance painters ground it to obtain the ultramarine pigment, more expensive than gold, and in the Andes it has been worked into ornaments for centuries. Today, when someone walks through the shops in the Bellavista neighborhood in Santiago, they find it turned into rings, pendants, carved figures and decorative objects, and it is easy to forget that it is one of the rarest ornamental stones on the planet.

Chile is one of the few countries in the world with an important deposit of lapis lazuli, located high in the Andes mountain range. In this guide you will see what exactly this stone is, how it is formed, where it is extracted in Chile, how it differs from lapis lazuli from Afghanistan, how it is valued, what treatments and imitations it is worth knowing about and how to care for it.

## What is lapis lazuli

Lapis lazuli is not a mineral, but a rock made up of several minerals. Its color and appearance depend on the proportion of each one:

| Mineral | What does it contribute? |
|---|---|
| Lazurite | the characteristic deep blue; It is the main component in quality parts |
| Calcite | white or grayish streaks and spots |
| Pyrite | golden dots and veins with metallic shine |
| Other minerals | such as diopside or sodalite, which modify the tone and texture |

Its name combines the Latin *lapis*, stone, with a word of Persian origin that designated the color blue. It is relatively soft, with a hardness of around 5 to 5.5, and somewhat porous, which influences its care.

## How it is formed

Lapis lazuli is formed by contact metamorphism: when limestone or marble comes into contact with magma intrusions and fluids rich in sodium, sulfur and other elements, the rock is transformed and minerals such as lazurite appear. It is a rare combination of conditions, and that is why important deposits are very rare in the world. We explain it in more detail in the guide on [how gems are formed](/es/como-se-forman-las-gemas/).

## A blue with history

- **In the ancient world,** lapis lazuli, coming mainly from the mountains of Badakhshan, in modern-day Afghanistan, reached Mesopotamia and Egypt, where it was used in jewelry, seals, and sacred objects.
- **In Europe,** ground and purified, it gave the ultramarine blue pigment, reserved in many paintings for the most important cloaks due to its very high cost.
- **In the Andes,** there is evidence of its use in ornaments of pre-Hispanic peoples, and the tradition of working with it continues in Chile.

Chile considers lapis lazuli its national stone, and it is one of the most characteristic souvenirs of the country.

## The Chilean site

The main deposit in Chile is located in the Andes mountain range, in the Coquimbo region, in the interior of the province of Limarí, at high altitude. The best-known mine, called Flor de los Andes, is located in high mountains, which conditions the work: the climate, the snow and the difficult access set the pace of the extraction.

The material is extracted in blocks that are then transported to workshops, where they are selected, cut and worked. An important part goes to crafts and decorative objects, and the best pieces go to jewelry.

## Chile and Afghanistan: two styles of blue

The two big names in lapis lazuli on the market are Afghanistan and Chile, although there are also deposits in other countries, such as Russia, Pakistan and the United States.

| Aspect | High quality Afghan lapis lazuli | Typical Chilean lapis lazuli |
|---|---|---|
| Color | deep blue, often with a purplish tinge | blue often somewhat lighter or grayish |
| Calcite | little or none in the best pieces | often white or gray streaks and spots |
| Pyrite | fine and distributed golden dots | present, sometimes abundant |
| Use | high-end jewelry, historical pigments | jewelry, crafts and carved objects |

These are generalizations: in both countries there is material of very varied qualities, and there are Chilean pieces of excellent blue. The important thing is to value each piece for itself.

## How it is valued

| Factor | What to watch |
|---|---|
| Color | intense, saturated and uniform blue; deep violet tones are highly appreciated |
| Calcite | the less white or gray, the more value, in general |
| Pyrite | some fine, well-distributed golden dots can add attractiveness; large veins remain |
| Texture | fine and compact grain, without visible pores |
| Polished | uniform shine, without matte areas |
| Treatment | an undyed piece is worth more than a dyed one |

To understand how a colored gem is judged in general, see the guide on [quality of colored gems](/es/calidad-de-las-gemas-de-color/).

## Treatments and imitations

Lapis lazuli is one of the most imitated and treated stones. It is advisable to know the most common practices:

| material | What is it |
|---|---|
| Dyed Lapis Lazuli | stone of poor color or with a lot of calcite, dyed to intensify the blue |
| Waxed or oiled lapis lazuli | surface treated with wax or oil to improve shine and hide pores; it's usual |
| Reconstituted lapis lazuli | lapis lazuli fragments or powder agglomerated with resin |
| dyed jasper | a blue-dyed chalcedony, sometimes sold under misleading names such as "Swiss lapis" |
| Sodalite | another blue mineral, cheaper, with white veins and almost no pyrite |
| Artificial imitations | glass, ceramics and materials manufactured with the appearance of lapis lazuli |

A gemologist can detect dyeing and distinguish lapis lazuli from its imitations with simple tests. More information in the guides on [gem treatments](/es/tratamientos-de-gemas/) and [imitations and doublets](/es/imitaciones-y-dobletes/).

## From block to jewel

Lapis lazuli is worked in many ways:

- **Cabochons and beads,** showing the color and shine of the polished surface.
- **Inlays,** in which sheets of stone are embedded in silver, gold or other stones.
- **Carvings and sculptures:** figures, boxes, bowls and decorative objects, very common in Chilean crafts.
- **Combination with silver,** very characteristic of Chilean jewelry.

Due to its moderate hardness and texture, it is not faceted like transparent gems. We see it in the guides on [ornamental stone carvings](/es/tallas-en-piedras-ornamentales/) and [Latin American silverwork and filigree](/es/plateria-y-filigrana-latinoamericana/).

## Where to see and buy it in Chile

In Santiago, the Bellavista neighborhood is home to lapis lazuli shops and workshops, and in Pueblito Los Dominicos, a craft center, artisans of different crafts work. In both cases:

- **Compare qualities** with good natural light.
- **Ask if the stone is dyed** or reconstituted.
- **Ask for an invoice** and, for valuable pieces, a written description of the material.
- **Value the craftsmanship:** a well-carved or well-set piece is worth its work, not just the stone.

## Helpful questions before purchasing

A brief conversation with the seller says a lot about the piece and who is selling it:

1. **Is the stone natural, dyed or reconstituted?** An honest dealer answers bluntly.
2. **Does it have wax or any coating?** Waxing is common, but it is good to know.
3. **Where does it come from?** It is not always known with certainty, but the answer and its safety guide.
4. **Who did the silver work or carving?** In craftsmanship, the craft is also part of the value.
5. **What happens if the stone becomes loose or damaged?** Ask about warranty and repairs.

If the answer to the first question is vague, or the price seems too low for the size and color, take that as a sign to compare with other sellers before deciding.

## Lapis Lazuli Care

- **Avoid chemicals:** perfumes, creams, cleaners and acids can damage the surface and calcite.
- **Do not use ultrasound or steam.**
- **Clean it with a soft, slightly damp cloth** if necessary, and dry it well.
- **Protect it from bumps and scratches,** keeping it separate from harder gems.
- **Take it off for swimming,** playing sports or doing housework.

If it loses its shine over the years, a jeweler can repolish or re-wax it. More ideas in the guide on [jewelry maintenance and cleaning](/es/mantenimiento-y-limpieza-de-joyas/).

## Frequent errors

- **Believe that all deep blue lapis lazuli is natural:** dyeing is common.
- **Confuse it with sodalite** or dyed jasper.
- **Clean it with chemicals** or ultrasound.
- **Reject any piece with calcite:** it can be authentic and very beautiful, even if it is worth less.
- **Pay natural stone price** for reconstituted material.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Chilean lapis lazuli of good quality?

There are Chilean pieces of very varied qualities, from craft materials to intense blue stones suitable for fine jewelry. Judge each piece by its color, its calcite content, its polish and whether it is treated.

### Why does it have gold dots?

They are pyrite crystals, an iron sulfide with a metallic shine, which is part of the rock.

### How do I know if it is dyed?

At first glance it is difficult. A gemologist can detect it with simple tests. Be wary of colors that are too uniform and prices that are too low.

### Can this be used on an everyday ring?

Yes, carefully. It is softer than many gems and sensitive to shock and chemicals. A protective bezel and removing it for demanding tasks help preserve it.

## In summary

Lapis lazuli is a rock formed mainly by lazurite, with calcite and pyrite, which is born by contact metamorphism under very unusual conditions. Chile has one of its few large deposits, in the high mountain range of the Coquimbo region, and considers it its national stone. Its value depends on the blue, the little calcite, a fine pyrite, the texture, the polish and the absence of dye. Know the treatments and common imitations, such as dyed, reconstituted, sodalite or dyed jasper, buy with good lighting and invoice, and protect it from chemicals, ultrasounds and shocks.
