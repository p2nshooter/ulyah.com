There are few experiences as surprising in gemology as seeing a green stone next to a window and, minutes later, finding it red under the light of a lamp. It's neither a trick nor a treatment: some gems naturally change color depending on the light that shines on them. The most famous is alexandrite, but it is not the only one. Sapphires, garnets, spinels and other stones can show the same phenomenon, with very different nuances.

In this guide you will see why color change occurs, which gems exhibit it, how their quality is judged, what imitations abound, especially in tourist markets, and how to check it for yourself before buying.

## Why does a gem change color?

The color of a gem depends on which parts of the light it absorbs and which it lets through. Most gems allow only one area of ​​the spectrum to pass through, and that is why they appear a similar color in any light. Color change gems are different: they allow **two almost balanced zones** to pass through, for example one in teal and another in red.

What decides which wins is the light that illuminates them:

- **daylight** and many cold lights are rich in blues and greens, so the gem looks **green or bluish**;
- **incandescent light** from a traditional light bulb, or a candle, is rich in reds and yellows, so the gem appears **red, purple or violet**.

This phenomenon is often known as the **alexandrite effect**, after the gem that made it famous.

### Not to be confused with pleochroism

**Pleochroism** is something else: the color changes depending on the **direction** from which the stone is looked at, with the same light. We saw it in the guide on [tourmalines from Brazil](/es/turmalinas-de-brasil/). The color change, on the other hand, depends on the **light source**, even if it is always looked at from the same angle.

## Alexandrite

### What is it

Alexandrite is a variety of **chrysoberyl** that contains chromium. It has a **hardness of 8.5** and very good toughness, which makes it suitable for jewelry for daily use. Its typical change goes from **green or bluish green** in daylight to **red, purplish red or raspberry** under warm light.

### Your story

It was first described in the Ural Mountains in Russia in the first half of the 19th century, and was named in honor of the future Tsar Alexander II. Its colors matched those of the Imperial Russian army, which contributed to its fame. The Ural deposits were largely depleted, and for decades alexandrite was a rarity.

### Alexandrite from Brazil

At the end of the 1980s, an important deposit in Hematite, in Minas Gerais, was discovered, which returned alexandrite to the market. Brazilian stones are known for a marked color change, often from bluish green to purple. Today it is also mined in Sri Lanka, India and several East African countries. If you are interested in the gemological wealth of Minas Gerais, consult the guide on the [gems of Minas Gerais](/es/gemas-de-minas-gerais-topacio-y-aguamarina/).

### How it is valued

1. **The intensity of the change:** the more distinct and sharp the two colors are, the more valuable the stone is. A weak change, from grayish green to brown, is worth much less than a clear change from green to red.
2. **The quality of each color:** We look for bright greens and saturated reds, without excess gray or brown.
3. **Purity:** clean alexandrites are rare; fine inclusions are acceptable if they do not affect the gloss.
4. **The size:** stones larger than one carat with good exchange are rare and command a premium.
5. **The size:** a good size keeps the color homogeneous and avoids dark areas.

There is also the **cat's eye alexandrite**, which combines color change with a band of light on the cabochon. We explain it in the guide to [optical phenomena in gems](/es/fenomenos-opticos-en-gemas/).

## Synthetics and imitations

Color-changing gems attract imitations, and in some tourist markets they are almost the norm.

- **Color change synthetic corundum:** probably the most widespread imitation. It is made cheaply, changes from grayish blue to purple, and is frequently sold as "alexandrite" at fairs, markets, and in travel. It is real corundum, but it is not alexandrite.
- **Synthetic alexandrite:** exists and is chemically alexandrite. It is manufactured in the laboratory by various methods, has a very marked color change and costs much less than the natural one.
- **Synthetic spinel and color-change glasses** are also circulating.

Some warning signs:

- large, clean stones with a spectacular change at low prices;
- many identical parts in the same position;
- sellers who cannot indicate in writing whether the stone is natural or synthetic.

The guide to [synthetic and laboratory gems](/es/gemas-sinteticas-y-de-laboratorio/) explains how they are made and recognized.

## How to check the change before buying

You don't need sophisticated instruments to observe the phenomenon, although you do need to identify the gem:

1. **Look at it next to a window**, with indirect natural light, mid-morning or mid-afternoon.
2. **Look at it under a warm light bulb** or, if there is not one, under the light of a candle or a yellow-toned lamp.
3. **Compare the two impressions** without moving the stone.
4. **Be wary of window lighting**: many stores use lights that highlight a specific color.
5. **Be careful with LEDs**: depending on their tone, some show an intermediate color and can be confusing. A cold light flashlight and a warm light flashlight give a clearer comparison.

For high-priced stones, ask for a **lab report** indicating the species, whether it is natural or synthetic, and, if applicable, a description of the color change. Our guide to [gemological laboratory reports](/es/informes-de-laboratorio-gemologico/) explains how to read and verify them.

## How to set a color change gem

The montage influences how the change is perceived more than it seems:

- **The color of the metal is reflected in the stone.** Yellow gold warms the tones and can take away the freshness of the green of daylight; white gold, platinum or silver keep the colors more neutral. There is no right option for everyone, but it is a good idea to try the stone next to each metal before deciding.
- **Light must be able to enter.** A very tight setting, with the stone sunk into the metal, reduces the shine and makes the two colors look duller. Thin claws or open-sided bezels let in more light.
- **Size counts.** In very small stones the change is worse; In rings with several stones, it is advisable that they all have a similar change so that the set does not look uneven.

The guide on [types of crimping](/es/tipos-de-engaste/) explains the advantages of each system.

## Care

- **Alexandrite** and **color change sapphire** are very resistant: warm water, soap and a soft brush are enough.
- **Garnet** and **Spinel** also clean easily with soap and water.
- **fluorite** and **diaspore** are delicate: avoid shocks, ultrasounds and sudden changes in temperature.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Alexandrite a birthstone?

In some modern lists, alexandrite appears as a June stone, along with pearl and moonstone. You can see the lists and their variants in the guide on [birthstones](/es/piedras-de-nacimiento/).

### Can I tell if it is natural by looking at the color change?

No. The color change of a synthetic stone can be even more intense than that of a natural one. The difference can only be established by a gemologist or a laboratory.

### Does the color change wear off over time?

No. It is a property of the material, not a coating. As long as the stone is the same, the change will remain.

## In summary

Color change gems allow two almost balanced zones of the spectrum to pass through, and the light that illuminates them decides which color dominates: green or bluish in daylight, red or purple in warm light. Alexandrite, discovered in the Urals and today also mined in Brazil, Sri Lanka, India and Africa, is the most famous, but sapphires, garnets, spinels and other stones show the same phenomenon. Its value depends on the intensity of the change and the quality of each color. As synthetics are abundant, especially corundum sold as "alexandrite", it is advisable to observe the stone in two different lights, ask in writing if it is natural or synthetic, and request a report for valuable pieces.
