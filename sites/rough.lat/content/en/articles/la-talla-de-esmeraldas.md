Of all the precious gems, the emerald is perhaps the one that inspires the most respect in a cutting workshop. Its color can be extraordinary, but it is almost always accompanied by inclusions and cracks that make it fragile. One too many blows, careless heating, or excessive pressure on a crack can break a valuable glass in two. That is why emerald cutters work with a mixture of patience, prudence and experience that is often passed down from generation to generation.

In Colombia, the country most associated with this gem, the craft of cutting emeralds has a long tradition. In this guide you will see why the emerald is so delicate to work with, how the crystal is studied before cutting it, how the color is oriented, why the emerald cut was born and what other shapes are used, what happens at each stage of the workshop, why the emeralds are filled after being cut and how to judge the quality of a cut.

## Why cutting emeralds is so delicate

- **Inclusions and fissures:** Almost all emeralds have them, and many reach the surface.
- **Fragility:** Although it has a hardness of 7.5 to 8, its tenacity is lower than that of other gems due to these cracks.
- **Heat sensitivity:** Heating during cutting or polishing can open cracks.
- **Oils and resins:** Many raw or recut emeralds have fillers that alter with the work.
- **Irregular color:** Green can be concentrated in areas or vary depending on direction.

## Study the crystal

Before cutting, the cutter studies the rough emerald very carefully:

1. **He cleans it,** and if it has oils or resins from a previous treatment, he often removes them to see the actual cracks.
2. **Immerses and illuminates** from various angles to locate fissures, inclusions and areas of color.
3. **Mark the stone** with the areas to avoid and possible cutting lines.
4. **Decide whether to split:** Sometimes separating a crystal through a crack gives two stones that are healthier and more valuable than a single weakened one.

## Color and orientation

The emerald shows a somewhat bluish or more yellowish green depending on the direction in which it is looked. The carver looks for the orientation that gives the most appreciated green without sacrificing too much weight, and ensures that the color is distributed evenly when viewed from the front. If the crystal has areas of more intense color, try to place them so that they color the entire stone. We explain it in detail in the guide on [from raw to gem: guidance and performance](/es/del-bruto-a-la-gema-orientacion-y-rendimiento/).

## The emerald cut

The cut that bears the name of the gem is rectangular or square, with parallel stepped facets and cut corners. It was born largely to respond to the needs of this stone:

- **Protects the corners,** which in a carving with points would be fragile and easy to chip.
- **Reduces stress** during carving and setting.
- **Take advantage of the crystal:** Emerald crystals are usually elongated prisms, and a rectangular shape retains more weight.
- **Show off color** with large flat surfaces, like windows into its green interior.

Stepped facets do not hide inclusions as much as brilliant cuts, but in emerald the market accepts a certain inner garden if the color and transparency are good.

## Other shapes for the emerald

| Shape | When to use |
|---|---|
| Emerald cut | the classic option for stones of good transparency |
| Oval and cushion | in mixed sizes, to give more shine or take advantage of rounded crystals |
| Pear and drop | for pendants and earrings, with tip protection |
| Round | less common; can lose a lot of weight |
| Cabochon | for material with more inclusions or to show off an intense color without facets |
| Sheets | in the trapiche emeralds, to show their drawing of six arms |

## Cabochons, beads and engraved carvings

Material with more inclusions or less transparency is usually cut into cabochons or beads, shapes that show color softly and are less vulnerable to impact. Historically, some Colombian emeralds reached Mughal India, where they were engraved with floral motifs and inscriptions, and today they are museum pieces. The tradition of engraving emeralds continues in some workshops in different parts of the world.

## Emerald cutter's tools

The basic equipment does not differ much from that of other lapidaries, but is used with more caution:

- **Disc saw with diamond edge,** always cooled with water or cutting oil.
- **Coarse and medium grain grinding wheels** to give the initial shape.
- **Faceting machine** with a head that sets the angles and position of each facet.
- **Polishing discs** loaded with very fine abrasives.
- **Magnifying glass, microscope and dichroscope** to monitor cracks and color throughout the process.
- **Intense light sources** to check the stone at each stage.

More than the tools, what distinguishes a good emerald cutter is knowing when to stop: excessive pressure or a few seconds of extra heat can ruin hours of work.

## Step by step in the workshop

1. **Cutting:** the useful area is separated with a well-cooled diamond saw, with little pressure.
2. **Roughing:** the approximate shape is given on a wheel, controlling the heat with water.
3. **Mounting on the rod:** the stone is fixed with wax or adhesive, taking care not to subject it to sudden changes in temperature.
4. **Faceting:** The facets of the pavilion and crown are cut, with gentle pressure and monitoring each crack.
5. **Polishing:** with fine abrasives and moderate speed, without letting the stone get hot.
6. **Removal and cleaning:** Separates from the rod without forcing it and cleans it.

At each stage, the carver observes the stone frequently: if a crack progresses, he must change plans.

## After carving: cleaning and filling

Once cut, most emeralds are filled with oils or resins that penetrate the cracks that reach the surface and make them less visible. It is a traditional and common practice in commerce, but it must be declared, and its intensity greatly influences the value. Gemological laboratories describe the degree of filling, from negligible to significant. We see it in the guides on [gem treatments](/es/tratamientos-de-gemas/) and [gemological laboratory reports](/es/informes-de-laboratorio-gemologico/).

## How to judge the cut of an emerald

- **Front color:** Even green, without pale or too dark areas.
- **Windows:** As the emerald has a low refractive index, a cut that is too flat allows the background to be seen through the center. We explain it in the guide on [brightness, windows and extinction](/es/brillo-ventanas-y-extincion/).
- **Proportions:** Neither so deep that it looks small and dark, nor so flat that it loses color.
- **Symmetry:** regular contour and well-cut corners.
- **Corners and fillet:** without splinters, which are points of weakness.
- **Polished:** bright facets, no marks.

## Re-carving ancient emeralds

Many antique emeralds have deep native carvings or damaged corners. Recarving can improve their beauty, but it carries risks: old fillings must be removed, cracks can open with the work, and weight is always lost. An emerald with history may be worth more in its original cut, so it is a good idea to consult a cutter and a gemologist before deciding.

## Frequent errors

- **Carve without removing the old filling,** which hides real cracks.
- **Heat the stone** during cutting or polishing.
- **Choose sizes with prongs** without protecting them in the setting.
- **Judging an emerald** without taking into account its degree of fill.
- **Recarving an old piece** without valuing its history.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why is the emerald cut called that?

Because it was largely developed for this gem: it protects its fragile corners, takes advantage of the prismatic shape of the crystal and shows its color very well.

### Can an emerald be cut into a round shape?

Yes, but it is less common because it usually loses more weight and the shape of the crystal does not favor it.

### Do all cut emeralds contain oil?

Most have some filling. Those that do not have it, or have it to a negligible degree, are rare and highly valued.

### Why are so many emeralds cut cabochon?

Because the material with more inclusions shows off its color better without facets, and the cabochon is more resistant to impacts.

## In summary

Carving emeralds is a precision craft due to its inclusions, fissures and sensitivity to heat. The cutter studies the glass, removes old fillings, marks weak areas, decides whether to divide it, and guides the color. The emerald cut, rectangular and stepped with cut corners, protects the stone and shows off its green; The cabochon is ideal for most included material. In the workshop you work with little pressure, a lot of refrigeration and constant observation. Most emeralds are then filled with oils or resins, something that must be declared. When judging a size, check the face color, windows, proportions, symmetry and corners.
