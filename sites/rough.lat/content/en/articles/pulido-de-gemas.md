On a cut gem, polishing is the last thing done and the first thing seen. A good colored stone with a mediocre polish looks dull, dirty or cheap. The same stone, with impeccable polishing, reflects light like a mirror and allows you to see its interior clearly. For the hobbyist who is starting out in lapidary, polishing is also usually the most frustrating stage: scratches that do not disappear, surfaces with "orange peel", facets that become rounded. The good news is that almost all of these problems have a known cause and solution.

In this guide you will see what it really means to polish a stone, what the sequence of abrasives is, what discs and powders are used, what method is best for each family of gems and what the most common mistakes are. If you don't have your workshop organized yet, start with the guide to [learn lapidary safely](/es/aprender-lapidaria-con-seguridad/), which explains basic equipment and dust precautions.

## What is polishing?

Carving a stone is removing material with increasingly finer abrasives. Each abrasive leaves smaller scratches than the last, until there comes a point when the scratches are so fine that the surface reflects light without dispersing it: that's polishing.

In many stones, especially silicates such as quartz, the final polishing is not only a very fine scraping: chemical reactions are also involved between the polishing powder, water and the surface of the stone. That's why a powder works great with one gem and poorly with another. It is the main reason why "every stone asks for polishing."

## The sequence of abrasives

The golden rule of polishing is summarized in one phrase: **don't skip steps**. Each stage must completely remove the streaks from the previous one. If a single streak remains from the coarse step, the final polish will make it more visible.

A typical sequence for **cabochons** with diamond wheels could be:

1. **Roughing** with coarse grain, to give the shape.
2. **Refined** with medium grains, to eliminate grinding marks.
3. **Pre-polished** with fine grains, until the surface looks satin and uniform.
4. **Polishing** with very fine paste or powder on a soft disc, leather or felt.

In **faceting**, the sequence is similar but shorter and more precise: a cutting disc to create the facets, a pre-polishing disc and one or more polishing discs.

Grit numbers vary by manufacturer and system, so it's best to follow the chart for the equipment you use. The important thing is the principle: move from one grain to another only when the surface is uniform under the magnifying glass.

## Polishing materials

### Abrasives

| abrasive | What is it used for? | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Silicon carbide | traditional roughing and honing | economical; wears out and gets dirty |
| Diamond | all stages; essential on hard stones | fast and clean; in pastes, powders or discs |
| Cerium oxide | polishing quartz, beryl, glass and many silicates | very effective on medium hard stones |
| aluminum oxide | polishing various stones | there are very fine grades |
| tin oxide | polishing soft and opaque stones | traditional in leather or felt |
| chromium oxide | polishing some green stones like jade | stains a lot; use it on your own disk |

### Discs and media

- **Metal discs**, made of copper, tin or alloys, used with diamond in the faceting of hard stones.
- **Polymer or resin discs**, often used with cerium oxide or very fine diamond.
- **Ceramic discs**, very flat, for sharp facets.
- **Leather, felt and fabric**, for polishing cabochons.
- **Expandable wheels and bands**, for cabochons with curved surfaces.

Each disc and dust combination has its own behavior. The usual thing is to have **one disc for each powder** and never mix them: a single coarse grain on a polishing disc is enough to ruin a stone.

## Which method suits each stone

### Quartz and chalcedony

Agate, amethyst, citrine, rock crystal, jasper and their relatives respond very well to **cerium oxide** on leather, felt or a polymer disc. In faceting, cerium on a suitable disc gives a brilliant polish in a short time. You can learn more about this family in the guide on [quartzes from Latin America](/es/cuarzos-de-america-latina/).

### Corundum, spinel and chrysoberyl

They are very hard, so the **diamond** is almost mandatory in all stages. Very fine diamond pastes or powders are used on metal or ceramic discs. What you have to watch out for is patience: moving too quickly leaves streaks that are only visible at the end.

### Beryls and tourmalines

Aquamarine, morganite, emerald and tourmaline polish well with fine diamond or cerium oxide, depending on the disc. The emerald requires special care due to its fissures and its sensitivity to heat, as we tell in the guide on [emerald cutting] (/es/la-talla-de-esmeraldas/).

### Jade and stones with areas of different hardness

Jade, rhodochrosite, malachite, lapis lazuli and other composite stones have harder and softer areas. When polishing, the soft ones wear out faster and the surface is wavy, with an appearance called **orange peel** or **undercut**. To avoid it:

- work with a **firm support**, not too soft;
- use **low pressure** and **short strokes**;
- Try different powders: fine diamond, chromium or cerium oxide, depending on the stone.

### Opal, turquoise and soft or porous stones

These stones get hot and scratch easily. Suitable:

- polish at **low speed** and with lots of water;
- use **leather or felt** with cerium or tin oxide;
- Do not press or let the stone get hot, because the opal may crack.

### Obsidian and volcanic glass

They respond well to cerium, but they scratch easily and get hot. Obsidian also has a very sharp fracture: handle the edges carefully.

## The most common mistakes

| Problem | probable cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Fine stripes that don't go away | skipped a step or left large grains | return to previous step; clean stone, hands and disc |
| orange peel | areas of different hardness; soft support | Firmer support, less pressure, other powder |
| rounded facets | soft disc or uneven pressure | more rigid disk; constant and light pressure |
| Chipped edges | excessive pressure or stone with exfoliation | gentle pressure; better orient the stone |
| Heat cracks | high speed, little water | more water, less speed, pauses |
| "Milky" polish | contamination or inappropriate dust | change disc or powder |

## Cleaning: half the secret

- **Wash the stone, support and hands** between each stage.
- **Keep each disk covered** and labeled.
- **Do not use the same cloth** to clean in different stages.
- **Work neatly**: Coarse grain, away from the polishing station.

A clean workshop polishes better than a dirty one with better machines.

## How to check good polishing

1. **Using a 10x magnifying glass**, view the surface under spot light: no streaks or matte areas should be visible.
2. **Reflects a straight line**, such as the edge of a window or a light tube: If the image looks sharp and without ripples, the surface is good.
3. **For facets**, check that the edges are fine and that the facets are at precise points.

A quality polish makes the light behave as we explain in the guide on [brightness, windows and extinction] (/es/brillo-ventanas-y-extincion/).

## Security

- **Always work when wet** to avoid breathing dust.
- **Do not breathe polishing powder** dry; some are irritating and chromium oxide stains and should be handled with gloves.
- **Wear glasses** when working on wheels and discs.
- **Ventilate** the workshop and clean with damp cloths.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does my cabochon shine, but under the magnifying glass I see streaks?

Because some stage did not completely eliminate the stripes of the previous one. Go back a step, work until the surface is smooth, and repeat.

### Can I polish with toothpaste or homemade products?

Some hobbyists try it on soft stones, but the results are irregular. Specific polishing powders are cheap and give much better results.

### Can a professional polisher improve an already cut gem?

Yes. A repolishing removes scratches and wear from years of use and can restore a lot of shine to a stone, losing very little weight.

## In summary

Polishing involves removing scratches with increasingly finer abrasives until the surface reflects light like a mirror, and many stones also involve chemical reactions with the polishing powder. The key is not to skip steps, clean thoroughly between stages, use a disc for each powder and choose the method according to the stone: cerium oxide for quartz and chalcedony, diamond for corundum and hard gems, firm supports and little pressure for stones of irregular hardness, and low speed with a lot of water for opals and soft stones. With a magnifying glass and a line of reflected light, you check if the result is up to par.
