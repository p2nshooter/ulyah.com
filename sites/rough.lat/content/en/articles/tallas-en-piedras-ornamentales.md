Not all stones that fascinate artisans are transparent gems. In Latin America there is a very rich tradition of ornamental stone carvings: softer, opaque or translucent materials that become figures, altarpieces, vessels, windows, masks or small sculptures. Some traditions come from pre-Hispanic times; others were born in the colonial era; and many are still alive in family workshops in towns and cities.

Ornamental stones have their own rules: they are cut with different techniques than gems, they are cared for in a different way and, in the markets, they coexist with resin imitations. In this guide you will see what distinguishes an ornamental stone from a gem, what are some of the great carving traditions of the region, how a carving is worked, how to distinguish real stone from resin and how to care for each material.

## Gems and ornamental stones

The border is not rigid, but in general:

- **Gems** are durable, rare and beautiful materials used in jewelry, often clear and faceted.
- **Ornamental stones** are rocks or minerals used for their color, texture or ease of carving into decorative objects, sculptures or architectural elements. Many are softer and more abundant.

Some, such as lapis lazuli, jade or rhodochrosite, are used in both jewelry and decorative carving.

## The Huamanga stone

In Ayacucho, in the Andes of Peru, artisans have been carving a soft, white alabaster-type stone, known as Huamanga stone, for centuries. With it they make religious figures, nativity scenes, scenes of rural life and small sculptures of great delicacy, sometimes painted. It is one of the most recognized artisan traditions in the country. Due to their softness, these pieces are easily scratched and should not get wet.

## Puebla tecali

In the state of Puebla, in Mexico, the town of Tecali de Herrera gives its name to a banded and translucent stone, of cream, honey and greenish tones, which in the trade is often called onyx, although it is calcite. It has been worked since pre-Hispanic times, and in colonial times it was cut into thin sheets that, placed in the windows of some churches, let in a golden light. Today decorative objects, figures and table pieces are carved. Like all calcite, it is sensitive to acids and scratches easily.

## The soapstone of Minas Gerais

Soapstone, called soapstone because of its soft touch, is very easy to carve. In Minas Gerais it was used in baroque architecture and sculpture: the famous prophets of the Congonhas sanctuary, the work of Aleijadinho, are carved in this material. Today, in towns such as the surroundings of Ouro Preto and Mariana, sculptures, decorative objects and also soapstone pots and pans are made, highly appreciated in traditional cuisine for how they preserve heat.

## Obsidian in central Mexico

Obsidian, a natural volcanic glass, was essential to Mesoamerican peoples, who used it in tools, weapons, mirrors, and ceremonial objects. In the Teotihuacan area, near Mexico City, many workshops today work with black, gold or iridescent obsidian, and the green from the mountains of Hidalgo, into figures, masks and souvenirs. Be careful: its edges are extremely sharp before being polished, and it is fragile when hit.

## The Chilean combarbalite

In Combarbalá, in the Coquimbo region, a stone of volcanic origin with spots and veins of many colors is extracted, known as combarbalite. The town's artisans transform it into decorative objects, figures and souvenirs, and it is a symbol of the town.

## Lapis lazuli, rhodochrosite, sodalite and other colored stones

The intensely colored stones are also carved into decorative objects: lapis lazuli figures and boxes in Chile, rhodochrosite spheres and sheets in Argentina, sodalite objects, a blue mineral abundant in some areas of Bolivia, or agates and amethysts in southern Brazil and northern Uruguay. We see it in the guides on [Chilean lapis lazuli](/es/lapislazuli-chileno/) and [Argentine rhodochrosite](/es/rodocrosita-argentina/).

## How to work a size

1. **Choose the block:** without cracks or weak areas that could break during work.
2. **Roughing:** With saws, discs or hand tools, excess material is removed and the general shape is marked.
3. **Carve the details:** with files, rasps, burins and rotary burrs.
4. **Sand:** with increasingly finer abrasives, often with water.
5. **Polish:** to give shine or, depending on the stone, a satin finish.
6. **Finishing:** Some carvings are painted, waxed or mounted on bases.

Wet working reduces dust, which in stones with silica can be harmful to the lungs. We explain it in the guide on [learning lapidary safely](/es/aprender-lapidaria-con-seguridad/).

## Real stone or resin

Resin copies, sometimes mixed with stone dust, abound in tourist markets. Some clues:

| Signal | stone | Resin |
|---|---|---|
| Weight | weighs more than it looks | light for its size |
| Temperature | cold to the touch at first | lukewarm |
| Details | tool marks, minor differences | identical details on identical pieces, mold marks |
| Interior | natural grain and texture | bubbles, colors too uniform |
| Sound | sharper when tapped with a fingernail | more deaf |

A resin is not a scam if it is sold as such, but its price should be that of a reproduction. We also see it in the guide on [imitations and doublets](/es/imitaciones-y-dobletes/).

## Buy sizes wisely

- **Ask who made the piece** and in what workshop; Many artisans sign their works or can explain their process to you.
- **Ask about the stone:** its name, its origin and if it is dyed, waxed or reinforced with resin.
- **Check the piece calmly:** Cracks, glued joints or repaired areas may indicate that it was broken and fixed.
- **Plan transportation:** Ask for protective packaging and pack fragile items in carry-on luggage if possible.
- **Respect heritage:** replicas of pre-Hispanic pieces must be sold as replicas; Authentic archaeological pieces cannot be purchased or taken out of the country.
- **Assess the hours of work:** A fine carving in Huamanga stone or a well-polished obsidian figure can take days.

Buying directly from workshops or craft fairs allows you to meet those who work with the stone and ensures that more of the price reaches them.

## Care according to the stone

| stone | Main care |
|---|---|
| Huamanga stone and alabasters | do not wet; remove dust with a soft brush |
| Tecali and other calcites | avoid acids and cleaning products; dry cloth |
| soap stone | resistant; mild soap and water; pots require curing before first use |
| Obsidian | protect from impacts; clean with a soft cloth |
| Lapis lazuli and rhodochrosite | avoid acids and chemicals; dry or slightly damp cloth |
| Agate and quartz | resistant; mild soap and water |

In general, keep carvings away from furniture edges and walking areas, and clean them without chemicals.

## Frequent errors

- **Wash alabaster carvings** or Huamanga stone with water.
- **Use acidic cleaners** on tecali, calcites or rhodochrosite.
- **Buy resin** at the price of hand-carved stone.
- **Ignore the sharpness** of unpolished obsidian.
- **Bargain hard** pieces that take many hours of work.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Mexican onyx onyx?

In the strict gemological sense, no: it is usually banded calcite, like tecali. Onyx itself is a variety of chalcedony.

### Can you cook in a soapstone pot?

Yes, it is a traditional use in Minas Gerais. Ask the seller how to cure it before using it for the first time.

### How do I know if a carving is stone or resin?

Due to the weight, the temperature to the touch, the details and the mold marks. If in doubt, ask the seller directly.

### Is obsidian a stone?

It is a natural volcanic glass, formed by the very rapid cooling of silica-rich lava.

## In summary

Ornamental stones, softer, more opaque or translucent than gems, support some of the most beautiful artisan traditions in the region: the Huamanga stone in Ayacucho, the tecali in Puebla, the soap stone in Minas Gerais, the obsidian in central Mexico, the combarbalite in Chile and the carvings in lapis lazuli, rhodochrosite, sodalite or agate. They are worked with saws, files, milling cutters and abrasives, preferably wet. Learn to distinguish stone from resin, take care of each material according to its sensitivity to water, acids or shocks and value the work of the person who carves it.
