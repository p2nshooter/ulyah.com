When someone thinks of a colored gemstone, three names almost always come up: ruby, sapphire, and emerald. They are magnificent stones, but they are not the only ones, and many times they are not the best option for a specific budget, for an active lifestyle or for those who prefer an untreated stone. There is a group of less famous gems that offer rich colors, good resistance and, in many cases, a fascinating history. Some were confused for centuries with the rubies of royal crowns.

In this guide you will see which alternative gems deserve attention, what advantages and limits each one has, how to choose according to the use of the jewel and what should be checked before buying them.

## Why look beyond the classics

There are several reasons to consider an alternative gem:

- **Price:** For the same budget, you can often get a larger, cleaner or better colored stone.
- **Fewer Treatments:** Some of these gems are rarely treated, something many buyers value.
- **Originality:** a piece with a spinel or a green garnet stands out in a world full of identical solitaires.
- **Sufficient durability:** several of them withstand daily use well.

To compare with judgment, it is worth keeping in mind the ideas in the guide on [how to judge the quality of a colored gem](/es/calidad-de-las-gemas-de-color/) and [hardness, tenacity and stability](/es/dureza-tenacidad-y-estabilidad/).

## Spinel: the great confused

### What is it

Spinel is an oxide of magnesium and aluminum that crystallizes in octahedrons, often perfect. It has **hardness 8**, good tenacity and comes in reds, pinks, oranges, violets, grays and blues. The **cobalt blue spinel**, an electric blue, is one of the rarest.

### A story of crowns

For centuries, many red spinels were taken for rubies. Some of the most famous "rubies" in European royal jewelery are actually spinels, such as the large red stone of the British Imperial Crown known as the "Black Prince's Ruby". The confusion is understandable: both gems share a color and appear in the same deposits in Asia. Only with modern gemology could they be separated.

### Advantages

- Intense and bright color, without the brown tone that other reds sometimes have.
- **Rarely discussed**: Most spinels on the market are in their natural color.
- Very suitable for daily wear rings.

### What you need to know

- The best red, hot pink and cobalt blue spinels have risen sharply in price in recent decades. They still cost less than a ruby ​​of comparable quality, but they are no longer a bargain.
- **Synthetic spinel** has been made for a long time, in all colors, and is frequently used to imitate other gems, such as aquamarine or sapphire. If the color is suspiciously perfect and the price is very low, ask for verification. The [synthetic and laboratory gems](/es/gemas-sinteticas-y-de-laboratorio/) guide explains how to detect them.

## The garnet family

"Garnet" is the name of a group of related minerals. Their hardness ranges from 6.5 to 7.5 and they are almost never treated. What many do not know is that garnet is not just dark red: there are garnets of almost all colors, except true blue, which is very rare.

| Variety | Color | What is good to know |
|---|---|---|
| Compliment | deep red | the classic red garnet of antique jewelry |
| Almandine | red with violet or brown tinge | very common; sometimes too dark |
| Rhodolite | purple rose to raspberry | mixture of pirope and almandine, bright and versatile |
| Spessartine | orange to orange-red | bright and intense oranges are highly appreciated |
| Tsavorite | deep green | variety of grossularia; alternative to emerald, cleaner and harder |
| Hessonite | cinnamon, honey | variety of grossularia with characteristic "oily" appearance |
| Demantoid | yellowish green to green | variety of andradite with a fire higher than that of diamond; highly valued "horsetail" inclusions |
| color changing | varies depending on the light | from blue-green to purple, for example; highly appreciated by collectors |

### Advantages

- **Without treatments** in the vast majority of cases.
- Affordable prices on red varieties; Fine green and orange garnets are more expensive, but are still reasonable alternatives to emerald or orange sapphire.
- Good shine and, in the case of the demantoid, extraordinary fire.

### What you need to know

- Dark red garnets can look almost black in large stones; They are often carved small or shallow.
- The demantoid is somewhat softer than other garnets and should be protected with a good setting.

## Zircon: the forgotten brilliance

**Natural zircon** is an ancient gem, with brilliance and fire close to that of diamond. It should not be confused with cubic zirconia, a synthetic material that imitates diamond and became popular in the 20th century. They are different things.

- **Colors:** blue (almost always due to heating), colorless, gold, red, green and brown.
- **Hardness:** around 6 to 7.5 depending on the type, with edges that wear down with use.
- **Strong double refraction:** When looking through the table with a magnifying glass, the edges of the lower facets are seen doubled.

It is a beautiful gem for earrings and pendants. In rings used daily, their edges can chip when struck.

## Chrysoberyl and alexandrite

Chrysoberyl has **hardness 8.5**, one of the highest among colored gems, and excellent toughness. It comes in yellow, yellowish green and brown, and in two special varieties:

- the **chrysoberyl cat's eye**, with a band of sharp light that slides over the cabochon;
- **alexandrite**, which changes color between daylight and warm light.

We explain them in the guide on [gems that change color](/es/gemas-que-cambian-de-color/) and in the guide on [optical phenomena in gems](/es/fenomenos-opticos-en-gemas/).

## Other alternatives to consider

- **Tourmaline:** almost all colors, hardness 7 to 7.5. We tell it in the guide on [tourmalines from Brazil](/es/turmalinas-de-brasil/).
- **Aquamarine and Morganite:** Soft blue and pink beryls, hardness 7.5 to 8, very suitable for large stones.
- **Peridot:** bright olive green; somewhat softer, from 6.5 to 7, and sensitive to acids.
- **Iolite:** violet blue with a very marked pleochroism; economical, but with an exfoliation that requires care.
- **Topaz:** hardness 8, but with easy exfoliation; Many blue topazes in the trade are colorless irradiated and heated.
- **Tanzanite:** intense violet blue, almost always heated; relatively soft and with exfoliation, better in earrings and pendants than in rings for daily use.

## How to choose according to use

| Jewel | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Everyday ring | spinel, chrysoberyl, high hardness garnets, aquamarine with protective setting |
| Occasional wear ring | tsavorite, demantoid, tourmaline, zircon |
| Earrings and pendants | almost any, including tanzanite, iolite, peridot and zircon |
| Pieces for manual work or sport | best gems with hardness 8 or higher and bezel settings |

If the stone is going for an engagement ring, also review the guide on [engagement rings](/es/anillos-de-compromiso/), which compares gems for everyday wear.

## What to check before buying

1. **The exact species.** "Red stone" or "green gem" say nothing. Ask for the gemological name: spinel, pyrope, tsavorite.
2. **Natural or synthetic.** Synthetic spinel and other laboratory materials are common.
3. **Treatments.** Although spinel and garnet are rarely treated, other alternatives are: tanzanite, blue topaz, blue zircon.
4. **Color in various lights.** Some gemstones, like certain garnets or tanzanite, change quite a bit between daylight and warm light.
5. **The size.** A good proportion turns on the shine; too deep a cut darkens the stone.
6. **The report**, for high priced pieces, for color change garnets, fine demantoids or spinels of exceptional color. The [gemological laboratory reports](/es/informes-de-laboratorio-gemologico/) guide will help you read it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a red spinel a "fake ruby"?

No. It is a different natural gem, with its own beauty and its own value. It would only be a scam if it was sold as a ruby.

### Is garnet a cheap stone?

Commercial red garnets are inexpensive, but fine varieties, such as tsavorite, demantoid, or color change garnets, can be expensive.

### Is zircon dangerous due to radioactivity?

Some zircons contain traces of radioactive elements, but zircons sold in jewelry have levels that are considered safe for normal wear. If you have questions about a specific stone, consult a laboratory.

## In summary

Beyond ruby, sapphire and emerald there is a world of gems that offer color, brilliance and resistance: spinel, hard and almost never treated, which for centuries was confused with ruby; the garnet family, with intense reds, oranges and greens; natural zircon, with extraordinary brilliance; chrysoberyl and alexandrite; and many others, such as tourmaline, beryl or peridot. To choose well, you have to think about the use that the jewel will have, ask for the exact species, confirm if it is natural or synthetic, know its treatments and ask for information when the price justifies it.
