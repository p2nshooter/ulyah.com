Latin America is a land of copper. Chile, Peru and Mexico are among the world's largest producers, and the mountains and deserts where this metal is extracted also hide a family of intensely colored stones: sky blues, forest greens, sea turquoises. They are copper stones, minerals that are born when water and air slowly alter rocks rich in this metal. Some have been used in jewelry for centuries, others are primarily collector's items, and almost all of them have something in common: they are more delicate than their color suggests.

In this guide you will see how these stones are formed, how to recognize turquoise, chrysocolla, malachite, azurite and Atacamite, what treatments and imitations are common, what precautions must be taken when cutting them and how to care for them once they are mounted into a piece of jewelry.

## How copper stones are born

At depth, copper is often combined with sulfur in metallic minerals with a golden or gray luster, such as chalcopyrite. When these rocks are close to the surface, rainwater and oxygen begin to dissolve them. The released copper travels with the water and precipitates again combined with other elements: carbonates, silicates, phosphates or chlorides. That altered strip, which geologists call **oxidation zone**, is where blue and green minerals appear.

That is why these stones have three common features:

- **very saturated blue and green colors**, due to copper;
- **low or medium hardness**, almost always below that of quartz;
- **porosity or chemical sensitivity**, which makes them react with acids, oils or heat.

If you want to review the role of water in mineral formation, the guide on [how gems are formed](/es/como-se-forman-las-gemas/) explains it in more detail.

## Turquoise

### What is it

Turquoise is a hydrated **copper aluminum phosphate**. Its color ranges from sky blue to apple green: the more iron it contains, the greener it becomes. Many pieces show a network of dark or brown veins, called **matrix**, formed by the rock in which it grew. The matrix is ​​not a defect: in many markets, a fine and well-drawn network is highly valued.

### History in America

The cultures of Mesoamerica appreciated turquoise in an extraordinary way. The Mixtecs and Mexica used it in mosaics on wood, shell or bone for masks, shields and ceremonial objects, and much of that stone arrived via trade routes from the north, in what is now the southwestern United States and northwestern Mexico. Several of these works can be seen today in museums in Mexico and Europe.

### What to check

- **Hardness:** between 5 and 6 on the Mohs scale; It is scratched by steel objects and quartz dust in the environment.
- **Porosity:** Absorbs oils, creams, perfume and sweat, and may turn greenish with use.
- **Stabilization:** Much of the turquoise in the trade is **impregnated with resin** to harden it and set the color. It is an accepted practice if stated.
- **Reconstituted Turquoise:** Turquoise powder agglomerated with resin. It should be sold as such.
- **Imitations:** dyed **howlite** and **magnesite**, plastic and glass are the most common. In dyed pieces, the color accumulates in the cracks and grains.

## Chrysocolla

### What is it

Chrysocolla is a hydrated **copper silicate**, with an intense greenish blue color. It is very common in the oxidation zones of copper deposits in **Peru, Chile** and other countries in the region, often mixed with malachite, quartz or turquoise.

### The big problem: it is soft

Pure chrysocolla is very soft, between 2 and 4 on the Mohs scale, and brittle. That is why it is mainly used in protected cabochons, beads, decorative pieces and mosaics, and is often stabilized with resin.

### The valuable exception

When chrysocolla is trapped inside chalcedony, the result is a translucent stone of intense blue-green color with the hardness of quartz, around 7. In the trade it is known as **"chrysocolla in chalcedony"** or "gem silica", and is one of the most sought-after stones in this family. To distinguish it from common chrysocolla, it is enough to observe the transparency and check the hardness in a laboratory. The guide to [Latin American quartz](/es/cuarzos-de-america-latina/) explains what chalcedony is.

## Malachite

### What is it

Malachite is a green **copper carbonate**, famous for its **concentric bands** in light and dark greens. It is formed in layers, like agate, and when cut, eyes, waves and circles of great beauty appear.

### What to check

- **Hardness:** between 3.5 and 4: it scratches easily and loses its polish with use.
- **Chemical reaction:** Like all carbonates, it reacts with acids, including lemon juice and vinegar.
- **Origin:** It appears in many copper deposits in the region, but most of the ornamental malachite in international trade comes from central Africa.
- **Imitations:** plastic, glass and dyed stones with printed bands. True malachite is heavy, cold, and its bands do not repeat regularly.

## Azurite

### What is it

Azurite is another **copper carbonate**, a deep blue that for centuries was ground to make pigments. It forms brilliant crystals highly sought after by collectors and masses that often appear mixed with malachite, a combination known as **azurmalachite**.

### What to check

- **Hardness:** between 3.5 and 4, the same as malachite.
- **Stability:** Over time, and in the presence of moisture, azurite can slowly transform into malachite. Many pieces show precisely that transition.
- **Use:** more common in collections and decorative objects than in jewelry for daily use.

## Atacamite

Atacamite is a **copper chloride** of dark green and shiny color, which owes its name to the **Atacama Desert**, in Chile, where it was described. It is above all a collector's mineral: its crystals are small and delicate, and it does not withstand use in jewelry. It is worth knowing because it frequently appears at mineral fairs in Chile and northern Argentina.

## Carving stones from copper: precautions

These stones are soft and easy to work with, but they have a particular risk: **copper mineral dust should not be breathed or ingested**. If you are going to cut or polish them:

- **always work wet**, so that dust does not remain in the air;
- wear **mask with appropriate filter** and glasses;
- clean the workshop with damp cloths, not sweeping;
- wash your hands and do not eat or drink in the work area.

The guide to [learning lapidary safely](/es/aprender-lapidaria-con-seguridad/) explains how to set up a safe workspace.

## How to buy them judiciously

1. **Ask about stabilization.** In turquoise and chrysocolla it is almost the norm. It must be declared on the invoice.
2. **Be wary of perfectly uniform colors.** Natural turquoise rarely has an identical blue throughout the piece; the dye usually shows through in the cracks.
3. **Look at the malachite bands.** If they repeat like a pattern, be suspicious.
4. **Ask for exact species.** "Copper stone" or "Chilean stone" are not mineral names.
5. **Value the matrix.** In turquoise, a fine, well-drawn network is an appreciated feature, not a defect.
6. **Choose the appropriate setting.** A full bezel protects these soft stones much better than claws. The [setting types](/es/tipos-de-engaste/) guide will help you.

The [imitations, doublets and triplets](/es/imitaciones-y-dobletes/) guide expands on how to recognize substitutions.

## Care

- **Put the jewelry on last**, after creams, perfume and makeup.
- **Take it off** when washing dishes, swimming, exercising, or cleaning with chemicals.
- **Clean only with a soft, slightly damp cloth**; no ultrasound, steam, alcohol or acids.
- **Store separately**, in a padded case, away from harder stones.
- **Avoid heat**, which can dry out the turquoise and alter its color.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is stabilized turquoise fake?

No. It is natural turquoise to which resin has been added to make it more resistant. The important thing is that it is declared and that the price is consistent with it.

### Can I wear malachite every day?

You can, but it will lose shine and accumulate streaks. It is better suited for pendants and earrings than for rings that take a beating.

### Why did my turquoise turn green?

It probably absorbed skin oils, creams, or sweat. The change is usually permanent, so it is advisable to protect it from the beginning.

## In summary

Copper stones are born in the oxidation zone of the deposits, where water and air transform metallic minerals into carbonates, silicates, phosphates and chlorides of blue and green colors. Turquoise, chrysocolla, malachite, azurite and Atacamite are the best known in the region. They are all soft or porous, many are stabilized with resin and dyed imitations abound, so it is a good idea to ask, look carefully and ask for the exact species on the invoice. When carving them, you must work wet and with protection; When wearing them, protect them from creams, acids, heat and shocks.
