The diamond is the most well-known gem in the world and, at the same time, one of the most misunderstood. Many buyers know that there are the famous "four Cs," but they don't always understand what they mean in practice, which of them matters most for the beauty of the stone, or why two diamonds of the same weight can have very different prices. Added to this is the massive arrival of laboratory diamonds, which has changed the market in a few years.

This guide brings together the essentials to look at a diamond with judgment: what it is and how it is formed, how color, purity, cut and weight are classified, what role fluorescence and treatments play, how natural diamonds, laboratory diamonds and imitations differ, what the Kimberley Process is and how to allocate a budget to obtain the most beautiful stone possible.

## What is a diamond

| Property | Diamond |
|---|---|
| Composition | pure carbon |
| crystalline system | cubic |
| Hardness | 10 on the Mohs scale, the highest of natural minerals |
| Exfoliation | perfect in four directions; can chip with a sharp blow |
| Brightness | adamantine, the most intense among transparent gems |
| Density | about 3.52 |

Its hardness makes it resistant to scratches, but not indestructible: a strong blow in the right direction can cause a chip or fracture. We explain it in the guide on [hardness, toughness and stability] (/es/dureza-tenacidad-y-estabilidad/).

## How it is formed and where it comes from

Natural diamonds are formed in the Earth's mantle, at great depth, and reach the surface in eruptions of special magmas that form kimberlite chimneys. We tell it in the guide on [how gems are formed](/es/como-se-forman-las-gemas/).

For centuries, India was practically the only known source. In the eighteenth century, the mines of Minas Gerais turned Brazil into the world's largest producer, and in the nineteenth century it was South Africa's turn. Today, most of the production comes from countries in Africa, Russia, Canada and Australia. In Latin America there is production, especially alluvial, but it is small compared to those countries.

## The four C's

The four C system, widely spread internationally, classifies a colorless diamond according to:

1. **Color:** How close it is to the total absence of color.
2. **Purity:** the quantity and visibility of its inclusions.
3. **Size:** the quality of its proportions, symmetry and polish.
4. **Carat weight:** Its mass, which is related to its size.

## The color

Diamonds that the market calls white or colorless are graded on a scale with letters starting with D:

| Degrees | Overview |
|---|---|
| D,E,F | colorless; the rarest |
| G, H, I, J | almost colorless; with little visible tint on the mounted stone |
| K,L,M | light yellowish tint |
| N to Z | increasingly visible yellowish or brown tint |

The differences between neighboring grades are subtle and are best seen under controlled conditions, with the stone face down on a white background. Once assembled and seen from the front, many people do not distinguish two adjacent grades. The metal also plays a role: a somewhat warm diamond can look great in yellow gold.

## The purity

Purity describes internal inclusions and surface markings, observed with a 10x magnifying glass:

| Degree | Meaning |
|---|---|
| FL and IF | no inclusions visible at 10x magnification, or only minimal surface markings at IF |
| VVS1 and VVS2 | very, very small inclusions, difficult to see even with a magnifying glass |
| VS1 and VS2 | very small inclusions, visible with a magnifying glass but not with the naked eye in general |
| SI1 and SI2 | small inclusions, visible with a magnifying glass and sometimes with the naked eye |
| I1, I2, I3 | inclusions visible to the naked eye that may affect beauty or strength |

The important thing for most buyers is that the diamond is clean to the naked eye. Many diamonds are VS grade or even SI grade, at a significantly lower price than higher grades.

## The size

The cut is the factor that most influences the beauty of a diamond, because it determines how light enters, bounces off the facets and returns to the eye in the form of brilliance, flashes of color and sparkle. For round brilliants, large laboratories classify the cut into grades that range from excellent to poor, according to their proportions, symmetry and polish.

- **A diamond that is too flat** allows light to escape from below and shows dull areas.
- **One that is too deep** loses light on the sides and looks smaller from the front.
- **A good cut** can make a diamond of average color or clarity look better than a better graded but poorly cut one.

In addition to the round brilliant, there are shapes such as the princess, the oval, the cushion, the emerald, the pear, the marquise or the heart, each one with its own character. We see it in the guide on [types of gem cutting](/es/tipos-de-talla-de-gemas/).

## The weight

The carat is equal to 0.2 grams and is divided into one hundred points. The price per carat increases with size, because large diamonds are rarer, and usually jumps in round weights, such as half carat or carat. A 0.95 carat diamond can cost significantly less than a 1.00 carat diamond with similar characteristics, and at first glance the difference in size is minimal.

## Fluorescence

Many diamonds emit a light, often blue, under ultraviolet light. In most cases it does not affect their appearance in normal light, and in somewhat warm colored diamonds it can even make them appear whiter. In rare cases, very strong fluorescence gives a slightly milky appearance. The market values ​​it differently depending on the color grade, so it is advisable to see the stone and not just read the report.

## colored diamonds

Outside the colorless scale are fancy diamonds, with intense colors such as yellow, pink, blue or green, caused by elements such as nitrogen or boron, by deformations of their structure or by natural radiation. They are classified according to their own criteria and some are among the most expensive gems in the world. There are also treated color diamonds, which must be declared.

## Treatments

- **Laser drilling:** a fine channel allows a dark inclusion to be whitened.
- **Filling cracks:** glass hides cracks that reach the surface; It is sensitive to heat.
- **High pressure and high temperature:** may enhance the color of some diamonds.
- **Irradiation and annealing:** create fantasy colors.
- **Coatings:** thin layers that modify the color.

All must be declared, and the laboratory reports indicate them. We explain it in the guide on [gem treatments](/es/tratamientos-de-gemas/).

## Natural, laboratory and imitations

| material | What is it | How to distinguish |
|---|---|---|
| natural diamond | carbon formed in the earth's mantle | laboratory report |
| laboratory diamond | laboratory crystallized carbon, identical in composition and structure | laboratory instruments; often laser inscription |
| Moissanite | silicon carbide, other material | double refraction visible with magnifying glass; specific instruments |
| cubic zirconia | zirconium oxide, imitation | heavier, less hard, with very marked flashes of color |
| Colorless sapphire, glass | other materials | properties other than diamond |

Lab diamonds are real diamonds, but their price is much lower and their resale value is low. We see it in the guide on [synthetic and laboratory gems](/es/gemas-sinteticas-y-de-laboratorio/), and in the guide on [imitations and doublets](/es/imitaciones-y-dobletes/) we explain the limits of diamond testers.

## The Kimberley Process and the origin

The Kimberley Process is an international certification system created at the beginning of this century to prevent the trade in rough diamonds that finance armed conflicts. It has reduced that trade, but it has limitations: it applies to rough diamonds, it certifies countries and not specific mines, and its definition of a conflict diamond is narrow. If the origin matters to you, ask the seller about the traceability of the stone and their sourcing policies. The guide on [responsible gold and traceability](/es/oro-responsable-y-trazabilidad/) expands on these ideas.

## How to choose on a budget

1. **Prioritize size:** An excellent or very good size is what is most noticeable.
2. **Look for an almost colorless color** if you are mounting it in white metal, or accept a little more warmth in yellow gold.
3. **Choose purity that is clean to the naked eye,** without paying for grades you won't see.
4. **Consider weights just below round ones** to save with little visible difference.
5. **Request a report from a recognized laboratory** and compare stones with reports from the same laboratory.
6. **See the stone in person** or on high quality video, not just the numbers.

If it is for an engagement ring, also see the guide on [engagement rings](/es/anillos-de-compromiso/).

## Diamond care

- **Cleanse it frequently:** Skin oil and creams dull its shine. Warm water, mild soap and a soft brush are enough.
- **Check the settings:** Loose claws can cause you to lose the stone.
- **Keep it separately:** A diamond scratches other gems and other diamonds.
- **Avoid sharp blows,** which can chip it.
- **Do not use ultrasound** on diamonds with filled fissures or many inclusions.

More tips in the guide on [jewelry maintenance and cleaning](/es/mantenimiento-y-limpieza-de-joyas/).

## Frequent errors

- **Buy only for color and purity**, ignoring the size.
- **Pay for degrees of purity** that cannot be seen with the naked eye.
- **Compare diamonds** with reports from different laboratories as if they were equivalent.
- **Rely on a simple tester** to rule out moissanite.
- **Buy a laboratory diamond** thinking about reselling it at a profit.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which of the four Cs is the most important?

For beauty, size. Color and purity matter, but a well-cut diamond shines brighter than a poorly cut, higher-graded one.

### Can a diamond break?

Yes. It is very resistant to scratches, but a dry blow can chip it due to its exfoliation.

### Are lab diamonds fake?

No. They are real diamonds created in a laboratory, with the same composition and structure. Its origin must be declared and its market value is much lower.

### Is a report needed?

For diamonds of a certain size, it is highly recommended, from a recognized laboratory. We explain it in the guide on [gemology laboratory reports](/es/informes-de-laboratorio-gemologico/).

## In summary

Diamond is pure carbon, the hardest gem, but it can chip. It is evaluated by the four Cs: color, graduated from D; purity, from FL to I3; the size, which is the one that most influences its beauty; and the weight in carats. Fluorescence, treatments and origin complete the picture, and laboratory diamonds, identical in composition, cost much less and have almost no resale value. Prioritize the size, choose color and purity that look good to the naked eye, take advantage of weights close to round and buy with a report from a recognized laboratory.
