Whoever goes out to an abandoned quarry, a mountain river or a mineral fair with the desire to learn always ends up asking themselves the same question: what is this thing I have in my hand? The color is deceiving, the size too, and the names that circulate in the stands are not always reliable. The good news is that, with a few cheap tools and a neat method, you can recognize many of the common minerals and rule out many confusions before you ever get to a lab.

In this guide you will see what to carry in your backpack, what properties to observe and in what order, how to do simple tests without damaging a valuable piece, what are the most frequent confusions and what safety precautions should never be skipped.

## What to wear

There is no need for a portable laboratory. A basic kit fits in a small bag:

- **10x magnifying glass**, the most useful tool of all.
- **Scratch plate**, a piece of unglazed porcelain (the back of a tile works).
- **Hardness references:** a copper coin, a steel knife, a piece of glass and, if possible, a quartz crystal.
- **Small magnet**, for magnetic minerals.
- **Flashlight**, and if you are interested in fluorescence, a safe ultraviolet lamp.
- **Notebook, bags and labels**, to write down the place, date and what you observed.
- **Protective glasses and gloves**, if you are going to hit rocks.

With this you can advance a lot. The rest comes from practice.

## A method in seven steps

The important thing is not to jump directly to the conclusion. It is advisable to look at several properties and see if they fit together.

### 1. Look at the context

Before you look at the mineral, look where it is. Is it a volcanic rock with cavities? A coarse-grained pegmatite with large crystals? A river gravel? The type of rock greatly reduces the possibilities. Agates appear in cavities of volcanic rocks; beryls and tourmalines, in pegmatites; garnets, in metamorphic rocks. The guide on [how gems are formed](/es/como-se-forman-las-gemas/) explains these environments.

### 2. Look at the shape of the glass

When the mineral has been able to grow freely, its shape, called **habit**, is one of the most reliable clues:

- **six-sided prisms** ending in a point: quartz;
- **hexagonal prisms with flat faces at the tip**: beryl, such as aquamarine or emerald;
- **striated prisms with almost triangular section**: tourmaline;
- **almost spherical crystals with many faces**: garnet;
- **perfect cubes with striations**: pyrite;
- **sheets that separate like the pages of a book**: micas.

Many times the glass is broken or worn, and other properties must be used.

### 3. Look at the shine

Gloss describes how the surface reflects light, and you learn to recognize it quickly:

| Brightness | How it looks | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Metallic | like polished metal | pyrite, galena, hematite |
| Vitreous | like glass | quartz, beryl, tourmaline |
| Adamantine | very intense, almost like a diamond | zircon, some garnets |
| Resinous | like resin or honey | amber, sulfur |
| Pearly | like the inside of a shell | some micas, the plaster on certain faces |
| Silky | like a shiny fabric | fibrous minerals |
| Fatty | as if it were smeared | some chalcedonies, talc |

### 4. Do the stripe test

Rub the mineral against the porcelain plate and see the color of the powder it leaves behind. The color of the stripe is much more constant than the color of the mineral. Two classic examples:

- **hematite** can be black or metallic gray, but its stripe is **reddish**;
- **pyrite**, gold, leaves a **greenish black** stripe, while gold leaves a golden stripe.

Minerals harder than porcelain, such as quartz or beryl, do not leave a streak: they scratch the plate. **Do not do this test on a cut gem** or a pretty face of a crystal: choose a corner that cannot be seen.

### 5. Estimate the hardness

The Mohs scale ranks minerals from 1 (talc) to 10 (diamond) depending on which one is closest to which. Approximate references are used in the field:

- the **nail** scratches very soft minerals, such as talcum powder or plaster;
- a **copper coin** scratches the calcite;
- a **steel knife** and **glass** mark the border of medium minerals;
- **quartz** scratches glass and steel.

The golden rule is to test gently, in a hidden spot, and check with the magnifying glass if a groove is really made or if there is just dust left from the object you used to scratch. To understand why hardness isn't everything, read the guide on [hardness, toughness and stability](/es/dureza-tenacidad-y-estabilidad/).

### 6. Watch it break

- **Exfoliation** is the tendency to break on flat, shiny surfaces, always in the same directions. Calcite breaks into rhombohedrons; mica, in sheets; feldspar, in two planes almost at right angles.
- The **conchoidal fracture**, curved like the inside of a shell, is typical of quartz, obsidian and glass.

This property easily separates quartz, without exfoliation, from feldspar or calcite, which do have it.

### 7. Feel the weight and test the magnet

With practice, the hand learns to notice **density**: a piece of barite or galena weighs much more than its size suggests. The **magnet** strongly attracts magnetite and, more weakly, some other iron-containing minerals.

## Additional tests, with caution

- **Fizzing with dilute acid:** Calcite fizzes with a drop of very dilute hydrochloric acid or even vinegar. It is a classic test, but acid damages many stones and is dangerous to the skin and eyes. If you use it, do it with gloves and glasses, in a hidden area, and rinse well afterwards. Never in a gem.
- **Fluorescence:** some minerals glow under ultraviolet light, such as certain calcites, fluorites or willemites. Use only lamps designed for this purpose and do not look directly into the light.
- **Transparency and color against the light:** with a flashlight you can see areas of color, inclusions and internal fractures.

There are tests that should be **avoided**: licking or tasting a mineral, scratching cut gems, heating stones with a lighter, or tapping a valuable crystal to "see what's inside."

## The most frequent confusions

| It seems | But it can be | How to distinguish them |
|---|---|---|
| Gold | pyrite or golden mica | gold is malleable and leaves a golden streak; pyrite is brittle and leaves a greenish black streak |
| Quartz | calcite | calcite is soft, has exfoliation and bubbles with acid |
| Diamond | quartz or glass | quartz has a hexagonal shape; the glass shows bubbles and rounded edges |
| Emerald | green glass, fluorite, apatite | fluorite and apatite are soft; glass lacks crystalline habit |
| agate | glass or resin | agate is cold, hard and shows irregular natural bands |
| Turquoise | dyed howlite or magnesite | dye builds up in cracks; a cotton pad with acetone can give it away |

Remember that river pebbles lose their shape and original shine. Wetting them helps to see the color and transparency.

## Security: what is never negotiated

- **Do not enter abandoned mines.** Cave-ins, hidden shafts, and oxygen-depleted air kill experienced people every year.
- **Do not taste minerals with your tongue.** Some contain mercury, arsenic or lead, such as cinnabar, orpiment or galena.
- **Wash your hands** after handling minerals and before eating.
- **Wear glasses** when hitting rocks: Quartz chips cut like glass.
- **Be informed before collecting.** National parks, reserves, private lands and mining concessions have their own rules, and many times collecting is prohibited.

The guide on [collecting gems and minerals](/es/coleccionar-gemas-y-minerales/) explains how to do it legally, and the one on [learning lapidary safely](/es/aprender-lapidaria-con-seguridad/) explains the risks of mineral dust if you decide to cut or polish what you find.

## Write down everything

A mineral without a label loses much of its scientific and collection value. Write down at the moment:

- the **exact place**, with coordinates if you have them;
- the **date**;
- the **rock** he was on;
- the **properties** you observed: gloss, stripe, approximate hardness, shape, weight;
- your **hypothesis** and the doubts you had left.

Over time, that notebook becomes your best manual.

## When to go to a laboratory

Field tests serve to guide you, not to certify. If the piece may be a valuable gem, if you are going to buy it for a high price or if you want to sell it, you need a gemologist or a laboratory. With a refractometer, a polariscope, a hydrostatic balance and, if necessary, spectroscopic analysis, the species is identified and treatments and imitations are detected. Our guide on [buying raw gems without cheating](/es/comprar-gemas-en-bruto-sin-enganos/) explains when it's worth it and how to order it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does color serve to identify a mineral?

Bit. Many minerals appear in various colors and many different minerals share the same color. Use it as another clue, never the only one.

### What do I do if I can't identify it?

Photograph it well, with scale and in natural light, note all the properties and consult a mineralogy club, a museum or a gemologist. Many universities and museums in the region answer questions from the public.

### Does scratching a stone take away its value?

You can do this if the mark is visible. That is why it is always tested on a hidden spot and never on cut gems or well-formed crystal faces.

More guides on minerals and crystals in the [Rough Gems](/es/categoria/gemas-en-bruto/) section.

## In summary

Identifying minerals in the field is a matter of method: first look at the rock and the environment, then the crystal shape, luster, streak, hardness, way of breaking, weight and magnetism, and check that all the clues fit. The tools are simple: a magnifying glass, a porcelain plate, some hardness references and a magnet. Precautions too: do not enter abandoned mines, do not taste minerals with your tongue, wear glasses and respect collection regulations. And when the piece may be valuable, a laboratory always has the last word.
