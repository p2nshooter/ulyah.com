Giving someone the stone of their birth month is one of the most personal ways to choose a piece of jewelry. A ring with a garnet for someone born in January, a pendant with an emerald for May or earrings with sapphires for September turn the piece into something of its own, linked to the person. The tradition is popular throughout the world and also in Latin America, where many of these gems are also found.

Behind the list of birthstones is a story that mixes ancient texts, jewelers' traditions and relatively recent business decisions. Knowing it helps you enjoy it without confusion and choose well, because not all calendar stones withstand daily use equally. In this guide you will see where the tradition comes from, what the modern list is month by month, what stones are found in the region, what alternatives exist for jewelry for daily use and how to buy them judiciously.

## Where does tradition come from?

The idea of associating twelve stones with a symbolic order is very old. The book of Exodus describes the breastplate of the high priest Aaron, adorned with twelve stones representing the twelve tribes of Israel, and the book of Revelation mentions twelve stones at the foundation of the heavenly Jerusalem. Centuries later, ancient authors related these stones to the months of the year and the signs of the zodiac.

The modern list, on the other hand, is much more recent: at the beginning of the twentieth century, an association of jewelers in the United States established a list of stones per month, which has since been updated several times by adding options. It is what most jewelry stores use today.

Alternatives have been added over time, in part to offer more affordable or more resistant options. We explain it in the guide on [hardness, toughness and stability] (/es/dureza-tenacidad-y-estabilidad/).

## The stones, month by month

- **January, garnet:** is usually imagined as red, but the garnet family also includes oranges, greens, and violets.
- **February, amethyst:** violet quartz, accessible and resistant, perfect for everyday jewelry.
- **March, aquamarine:** the soft blue of beryl, which evokes the sea and looks great in large, clean pieces.
- **April, diamond:** the hardest gem, with natural and laboratory options for different budgets.
- **May, emerald:** the most famous green, with its garden of inclusions and the need for special care.
- **June, Pearl:** The only organic gem on the main list; Alexandrite surprises with its color change.
- **Julio, ruby:** the red of corundum, very hard and very tenacious.
- **August, Peridot:** a luminous olive green; Spinel offers intense colors and great resistance.
- **September, sapphire:** blue par excellence, although it exists in almost all colors.
- **October, opal:** famous for its play of colors; Tourmaline offers a huge palette and more resistance.
- **November, topaz:** from gold to imperial; Citrine provides warm tones at a good price.
- **December, turquoise:** the opaque sky blue with a long tradition in America; zircon and tanzanite shine in intense blues.

## Combine stone with metal

Metal changes the look of a birthstone a lot. Warm colors, such as garnet, citrine, imperial topaz or peridot, look great in yellow gold. Cold ones, such as aquamarine, sapphire or tanzanite, stand out in white gold, silver or platinum. Emerald and ruby ​​combine with almost any metal. We explain it in the guide on [gold, silver and platinum](/es/oro-plata-y-platino/).

## Choose according to use

Some calendar stones are delicate for a ring worn every day. Some ideas:

- **Mayo, Emerald:** Best in a pendant, earrings or ring with protective setting and careful use.
- **June, pearl:** ideal for earrings and necklaces; If you prefer a ring for daily wear, alexandrite is much more resistant.
- **October, opal:** beautiful in pendants; Tourmaline is a stronger alternative for rings.
- **December, turquoise or tanzanite:** delicate for intense use; zircon or blue topaz resist better, with care.

We see this in the guide on [types of setting](/es/tipos-de-engaste/), where we explain how to protect the most fragile gems.

## Jewelry with stones for the whole family

A highly appreciated idea is to combine the birthstones of several people in a single piece: children in a ring or pendant for a mother or father, or the members of a couple in a shared piece of jewelry. When designing it:

- **Choose stones of similar size** so the piece looks balanced.
- **Please note the colors:** some combinations look better in a certain order.
- **Plan for family growth:** some designs allow stones to be added later.

A jeweler can help you design it, as we explain in the guide on [custom jewelry](/es/joyas-a-medida/).

## Other lists and variants

In addition to the modern list, there are previous traditional lists, lists based on the signs of the zodiac and lists specific to different countries and cultures, which do not always coincide. If a person has a favorite stone or a different family tradition, it is better to respect it than to follow the list to the letter.

## Beliefs and reality

Many stones are traditionally attributed protective or healing properties. They are part of the cultural allure of gemstones, but they have no scientific backing, and no stone is a substitute for medical advice or treatment. Enjoy tradition for what it is: a beautiful way to give meaning to a piece of jewelry.

## Buy wisely

- **Ask about treatments:** Blue topaz is usually irradiated, citrine is often heated amethyst, turquoise is usually stabilized, and tanzanite is almost always heated. We explain it in the guide on [gem treatments](/es/tratamientos-de-gemas/).
- **Distinguish natural, synthetic and imitation:** laboratory versions of many stones exist, and glass imitations sold as birthstones circulate.
- **Ask for a written description** of the stone and metal.
- **Choose quality over size:** a small stone with a good color looks better than a large, dull one.

## Frequent errors

- **Choose a delicate stone** for an unprotected everyday wear ring.
- **Believe that the list is very old** and immutable: the modern version is just over a century old.
- **Buy glass "birthstones"** at gem prices.
- **Ignore the usual treatments** on some calendar stones.
- **Impose the list** when the person has another favorite stone.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is my birthstone?

According to the modern list: January Garnet, February Amethyst, March Aquamarine, April Diamond, May Emerald, June Pearl, July Ruby, August Peridot, September Sapphire, October Opal, November Topaz and December Turquoise, with the alternatives indicated in the table.

### Why do some months have several stones?

Because the list has been updated over time, adding more affordable or more resistant options.

### Does it have to be the real stone?

It is not mandatory, but if it is sold as a natural gem it must be. Synthetic or imitation stones must be identified as such.

### What stone should I choose for an everyday ring if my stone is delicate?

Wear one of the more resistant alternatives of your month or wear your stone in a pendant or earrings.

## In summary

The birthstone tradition has ancient roots, like the twelve-stone breastplate described in the Bible, but the modern list was established in the early twentieth century and has been expanded. Many of its gems are found in Latin America, from Colombian emeralds to Uruguayan amethysts and Mexican opals. Some are delicate for daily use, so choose sturdy alternatives or protective settings. Ask about the treatments, distinguish between natural and synthetic and choose quality over size.
