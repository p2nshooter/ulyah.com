Quartz is the mineral that almost all of us have had in our hands without knowing it. It is in the sand on the beach, in the granite of a countertop and in many of the stones sold at fairs, mineral stores and jewelry stores. Precisely because it is so common, it is often underestimated. However, the quartz family brings together some of the most beautiful and versatile gems that exist, and Latin America, with Brazil, Uruguay, Bolivia, Mexico and Argentina at the head, is one of its great pantries.

In this guide you will see what all these stones have in common, which are the crystalline varieties and the fine-grained varieties, where many of those seen in the region come from, which colors are usually natural and which are the result of treatments, and how to choose each one judiciously.

## A single mineral, two great families

Chemically, all quartz is **silicon dioxide**. What changes is the way its crystals are organized and the small impurities or defects that give it color. That is why it is convenient to separate two groups:

- **Macrocrystalline quartz:** crystals visible to the naked eye, transparent or translucent, shaped like a six-sided prism ending in a point. Here are rock crystal, amethyst, citrine, smoky quartz, ametrine, rose quartz and prasiolite.
- **Microcrystalline quartz or chalcedony:** crystals so tiny that they can only be seen under a microscope. It forms translucent masses, often with bands or patterns. Here are agate, carnelian, onyx, chrysoprase and many other varieties. Jasper, opaque and loaded with other minerals, is usually studied together with them.

Both families share a hardness of around 7 on the Mohs scale, making them suitable for almost any jewelry. Chalcedony is also very tenacious: it resists shocks well, which is why it has been carved for millennia into seals, beads and cameos.

## The crystalline varieties

### rock crystal

It is colorless and transparent quartz. It is used in spheres, faceted points, beads and decorative pieces, and as an inexpensive faceted stone. The large, clean crystals, with shiny faces, are prized by collectors. Brazil, with Minas Gerais and Bahia, is a historic source of large rock crystal.

### Amethyst

The violet variety, the most valued of crystalline quartz. Its color is due to small amounts of iron combined with natural radiation from the rocks. In the region, the amethyst geodes from northern Uruguay and southern Brazil are famous throughout the world. We dedicate our own guide to them: [amethyst geodes from Uruguay and Brazil] (/es/geodas-de-amatista-de-uruguay-y-brasil/).

### Citrine

Yellow, golden or orange quartz. Here appears the first major confusion in the market: **natural citrine is relatively scarce**, and much of the citrine sold comes from **heated amethyst**. Heat transforms violet into yellow, orange or reddish brown tones. Some clues that usually indicate this origin:

- intense orange or reddish tones, more than the light and soft yellow of natural citrine;
- in geode points, the base is whitish and the color is concentrated only at the end;
- large quantities of identical parts at very low prices.

Treated citrine is not a fake stone: it is still quartz. But it should be sold as such, and its price should reflect that.

### Ametrine

A crystal that combines areas of **amethyst and citrine**, with a border that is sometimes sharp and sometimes diffuse. The main natural commercial source is the **Anahí mine, in eastern Bolivia**, near the border with Brazil, and that is why ametrine is so associated with that country. Carvers usually cut it into bars or rectangles that show the two colors side by side, or in carvings that mix them in flashes.

It is worth knowing that there is also industrially produced **synthetic ametrine** and that some amethysts can be partially heated to imitate the effect. If a piece is sold because of its Bolivian origin, it is reasonable to ask about its provenance and, if the price justifies it, ask for an analysis.

### smoky quartz

Brown, gray or almost black tones (in the latter case it is called morion). Its color comes from the natural radiation that acts on the aluminum present in the glass. It is also produced artificially by irradiating rock crystal. The result is stable under normal conditions, but intense heat may clarify it.

### rose quartz

It almost always occurs in translucent masses, not well-formed crystals, which is why it is mostly seen in cabochons, spheres, beads, and carvings. True pink crystals exist, but they are rare and highly sought after by collectors. Brazil is one of its main sources. Some pieces may pale with prolonged exposure to strong sunlight.

### Prasiolite

Light green quartz that, for the most part, is obtained **by heating certain amethysts**. It is sometimes sold as "green amethyst," a misleading name because amethyst is, by definition, violet. It should be called prasiolite or green quartz.

### Quartz with inclusions

Some quartz are valued precisely for what they contain:

- **rutilated quartz**, with golden or reddish rutile needles; Bahia, in Brazil, is a recognized source;
- **tourmaline quartz**, with black tourmaline needles;
- **ghost quartz**, which show the silhouette of previous stages of growth;
- **quartz with liquid inclusions**, which contain small mobile bubbles.

These stones are usually cut in cabochon or free shapes to show off the inclusions.

## Chalcedonies and agates

### agate

Agate is a chalcedony with **concentric bands** that form when cavities in volcanic rocks are slowly filled. His drawings are unique in each piece. The region has several famous agates:

- the ** agates from Rio Grande do Sul and Uruguay **, with gray, white and brown bands, which are cut into sheets, bowls and decorative objects;
- the **Laguna agate** from northern Mexico, known for its fine bands and intense colors, and other Mexican agates with very intricate patterns;
- the **Condor agate** from Mendoza, in Argentina, with bright and contrasting colors.

A practical warning: many agates sold in colored sheets **electric blue, fuchsia pink, green or purple** are **dyed**. Chalcedony is porous and absorbs dyes well. There is nothing wrong with buying them as decorative objects, as long as you know what they are.

### Carnelian, onyx and others

- **Carnelian:** orange to red chalcedony. Much commercial carnelian is heated or dyed chalcedony.
- **Onyx:** chalcedony with straight bands in black and white, or completely black (almost always dyed). Be careful with the so-called **"Mexican onyx"**: in reality it is usually a banded carbonate, such as calcite or aragonite, much softer than chalcedony. It is beautiful for decorative objects, but does not have the hardness of quartz.
- **Chrysoprase:** apple green chalcedony colored by nickel, one of the most sought after in the family.
- **Jasper:** opaque, of many colors, loaded with other minerals. It is used in cabochons, mosaics and carvings.

## Treatments and synthetics: what to ask

Quartz is cheap to process and manufacture, so the market is full of modified parts. The most common practices are:

| Practice | on what stones | What is good to know |
|---|---|---|
| Warm-up | amethyst converted into citrine or prasiolite | stable; must be declared |
| Irradiation | rock crystal turned into smoky quartz | stable in normal use |
| Dyed | agates, onyx, carnelian, quartzite | may fade with solvents or over the years |
| synthetic quartz | citrine, amethyst, ametrine, rock crystal | It is manufactured in an autoclave; a laboratory can detect it |
| Coatings | "aura" quartz with metallic reflections | surface layer that can wear away |

The [gem treatments](/es/tratamientos-de-gemas/) guide explains each technique, and the [synthetic and laboratory gems](/es/gemas-sinteticas-y-de-laboratorio/) guide explains how they are made and recognized.

## How to buy quartz judiciously

1. **Be wary of impossible colors.** Electric blues in agate, fluorescent pinks or bright, uniform greens usually indicate dye.
2. **Ask about the origin of the color.** When faced with an intense citrine, ask if it is natural or heated amethyst. A serious seller responds without problem.
3. **Compare price to rarity.** A large, clean ametrine at the price of common quartz deserves questions.
4. **Look against the light.** In crystalline quartz, the areas of color, natural inclusions and fractures say a lot about the piece.
5. **For decorative objects, ask the species.** "Onyx" can be chalcedony or calcite; They are both beautiful, but they are cared for in different ways.
6. **Ask for invoice with description.** Variety, known treatment and, if relevant, origin.

## Care

Crystal quartz and chalcedony are hardy, but some varieties beg for attention:

- **amethyst** and **rose quartz** can lose intensity with prolonged exposure to the sun;
- **smoky quartz** and **amethyst** may discolor with high heat;
- **dyed agates** can lose color with alcohol, acetone or aggressive products;
- **"Mexican onyx"** and other carbonates are damaged by acids, including lemon juice and vinegar.

To clean, warm water, mild soap and a soft cloth or brush are enough. You will find more details in the [jewelry maintenance and cleaning] guide (/es/mantenimiento-y-limpieza-de-joyas/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is quartz worth little because it is common?

Commercial pieces are inexpensive, but rare crystals, large, well-formed geodes, clean ametrines, or uniquely patterned agates can command high prices among collectors.

### How do I know if a crystal is quartz or glass?

Quartz has a hexagonal shape with horizontal striations on its faces, it feels cold to the touch for longer and does not show the round bubbles typical of glass. A gemologist confirms this in minutes with a polariscope or refractometer.

### Can I collect agates myself?

It depends on the place. In many countries, collecting on public lands, reserves or private property is regulated or prohibited. Our guide to [collecting gems and minerals](/es/coleccionar-gemas-y-minerales/) explains how to do it legally.

## In summary

Quartz brings together two families: crystalline varieties, such as rock crystal, amethyst, citrine, ametrine, smoky, pink and prasiolite, and chalcedonies, such as agate, carnelian, onyx and chrysoprase. Latin America provides some of the best known in the world, from the Bolivian ametrine to the agates of Uruguay, Brazil, Mexico and Argentina. To buy well, you have to know that a good part of the citrine and prasiolite comes from heated amethyst, that many colored agates are dyed, that "Mexican onyx" is usually a carbonate and that synthetic quartz is common. Asking, looking against the light and requesting a detailed bill is enough to enjoy this family without surprises.
