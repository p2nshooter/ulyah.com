Few stones are as linked to a country as the emerald is to Colombia. For centuries, the mountains of Boyacá and Cundinamarca have produced some of the most prized emeralds in the world, an intense and luminous green that merchants recognize at first sight. They traveled on galleons to Seville, adorned jewels of Mughal emperors in India and European crowns, and today they continue to arrive every day on the streets of downtown Bogotá, where they are negotiated on white papers with a magnifying glass in hand.

Behind each Colombian emerald there is exceptional geology, a generations-old mining trade and a complex commercial chain. Knowing it helps you better appreciate these gems and buy them with more discretion. In this guide you will see where they come from, why they are different from those in other countries, what their inclusions reveal, what the famous mills are, how their quality is judged, why almost all of them contain oil and what precautions to take when buying.

## a little history

The Muisca peoples were already extracting emeralds in the Chivor area before the arrival of the Spanish, and used them as offerings and in ceremonial objects. In the sixteenth century, the conquerors found the deposits and, after years of confrontations with the people of the Muzo region, organized their exploitation. Colombian emeralds became a global commodity: many ended up in the courts of Europe, the Ottoman Empire, and Mughal India, where some of the most famous historical emeralds were cut and engraved.

## Where are the mines

Production is concentrated in two strips of the Eastern mountain range:

| Area | Department | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| muzo | Boyacá | the most famous name; intense and warm green emeralds |
| Coscuez | Boyacá | part of the western belt, next to Muzo |
| Peñas Blancas and other nearby mines | Boyacá | also from the western belt |
| Chivor | Boyacá | eastern belt, exploited since pre-Hispanic times |
| Gachala | Cundinamarca | eastern belt, known for some large historic crystals |

Mine names are often used as a trade reference, but color and quality vary greatly within each mine.

## An exceptional training

In most deposits in the world, emerald is formed where granitic magmas rich in beryllium come into contact with rocks that provide chromium. The Colombian emeralds followed another path: they were formed from very saline hydrothermal fluids that circulated through fractures in black slates, without the intervention of magmas. These fluids dissolved the beryllium, chromium and vanadium contained in the sedimentary rocks themselves and deposited them in veins of calcite, albite and pyrite, where the crystals grew.

This origin explains two highly appreciated features:

- **Color:** chromium and vanadium, with very little iron, produce a pure and vivid green, without dark or yellowish tones.
- **Characteristic inclusions** that help laboratories recognize the Colombian origin.

## What the interior reveals: the garden and the three phases

Almost all emeralds have inclusions visible with a magnifying glass, and many with the naked eye. In the trade they talk about the emerald garden, due to the vegetal appearance of those small internal marks. In Colombian ones, the inclusions of three phases stand out: tiny cavities that contain a saline liquid, a gas bubble and a small salt crystal, trapped during growth.

A completely clean emerald is extraordinarily rare. That is why the market accepts inclusions much more than in other gems, as long as they do not affect the transparency or resistance of the stone too much.

## The trapiche emeralds

One of Colombia's most fascinating rarities are the Trapiche emeralds, which show six dark arms radiating from the center, like the spokes of a wheel. Its name comes from the sugar cane mills. The arms are formed by carbonaceous material and minerals from the host rock, incorporated during a very particular growth of the crystal. They are usually carved in cabochon or in sheets to show the drawing, and are highly sought after pieces by collectors.

## From mine to market

The journey of a Colombian emerald usually passes through several hands:

1. **Extraction:** In tunnels and galleries, miners follow veins of calcite in the black rock. There are corporate farms and other smaller ones.
2. **First selection:** The material is separated by quality in the mining area itself.
3. **Guaqueros:** In mining areas, independent prospectors review discarded earth and stones in search of escaped emeralds.
4. **Intermediaries:** they buy in the region and take the production to the shopping centers.
5. **Bogotá:** in the center of the city, around Jiménez Avenue, merchants, carvers and specialized laboratories work.
6. **Carving and export:** many stones are cut in Colombia and others go raw to large international centers.

## What determines quality

| Factor | What to watch |
|---|---|
| Color | is the main factor: a deep, saturated green, neither too light nor too dark, often with a slight bluish tint |
| Transparency | the stone should have life and light, not a milky or dull appearance |
| Inclusions | They are accepted, but they must not cloud the stone or threaten its resistance |
| Size | good proportions, symmetry and a polish that makes the color shine |
| Weight | large, good quality pieces are much rarer |
| Treatment | The type and amount of filling greatly influence the value |

## Oil and other fillers

Since emeralds have fissures that reach the surface, for centuries they have been filled with oils to disguise them. Traditionally cedar oil was used; Today resins are also used. The treatment improves the appearance, but you have to know:

- **It is very common:** the vast majority of emeralds on the market have some degree of filler.
- **Must be declared:** A serious seller reports the treatment, and laboratories classify it according to its intensity, from negligible to important.
- **Influences value:** An emerald with little or no filling is worth much more than a similar, highly treated one.
- **Not always permanent:** Oils can dry out or come off with time, heat, or harsh cleaners.

More information in the guide on [gem treatments](/es/tratamientos-de-gemas/).

## Emerald and cabochon cut

The so-called emerald cut, rectangular, with stepped facets and cut corners, was largely born for this stone: it protects the corners, which are fragile, retains weight and shows the color well. The most included material is cut into cabochons, a rounded shape without facets, or into beads. We explain it in the guide on [emerald cutting](/es/la-talla-de-esmeraldas/).

## Buy wisely

Bogotá is a fascinating place to see emeralds, but also a market where experience is needed.

- **Be wary of bargains:** an emerald of good color and large size is never cheap.
- **Get to know the imitations:** green glass, synthetic emeralds and doublets, pieces made up of two parts glued together with a green layer.
- **Request a lab report** for important parts, from a recognized laboratory. We see it in the guide on [gemological laboratory reports](/es/informes-de-laboratorio-gemologico/).
- **Buy from established businesses,** with invoice and return policy.
- **Learn about exporting:** Taking gems out of a country may require documentation; consult current regulations.

If you are interested in uncut material, the guide on [buying rough gems without deception](/es/comprar-gemas-en-bruto-sin-enganos/) expands on these tips.

## Care of an emerald

- **Avoid ultrasonic and steam cleaners,** which can remove oil or enlarge cracks.
- **Do not expose it to strong heat** or sudden changes in temperature.
- **Clean it with warm water and mild soap,** with a soft brush.
- **Take it off for manual work,** sports or housework.
- **Consult a jeweler** if the stone loses shine: sometimes it can be re-treated, always stating it.

## A story with lights and shadows

Emerald mining has generated wealth, but also conflicts. During the 1980s, disputes between groups that controlled the mining areas of Boyacá led to a period of violence known as the green war, which ended with a peace agreement in the early 1990s. Since then, the region has made progress in formalizing mining and improving working conditions, although challenges remain. Asking about the origin of a stone and preferring transparent merchants is part of responsible consumption, as we explain in the guide on [responsible gold and traceability] (/es/oro-responsable-y-trazabilidad/).

## Frequent errors

- **Look for an emerald without inclusions,** which almost does not exist in nature.
- **Do not ask about the oil** or the degree of filling.
- **Clean it with ultrasound** or steam.
- **Buy an important part without a laboratory report**.
- **Paying a premium for a mine name** without proof of origin.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why are Colombian emeralds so famous?

For its pure and intense green color, due to chromium and vanadium with little iron, for the long history of its mines and for the quality of some of its stones, which have set the market benchmark for centuries.

### Do all emeralds have oil?

The vast majority have some filling. Those that do not have it, or have it to a negligible degree, are rare and highly valued. A laboratory report indicates the degree of treatment.

### Can you tell if an emerald is Colombian at first glance?

Not for sure. The three-phase inclusions and the chemistry of the stone help, but the origin can only be suggested by a specialized laboratory after analysis.

### What is a trapiche emerald?

An emerald with a drawing of six dark arms that start from the center, characteristic of some Colombian deposits. They are collector's items.

## In summary

Colombian emeralds come mainly from the mines of Muzo, Coscuez, Chivor and Gachalá, and were formed in an exceptional way: from saline hydrothermal fluids in black slates, without magmas. That is why they have a pure, vivid green and characteristic inclusions, such as those of three phases. Its quality depends on the color, transparency, inclusions, size, weight and the degree of filling with oils or resins, which almost all have and must be declared. Buy with a lab report for important pieces, from established businesses, and take care of the stone away from ultrasound, steam and heat.
