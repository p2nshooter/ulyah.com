Two gems of the same color, the same weight and the same purity can look completely different. One seems lit from within, full of sparkles; the other looks dull, with a washed central area through which the background shows through or with dark spots that do not go away when moved. The difference is almost always in the cut, that is, in how the facets guide light into the stone.

Understanding this play of light does not require knowing advanced physics, but it does require some concepts that change the way you look at a gem. In this guide you will see what happens to light when it enters a stone, what the refractive index and critical angle are, what brilliance, fire and scintillation mean, why windows and extinction appear, what the bow tie effect is and how to evaluate a gem in a few minutes with the right lighting conditions.

## What happens to light inside a gem

When light enters a transparent gem, three things happen:

1. **It refracts:** it changes direction when going from the air to the stone, because it travels more slowly inside it.
2. **Reflects on interior facets:** If it hits a facet with a steep enough angle, it bounces back instead of coming out. It is total internal reflection.
3. **Comes out again:** If the carving is good, the light bounces off the pavilion and comes out through the crown, towards the eye of the viewer.

The limiting angle beyond which light bounces instead of escaping is called the critical angle. The higher the refractive index of the gem, the smaller that angle is and the easier it is to trap light inside and return it.

## The refractive index

| Gem | Approximate refractive index | Approximate dispersion |
|---|---|---|
| Moissanite | 2.65 to 2.69 | very high |
| Diamond | 2,42 | high |
| cubic zirconia | about 2.15 | high |
| Garnets | 1.74 to 1.89, depending on the species | from low to high |
| Corundum: ruby and sapphire | about 1.76 to 1.77 | low |
| Spinel | about 1.72 | low |
| Topaz | about 1.61 to 1.63 | low |
| Tourmaline | about 1.62 to 1.64 | low |
| Beryl: emerald and aquamarine | about 1.57 to 1.58 | low |
| Quartz | about 1.54 to 1.55 | very low |

A high index allows for brighter cuts; a high dispersion produces more flashes of color. That is why diamond, moissanite or demantoid, a high dispersion green garnet, show so much fire, while an aquamarine shines with a more serene light.

## Brightness, fire and twinkle

| Effect | What is it | What does it depend on? |
|---|---|---|
| Brightness | the amount of white light that the gem returns | refractive index, proportions and polishing |
| Fire | the colorful flashes of the rainbow | gem scatter and crown design |
| Twinkle | the play of flashes and shadows when the stone, the light or whoever looks moves | number and arrangement of facets, and contrast |

A great size balances all three. A high crown and large facets favor fire; a wide table favors brightness; a good distribution of facets, scintillation.

## The windows

A window is an area, usually in the center of the stone, through which you can see through it as if it were glass. It appears when the pavilion is too flat: the light entering through the table reaches the lower facets at an angle that does not allow total internal reflection and escapes from below.

- **It is common in low index gems** such as quartz or beryl, cut flat so that they appear larger from the front.
- **Lightens the color** in that area and takes life away from the stone.
- **How ​​to spot it:** Place the gem face-on on printed text or lined paper. If you can read or see the lines through the center, there is a window.

## The extinction

Extinction is the dark areas that appear on a gem seen from the front. It has several causes:

- **Canopy too deep:** Light bounces at angles that send it out the sides instead of the crown.
- **The shadow of the viewer:** The viewer's head blocks part of the light that reaches the stone and is reflected inside, which produces dark areas normal in any gem.
- **Very dark gems:** A very dark stone can look almost black in large areas with low light.

A little distributed darkness is normal and necessary, because it creates contrast. What detracts from value is excessive or concentrated extinction.

## The bow tie effect

In elongated cuts, such as oval, pear or marquise, it is common to see a dark area in the shape of a bow tie or bowtie that crosses the center of the stone. It is due to the elongated shape, which means that the central facets do not reflect light well. Almost all elongated stones have some bow tie; The important thing is that it is light and does not dominate the stone. Look at her moving it: a marked bow tie that does not disappear with movement makes it less attractive.

## Contrast: the necessary balance

A gem without any dark areas would not appear brighter, but rather flat. The eye perceives brightness thanks to the contrast between light and dark areas that move. That's why carvers look for a balanced contrast pattern, with small, well-distributed dark areas that change as the stone moves.

## Proportions and angles

The way the size manages light depends above all on:

- **The angle of the pavilion:** neither too flat, which produces windows, nor too deep, which produces extinction.
- **The angle and height of the crown:** influence the fire and how light enters.
- **The size of the table:** a large table gives shine, but reduces the fire; a small one, the opposite.
- **The girdle:** too thick adds weight that does not add beauty.
- **Symmetry and polishing:** small deviations scatter light.

Diamond laboratories measure these proportions with optical instruments and take them into account in their cut classification, as we explain in the guide on [diamonds: essential guide](/es/diamantes-guia-esencial/).

## In the colored gems

In colored gems, the cut also controls the intensity of the color:

- **A pale stone** gains color with a somewhat deeper pavilion, because the light travels further inside it.
- **A very dark stone** is lightened with a flatter cut or with more facets that return light.
- **Windows** dilute the color in the center, and extinction turns it black.

That is why the cutter adjusts the proportions to each stone, as we explain in the guide on [from rough to gem: orientation and performance](/es/del-bruto-a-la-gema-orientacion-y-rendimiento/).

## The light with which you look

- **Jewelry store spotlights** make any gem shine and hide windows and extinction.
- **Diffused natural light,** next to a window without direct sun, is the most honest to judge.
- **A warm light lamp** shows what the stone will look like at night indoors.

Always look at the stone in various conditions before deciding.

## How to evaluate a gem in a few minutes

1. **Look at it from the front in diffused natural light:** Does it look alive or dull?
2. **Do the text test:** Is the background transparent in the center?
3. **Move it slowly:** Do the dark areas change and spread out, or do they stay fixed?
4. **In elongated sizes, look for the bow tie:** Is it slight or does the stone dominate?
5. **Compare it with other stones** of the same species and size.
6. **Check symmetry and polish** with a magnifying glass.

## Can it be corrected?

A stone with windows or excessive extinction can improve with recarving, but weight is always lost, and sometimes the improvement requires reducing the size considerably. Before deciding, consult an experienced carver.

## Frequent errors

- **Judge a stone only under the lights** of the store.
- **Choose a stone based on its front size** without checking to see if it has a large window.
- **Reject all stone with dark areas:** a balanced contrast is desirable.
- **Ignore the bow tie** in ovals, pears and marquises.
- **Compare stones of different species** without taking into account their refractive index.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does my gem look dull at home and shiny in the store?

Because jewelry store spotlights concentrate a lot of light and enhance the shine. At home, with diffused light, size defects are better seen.

### Can all gems shine like a diamond?

No. The brightness depends largely on the refractive index and dispersion of each species. A good cut brings out the best in each gem, but does not turn it into another.

### What is better, a large or small table?

It depends on the desired effect: the large table favors brightness and the small one favors fire. The important thing is the balance with the rest of the proportions.

### Is the bow tie effect a defect?

A slight bow tie is normal in elongated sizes. It is only a defect when it is very marked and does not disappear when the stone is moved.

## In summary

The light that enters a gem is refracted, bounces off the interior facets and, if the cut is good, exits through the crown towards the eye. The refractive index and dispersion of each species determine its potential for brilliance and fire, and the size decides how much of that potential is used. A pavilion that is too flat creates windows; one too deep, extinction; and elongated sizes can show a bow tie effect. Judge gems in diffused natural light, do the text test, move them around to see the contrast and always compare with similar stones.
