Pearls are the only gems that are born inside a living being and come out of the water ready to shine, without the need for cutting. For centuries they were the supreme luxury: rarer than diamonds, reserved for kings and nobles, sought after by divers who risked their lives at the bottom of the sea. Latin America occupies a central place in that history, because some of the most famous pearls in the world came from its shores.

Today almost all the pearls sold are cultured, and there are very different types in size, color, shape and price. Understanding how they are formed, what differentiates them and how they are valued helps to choose well and take care of them, because they are also delicate gems. In this guide you will see how a pearl is born, what distinguishes a natural one from a cultured one, what role America had in its history, what are the main types of pearls, how their quality is judged and what care they need.

## How a pearl is formed

When an irritant, such as a small parasite or a piece of tissue, becomes trapped inside a mollusk, it covers it with layers of nacre, the same shiny material that lines the inside of many shells. Nacre is made up of tiny calcium carbonate plates held together by an organic substance, arranged in very thin layers. The way those layers reflect and break up light produces the pearl's characteristic shine, called luster, and sometimes superimposed reflections of color.

## Natural and cultivated

- **Natural pearl:** It is formed without any human intervention. Today it is very scarce and reaches very high prices, especially for old pieces with documented history.
- **Cultured pearl:** It is formed with human help. In saltwater pearls, a nucleus, usually a shell sphere, is introduced into the mollusk, along with a piece of tissue from another mollusk; In many freshwater pearls only tissue is introduced. The mollusk does the rest, covering the nucleus with nacre for months or years.

At the beginning of the twentieth century, cultivation techniques were perfected in Japan that transformed the market. A cultured pearl is a real pearl, formed from a mollusk, but its origin must be declared. Laboratories distinguish natural ones from cultivated ones mainly through x-rays, which show the internal structure.

## an american story

A few years after the arrival of the Europeans, the islands of Cubagua and Margarita, off the coast of present-day Venezuela, became one of the world's great sources of pearls. Hundreds of indigenous and enslaved African divers extracted oysters under harsh conditions, and within a few decades the oyster beds were depleted. In the Gulf of Panama, the Pearl Archipelago produced legendary pieces, such as La Peregrina, which belonged to the Spanish crown for centuries.

In the Gulf of California, in Mexico, fishing for natural pearls around La Paz was an important activity for centuries, until the banks were depleted in the first half of the twentieth century. Today, on the coast of Sonora, pearls are grown with species of oysters native to the Sea of ​​Cortez, with very particular colors and reflections.

## The large types of cultured pearls

| Type | Mollusk and main origin | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Akoya | pearl oyster from Japan and China | round, small to medium in size, white or cream with pink or silver reflections; very intense luster |
| From the South Seas | large golden or silver lip oyster, in Australia, Indonesia and the Philippines | large, white, silver or gold, thick mother-of-pearl |
| From Tahiti | black lip oyster, French Polynesia | natural dark colors: gray, green, eggplant, blue |
| fresh water | freshwater mussels, especially in China | great variety of shapes, sizes and colors; very affordable |
| Keshi | by-product of cultivation, without core | irregular shapes and very vivid luster |
| Mabe | pearl that grows attached to the shell | hemispherical shape, used in earrings and rings |

## The value factors

| factor | What to watch |
|---|---|
| Luster | sharp and bright reflections, like a mirror; is the most important factor |
| Surface | few marks, stains or bumps |
| Shape | the round ones are the rarest; baroque ones have their own charm |
| Color | the background color, the superimposed reflections and, in some, the orient or iridescence |
| Size | With quality being equal, the larger ones are more valuable |
| Mother of pearl quality | a thick mother-of-pearl gives more shine and durability |
| Harmony | in necklaces and pairs, that the pearls match in size, shape, color and luster |

## Luster: the first thing to look at

A pearl with good luster reflects light clearly: if you look at the reflection of a lamp or your face, it looks defined. Pearls of poor luster look dull, like chalk. Observe the pearls in natural light or diffused white light, on a neutral background, and compare them to each other: luster is what most differentiates an excellent pearl from a mediocre one.

## Shapes: from round to baroque

Pearls are classified by their shape as round, almost round, oval, button, drop, semi-baroque and baroque. The perfect round ones are the rarest and most expensive, but the baroque ones, with irregular shapes, have gained much appreciation in contemporary jewelry for their unique character.

## Treatments

- **Bleached:** to uniform the color of white pearls.
- **Dyed:** to obtain dark or fantasy colors.
- **Irradiation:** to darken some freshwater pearls.
- **Polishing and coatings:** to improve surface gloss.

As with any gem, they must be declared. We see it in the guide on [gem treatments](/es/tratamientos-de-gemas/).

## Imitations and how to suspect

The most common imitations are glass, plastic or shell beads coated with a pearlescent lacquer. Some clues:

- **Too perfect surface** and uniform on all beads.
- **Weight:** The plastic ones are very light.
- **Holes:** On imitations, the outer layer may peel off around the hole.
- **Rubbing Test:** Gently rubbing a pearl against the edge of your teeth gives a slightly gritty feel to real pearls and a smooth feel to many imitations. It is only indicative.

Confirmation is given by a gemologist or a laboratory. More information in the guide on [imitations and doublets](/es/imitaciones-y-dobletes/).

Good necklaces have a knot between pearls: it prevents them from rubbing together and, if the thread breaks, from losing all of them. Also check the quality of the closure, which must be secure.

## How to buy pearls

1. **Ask the type of pearl and its origin:** akoya, from the South Seas, Tahiti or freshwater, and if it is natural or cultured.
2. **Ask about treatments,** especially in dark or very uniform colors.
3. **Roll the necklace** slowly on a flat surface: slightly round pearls give themselves away because they rotate irregularly.
4. **Look at the holes:** They should be centered and clean, with no flaking mother-of-pearl around.
5. **Compare the luster** of several pieces under the same light.
6. **In akoya,** ask about the thickness of the mother-of-pearl: a very fine mother-of-pearl loses shine and can wear out.
7. **Request in writing** the description of the piece on the invoice.

## Pearl care

- **The last thing when dressing and the first thing when taking off:** perfumes, hairsprays, creams and makeup damage the mother-of-pearl.
- **Wipe them with a soft cloth** after use to remove sweat and grease.
- **No ultrasound, steam, detergents or acidic products.**
- **Store them in a soft cloth bag,** separate from other jewelry that may scratch them, and not in airtight plastic bags for a long time.
- **Store the necklaces horizontally,** not hanging, so the thread does not stretch.
- **Restring collars from time to time,** depending on use; A worn or dirty thread breaks easily.

## Frequent errors

- **Choose pearls without looking at the luster** under good light.
- **Spray perfume** with the necklace on.
- **Store necklaces hanging** or in closed plastic bags.
- **Do not ask if they are dyed** or treated.
- **Use a knotless necklace** for value pearls.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are cultured pearls fake?

No. They are real pearls formed by a mollusk, with human help to start the process. The fake ones are glass or plastic imitations.

### Which pearls are the most expensive?

Natural pearls with documented history are the most valuable. Among the cultivated ones, the large ones from the South Seas and Tahiti, and the highest quality akoya, are usually the most expensive.

### How do I know if a pearl is natural or cultured?

Only a laboratory can confirm it for sure, especially through x-rays that show its internal structure.

### Can they get wet?

Brief contact with clean water does not harm them, but it is advisable to avoid water with chlorine, salt or soaps and dry them well.

## In summary

Pearls are born when a mollusk covers an irritant with layers of nacre. The natural ones are very scarce today; Almost all of those on the market are cultured, real pearls started with human help. America played a prominent role in its history, from Cubagua and Margarita to the Gulf of Panama and the Gulf of California. The main cultured pearls are akoya, South Sea pearls, Tahitian pearls and freshwater pearls. Its value depends above all on the luster, and also on the surface, shape, color, size, mother-of-pearl and harmony. Keep them away from perfumes and chemicals and re-string the necklaces from time to time.
