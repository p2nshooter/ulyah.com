Beads are probably the oldest form of jewelry. Long before rings and settings, humans drilled shells, teeth, seeds and stones to hang around their necks or wrists. In America, the Andean people appreciated *Spondylus* shell beads, brought from the warm coasts of the Pacific, and the Mayans and Olmecs carved jade beads that accompanied their rulers. Today, stone beads continue to fill workshops, fairs and stores, and the polished spheres have become collector's and decorative objects.

In this guide you will see how stone beads and spheres are made, how they are drilled and polished, what threading methods exist, what American traditions are still alive, and how to recognize a quality bead versus one that is dyed, reconstituted, or made of glass.

## From stone to account

### 1. Cutting the material

It all starts with a block or a boulder. With a diamond saw it is cut into **sheets** and then into **cubes** or prisms of the approximate size of the bead. At this stage, areas with fractures are discarded and the best color is chosen.

### 2. The rounded

To obtain round beads, the cubes go through:

- **bead mills**, machines with two abrasive discs or plates that roll the pieces until they become spheres;
- **tumbled**, in rotating barrels with abrasives, which smoothes the edges of irregular or free-shaped beads;
- **manual work** on a wheel, for carved, faceted or special shaped beads.

Faceted beads are carved on special machines that cut small facets across the entire surface.

### 3. Drilling

It is the most delicate stage. **Hollow drill bits or diamond needles** are used, with water to cool and not breathe dust. The usual practice is to drill **from both sides** until the holes meet in the center, thus preventing the stone from splintering when the drill bit comes out. There are also **ultrasonic drilling** machines for mass production.

A good hole is straight, centered and without chips on the edges. A poorly made hole cuts the thread over time.

### 4. The polishing

The beads are almost always tumble polished, for hours or days, with increasingly finer abrasives and a final polishing powder. Large or carved pieces are polished by hand. The guide on [gem polishing](/es/pulido-de-gemas/) explains which abrasives are suitable for each stone.

### 5. Calibration

The beads are classified by **diameter in millimeters**, by color and by quality. A well-made necklace uses beads of the same size or regular graduation, and of the same shade or with an intentional transition.

## The stone spheres

The polished stone spheres, measuring a few centimeters to several decimeters, are very popular collection and decoration pieces. They are manufactured:

1. cutting a **cube** from the block;
2. eliminating the corners until obtaining an approximate **polyhedron**;
3. rounding it on a **sphere machine**, with three or more rotating cups that wear the stone evenly;
4. tuning and polishing with increasingly finer abrasives in the same cups.

A good dial is perfectly round, with no flat spots, uniform polishing, and no open fractures. Many spheres of agate, amethyst, quartz, onyx, obsidian and calcite are seen in the region. The guide on [Latin American quartz](/es/cuarzos-de-america-latina/) explains these stones.

## American beading traditions

- **Spondylus in the Andes:** The red and orange shell of the *Spondylus* was worked into beads and ornaments of enormous ritual value, and circulated along long exchange routes.
- **Jade in Mesoamerica:** Jade beads, earmuffs, and masks, actually jadeite from Guatemala, were among the most precious objects of the Mayan and Olmec cultures.
- **Chaquira:** tiny glass beads, called chaquiras in many countries, are the basis of living artisan traditions, such as Wixárika art in Mexico or the necklaces of the Embera peoples in Colombia and Panama.
- **Local ornamental stones:** many workshops in the region work with lapis lazuli, rhodochrosite, chrysocolla, turquoise, onyx or obsidian beads, as we tell in the guide about [ornamental stone carvings] (/es/tallas-en-piedras-ornamentales/).

## The threading

### Knotted silk or nylon thread

It is the traditional method for pearls and fine beads. Between account and account a **knot** is made, which:

- prevents beads from rubbing against each other and wearing out;
- It prevents all the beads from being lost at the same time if the thread breaks;
- gives the necklace a flexible and elegant drape.

Knotted necklaces should be **renewed** from time to time, especially if they are worn frequently, because the thread stretches and gets dirty. The guide on [natural and cultured pearls](/es/perlas-naturales-y-cultivadas/) explains how to care for pearl necklaces.

### coated cable

A very resistant, braided and nylon-coated steel cable that is closed with **snaps** or flattened tubes. Ideal for heavy beads or beads with rough holes.

### elastic thread

Widely used in bracelets. It is practical, but it stretches and degrades with the sun, sweat and cosmetic products. It is advisable to renew it and not stretch the bracelet when putting it on.

### Wire and links

The beads can be assembled with wire bent into links, forming rosaries and bead chains. It is laborious work, but very resistant.

For clasps and lengths, see the guide on [earrings, necklaces and bracelets](/es/aretes-collares-y-pulseras/).

## Design a bead necklace or bracelet

A beautiful string does not depend only on the quality of each bead, but on how they are combined:

- **The size:** 6-8mm beads are the most versatile; those of 10 mm or more give presence, but are heavy. In a long necklace, a slight graduation in size, larger in the center, improves the fall.
- **The rhythm:** alternating stone beads with small metal or other stone separators gives order to the design and protects the beads from rubbing.
- **The total weight:** dense stones, such as hematite or some agates, make a long necklace heavy; It is advisable to try it on before finishing it.
- **The closure:** must be in proportion to the beads and resist their weight.
- **The length:** think about the neckline and the use, as we explain in the guide on [how to wear jewelry judiciously](/es/como-llevar-joyas-con-criterio/).

## How to recognize a quality account

| Trait | Quality | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | round and uniform | oval beads or beads with flat areas |
| Hole | straight, centered, without splinters | broken edges or skewed holes |
| Polished | shiny on the entire surface | matte or streaked areas |
| Color | natural, with logical variations | identical color in all, accumulated in the hole or in the cracks |
| Weight | the one that corresponds to the stone | too light for its size |
| Temperature | cold to the touch | warm like plastic |

## Dyes, reconstituted and imitations

The world of beads is full of treated or substituted materials:

- **Dyed beads:** Agates, chalcedonies, quartzites, howlite and magnesite are easily dyed. A typical sign is color concentrated around the hole or in the cracks.
- **Imitation "Jade":** Dyed quartzite, serpentine, glass or resin are often sold as jade.
- **Reconstituted or imitation turquoise**, as we saw in the guide on [copper stones] (/es/piedras-del-cobre/).
- **Glass and plastic** that imitate almost any stone.
- **Reconstituted stones:** stone powder mixed with resin and molded.

A simple test, if the seller allows it, is to wipe a cotton ball with a little alcohol or acetone on an inconspicuous bead: if it fades, it is dyed. For valuable pieces, a gemologist is better. The [imitations, doublets and triplets](/es/imitaciones-y-dobletes/) guide expands on the methods.

## If you want to make your own accounts

Making beads at home is possible, but requires specific equipment and precautions:

- **always work wet** to avoid breathing silica dust;
- wear **goggles, mask and gloves** when appropriate;
- Start with **stones of medium hardness** and without fractures;
- Consider starting with **factory punched beads** to learn how to thread and design before carving.

The guide to [learning lapidary safely](/es/aprender-lapidaria-con-seguridad/) explains how to set up a safe workspace.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do beads on the same string have different colors?

Some variation is normal in natural stones. What should raise suspicion is the opposite: an identical color in all the beads of a stone that is variable in nature.

### How often should you renew the thread of a necklace?

It depends on the use. A collar that is worn daily may need it every one or two years; one for occasional use, less frequently. If the thread looks stretched, dirty or frayed, it's time to renew it.

### Do stone spheres have special properties?

They are very beautiful collection and decoration objects. The healing or energetic properties attributed to them have no scientific support, so it is best to buy them for their beauty and quality.

## In summary

Stone beads are born from a block that is cut into cubes, rounded in mills or drums, drilled with diamond drill bits from both sides and polished to a shine. The spheres follow a similar path in cup machines. In America they have a long tradition, from the Andean *Spondylus* and Mesoamerican jade to the beads of the Wixárika and Embera peoples. A good bead is round, with a straight, clean hole, uniform polishing, and natural color. The threading with knots protects the thin pieces, the cable supports the heavy ones and the elastic needs to be renewed. And since dyes and imitations abound, it is advisable to look at the hole, weight, temperature and color regularity before purchasing.
