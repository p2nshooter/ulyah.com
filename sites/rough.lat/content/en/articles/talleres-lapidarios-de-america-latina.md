The gems of Latin America are not only mined: they are also cut, polished, carved and set in thousands of workshops throughout the region. Some are large companies that export all over the world; Many more are family workshops where the trade is learned by watching the elderly at work. In them, an opaque crystal becomes a shiny gem, a block of agate becomes translucent sheets and a piece of jade becomes a figure that refers to ancient cultures.

Knowing these centers helps to understand where the stones we see in jewelry stores come from, to appreciate the work behind them and, if you travel, to visit workshops and buy directly from those who work with the stone. In this guide you will see a map of the major lapidary trades in the region, the particularities of each place and some tips for visiting a workshop and purchasing wisely.

## Minas Gerais: the heart of colored gems

In the northeast of Minas Gerais, cities like Teófilo Otoni and Governador Valadares grew around the trade in gems from nearby pegmatites. Buyers, carvers, exporters and lapidaries work there, converting aquamarines, tourmalines, topazes, quartz and other gems into carved stones for the whole world. Gemstone fairs are organized in the region, bringing together international traders. We see it in the guide on the [Gems of Minas Gerais](/es/gemas-de-minas-gerais-topacio-y-aguamarina/).

## Southern agates and amethysts

In Rio Grande do Sul, Brazil, and northern Uruguay, the abundance of agates and amethysts in ancient basalt flows gave rise to an industry of its own. In towns like Soledade, known as a large stone processing center, agates are cut into sheets, geodes are polished, spheres, ashtrays, bookends and decorative objects are made, and agates are dyed in bright colors for the international market. We explain it in the guide on the [amethyst geodes from Uruguay and Brazil](/es/geodas-de-amatista-de-uruguay-y-brasil/).

## Bogotá: the emerald workshop

In the center of Bogotá, next to the places where emeralds are traded, numerous carving workshops operate, many family-owned, specializing in a particularly delicate stone. We tell it in the guide on [the size of emeralds] (/es/la-talla-de-esmeraldas/).

## Querétaro and the opal carving

In Querétaro, around San Juan del Río and Tequisquiapan, and in some areas of Jalisco, opal workshops work with a material that requires a lot of experience: you have to know how to read its play of colors, decide whether to face it or cut it as a cabochon and prevent it from cracking. Many workshops preserve quarry pieces, with the opal within its volcanic rock. We see it in the guide on [Mexican fire opal](/es/opalo-de-fuego-mexicano/).

## Larimar and amber in the Dominican Republic

The Dominican Republic has two materials that identify it:

- **Larimar:** a blue variety of pectolite that, as a gem, is known almost exclusively in the province of Barahona, in the southwest of the country. Its sky blue with white veins is reminiscent of the Caribbean sea, and is carved into cabochons, beads and silver pieces.
- **Amber:** the fossil resin from the Northern mountain range, in the north of the country, known for its transparency and, in some pieces, for inclusions of insects or plant remains and for a highly appreciated bluish fluorescence.

Both are worked on in workshops and stores throughout the country. Be wary of very low prices and pieces that are too perfect, because there are imitations of both materials, as we explain in the guide on [imitations and doublets](/es/imitaciones-y-dobletes/).

## Jade in Guatemala

The Motagua River Valley in Guatemala is one of the sources of jadeite on the American continent, the jade that the Mayans and other Mesoamerican people worked for masks, necklaces and ritual objects. In Antigua Guatemala there are workshops that carve jade with modern techniques and reproduce designs inspired by ancient pieces. Always ask if the piece is natural jadeite, if it is dyed or treated, and if it is a replica, and remember that archaeological pieces cannot be purchased.

## Ametrine in Bolivia

In eastern Bolivia, a mine known as Anahí is the world's main source of ametrine, a quartz that combines areas of violet amethyst and golden citrine in the same stone. Carvers orient the stone so that both colors face each other, often in rectangular cuts that show the border between them. In the trade it is also known as bolivianite.

## Andean stones: lapis lazuli, rhodochrosite and more

- **Chile:** Lapis lazuli from the Coquimbo region is worked in workshops in Santiago and other cities, along with ornamental stones such as combarbalite. We see it in the guide on [Chilean lapis lazuli](/es/lapislazuli-chileno/).
- **Argentina:** Catamarca rhodochrosite is transformed into sheets, cabochons and figures. More information in the guide about [Argentine rhodochrosite](/es/rodocrosita-argentina/).
- **Peru:** In addition to gem carving, the stone sculptures of Huamanga de Ayacucho and the work of stones such as chrysocolla and Andean opals stand out.

Many of these ornamental stones have their own guide at [ornamental stone carvings](/es/tallas-en-piedras-ornamentales/).

## A job that seeks its future

Over the past few decades, a significant portion of the world's colored gems, including many mined in Latin America, have been cut in large centers in Asia, with lower labor costs. This has led, in many cases, to the raw material leaving the region without the added value of the carving remaining in it. At the same time, Latin American carvers have emerged who are committed to quality, signature carvings and custom work, and projects that seek to have more stones carved near where they are extracted.

For the buyer, choosing stones cut in the region, when the quality is comparable, is a way to support that craft. If you are interested in learning, the guide on [learn lapidary safely](/es/aprender-lapidaria-con-seguridad/) explains how to get started, and the one on [signature and modern carvings](/es/tallas-de-autor-y-modernas/) shows where the craft is evolving.

## Visit a workshop

Many workshops receive visitors, especially in areas with tourism. To make the experience good for everyone:

- **Ask before entering** and respect working hours.
- **Ask for permission to photograph** people and their tools.
- **Do not touch the machines** or parts in process.
- **Listen to the explanations:** The carving process is fascinating and helps to understand the value of each piece.
- **Keep safety in mind:** Stone dust and moving machines require distance.

## Buy from a workshop

- **Buy directly** when you can, so the money goes to whoever does the work.
- **Ask about the material:** if it is natural, treated, dyed or reconstituted.
- **Ask for invoice or receipt** with the description of the piece.
- **Value the workmanship:** A complex carving or a large piece polished without defects takes many hours.
- **Find out about export** if you buy large pieces or in quantity, as we explain in the guide on [buying raw gems without deception](/es/comprar-gemas-en-bruto-sin-enganos/).

## Frequent errors

- **Buying larimar or amber** at prices that are too low without checking that they are natural.
- **Pay jadeite price** for green stones of another type.
- **Taking photos** inside a workshop without permission.
- **Buy agates with very bright colors** without knowing that they are usually dyed.
- **Confuse a replica** with an antique piece, or buy an archaeological piece.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where are the most gems cut in the region?

Brazil, and especially Minas Gerais, is one of the largest trading and cutting centers for colored gems in the region, along with other specialized centers, such as Bogotá for emeralds.

### Does larimar only exist in the Dominican Republic?

As a gem, it is known practically exclusively in the Dominican province of Barahona.

### What is ametrine?

A quartz that combines areas of violet amethyst and golden citrine in the same stone. Its main world source is in Bolivia.

### Can I visit a lapidary workshop?

Many receive visitors, especially in tourist areas. Ask before and respect the rules of the place.

## In summary

Latin America has a rich network of lapidary workshops: Minas Gerais for colored gems, southern Brazil and northern Uruguay for agates and amethysts, Bogotá for emeralds, Querétaro and Jalisco for opal, Dominican Republic for larimar and amber, Guatemala for jade, Bolivia for ametrine and the Andes for lapis lazuli, rhodochrosite and stones. ornamental. Visit the workshops with respect, ask permission to photograph, ask about the nature and treatments of each stone and buy directly, valuing the work of those who transform it.
