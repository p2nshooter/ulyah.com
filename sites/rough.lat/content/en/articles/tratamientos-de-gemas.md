Most colored gemstones sold in the world today have received some treatment. It is neither a secret nor, in itself, a hoax: the heating of rubies and sapphires has been practiced for centuries, and the oil in emeralds has a long tradition. The problem appears when the treatment is not declared, when it is unstable and the buyer does not know it, or when a heavily treated stone is sold at the price of an untreated natural stone.

Understanding the treatments is, therefore, one of the keys to purchasing gems wisely. In this guide you will see what is considered a treatment, why gems are treated, what the most common methods are, which are stable and which are not, how they influence value and care, what a laboratory can detect and what to ask before purchasing.

## What is a treatment

A treatment is any process, other than cutting and polishing, that improves the appearance, durability or value of a gem: its color, purity or stability. Some imitate natural processes, such as heat; others add materials foreign to the stone, such as oils, resins, glass, dyes or coatings.

## Why are gems treated?

- **To improve color:** lighten stones that are too dark, eliminate unwanted tones or intensify pale colors.
- **To improve apparent purity:** disguise cracks and inclusions.
- **To increase stability:** reinforce porous or fragile materials.
- **For market reasons:** without treatments, many gems would be much rarer and more expensive.

## The most common treatments

| Treatment | Common Gems | Overall stability |
|---|---|---|
| Warm-up | ruby, sapphire, aquamarine, tanzanite, zircon, quartz | stable |
| Heating with fluxes | ruby, sapphire | stable, with vitreous residues in fissures |
| Oils and resins | emerald and other fissured gems | variable; the oil may dry out or leak |
| Filled with lead glass | low quality ruby | little resistant to acids and heat |
| Diffusion | sapphire, especially with beryllium or titanium | stable, but can be superficial |
| Irradiation | blue topaz, some diamonds and quartz | generally stable; some colors lighten in light |
| Dyes | chalcedony, agate, jade, quartz, pearls, lapis lazuli | variable; can fade |
| Surface waxes and oils | jade, lapis lazuli, turquoise | they wear out over time |
| Resin stabilization | turquoise, porous materials | quite stable |
| Coatings | topaz, tanzanite, quartz, diamond | they can get scratched or worn |

## Warming up: the oldest treatment

Heat modifies the state of some coloring elements or dissolves fine inclusions, and thus changes the color and transparency of the stone.

- **Rubies and sapphires:** are heated to intensify or lighten the color and improve transparency. It is a very widespread practice.
- **Tanzanite:** Almost all tanzanite is heated to obtain its characteristic violet-blue color.
- **Aquamarine:** is heated to eliminate greenish tones.
- **Quartz:** heated amethyst gives citrine, as we explain in the guide on [amethyst geodes](/es/geodas-de-amatista-de-uruguay-y-brasil/).

In some cases, heating is done with fluxes that leave a glassy residue inside the cracks, improving apparent purity. Laboratories usually indicate if there is residue and in what quantity.

## Fillers: oils, resins and glass

- **Oils and resins in emeralds:** they fill the cracks that reach the surface and make them less visible. It is a traditional and common practice; The important thing is to know your degree. We see it in the guide on [Colombian emeralds](/es/esmeraldas-colombianas-de-la-mina-a-la-mesa/).
- **Lead glass in rubies:** Very cracked and low quality material is filled with a lead-rich glass that makes it appear more transparent. The result looks like a ruby, but is partly glass, much less valuable and fragile to acids, heat and some cleaning products. Laboratories often describe it as compound ruby.
- **Diamond fillings:** some cracks are filled with glass to hide them; We see it in the guide about [diamonds](/es/diamantes-guia-esencial/).

## Diffusion

In diffusion, the stone is heated along with chemical elements that penetrate its structure and change its color.

- **Diffusion with beryllium:** can transform unattractive colored sapphires into highly sought after orange, yellow or pink tones. At the beginning of this century, its undeclared use caused a huge scandal in the market.
- **Diffusion with titanium:** creates a blue layer that is sometimes very superficial, so re-polishing can remove some of the color.

## Irradiation

Radiation creates color centers in some gems. The best-known case is blue topaz, obtained from irradiated and heated colorless topaz, as we tell in the guide on [Minas Gerais gems](/es/gemas-de-minas-gerais-topacio-y-aguamarina/). Some diamonds, quartz and tourmalines are also irradiated. The stones treated in this way are checked for residual radioactivity before being sold.

## Dyes, waxes and stabilization

- **Dyes:** are used on porous or fissured materials, such as chalcedony, agate, jade, quartz or pearls. Color may concentrate in cracks and fade over time or with solvents.
- **Surface waxes and oils:** improve the shine of jade, turquoise or lapis lazuli, as we explain in the guide on [Chilean lapis lazuli] (/es/lapislazuli-chileno/).
- **Stabilization:** Porous turquoise is often impregnated with resins to give it hardness and stable color.
- **Bleaching and impregnation:** Some jades are bleached with acid to remove stains and then impregnated with polymers, which greatly reduces their value and durability.

## Coatings

A very thin layer applied to the surface can give color or iridescent effects, as in some multicolored topazes. These coatings can scratch, wear or peel off with use, and should be treated with great care.

## Declare treatment: a duty

International organizations in the sector and consumer protection regulations in many countries agree that the seller must inform about treatments, especially those that influence the value or require special care. A serious seller does this in writing, on the invoice or in the description of the part. If he doesn't mention it, ask.

## How they affect value and care

- **Value:** An untreated natural gem is usually worth more than a similar treated one. Some treatments, such as heating sapphires, are so common that the market assumes them; others, such as filling with lead glass, reduce the value dramatically.
- **Caution:** many treatments require precautions. Filled or coated stones should not be ultrasonic or steamed, and some will not tolerate the heat of a blowtorch during a repair. Always notify the jeweler of known treatments before any repairs.

## What a laboratory can detect

Gemological laboratories combine the microscope, which reveals heat-altered inclusions, fillers or residues, with spectroscopy and chemical analysis techniques capable of detecting resins, dyes or diffused elements. Still, not all treatments can be demonstrated in all cases: in some gems, not even a laboratory can say with certainty whether they were heated. We explain it in the guide on [gemology laboratory reports](/es/informes-de-laboratorio-gemologico/).

## Frequent errors

- **Assume a gem is natural untreated** because the seller did not say otherwise.
- **Pay natural ruby ​​price** for a glass filled ruby.
- **Ultrasound clean** filled or coated gems.
- **Do not notify the jeweler** of the treatments before a repair.
- **Distrust all treatments:** many are stable and accepted if they are declared and the price is appropriate.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a treatment a deception?

No, if it is declared and the price corresponds to a treated stone. The trick is to hide it or sell it as natural, untreated.

### What gems are usually treated?

Many: heated rubies and sapphires, emeralds with oil or resin, irradiated blue topaz, heated tanzanite, dyed agates or stabilized turquoises, among others.

### Are the treatments permanent?

Some do, like the warm-up; others do not, such as oils, waxes, dyes or coatings, which can change with time and use.

### How do I know if my stone is treated?

At first glance, almost never with certainty. A gemologist or laboratory can detect many treatments with the right equipment.

## In summary

Most colored gemstones receive some treatment: heating, oil, resin or glass fillings, diffusion, irradiation, dyes, waxes, stabilization or coatings. Some are stable and accepted, like warming; others are brittle or greatly reduce value, such as lead glass filling. The essential thing is that the treatment is declared in writing, that the price is appropriate and that you know the care required. For important pieces, rely on a laboratory report and always notify the jeweler before any repair.
