If a single gem had to be chosen to explain Brazil's mineral wealth, tourmaline would be a serious candidate. It appears in almost every color imaginable, sometimes several in the same crystal, and the sites in Minas Gerais, Bahia, Paraíba and Rio Grande do Norte have been feeding workshops and collectors around the world for decades. For those who begin to look at raw gems, tourmaline is also a great teacher: it teaches how to read a crystal, to understand why the color changes depending on where you look from and to distrust commercial names that promise more than they demonstrate.

In this guide you will see what exactly tourmaline is, why it has so many colors, what are its best-known varieties, what makes Brazilian tourmalines special, how a crystal is oriented before cutting it, what treatments are common and what should be checked before buying.

## What is tourmaline

"Tourmaline" is not a single mineral, but the name of a **group of related minerals**: complex borosilicates that share the same crystal structure but change their chemistry. Most colored tourmalines that are cut as gems belong to the lithium-rich **elbaite**. **Plotter**, black and rich in iron, is the most common tourmaline in nature and is rarely used in fine jewelry, although it appears in rustic-style pieces and in mineral collections. The **dravita**, with brown tones, and the **liddicoatite**, known for its cross sections with triangular patterns, complete the list that an amateur sees the most.

Some properties to remember:

- **Hardness:** between 7 and 7.5 on the Mohs scale, sufficient for rings with a protective setting and for worry-free earrings and pendants.
- **Crystal shape:** Elongated prisms with very marked vertical striations and a section reminiscent of a triangle with bulging sides. Those stretch marks are one of the best clues to recognize it in the rough.
- **Strong pleochroism:** the color changes depending on the direction from which you look. In many glasses, looking along the main axis shows a much darker tone than looking from the side.
- **Pyroelectricity:** When heated or cooled, the glass becomes electrically charged at its ends and attracts dust or ash. Dutch merchants of past centuries knew it for this curiosity, and it still explains why a tourmaline exposed to a hot display case collects dust before other stones.

If you want to review how these types of crystals are born, the guide on [how gems are formed](/es/como-se-forman-las-gemas/) explains the role of pegmatites, the coarse-grained rocks where most gem-quality tourmaline grows.

## Why does it have so many colors?

The structure of tourmaline supports many different elements in their positions, and small amounts of each change the color. Broadly speaking:

- **manganese** is associated with pinks and reds;
- **iron** provides greens, blues and, in excess, blacks;
- **copper**, along with manganese, produces the electric blues and greens of Paraíba-type tourmalines;
- **chromium and vanadium** give intense greens in some tourmalines from other continents.

As the chemistry of the liquid in which the crystal grows changes over time, tourmaline records those changes in layers. The result is **bicolor or multicolor** crystals, with green, pink or colorless areas along the prism, or with a core of one color and an edge of another.

## The best known varieties

In the trade, variety names are used according to the dominant color. They are not different mineral species, but useful labels for buying and selling:

| Name | Color | What is good to know |
|---|---|---|
| Rubellite | deep pink to red | The color is expected to remain the same under daylight and warm light; usually has inclusions |
| Indicolite | blue | clean and not too dark blues are rare |
| Verdelite | green | the most abundant variety; very dependent on orientation |
| acroite | colorless | rare and uncommercial |
| watermelon | pink core, green border | It is cut into transverse slices to show off the color rings. |
| Paraíba or Paraíba type | blue or neon green | color due to copper; confirmed with chemical analysis in the laboratory |
| cat's eye | any color, with light band | the band is due to parallel growth tubes; is cut in cabochon |

The borders between names are not exact. A pale pink stone may be sold as "rubellite" by one dealer and as "pink tourmaline" by another. What matters is the color you see, not the word on the label.

## The tourmalines of Brazil

Brazil is one of the largest producers of tourmaline in the world. Most of it comes from pegmatites from eastern **Minas Gerais**, the same province that produces aquamarine, morganite and topaz, and which you know if you read our guide on [Minas Gerais gems](/es/gemas-de-minas-gerais-topacio-y-aguamarina/). Rubellites, verdelites, indicolites and large multicolor crystals have been extracted there, in famous mines among collectors such as the Cruzeiro mine, in the São José da Safira region. Bahia and other northeastern states also produce tourmaline in pegmatites.

The most famous story, however, is that of **Paraíba**. At the end of the 1980s, in a small site in the state of Paraíba, tourmalines of such intense blue and green appeared that they seemed illuminated from within. The analysis showed that its color was due to copper, something unknown until then in this gem. Their scarcity and color made them one of the most sought-after colored gems. Copper-bearing tourmalines were later found in the neighboring state of Rio Grande do Norte and, years later, in Nigeria and Mozambique.

This opened a debate that still confuses many buyers today: what is a "Paraíba"? Much of the trade and laboratories use the term **"Paraíba type"** as a variety name for any tourmaline with copper and manganese that has that characteristic color, regardless of the country. Geographic origin, when a laboratory can determine it, is indicated separately. Therefore, if a stone is sold as "Brazilian Paraíba", it is reasonable to ask for a laboratory report confirming the copper and, separately, the origin. Our guide on [gemological laboratory reports](/es/informes-de-laboratorio-gemologico/) explains how to read these documents and verify that they are authentic.

## Read a raw tourmaline crystal

Before buying or cutting a crystal, it is a good idea to observe it calmly, against the light and from various directions.

### Color orientation

Due to its strong pleochroism, tourmaline has a **"closed"** axis: looking along the prism the color looks much darker, sometimes almost black, and looking from the side it looks lighter and more vivid. The cutter decides in which direction to place the gem table depending on what the crystal asks for:

- in a **dark** crystal, the table is placed parallel to the length of the prism to lighten the color;
- In a **clear** crystal, the table is sometimes oriented towards the axis to concentrate the color and prevent the stone from looking washed out.

That decision changes the final look more than any other. We explain it in detail in the guide on [orientation, color and performance](/es/del-bruto-a-la-gema-orientacion-y-rendimiento/).

### Inclusions and fractures

Green and blue tourmalines tend to be cleaner than pink and red ones. In rubellites it is normal to see fluid-filled fractures, fine inclusions in the form of threads and veils. A pink crystal that is completely clean to the naked eye is rarer and therefore more valuable. Raw, check:

- **fractures** that reach the surface, because they can cause the stone to break when carved;
- the **growth tubes**, which spoil a faceted gem but can give a good cat's eye if they are many and parallel;
- the **color zones**, to know if the crystal allows a gem of a single color or requests a size that shows off the bicolor.

### Completions and collection value

A crystal with its faces intact, shiny and with a natural finish may be worth more as a specimen than cut. Before cutting a beautiful crystal, ask yourself if it doesn't deserve to stay whole in a display case. The guide to [buying rough gems without deception](/es/comprar-gemas-en-bruto-sin-enganos/) gives more ideas for separating cutting material from collecting material.

## Common treatments

Tourmaline is treated less than other gems, but there are common practices that the seller must declare:

- **Warming:** It is used to lighten very dark greens and blues or to modify the tone of some tourmalines with copper. It is usually stable.
- **Irradiation:** can intensify or create pink and red colors. The resulting color is usually stable under normal conditions, but it is worth knowing.
- **Fracture filling:** Resins are used in highly fractured rubellites to improve transparency. It is the treatment that most affects durability and value.

If you want to know how each one is detected, the guide on [gem treatments](/es/tratamientos-de-gemas/) reviews them all.

## Imitations and confusions

There is no significant commercial production of synthetic tourmaline for jewelry, so the main risk is not the laboratory stone, but the **substitution** with another gem or glass:

- **glass** imitates any color, but has no pleochroism and often shows round bubbles;
- blue-green **apatite** has sometimes been sold as an alternative to Paraíba; It is much softer and scratches easily;
- some treated **quartz and topaz** imitate pinks and blues.

A gemologist distinguishes these stones with simple instruments: the refractometer, the polariscope and the dichroscope, which shows the two colors of pleochroism.

## How to buy tourmaline judiciously

1. **Look at the color in various lights.** Daylight, warm light from a lamp, and cool light from an office can greatly change the appearance of a rubellite or indicolite.
2. **Turn the stone.** A misoriented gem appears dark on the table and light on the sides. A good size shows the color evenly.
3. **Ask about the treatments.** Ask that they be indicated in writing on the invoice.
4. **Requires a report for expensive stones.** Especially for tourmalines with copper, large rubellites and stones sold by their origin.
5. **Be wary of unsupported names.** "Paraíba", "fine rubellite" or "collectible indicolite" are commercial expressions. What counts is the stone in front of you and what a laboratory shows.
6. **Check the setting.** For everyday rings, a sturdy bezel or prongs protect the edges. The [setting types](/es/tipos-de-engaste/) guide helps you choose.

## Care

Tourmaline is a grateful gem in everyday life, with some precautions:

- clean it with warm water, mild soap and a soft brush;
- avoid **ultrasonic cleaners and steam** on fractured or filled stones;
- do not expose it to **sudden changes in temperature**, which can enlarge fractures;
- Store it separately from harder gems, such as sapphire or diamond, which can scratch it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a watermelon tourmaline be faceted?

Yes, but the usual thing is to cut it into transverse sheets and polish them, so that the color rings can be seen. Faceted, the mix of colors can look confusing if the size is not well thought out.

### Does tourmaline lose color in the sun?

Not under normal conditions. Some irradiated stones can lighten with intense heat or prolonged exposure to high temperatures, so avoid leaving them in very hot places.

### Is it good for an engagement ring?

It can be with a protective setting and reasonable care. It is less hard than sapphire, so it will accumulate minor wear on the edges over the years if worn daily.

## In summary

Tourmaline is a group of minerals with such variable chemistry that it produces almost all colors, often several in a single crystal. Brazil is one of its great sources, from the pegmatites of Minas Gerais to the copper tourmalines that made Paraíba famous. To choose well, you have to look at the color in various lights and directions, understand that the orientation of the carver decides a good part of the result, ask about heating, irradiation and fillings, and ask for a laboratory report when the price depends on the copper or the origin. With a protective setting and simple care, a good tourmaline lasts for many years.
