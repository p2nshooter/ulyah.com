Silver is at the origin of the modern history of Latin America. From the mines of Potosí, in present-day Bolivia, and Zacatecas, Guanajuato or Taxco, in Mexico, enormous quantities of silver came out for centuries that transformed the world economy. Along with this wealth, a tradition of goldsmiths and silversmiths was born that mixed European techniques, indigenous knowledge and, over time, African influences, and which continues today in workshops in towns and cities throughout the region.

Among all these techniques, filigree stands out, the art of turning very fine metal threads into silver or gold lace. In this guide you will see where the Latin American silver tradition comes from, how filigree is worked, what are some of the great silversmithing centers in the region, what characterizes Mapuche and Creole silversmithing, how to recognize a good piece and how to care for it.

## Silver in the history of the region

Since the sixteenth century, American silver became the great commodity of the Spanish empire. The Cerro Rico de Potosí was for a time one of the largest sources of silver in the world, and in Mexico cities such as Zacatecas, Guanajuato or Taxco grew thanks to its mines. That wealth financed churches, palaces and pieces of high-quality religious and domestic silverware, many of them visible today in museums and temples in the region.

At the same time, indigenous people incorporated silver into their own traditions, and in several regions styles were born that mixed European forms with local symbols.

## What is filigree

The word filigree comes from Latin and refers to the union of thread and grain. It is a technique in which very fine metal threads, often twisted and flattened, are curved and welded to form openwork patterns, like metallic lace.

The process, in general terms, is like this:

1. **Prepare the metal:** High-grade silver or gold, which is more malleable, is melted and turned into a bar.
2. **Stretch the thread:** the bar is passed through the increasingly smaller holes of a row until a very fine thread is obtained.
3. **Twisting and flattening:** Two strands are twisted together and flattened slightly, which gives the characteristic shine.
4. **Make the frame:** with a thicker thread the outline of the piece is formed.
5. **Filling:** Using tweezers, the craftsman bends the fine thread into spirals, waves or flowers and places them inside the frame.
6. **Welding:** Powder welding and heat are applied to join all the pieces together.
7. **Clean and polish:** Bleach and polish to give the final finish.

A piece of filigree can require many hours of painstaking work, and its value is as much in the metal as it is in that craft.

## Taxco and modern Mexican silverware

Taxco, in the state of Guerrero, is a mining town with steep streets and white houses that preserves a spectacular baroque temple, built with the wealth of silver in the eighteenth century. In the 1930s, the American William Spratling set up a workshop there that trained many local artisans and promoted a style of designer silversmithing that brought international fame to the town. Today Taxco brings together a huge number of workshops and shops, and holds a fair dedicated to silver every year.

Many Mexican silver pieces are marked "925", the word "Mexico" and a code that identifies the workshop. When buying in Taxco, compare stores, check the brands and ask about the weight of the piece.

## The filigree of Catacaos, Mompox, Chordeleg and Luque

In different corners of the region, filigree has remained a family craft that is passed from generation to generation:

- **Catacaos,** near Piura in northern Peru, is known for its silver and gold filigree earrings, pins, and ornaments.
- **Mompox,** in Colombia, preserves a colonial center declared a world heritage site and a highly recognized filigree tradition, with gold and silver pieces with very delicate designs.
- **Chordeleg,** in the south of Ecuador, is a town of jewelers where filigree and other techniques are worked.
- **Luque,** near Asunción, is the great center of Paraguayan filigree.

Visiting these workshops allows you to see the process up close and buy directly from those who make the pieces.

## Mapuche silverware

The Mapuche people, in southern Chile and Argentina, developed their own silversmithing since the colonial period, partly from melted coins. Silversmiths, called rütrafe, created pieces full of meaning, such as large pectorals, head decorations, hoops and pins to hold clothing, which were worn at ceremonies and special occasions. Many museums in Chile and Argentina preserve collections of Mapuche silverware, and current artisans keep the tradition alive. If you buy a Mapuche-inspired piece, prefer artisans from the communities themselves.

## The Creole silversmithing of the Southern Cone

In the plains of Argentina, Uruguay and southern Brazil, the gaucho culture generated its own silverware: mates and bulbs, knives, harrows for belts, spurs and horse implements decorated with silver. Today, pieces are made of silver and also many of nickel silver, an alloy without silver, so it is a good idea to always ask about the material.

## Gold with Afro-Brazilian history

In Bahia, Brazil, the so-called crioula jewelry, large gold pieces worn by Afro-descendant women, are part of the cultural history of the region and today are studied and exhibited as heritage. They remember that Latin American goldsmithing also has African roots.

## How to recognize a good piece

- **Brands:** look for the indication of the law, such as 925 or 950, and, if it exists, the workshop mark.
- **Clean welds:** in filigree, the joints must be discreet and firm, without stains or remains.
- **Symmetry and regularity:** the threads must form orderly and even drawings.
- **Finish:** without sharp edges or poorly polished areas.
- **Weight:** A solid silver piece weighs more than a hollow or plated metal piece.
- **Closures and hooks:** firm and well finished, because they are usually the weak point.

## Silver, nickel silver and bathrooms

Sterling silver, 950 silver, nickel silver and silver-plated metals coexist in the region's markets. They all have their place, but their price must correspond to what they are. We explain it in the guide on [gold, silver and platinum](/es/oro-plata-y-platino/).

## Buy directly from artisans

Buying in the workshop or at craft fairs allows you to meet the person who made the piece, understand the process and pay a price that remunerates their work. Some ideas:

- **Ask about the working time** of the piece: it helps to understand its price.
- **Do not haggle excessively:** a well-made piece of filigree requires many hours of craftsmanship behind it.
- **Ask for invoice or receipt** with the description of the metal.

## Take care of silver and filigree

- **Store it in closed bags** or with antioxidant strips, away from moisture.
- **Clean it with a specific cloth** for silver, gently.
- **Avoid aggressive chemical baths** on pieces with intentional patina or stones.
- **Treat the filigree delicately:** it is openwork and can be deformed if crushed.
- **Take it off** to swim in chlorinated pools or in the sea.

More tips in the guide on [jewelry maintenance and cleaning](/es/mantenimiento-y-limpieza-de-joyas/).

## Frequent errors

- **Pay silver price for alpaca.**
- **Buy filigree by weight** without valuing the work.
- **Clean the filigree with hard brushes** or abrasive products.
- **Store silver in the air,** where it darkens faster.
- **Buying indigenous-inspired pieces** without knowing who made them.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Taxco silver of good quality?

In Taxco there are workshops of all levels. Check the brands, weight and finishes, and compare between stores.

### Why is filigree expensive if it weighs little?

Because its value is above all in the work: many hours of meticulous craftsmanship to form and solder each thread.

### How do I know if a piece is alpaca?

Ask directly and look for the law marks. A jeweler can check the metal with specific tests.

### Does silver always darken?

Over time, yes, by reacting with the air. It recovers with a gentle cleaning, and some pieces are purposely darkened to highlight the drawing.

## In summary

Silver marked the history of Latin America, from Potosí to Taxco, and gave rise to silver traditions that are still alive. Filigree, made with very fine curved and welded threads, is worked in places such as Catacaos and San Jerónimo de Tunán in Peru, Mompox in Colombia, Chordeleg in Ecuador or Luque in Paraguay. Mapuche and Creole silversmiths from the Southern Cone have their own styles. Recognize a good piece by its marks, welds and finishes, distinguish silver from nickel silver, buy directly from artisans at a fair price and take care of the silver by keeping it closed and cleaning it gently.
