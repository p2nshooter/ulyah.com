The ruby ​​and the sapphire seem like two different gems, with their own stories, legends and prices. They are actually the same mineral: **corundum**. The only thing that separates them is color. A red corundum is a ruby; a corundum of any other color is a sapphire. This family brings together some of the hardest, most durable and most appreciated gems in the world, ideal for everyday jewelry, and also one of the markets with the most treatments and synthetics, so it is a good idea to know it well before buying.

In this guide you will see what corundum is, what each color originates from, where it comes from, what treatments are common and which ones affect the value, how synthetics are recognized and what to ask for before paying for a piece.

## What is corundum

Corundum is crystallized **aluminum oxide**. Pure, it would be colorless; small amounts of other elements give it color. Its properties explain its fame:

- **Hardness 9** on the Mohs scale, just below diamond. Resists daily friction without scratching.
- **Good toughness**, except in very fractured stones or with filler treatments.
- **Stability** against light and most chemicals.

That's why corundum is one of the best options for engagement rings and jewelry that is worn every day, as we explain in the guide on [hardness, toughness and stability] (/es/dureza-tenacidad-y-estabilidad/).

## Where does each color come from?

| Color | Name | Responsible elements |
|---|---|---|
| Red | ruby | chrome |
| Blue | blue sapphire | iron and titanium |
| Pink | pink sapphire | chromium in smaller quantity |
| orange pink | padparadscha | chromium and other elements, sometimes with the help of treatments |
| Yellow and orange | yellow or orange sapphire | iron and others; often treated |
| Violet and purple | violet sapphire | combinations of chromium, iron and titanium |
| Green | green sapphire | iron |
| Colorless | white sapphire | almost no impurities |
| color change | color change sapphire | varies between daylight and warm light |

The border between a ruby and a pink sapphire is not exact. Different laboratories and markets place it in slightly different places, and the same stone can be described as a light ruby ​​or a deep pink sapphire. What you should look at is the real color and what the report says.

## Special phenomena

- **Rubies and star sapphires:** When the crystal contains very fine rutile needles oriented in three directions, a well-cut cabochon shows a six-armed star that slides when the light moves. We explain it in the guide on [optical phenomena in gems](/es/fenomenos-opticos-en-gemas/).
- **Silk:** those same needles, in smaller quantities, soften the color and give it a velvety shine that is highly appreciated in some sapphires.
- **Color Zones:** Many sapphires show bands of deeper and paler color. A good cutter places them so that the gem looks homogeneous from the table.

## Origins

Gem quality corundum comes primarily from Asia, Africa and Oceania. Among the best-known historical and current regions are Myanmar, Sri Lanka, Thailand and Cambodia, Kashmir, Madagascar, Mozambique, Tanzania and Kenya, Australia and Montana, in the United States.

In Latin America, gem-quality corundum is **rare**, and most of what is sold in jewelry stores in the region is imported. This has a practical consequence: when buying a ruby ​​or sapphire, it is good to know that it has almost always traveled a long commercial chain, and that the documents and reputation of the seller matter more than a story of origin.

Does the origin influence the price? In some cases yes. Certain historical origins have prestige in the market, and a laboratory report confirming them can increase the value. But origin does not guarantee beauty: a sapphire of any origin with good color and good cut is worth more than one famous for its origin but mediocre.

## Treatments: the most important chapter

Most corundum on the market has received some treatment. It's not hidden or necessarily negative, but each treatment has a different effect on durability and value, and **all must be declared**.

### Warm-up

It is the most widespread treatment. Heat improves color, lightens dark areas, dissolves part of the silk and increases transparency. It is stable and accepted in the trade, although **unheated** corundums with good color command a premium. In some cases, heating is done with fluxes that leave small glassy residue in healed fractures, something a laboratory can indicate.

### Filled with lead glass

It is especially applied to low-quality, highly fractured rubies. The cracks are filled with a lead-rich glass which greatly improves the appearance. The result, which some laboratories describe as **composite ruby**, is much more fragile:

- it is damaged by the **heat of a jeweler's blowtorch**;
- corrodes with mild **acids**, including lemon juice;
- May be damaged by household cleaners.

These stones should be sold as what they are and at a price much lower than that of an unfilled ruby.

### Diffusion

It consists of introducing coloring elements from outside, at high temperatures:

- **surface diffusion** with titanium creates a thin blue layer that may disappear if the stone is repolished;
- **diffusion with beryllium** produces deeper penetrating yellows, oranges and padparadscha tones.

Both are difficult to detect without a laboratory.

### Others

**Oils and resins** are also used in fractures, **dyes** in low quality stones and even **coatings**. The general guide to [gem treatments](/es/tratamientos-de-gemas/) explains how each affects value.

## Synthetics and imitations

Corundum was one of the first gems made in a laboratory. Since the beginning of the 20th century it has been produced by **flame fusion** (Verneuil method), cheap and massive, and later by **flux** and **hydrothermal**, methods that give crystals more similar to natural ones.

- Flame fusion corundums can show **curved growth lines** and round **gas bubbles** under the microscope.
- Flux and hydrothermal ones require laboratory and often advanced analysis.

Synthetics have the same composition, hardness and shine as natural ones: they are real corundum, but much cheaper, and should be sold as synthetics. We explain them in the guide on [synthetic and laboratory gems](/es/gemas-sinteticas-y-de-laboratorio/).

**imitations** are something else: glass, synthetic spinel or garnet and glass **doublets** that imitate the color without being corundum. The guide to [imitations, doublets and triplets](/es/imitaciones-y-dobletes/) teaches how to recognize them.

## What determines the value

1. **Color:** the main factor. We are looking for saturated, medium-toned colors, without excess gray or brown. We explain it in the guide on [how to judge the quality of a colored gem](/es/calidad-de-las-gemas-de-color/).
2. **Purity:** Rubies tend to have more inclusions than sapphires; A clean ruby ​​to the naked eye is rare.
3. **Cut:** Many stones are originally cut very deep to conserve weight. A well-proportioned size shines more and looks older.
4. **Size:** the price per carat goes up with size, especially in rubies.
5. **Treatment:** unheated, heated, filled or diffused are very different price categories.
6. **Origin:** relevant in certain cases, always documented by a laboratory.

## How to buy a ruby or sapphire

- **Request treatment in writing.** "Heated", "no signs of heating", "glass filled" or "diffusion" must be listed on the invoice.
- **Laboratory report required** for high-priced stones, for stones sold as unheated, and for any stone sold based on its origin. The [gemological laboratory reports](/es/informes-de-laboratorio-gemologico/) guide explains how to read and verify them.
- **Look at the stone in various lights.** Some sapphires darken under artificial light and some rubies become more purple.
- **Be wary of too low prices** for large, transparent rubies: they are often filled or synthetic.
- **Ask about the return policy** and the possibility of having the stone verified in an independent laboratory.

## Care

Natural or heated corundum is very resistant:

- clean it with warm water, mild soap and a soft brush;
- Ultrasounds are usually safe for stones without fractures or fillings;
- **avoid ultrasound, steam, acids and heat** on glass-filled rubies or highly fractured stones;
- Inform the jeweler if the stone is filled before any torch repair.

The [jewelry maintenance and cleaning](/es/mantenimiento-y-limpieza-de-joyas/) guide has more details.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a heated sapphire worth less?

It is worth less than an equivalent unheated sapphire, but heating is stable and accepted. The important thing is that it is declared and reflected in the price.

### Can white sapphire replace a diamond?

It can, as an economical and very hard alternative. It shines differently, with less fire, and accumulates dirt that dulls its appearance if it is not cleaned frequently.

### How do I know if a ruby ​​is synthetic?

At first glance, almost never. A gemologist with a microscope can recognize the cheapest synthetics; flux or hydrothermal ones usually require a specialized laboratory.

## In summary

Ruby and sapphire are the same mineral, corundum: red in the first case, any other color in the second. Its hardness and stability make it one of the best gems for daily use. Almost all corundum on the market is treated, especially heated, and some treatments, such as lead glass filling or diffusion, greatly change durability and price. Synthetics have been around for more than a century and are difficult to distinguish without a laboratory. To buy well you have to look at the color in various lights, ask for the treatment in writing, demand information for important pieces and be wary of bargains.
