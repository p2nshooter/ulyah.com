A gold ring seems like a simple object, but its history can be very long and not always clean. The metal could have come from a large industrial mine with strict environmental controls, from an artisanal cooperative that works with fair trade standards or from an illegal exploitation in the heart of the Amazon that contaminates rivers with mercury and destroys the jungle. At first glance, the three rings would be identical. The same goes for many gems.

Latin America is at the center of this debate: it is one of the largest gold-producing regions in the world and hundreds of thousands of artisanal miners work there, many in precarious conditions. In this guide you will see why the origin of gold and gems matters, what artisanal and small-scale mining is, why mercury is such a serious problem, what are the best-known certifications and standards, what recycled gold provides, what to ask a jeweler and how to avoid greenwashing.

## Why the origin of gold matters

- **Environmental impact:** Mining moves enormous amounts of earth and rock, consumes water and can contaminate soils and rivers.
- **Social impact:** Working conditions, miners' safety, child labor or conflicts with communities vary enormously depending on the mine.
- **Legality:** Gold from illegal exploitations can finance criminal activities and enter the market mixed with legal gold.

For this reason, more and more buyers and jewelers are wondering where the metal they use comes from.

## Artisanal and small-scale mining

In countries such as Peru, Colombia, Bolivia, Ecuador, Brazil or the Guianas, an important part of the gold comes from artisanal and small-scale mining: people, families or small cooperatives that work with simple tools and little machinery.

- **It is a way of life** for many rural families, often in areas with few economic alternatives.
- **It can be formal or informal:** some cooperatives are legalized, comply with standards and improve their practices; others operate without permits.
- **Their conditions are very variable:** from organized and safe farms to dangerous and precarious jobs.

Supporting the formalization and good practices of artisanal mining is one of the keys to more responsible gold.

## Mercury: the big problem

In many artisanal exploitations, the gold is separated from the material with mercury, which forms an amalgam with it; It is then heated to evaporate the mercury and obtain the gold. The process is cheap and simple, but very harmful:

- **For health:** Mercury vapors affect the nervous system of miners and their families.
- **For the environment:** Mercury reaches rivers, accumulates in fish and enters the food chain of entire communities.

Artisanal gold mining is one of the largest sources of human-caused mercury emissions in the world. The Minamata Convention on Mercury, an international treaty, commits countries that have ratified it to reduce and, where possible, eliminate its use in this activity. There are alternative methods that allow gold to be recovered without mercury.

## Illegal mining in the Amazon

In several areas of the Amazon, illegal gold mining has caused deforestation, contamination of rivers with mercury and sediment, invasion of indigenous territories and increased violence. Regions such as Madre de Dios, in Peru, or some indigenous territories in Brazil have become well-known examples of these impacts. That gold can enter the legal market through intermediaries, mixed with gold from other sources, which makes traceability so important. What is at stake is one of the richest ecosystems on the planet and the lives of the people who inhabit it.

## Certifications and standards

| Initiative | What does it do |
|---|---|
| Fairmined | certifies gold from artisanal and small-scale mining organizations that meet social and environmental standards; powered by an alliance based in Colombia |
| Fair trade in gold | gold certification of small producers under fair trade criteria |
| Rules for jewelers | codes of practice and chain of custody for jewelry companies undergoing audits |
| Guides for refineries | Due diligence criteria for refineries that process gold |
| OECD Due Diligence Guide | international framework for responsible mineral supply chains |

Certifications do not solve all problems, but they offer verifiable guarantees. Always ask for the document or seal that certifies the certification, not just a verbal statement.

## recycled gold

An important part of the gold circulating in the market comes from recycling jewelry, coins and electronic components.

- **Advantage:** Recycled gold requires no new mining and is the same quality as freshly mined gold.
- **Caution:** "Recycled" does not always mean traceable. Ask where it comes from and whether the supplier has controls to prevent illegally sourced gold from being presented as recycled.

## Silver and other metals

The same questions apply to silver and platinum. Much of the world's silver is obtained as a by-product of the mining of other metals, such as lead, zinc or copper, which makes it difficult to trace its specific origin; That is why recycled silver is an interesting alternative. Platinum and its group metals come from very few countries, and its mining also has impacts that should be known. In all cases, the principles are the same: prefer recycled or certified metal, ask the supplier for information and distrust promises without evidence.

## Gem traceability

Tracing the origin of colored gems is even more difficult than with gold: many stones pass through numerous intermediaries and workshops in different countries before reaching a jewelry store. Even so, there is progress:

- **Source and traceability programs,** that follow a stone from the mine to the store.
- **Laboratory reports** that include an opinion of geographical origin, as we explain in the guide on [gemological laboratory reports](/es/informes-de-laboratorio-gemologico/).
- **The Kimberley Process** for rough diamonds, with its limitations, which we discuss in the guide on [diamonds: essential guide](/es/diamantes-guia-esencial/).
- **Direct trade** with cooperatives and miners, increasingly common in designer pieces.

## What to ask a jeweler

1. **Where does the gold come from?** Is it recycled, certified or from what supplier?
2. **Do you have any certification** or are you part of any responsible sourcing program?
3. **Do you know the origin of the stones?** Is there documentation?
4. **What controls do you do on your suppliers?**
5. **Can you put it in writing** on the invoice?

A committed jeweler will respond with concrete data. If the answers are vague, consider other options.

## Beware of greenwashing

Words like "ethical", "sustainable", "conscious" or "eco" are often used without any support. Be wary of:

- **Generic statements** without certification or verifiable data.
- **Nature images** that replace information.
- **Absolute promises,** like "zero impact".
- **Simplistic comparisons,** for example between laboratory and natural gems, which ignore the energy consumption or conditions of each mine, as we explain in the guide on [synthetic and laboratory gems](/es/gemas-sinteticas-y-de-laboratorio/).

## What you can do

- **Buy certified or recycled gold** with documentation.
- **Choose second-hand jewelry** or reuse the ones you already have; We see it in the guide on [inherited and second-hand jewelry](/es/joyas-heredadas-y-de-segunda-mano/).
- **Support transparent jewelers and workshops** that explain their supply chain.
- **Buy less and better:** Durable pieces that will be used for decades.
- **Get informed** and ask questions: Buyer demand drives changes in the industry.

## Frequent errors

- **Assume that all gold is equal.**
- **Accept sustainability labels** without asking for proof.
- **Believe that "recycled" always equals traceable.**
- **Ignore the origin of the gems** when taking care of the metal.
- **Thinking that a buyer cannot influence** the industry.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is Fairmined gold?

Certified gold from artisanal and small-scale mining organizations that meet social and environmental standards, including controlling the use of chemicals.

### Is recycled gold of poorer quality?

No. Recycled gold is refined and has the same quality as mined gold.

### How do I know if a piece of jewelry is responsible?

Ask for documentation: certificates, origin of the metal and stones and written commitments. Claims without evidence guarantee nothing.

### Is artisanal mining always bad?

No. It is a way of life for many families, and with formalization, good practices and without mercury it can be a responsible source of gold.

## In summary

The origin of gold and gems matters for its environmental, social and legal impacts. In Latin America, artisanal and small-scale mining is a way of life for many families, but the use of mercury and illegal mining in the Amazon causes serious damage. Certifications such as Fairmined, jeweler and refiner standards and due diligence help provide assurance, and recycled gold reduces the need for new mining. Ask your jeweler, demand documentation, be wary of greenwashing and choose durable, recycled, certified or second-hand pieces.
