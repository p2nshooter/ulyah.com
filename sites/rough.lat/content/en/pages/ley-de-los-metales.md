The grade of a precious metal is the proportion of pure metal in the alloy. It is expressed in thousandths (750 means 750 parts of gold per 1,000) or, in the case of gold, in carats, out of a total of 24. The calculator above indicates how much pure metal a piece contains; It does not give prices or investment recommendations.

## Gold: carats and thousandths

| Carat | Thousandths | Gold in alloy |
|---|---|---|
| 24 | 999 | practically pure |
| 22 | 916 | about 91.6% |
| 18 | 750 | 75 % |
| 14 | 585 | about 58.5% |
| 10 | 417 | about 41.7% |
| 9 | 375 | 37,5 % |

The conversion is simple: thousandths = carats ÷ 24 × 1,000. An 18-karat ring weighing 4 g contains 3 g of gold; the rest are other metals that give it hardness and color.

## Silver and platinum

| Denomination | Thousandths | Use |
|---|---|---|
| fine silver | 999 | special pieces, filigree threads |
| 950 silver | 950 | common in many workshops in Peru |
| Sterling silver | 925 | the most common in jewelry |
| Platinum 950 | 950 | usual platinum jewelry |
| Platinum 900 | 900 | platinum jewelry |

Nickel silver, also called German silver, does not contain silver: it is an alloy of copper, nickel and zinc.

## How to read the mark

The grade is usually engraved on the inside of the ring or on the clasp: a number (750, 925, 950) or an indication in carats (18K, 14K). In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission's Jewelry Guides require that the carat or grade listed be accurate and prohibit calling anything that is just a plating "gold." In other countries their own contrast standards govern. If the total weight includes stones, the actual pure metal will be less than what the calculator shows.
