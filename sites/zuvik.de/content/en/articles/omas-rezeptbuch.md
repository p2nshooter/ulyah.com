The book was at the bottom of a box that Grandma Hilde had taken from the attic. She moved to a smaller apartment, closer to her daughter's family in Würzburg, and had to sort out a lot of things. Emil, nine years old, helped her clear things out, or actually he mostly rummaged around. Between old magazines, photo albums and a stack of yellowed letters, he found a thick, worn notebook with a blue cloth cover.

He opened it. The pages were written tightly, in a squiggly handwriting that he could barely read. There were stains everywhere: grease stains, chocolate stains, a large brown ring where a coffee cup had once stood. Between the pages were pieces of paper, newspaper clippings, a dried flower.

“Grandma, what is that?”

Grandma Hilde looked up from her box and her face changed. “This is my recipe book,” she said quietly. "I've been looking for this. For years."

## Sixty years in one issue

They sat next to each other on the old sofa, which was due to be picked up the next day, and looked through the pages. Grandma Hilde read aloud because Emil couldn't decipher the old writing. On the first page was the date: October 1964. “I was seventeen then,” Grandma said. “My mother gave me the notebook so that I could write down her recipes before I started my apprenticeship.”

There were recipes for plum cakes and beef roulades, for potato soup and pancakes, for Christmas cookies and pickled cucumbers. Next to many recipes were small notes: “For Walter on his birthday, he liked him very much” (Walter was a grandfather who died four years ago). “Too much sugar, less next time.” “Baked with Petra, she ate half of the dough” (Petra was Emil’s mother).

Emil laughed at the last note. “Mommy ate dough?” – “Every time,” said Grandma and smiled. “Just like you.”

## A summer in the kitchen

The idea came from Emil's mother when she saw the book lying on the kitchen table in the evening. “What if you bake some recipes together this summer?” she suggested. "Grandma, you show Emil. And Emil, you rewrite the recipes so that people can read them."

Grandma Hilde was unsure at first. Her hands were now shaking sometimes and she no longer liked standing in the kitchen for long periods of time. But Emil was thrilled. “I’ll do the hard stuff,” he promised. “You just tell me how to do it.”

This is how the summer of recipes began. Every Saturday afternoon Grandma Hilde came to them, sat at the kitchen table with a cup of tea and explained. Emil weighed, stirred, kneaded. On the first Saturday they made the plum cake. The dough stuck to everything, the plums were crooked, and the cake was a little too dark around the edges. But when Grandma Hilde tried the first piece, her eyes moistened. “Exactly,” she said. “That’s exactly how it tasted.”

## The stories behind the recipes

Grandma Hilde talked while baking. Every recipe had a story. Her mother made potato soup in the post-war years, when there was hardly anything else available. "We didn't have any sausage in it, just potatoes and onions. But my mother seasoned it so that it still tasted like something." She first cooked the roulades for Grandpa Walter when he asked her parents for her hand in marriage. “They were as tough as shoe soles,” she laughed. “And he still ate everything.”

Emil listened and wrote down. Not just the recipes, but also the stories. His mother had bought him a new, empty notebook, and on the top of each page he wrote the recipe in his still somewhat scraggly childish handwriting and underneath what grandma had said about it.

Some stories were sad. When they baked the recipe for Grandpa's favorite cake, a bee sting, Grandma Hilde became very quiet. Emil didn't know what to say. So he said nothing but continued stirring. After a while, Grandma placed her hand on his. “You have his hands,” she said. “He always stirred with such concentration.”

## The failed bee sting

Not every recipe worked. Pretty much everything went wrong with the bee sting, grandpa's favorite cake. The yeast dough didn't want to rise because Emil had made the milk too hot. The almond layer burned on the edges and the custard became lumpy. When Emil took the cake out of the oven, it looked more like a map than a bee sting.

Emil was close to tears. He had put so much effort into this cake. But Grandma Hilde laughed. She laughed so hard she had to wipe her eyes. “Do you know what my first bee sting looked like?” she asked. "Exactly like that. And Walter ate it anyway and said it was the best in the world. Then I practiced three more times until I succeeded."

They leafed back through the old magazine. In fact: next to the bee sting recipe there were four dates, written one below the other, and small notes next to them. “Milk too hot.” “Cream lumpy.” “Burned almonds.” And on the fourth date: "Finally! W. ate two pieces."

Emil immediately felt better. The next Saturday they tried again, and this time they almost succeeded. Emil entered the date in his new notebook and wrote next to it: "Second attempt. Almost as good as grandma's fourth."

## What Emil learned

By the end of the summer they had baked and cooked eleven recipes. Emil could now make a yeast dough, cut onions without crying (almost), and make a roux. He knew to refrigerate cookie dough and to season potato soup with nutmeg. But he had learned something else, something he couldn't put into words.

He now knew his grandma in a new way. Not just as the old woman who gave him money for his birthday and sometimes mixed up names. But as a girl who baked cakes for the first time at seventeen, as a young woman who cooked tough roulades, as a mother whose daughter snacked on the dough. Her life was written in that stained notebook, and now a little bit in his too.

## The gift for Christmas

For Christmas, Emil gave his grandma the new magazine. He had secretly added photos that his mother had taken while baking and a dedication on the first page: "For Grandma Hilde, so that the recipes are never lost again. From Emil, who eats the dough."

Grandma Hilde leafed through it for a long time. Then she took her old, stained notebook and gave it to Emil. “This is yours now,” she said. "And at some point you'll write your own recipes. And your own stories."

Emil has treasured the magazine ever since. It sits on his shelf, between comics and school books, and on the first Advent they bake the cookies from page 34 together. With grease stains.

## For your everyday life

Shared activities with grandparents, such as cooking, gardening or crafts, connect the generations in a special way. Conversations arise that often do not occur during a simple visit. Ideas from Emil's summer:

- **Look for shared projects.** A recipe, a flower bed, a photo album: Projects with a clear goal give grandparents and children a reason to spend time together on a regular basis.
- **Distributes the roles.** When grandparents can no longer physically do everything, children take on the strenuous parts and the grandparents pass on their knowledge. So both feel needed.
- **Ask about the stories.** Recipes, objects and photos often have memories behind them. Children love hearing about what their grandparents and parents were like as children.
- **Capture memories.** A self-written recipe booklet, recorded stories or a photo book preserve family history for future generations.
- **Allow for sad moments.** Memories of deceased family members can be painful. Children don't have to be able to comfort; Often it's enough to just be there.
- **Plan rituals.** Baking or cooking every year connects the family for years.

### Bake safely with children

For many families, snacking on dough is part of baking, just like Emil's mother. Nevertheless, it's worth pointing out: raw flour and raw eggs can contain germs that cause gastrointestinal diseases. The Federal Institute for Risk Assessment therefore advises against eating raw dough, especially for children. If you don't want to give up snacking, you can mix a small portion especially for this purpose, without eggs and with flour that has been heated beforehand. And before baking, the following applies to everyone: wash your hands, tie up long hair, and keep an adult nearby at the stove and oven.

### Making old recipes readable

Old recipe books are full of puzzles. Amounts are given in pounds, cups or “for 20 pfennigs yeast”, and baking temperatures are often completely missing because ovens used to only know “low” or “high heat”. When baking, write down what you are using today, for example 500 grams instead of one pound and 180 degrees fan oven. Take photos or scan the old pages so they don't get lost. And feel free to record your grandparents’ stories on your cell phone. Hearing her voice will be precious to the whole family later.

### When strength fades

Grandparents who can no longer stand for long do not have to miss out on cooking together. A comfortable chair at the kitchen table, simple recipes and short sessions make it easier. As with Grandma Hilde and Emil, the roles can change: the grandparents explain that the children will do the work.

### To talk to your children

Ask your children: What would you like to do with Grandma or Grandpa? And ask your grandparents: Which recipe, song or game from your childhood would you like to pass on?
