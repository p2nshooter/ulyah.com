The note was hanging on the noticeboard at the daycare center in Cologne-Ehrenfeld at the beginning of October, with a painted horse and a man in a red coat. "Dear parents, on Thursday, October 22nd, we invite you to a lantern-making afternoon. Please bring a good mood and a pair of scissors. The St. Martin's Day parade will take place on November 11th at 5 p.m."

Malte read the note twice and felt a slight discomfort. He was an electrician, he could lay cables and wire fuse boxes. But crafting with paste, tracing paper and cardboard was not one of his strengths. For Frieda's daycare birthday in the spring, he made a crown that fell apart as soon as it was put on.

"Dad," said Frieda, five years old, who was standing next to him at the bulletin board, "I want a fox lantern. Like Lotta's last year."

## The craft afternoon

The foxes' group room smelled of paste and cocoa. Parents sat at the tables on chairs that were much too small, and next to them the children wore aprons that reached down to their ankles. On every table there were cheese boxes, tracing paper in all colors, wire and scissors.

Lotta's mother, Annika, had brought her own folder. Inside were stencils that she had printed out on the computer at home: a fox's head with pointed ears, a bushy tail, eyes made of black construction paper. She trimmed each edge with millimeter precision using small nail scissors. Lotta sat next to it and painted a picture.

Malte and Frieda had no template. Malte drew a fox's head freehand on orange cardboard, and it looked more like a cat with a toothache. Frieda wanted to cut it herself and Malte let her. The ears grew to different lengths. When gluing, the tracing paper slipped and one side wrinkled. The tail, which Frieda had painstakingly knotted together from scraps of wool, ended up hanging askew at the side like a tired paintbrush.

“It’s crooked,” said Frieda and looked over at Lotta’s lantern, which looked like it came from a catalog.

“A little,” admitted Malte.

## A fox with a list

At home, Frieda put the lantern on the windowsill in her room and looked at it for a long time. Malte saw her struggling with herself. On the one hand, she had cut the ears herself and knotted the tail herself. On the other hand, there was Lotta's perfect fox.

“Can you make another one?” she asked over dinner. “A real one?”

Malte felt a small pang. He thought of Annika with her nail scissors and her stencils, and for a moment he was tempted to order a ready-made fox lantern online. They were available for twelve euros ninety-nine, with delivery by the day after tomorrow.

His wife, Derya, who was a nurse and had gotten off work early that evening, looked across the table at him. “Frieda,” she said, “what do you like about your lantern?”

Frieda thought about it. “The tail,” she finally said. "I made that all by myself. And the ears." She hesitated. “But Lotta’s is prettier.”

“Lotta’s is neater,” said Derya. “Whether she is more beautiful is another question.” Malte didn't order a lantern.

## The evening before the move

On November 10th, the evening before the St. Martin's Day parade, it rained in Cologne as it rains in Cologne in November: not heavily, but incessantly. Malte picked Frieda up from daycare on the cargo bike. She wanted to take the lantern with her to show the teachers. He had put it in a plastic bag and placed it on the bench in the cargo bike.

He braked sharply at the traffic light because a car was turning. The bag slid forward, Frieda slid behind it, and when they got home the fox was crushed. One ear was broken and the tracing paper on one side was torn.

Frieda cried. Not loudly, but quietly and desperately, the way children cry when something really important is broken. “Now I don’t have a lantern anymore,” she sobbed. “And tomorrow is Saint Martin.”

Malte sat down with her on the floor in the hallway, his wet jacket still on, and took her in his arms. He felt guilty and helpless at the same time. Then he said: “Do you actually know what Saint Martin did?”

## What Saint Martin actually did

Frieda knew the song, of course. “He shared his coat,” she said, wiping her nose on her sleeve. “With a beggar.”

"Exactly. Martin was a soldier, and he rode into a city on a cold evening. There was a man sitting at the gate who had almost nothing on and was cold. Everyone else passed him. Martin had no money with him, just his coat. And then he took his sword and cut the coat in half."

“Then he only had half a coat,” said Frieda.

"Yes. And the beggar too. That certainly wasn't nice to look at. Two men with half coats. But both of them were warm." Malte paused. "Martin didn't wait for a perfect second coat. He gave it what he had."

Frieda thought about it. Then she asked, “Can you fix a broken lantern?”

## Repair and share

They repaired them together at the kitchen table. Malte got the electrical tape from his toolbox, black and red. The broken ear was given a splint made from a popsicle stick. Frieda mended the torn tracing paper with a piece of yellow paper that she cut out in the shape of a star. In the end, the fox had a bandaged ear, a star on its cheek and still had a crooked tail. He looked like a fox who had already experienced a lot.

While cleaning up, Frieda said something that had obviously been bothering her for a long time. “Yusuf doesn’t have a lantern,” she said. Yusuf had only been in daycare for three weeks; his family came to Cologne from Iraq in the summer. He was absent from the craft afternoon because his parents couldn't read the note.

Derya and Malte looked at each other. “Then we’ll give him one,” Derya said. She rinsed an empty jam jar. Frieda covered it with scraps of tracing paper in all the colors that were left over from the fox. Malte bent a handle out of a piece of wire and tied the glass to a stick. A small LED tea light went in. It wasn't a fox lantern. But it shone colorfully like a church window.

## The move

By five o'clock in the afternoon on November 11th the rain had stopped. The families gathered in front of the daycare center, the children with their lanterns, the parents with thermoses. A man in a red coat rode in front on a patient brown horse. The congregation's trumpet choir sang “I'm going with my lantern.”

Yusuf stood a little apart next to his mother, his hands in his jacket pockets. Frieda walked over to him, the jar of jam in her hand, and held it out to him. "For you. I did that. With dad." Yusuf looked at the glass, then Frieda, then his mother. His mother smiled and said something in Arabic, and Yusuf took the stick with both hands.

They walked side by side through the dark streets of Ehrenfeld, a fox with a bandaged ear and a colored glass, and behind them Lotta with her perfect fox. At the St. Martin's fire in the schoolyard there was a wake-up man with a clay whistle for every child. Frieda broke hers in half and gave one half to Yusuf, even though he had one of his own. “Like Saint Martin,” she told him seriously. Yusuf laughed and took a bite.

On the way home, Lotta said to Frieda: "Your fox looks funny. It has a plaster." Frieda nodded happily. “He’s experienced something too.”

## For your everyday life

For many children, St. Martin's Day is one of the most beautiful celebrations of the year: light in the dark season, singing together, a fire, a sweet pastry. At the same time, making lanterns can put parents under pressure, especially when others produce seemingly perfect results. What helped Frieda's family:

- **Let the child do it.** A lantern that the child cut, glued and knotted themselves usually means more to them than a perfect one that they just watched. Crooked ears are part of it.
- **Don’t compare, just ask.** “What do you like about your lantern?” draws attention to what the child has achieved themselves instead of what others have.
- **Fix something broken instead of replacing it.** A mended ear tells a story. Children learn that things do not become worthless just because they are no longer flawless.
- **Tell the legend.** The story of the split coat is short and easy for children to understand. It gives the festival a meaning that goes beyond lights and songs.
- **Thinking about new families.** Not every family knows the traditions or can read the parents' letters. A quick personal note or a second, quickly made lantern can make a big difference.
- **Be safe.** Electric lantern sticks or LED lights are safer for small children than real candles, especially in windy and crowded conditions.

### Who Martin was

Martin lived in the 4th century. He was a Roman soldier, was later baptized, became a monk and finally bishop of Tours in what is now France. November 11th is the day he was buried. Why he only gave away half of his coat is often explained as follows: As a soldier, some of the equipment belonged to the army, and Martin gave what belonged to him. There is a beautiful message for children: sharing does not mean giving everything away, but rather sharing what you have with someone who needs it.

### To talk to your children

- Why didn't Saint Martin give away his cloak entirely, but rather share it?
- What is more important to you: that something looks perfect or that you made it yourself?
- Do you know someone at daycare or school who is new and maybe doesn't know how we celebrate?
- What could you share with someone, even if you only have half of it?
