Johanna had already packed the bag the evening before: a change of clothes, diapers, slippers with rubber soles, a photo of the family for the wardrobe and Snuffi, the cuddly blanket that looked like a trampled rabbit. She had checked everything three times. Nevertheless, she lay awake until half past two the night before the first day of daycare.

Ida, fourteen months old, was sleeping in the cot next door, on her stomach, with her bottom in the air. Little did she know that something new would begin the next morning. Johanna knew it all the more precisely.

## One year and two months

Johanna was a civil engineer in a planning office in Münster. After Ida's birth, she took fourteen months of parental leave, her husband Ben two. Now she should go back to the office, initially for twenty-five hours a week. They got the daycare place after a long search, in a small daycare center three streets away, with a garden full of sandboxes and a group of ten children under the age of three.

She was looking forward to returning to work. On conversations with adults, on plans, on the feeling of creating something that wasn't messed up again after an hour. But the closer the first day of the nativity scene came, the louder another voice became within her: What if Ida cries? What if she thinks I'm letting her down? What kind of mother leaves her child in the hands of strangers at fourteen months old?

Her own mother hadn't made things any easier. “I was at home with you for three years,” she had said, without reproach in her voice, but Johanna had heard one nonetheless.

## The first three days

The daycare center worked according to the Berlin acclimatization model, which the director explained during the admission interview. For the first three days, Johanna and Ida would stay in the group for an hour each without saying goodbye. She was supposed to sit in a corner, approachable but passive, so that Ida could explore the new surroundings from a safe haven. Only on the fourth day would there be a first brief separation.

Ida's primary care teacher was called Merve. She was in her late twenties, had a calm, friendly face and a way of sitting on the floor that immediately intrigued children. On the first day, Ida clung to Johanna's leg and watched Merve suspiciously. On the second day, she crawled all the way to the play kitchen and came back every two minutes to make sure her mother was still there. On the third day she took a wooden spoon from Merve and gave it back to her, a game they repeated for twenty minutes.

Johanna sat in the corner on a child's chair, her knees almost to her chin, and watched. She was proud of Ida. And at the same time she felt a small, irrational pang every time Ida laughed at Merve.

## The fourth day

On the fourth day, Johanna was supposed to say goodbye after ten minutes and leave the room for half an hour. Merve had explained to her how it should work: briefly and clearly. Don't disappear secretly, but say goodbye, a kiss, and then leave, even if Ida cries. “If she lets Merve comfort her, that’s a good sign,” the manager had said. “If not, we’ll get you back in a few minutes.”

Johanna knelt down in front of Ida, who was throwing building blocks into a bucket. "Ida, Mom is going out for a moment. I'll be right back." Ida looked at her, her eyes wide, and clearly understood exactly what that meant. Her face contorted. She stretched out her arms.

Johanna left. She could hear Ida crying in the hallway, a high-pitched, desperate cry that went through her whole body. She stood in front of the door, her hand on the handle, struggling with herself. After four minutes Merve came out. "She can't be comforted yet. Come back in."

Ida immediately calmed down in Johanna's arms. Johanna didn't calm down. On the way home she cried herself, quietly, behind her sunglasses.

## The fear that goes along with it

In the evening she sat with Ben at the kitchen table. “Maybe she’s not ready yet,” said Johanna. “Maybe I should stay at home for another six months.”

Ben listened and then said something cautious. "Can I be honest? I think Ida is more ready than you are."

Johanna wanted to object. But Ben told her what Merve had said to him on the phone when he called in the afternoon to ask: Ida played happily for almost an hour after Johanna returned and brought Merve a book several times. “Children feel very clearly how their parents feel when they say goodbye,” Merve said. "That's not a reproach. That's completely normal. But sometimes it helps if the other parent takes over the settling in for a few days."

Ben took the following week off. This was difficult for Johanna. It felt like she was giving up. But she agreed.

## A farewell ritual

Ben did it differently than Johanna. Not better, he said later, just different. He didn't sit in the corner, but played with Ida and Merve for the first few minutes as if Merve were an old friend. Then the two of them developed a ritual that Ida liked: Papa and Ida went to the window together, Papa went out, and Ida and Merve waved at him from the window until he disappeared around the corner. Snuffy always came with me to the window.

On the first day Ida still cried, but after three minutes she let Merve hold her on her lap. On the second day she cried briefly and then continued playing. On the third day she waved and Ben saw from the sidewalk how she then ran with Merve to the play kitchen. He sent Johanna a photo of the window. Johanna looked at it in the office on her first day at work and cried again, but differently this time.

## After three weeks

After three weeks, Johanna brought Ida herself for the first time. She was afraid that everything would start again. She did the ritual as Ben had shown her: slippers on, snuffball in hand, together to the window. “Bye, mom is coming after her nap.”

Ida looked at her, briefly stretched out her arms and then let them fall again. Merve knelt next to her. Ida turned to Merve and said something that sounded like “Building.” Then she waved.

Johanna walked the three blocks to the office and felt something she hadn't expected: relief. And, underneath, a bit of melancholy. Both could be possible, she thought.

When she picked up Ida in the afternoon, Ida ran towards her with her arms outstretched and called out “Mom!” Merve said that Ida baked a cake for everyone in the sandpit and ate it alone with a spoon for the first time at lunch. “Almost alone,” she corrected herself with a laugh and pointed to Ida’s sweater.

## For your everyday life

Getting used to crèche or daycare is an emotional time for many families. Children have to build trust in a new caregiver, parents have to learn to let go. Tears are often part of it, on both sides. What helped Ida's family:

- **Plan enough time.** Getting used to it often takes two to four weeks, sometimes longer. If you don't start your career on the first day of crèche, you take a lot of pressure off.
- **Don't leave secretly.** A clear, short goodbye is more reliable for children than an unnoticed disappearance, even if it costs tears.
- **Develop a farewell ritual.** Waving at the window, a certain sentence, a cuddly toy: recurring processes give children security.
- **Talk about your own feelings.** Feelings of guilt and separation pain are normal. It helps to share them with your partner, teacher or other parents instead of carrying them alone.
- **Involve the other parent.** Sometimes the separation is easier when another trusted family member takes over the adjustment. This is not a defeat, but an opportunity.
- **Trust the teacher.** Professionals have accompanied many adjustments and recognize when a child is ready. Ask, let us tell you and stay in the conversation.

### Berlin or Munich model?

Many crèches in Germany work according to one of two well-known models. In the **Berlin Settlement Model**, like in Ida's daycare center, the child first builds a relationship with a permanent caregiver, while one parent remains in the room as a safe haven. After a few days, the first short separations follow, which slowly become longer. The **Munich model** usually takes a little longer. Here the child gets to know the whole group, the rooms and several specialists, and the parents initially provide more intensive support in everyday life. During the admission interview, ask how your daycare works so that you know what to expect.

### If the child is different at home

In the first few weeks after starting daycare, many children are more clingy at home, sleep worse or get angry more quickly. The new environment takes energy. Many people also get sick more often in the first year of crèche because they come into contact with many new pathogens. So plan quiet afternoons and buffers for sick days during this time. Things get better over time, for both children and parents.

### To talk to your children

With very young children, it's less about conversations than shared moments. If you have older siblings or kindergarten children who are about to make a change, you can ask:

- What was it like when you went to daycare for the first time? What do you remember?
- What helps you when you have to say goodbye to mom or dad?
- Which cuddly toy or object gives you courage when something is new?
- How could you help a small child who is new to daycare and is crying?
