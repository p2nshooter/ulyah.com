The sugar bag had been in the living room for a week, in a corner behind the sofa, wrapped in a bedsheet. Emil wasn't allowed to look at her, that was the agreement. But he saw her tip peeking out from under the sheet, blue with silver stars, and he knew she was almost as tall as he was.

Grandma Karin had made them in Pirna, out of cardboard and crepe paper and with a patience that Emil's mother Franziska admired. “For my first grandson to go to school,” she had said, “I won’t do it from the store.” She had been working on it for three weeks.

Emil, six years old, was supposed to start school on Saturday at a primary school in Dresden-Striesen. The whole family was looking forward to it. Grandma Karin and Grandpa Dieter came from Pirna, Aunt Susi from Chemnitz, and Emil's father Robert had planned a table for twenty people in the garden of the house where they lived, with cake, bratwursts and potato salad.

Only Emil wasn't happy.

## What Emil thinks at night

Emil didn't say it directly. He just said things like “I don’t want to get up so early” or “Can’t I go to daycare for another year?” He slept worse than usual, went to bed with his parents at night, and had stomach pains that came and went. The pediatrician found nothing. “That’s often the excitement,” he said friendly and looked at Emil over his glasses. “This will pass.”

Franziska spoke to Emil in the evening when they were lying together on his bed and the light was already off. Emil spoke more easily in the dark. "What's the hardest thing about school when you think about it?" she asked.

Emil was silent for so long that she thought he had fallen asleep. Then it came, one thing after another. That he couldn't read yet, but his friend Jakob from daycare could. That the school was so big and he would definitely get lost. That he didn't know where the toilet was and what would happen if he had to go in the middle of class. That he didn't know anyone because Jacob went to a different school. And that maybe the teacher was strict.

“And the sugar cone?” asked Franziska. “Aren’t you looking forward to it?”

“Yes,” said Emil. "But the sugar bag doesn't come with you to school. Only on the first day."

## A test run

Franziska and Robert decided to take Emil's concerns seriously one by one. On the Monday before starting school, they called the school office and asked if they could come over with Emil. The secretary, Ms. Lehmann, agreed immediately. "The teachers will be here on Thursday and will prepare the classrooms. Come at ten."

On Thursday they walked the route to school together, walking for twelve minutes, past a bakery, a large chestnut tree and a zebra crossing. Emil counted the steps to the school gate until he miscounted. At school, Ms. Lehmann showed them the ground floor where the first grades were located. She showed them the toilets, right next to the classroom, with small sinks at child height. "If you ever have to," she said to Emil, "then get in touch and just say it. Everyone does that here."

Then a woman came out of one of the rooms with a box full of name tags. She had gray curls and glasses on a colorful chain. “You must be Emil,” she said. "I'm Ms. Schubert, your class teacher. Would you like to see where you'll be sitting?"

Emil was allowed to look at his seat, second row by the window, with his name on a sign in the shape of an apple. He was allowed to touch the large blackboard and go to the window that overlooked the schoolyard with the climbing frame. Ms. Schubert told him that no one had to be able to read in first grade. “That’s what I’m here for,” she said. “Otherwise I would be completely unemployed.”

On the way home, Emil was quiet, but a different kind of quiet. He stopped at the big chestnut and picked one up. “I’ll take them with me on Saturday,” he said. “As a good luck charm.”

## The school introduction

On Saturday, Emil wore a new shirt that was itchy and pants that Grandma Karin had bought him. The chestnut was in his pocket. The school orientation began at ten o'clock in the school gym, which was full of families, with grandparents, aunts, uncles and cameras. The headmistress gave a speech that Emil didn't understand. Then the second graders put on a play about a raven that wouldn't fly, and Emil laughed out loud in two places.

The new classes were then called. Emil had to go forward, alone, without mom and dad. He clutched the chestnut in his pocket. Ms. Schubert stood at the front with a sign "1b" and smiled at him, and when he got to her, there was a boy with freckles and a gap in his teeth standing next to him who whispered to him: "I'm Malik. I can't read yet either."

The children went with Ms. Schubert to her classroom for their first lesson, which lasted half an hour. They drew a picture of themselves and stuck it on the wall. Emil painted himself with a chestnut in his hand.

When the class returned to the gym, the family was waiting with the bag of sugar. Grandma Karin had tears in her eyes when Emil got her. The bag was so heavy that Robert had to hold it so Emil didn't fall over. Emil had to smile five times for the photo.

## Too much

In the afternoon in the garden it was too much. Twenty adults who all wanted something from Emil: a photo, a hug, an answer to the question of what school was like. Aunt Susi wanted to know whether he liked the teacher yet. Grandpa Dieter wanted to show him how to put the case away properly. A neighbor asked if he could write his name yet.

Emil disappeared. After ten minutes, Franziska found him in his room, under the blanket, with the bag of sugar next to him, which he hadn't even unpacked yet. “I don’t want to talk anymore,” he said.

Franziska sat down next to him. She thought about the cake, about the guests, about Grandma Karin, who would definitely ask about Emil right away. Then she said, "Okay. You don't have to talk anymore. Do you want to unpack the sugar bag alone? Just the two of us?"

Emil nodded. They unpacked the bag together, slowly, piece by piece: colored pencils, a book about dinosaurs, a flashlight, gummy bears, a lunch box with a raven on it and at the bottom a small letter from Grandma Karin, which Franziska read out loud. "Dear Emil, learning is like climbing. It's hard at the beginning, but then you can keep going."

After half an hour, Emil went back into the garden, ate a piece of cake and showed Grandma Karin the raven lunch box. Then he played football with his cousin until it got dark. Nobody asked him what it was like at school anymore. And that's exactly why he told it himself, at dinner, in detail and with his mouth full of bratwurst: about Frau Schubert, about the raven who didn't want to fly, and about Malik, who couldn't read either.

## For your everyday life

For many children, the first day of school is exciting and beautiful - and sometimes scary. Behind stomach aches, bad sleep or the desire to go to daycare “for another year” there are often very specific worries. What helped Emil's family:

- **Listen to the concerns one at a time.** A quiet moment, such as in the dark in the evening, helps children to express their fears. Often it's small, very practical things, like the question of where to go to the toilet.
- **Visit the school beforehand.** Many schools allow a short visit before the first day. Having already seen the room, the toilets and the teacher takes away a lot of uncertainty.
- **Practice the route to school.** Walking together, with fixed landmarks such as a tree or a bakery, provides orientation.
- **Make it clear that no one has to be able to do everything.** You learn reading, writing and arithmetic at school. Hearing this takes the pressure off children who compare themselves to others.
- **Allow retreat.** A large family gathering can be overwhelming for an excited child. A quiet moment alone or with a parent often helps more than any program.
- **Don't question.** Children often talk on their own if they aren't constantly asked. Patience is worth it.

### Sugar cone or school cone?

In Saxony and Thuringia it is called a sugar cone, and in most other regions it is called a school cone. The custom of giving children starting school a filled bag has been documented in Central Germany since the early 19th century and has spread from there. It is no longer just filled with sweets. Many families put useful things in it, such as pens, a lunch box or a first book, as well as a little something to play with. A tip for large bags: Fill the bottom part with a small pillow or crumpled paper so that the bag doesn't become too heavy. A child who can barely hold her is only half as happy in the photo.

### The first weeks of school

Many first graders are surprisingly tired in the first few weeks. Sitting still, listening, new children, new rules: it all takes energy. Plan for rest and free play in the afternoons rather than new courses, and take into account that your child will want to sleep earlier than during daycare. Tears for no apparent reason also occur in this phase. They usually just show that a child has a lot to process.

### To talk to your children

- What are you looking forward to in school and what are you maybe a little worried about?
- What would help you if you got lost at school?
- What could be your lucky charm for the first day of school?
- What do you do when everything becomes too much for you, and what can we do to help you?
