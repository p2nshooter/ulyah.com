The class work was folded in the side pocket of Tim's backpack, between a crushed lunch box and a tennis ball. On the first page, written in red ballpoint pen and circled, was “5.” Below: "Please have the parents sign it. B. Krämer."

Tim, thirteen years old, seventh grade at a high school in Stuttgart-Vaihingen, got his work back on Tuesday. It was Tuesday of the following week. He didn't sign the work, didn't show it, didn't mention it. Every morning when he put his backpack on, he could feel her, even though she weighed almost nothing.

## The week of silence

Tim wasn't a bad student. He was good in German and English and very good in biology. But something had slipped in mathematics this semester. He had been home with a bad case of the flu for two weeks in October, just as the term and equations class had started. When he came back he didn't understand half of it. He hadn't said anything to anyone. He had thought he would catch up.

His parents, Andreas and Melanie, were both engineers at a large automotive supplier. Mathematics had always been a given in the family. Tim's older sister Hannah, now a senior, had never gotten lower than a B in math. Static and gears were discussed at the dinner table, and when Tim brought home a C in fifth grade, his father raised his eyebrows and said, “Well, there’s more.”

Tim thought of those eyebrows when he thought of the five. He slept worse this week. He was irritable, argued with Hannah over small things, and spent his evenings in his room with headphones on. Once he sat down at the desk, took a ballpoint pen and rehearsed his mother's signature on a piece of paper. Then he tore the note into small pieces and threw it into the trash can in the bathroom.

## The email

On Tuesday evening, Andreas sat at his laptop in the living room and read the emails that had come via the school's parent app. One was from Ms. Krämer, Tim's math teacher. "Dear family, I have not yet received Tim's signed class assignment from last Tuesday back. Since the grade is significantly below his previous achievements, I would be happy to have a short conversation."

Andreas read the email twice. He felt the anger rising within him, hot and fast. Not because of the grade, but because Tim had kept it a secret. He got up and went to the stairs. "Tim! Come down here. Now."

Melanie came out of the kitchen and looked at her husband. She knew that tone. "What's up?" Andreas showed her the email. She read it and then put her hand on his arm. “Take a quick breath before he’s down,” she said quietly. "Please."

## At the dining table

Tim came down the stairs, slowly, with the face of someone who knows what's coming. When he saw the laptop, he turned pale. He sat down at the dining table without being asked.

Andreas sat down opposite him. He had a sentence in his head that started with “How can you?” He swallowed it. Instead he said: "Ms. Krämer has written. Do you want to show me the work?"

Tim went upstairs and got her. He placed it on the table, note facing up, and looked at his hands. Andreas leafed through them. The first problem was correct, the second half. In the equations from problem three onwards there were almost only red lines.

“Why didn’t you show them to us?” asked Melanie.

Tim was silent for a long time. Then he said without looking up: "Because then you'll be disappointed. Because everyone here is good at math. And I'm the only one who can't do it."

It was quiet at the table. Andreas looked at his son, his shoulders hunched, his hands holding on to the edge of the table. And he remembered something he hadn't thought about in years.

## The father's story

“I have to tell you something,” said Andreas. "Something I've never told you before. And neither has Hannah."

Tim looked up.

"I got stuck in ninth grade. Because of math and physics. Two fives on my report card." Andreas smiled wryly. "Your grandpa didn't speak to me for three days. I was so ashamed that I barely spoke to anyone the entire repeat year. I thought I was stupid. I thought I'd never amount to anything."

“But you’re an engineer,” Tim said incredulously.

"Yes. Because I had a teacher in the repeat year, Mr. Seitz, who showed me that I wasn't stupid, but that I had just lost touch at some point. We went back to the point where I had dropped out and from there we went step by step." Andreas pushed the work back to Tim. "A five doesn't say who you are. It just says something is missing. And we have to find out what."

Tim talked about the flu in October, the two weeks he missed, and how he didn't dare ask in class because everyone else seemed to know how to do it. Melanie nodded. “That’s fine,” she said. “That’s exactly where it started.”

“And the signature,” Andreas said at the end, seriously but not loudly. "Hiding wasn't okay. Not because you got in trouble. But because we couldn't help you unless we knew."

Tim nodded. He didn't say anything about the torn note in the bathroom. But Andreas could see that there was something else and decided not to ask about it.

## A plan

Melanie signed the work. Andreas wrote Ms. Krämer a reply, and a week later the three of them sat with her in the school meeting room. Ms. Krämer was relieved that the cause was so clear. She gave Tim a list of practice problems on exactly the topics he had missed and offered to join him in the math remedial class on Thursday.

Hannah, of all people, helped at home. She sat down with Tim twice a week for half an hour, and she explained equations differently than teachers did. “Imagine a scale,” she said. “Whatever you take away from the left, you have to take away from the right, otherwise it will tip over.” Tim understood. For the first time since October.

Andreas didn't interfere. That was the hardest task for him. He wanted to explain it himself, but he sensed that Tim didn't want to hear it from him. Instead, every Sunday at breakfast he asked, “How are the equations going?” And listened.

## The next job

In February the class wrote the next math test. Tim came home the day he got it back and, without saying a word, placed it on the dining room table with the note face up. A three.

Andreas looked at her and raised his eyebrows. Tim winced. Then he noticed his father was smiling. “Do you know what that is?” asked Andreas. "That's two whole grades better. In three months. That's more than I managed in a whole year back then."

That evening, Andreas signed the work, and underneath it, in small font, he wrote something that Tim only saw later: "Proud of you. Dad."

## For your everyday life

Bad grades are part of school. For many children, however, the fear of their parents' reaction is worse than the grade itself - especially in families where certain subjects are taken for granted. Anyone who hides their grade often sends a signal: I'm afraid of disappointing you. What helped Tim's family:

- **Take a deep breath first, then talk.** The first reaction shapes the conversation. If you sort out anger or disappointment first, you can listen instead of blaming.
- **Look for causes instead of blame.** Absences, missed basics, exam anxiety or concentration problems: There is often a concrete, solvable reason behind a bad grade.
- **Tell about your own difficulties.** Children are relieved to hear that their parents also failed once and still made it.
- **Address the hiding, but don't dramatize it.** Honesty becomes easier when children experience that they get help after bad news, not punishment.
- **Work with the teacher.** Remedial lessons, targeted practice tasks and a joint look at the gaps often help more than expensive tutoring.
- **Don't always give help yourself.** Sometimes siblings, classmates or other adults explain something better because the relationship is less strained.
- **Recognize progress.** An improvement from a five to a three is a major achievement, even if it's not an A.

### When the fear of working grows

Some children know the material at home, but suddenly everything is gone in their class work. If blackouts, stomach aches or sleepless nights before work become more frequent, test anxiety may be behind it. It is helpful to practice working at home under real conditions, with a time limit and without assistance, so that the situation becomes more familiar. Simple breathing exercises before starting work calm many children. If the fear nevertheless increases, counseling teachers and school psychological services offer support.

### To talk to your children

- What would you do if you brought home a bad grade? How would you feel then?
- Is there a subject in which you currently feel like you are losing touch?
- Who would you confide in if you didn't understand something at school?
- What would you like us to say or do if you ever get a bad grade?
