The Albrecht family calendar hung on the refrigerator door, and it was colorful. Red for Lukas: football on Tuesdays and Thursdays, piano on Wednesdays. Green for Hanna: swimming on Mondays, ballet on Fridays, and the theater group on Saturday mornings. Blue for Mama Christine, who, as a tax advisor, often met clients on Saturdays. Yellow for dad Markus, who was the soccer coach of the E-youth twice a week, including Lukas' team. At the weekend there were birthdays, visits to the grandparents in Fulda, tournaments, performances.

There wasn't a single white spot. There was no field with nothing in it.

## The question at dinner

On a Friday evening in October, Hanna, nine years old, asked over dinner: “What are we doing on Sunday?” Christine looked at the calendar. “In the morning, Lukas has a game in Hünfeld, in the afternoon we have coffee at Aunt Petra’s, and in the evening we still have to prepare the presentation for your specialist lecture.”

Hanna put down her fork. “Can’t we just do nothing?”

It was quiet at the table. Lukas, eleven, looked up from his plate. "Yeah. Just nothing. Stay in your pajamas."

Markus wanted to say something that that wasn't possible, that the game was important, that Aunt Petra had already baked cake. But Christine beat him to it. “When was the last time we just did nothing?” she asked. Nobody could remember.

## A Sunday with nothing on the calendar

They canceled Aunt Petra, with a guilty conscience and promised to come in two weeks. They didn't reschedule Lukas' game, but Markus went there alone with him, and then it was over. Hanna finished the lecture on Saturday, spurred on by the prospect of a Sunday off.

And then came the first Sunday without a program. Nobody set an alarm. Hanna woke up at half past seven, crept into the master bedroom and crawled under the covers. Lukas came half an hour later, with a comic under his arm. At some point all four were lying in their parents' bed, reading, dozing, talking. Markus talked about his own childhood, when on Sundays there was only breakfast and a walk because the shops were closed and there were only three programs on television.

They got up at ten, not because they had to, but because they were hungry. Luke suggested pancakes. Christine pulled out her grandmother's recipe. The four of them baked in their pajamas, and the kitchen looked like an earthquake.

## The boredom in the afternoon

In the afternoon, something happened that Christine had expected: the children became bored. Hanna walked around the apartment and asked every ten minutes what she should do. Lukas wanted to go to the game console. “First outside,” said Markus. “What should we do outside?” asked Lukas. “I don’t know,” said Markus and shrugged his shoulders.

Nothing happened for fifteen minutes. Then Lukas took the old football out of the basement and shot it against the garage wall. Hanna found chalk and drew a huge hopscotch field on the sidewalk in front of the house. The neighborhood children came along. It was an afternoon of made-up games, scraped knees and an argument about the rules of the game that resolved itself.

That evening, as she went to bed, Hanna said, “That was the best Sunday.” Christine asked, “What was best?” Hanna thought for a long time. “That no one said we had to go.”

## Luke and the game console

The first Sunday off wasn't as easy for everyone. Lukas, the older one, came to his father in the evening and said somewhat embarrassedly: "I actually wanted to play games all afternoon. I'm a little angry that I wasn't allowed to." Markus nodded. He remembered how, even when he was eleven, he would have preferred to spend every free minute in front of the television.

"You know what," he said, "let's think about this together for the next Nothing Sunday. Maybe there's an hour of console time in the evening if we were outside beforehand. But not the whole day. Otherwise it's not a nothing day, but a screen day."

Luke thought that was fair. The next Sunday off, he played for an hour in the evening, and because he had spent the afternoon building a soapbox with the neighborhood children, it was surprisingly easy for him to stop. “I have to continue building tomorrow,” he explained when the hour was up and turned the console off by itself. Markus looked over at Christine and they both smiled.

## What the grandparents said

Aunt Petra also needed an explanation because she initially felt offended. Christine called her and told her about the calendar without white spaces and about Hanna's question at dinner. To her surprise, Petra on the other end of the line went completely quiet. “We used to have a Sunday like that,” she said finally. "Mom made pancakes and we were allowed to stay in our pajamas until Dad came from the bakery. I had completely forgotten about that."

Since then, Aunt Petra would sometimes just come over on a nothing Sunday, without registering and without cake, and bake pancakes with the children. This, everyone decided, was consistent with the rules. A visit that expects nothing is, after all, a kind of nothing.

## A white field in the calendar

On Monday, Christine took a white piece of paper and stuck it on the calendar over the first Sunday of each month. Then she wrote in large letters: “Nothing.” The family decided together the rules for these days: no alarm clock, no appointments, no visitors, breakfast in their pajamas. They decided spontaneously what else would happen.

Not everyone was immediately enthusiastic. Markus found it difficult to turn down invitations, and Lukas grumbled when a tournament fell on a non-Sunday. In this case, they decided to play the tournament and postpone the nothing day until the following Sunday. “The rule is important,” said Christine, “but it shouldn’t hold us captive.”

## What changed during the week

After a few months, Christine noticed something surprising. The nothing Sundays also changed the days of the week. The children asked themselves whether they really wanted to go to all the courses. Hanna gave up ballet because she preferred to play with her friend on Fridays. Lukas kept soccer and piano, but he practiced more calmly since he knew there were days without expectations.

“We thought we were doing something good for the children if we offered them a lot,” Christine said to her sister. “They showed us that sometimes the most valuable thing is what isn’t on the calendar.”

## For your everyday life

Many families have busy weeks with school, work, classes and appointments. But free, unplanned time is important for children: to relax, to play freely and to discover their own interests. Ideas from the Albrecht family’s everyday life:

- **Plans for unplanned time.** A set “nothing day” per month or one free afternoon per week protects free time from the calendar. Whatever is entered as an appointment will be kept.
- **Endure boredom.** Boredom is unpleasant, but it is often the beginning of your own ideas. Parents don't need to immediately fill them with suggestions or screens.
- **Find rituals without a program.** Breakfast in your pajamas, cooking together, cuddling up in your parents' bed: rituals that cost nothing and don't require anything to be achieved often become the most valuable memories for children.
- **Ask whether courses are still fun.** Hobbies should enrich, not burden you. Thinking together about what stays and what doesn't takes pressure off the week.
- **Stay flexible.** Fixed rules for free time help, but should not become rigid. If you have an important appointment coming up, you can go hiking on the day off.

### Why free play is so valuable

In free play, children decide for themselves what they do, with whom and according to which rules. This is precisely where its value lies: you invent, negotiate, fail, and try again without an adult dictating the goal. Pediatric and educational experts have emphasized for years that such play strengthens creativity, problem solving, social skills and the ability to self-regulate. A course can teach you a lot. But children only learn the feeling of being able to organize an afternoon themselves when they have free time.

### How many appointments are enough?

There is no fixed number because children and families are different. Watch out for signs of overwork: Your child is often tired or irritable, has little time for friends, does homework late in the evening or complains of a stomach ache before certain appointments. Then it's worth taking a look at the calendar together. Some families agree on a simple rule, such as a maximum of two fixed appointments per week per child, so that there is room every day.

### Ideas for nothing days

When boredom sets in, ready-made programs don't help, but sometimes a pool of options from which children choose themselves: build a cave out of blankets, paint with chalk, go into the forest, get an old board game out of the closet, look at photo albums, listen to a radio play, browse in the library or cook together. Write such ideas on small pieces of paper and place them in a jar. If you don't know what to do, take one.

### Parents don't need anything either

Free time is not just good for children. Parents who don't have to do anything themselves are more patient and present. A day of nothing is therefore not a sacrifice for the children, but rather a break for the whole family.

### To talk to your children

At the next dinner ask: If we didn't have to do anything for a whole day, what would you most like to do? The answers often reveal what children are missing in everyday life.

Feel free to hang the answers next to the calendar. When the week gets too busy again, the note reminds you what your children actually want, and sometimes it's just a morning in their pajamas.
