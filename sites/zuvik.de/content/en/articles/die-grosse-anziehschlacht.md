The tights were on the floor. Ronja lay next to it, on her back, her arms spread out like a starfish, and screamed. Her mother, Julia Schneider, knelt nearby, a boot in her hand, looking at the clock. 7:42 am. Ronja had to be at daycare at eight and Julia's video conference began at half past eight.

"Ronja, it's cold outside. You need tights."

"NO! I want the glittery dress! Without anything!"

The glittering dress was a gift from Aunt Sara, made of thin tulle with sewn-on sequins, and it was undoubtedly the most beautiful piece of clothing Ronja had ever owned. It was also completely unsuitable on a January morning in Hanover with minus four degrees.

## Three years and a will of your own

Ronja had just turned three, and with her third birthday a new word seemed to have entered her vocabulary, replacing all the others: “Self!” She wanted to go down the stairs herself, pour the milk herself, unlock the front door herself. And above all, she wanted to decide for herself what she wore.

Julia and her husband Ben had heard about this phase. They had read that children of this age discover their own will and that this is healthy and important. But no book had prepared her for what it feels like to wrestle for a pair of pantyhose every morning while the clock ticks.

In the end, that January morning, the clock won. Julia put Ronja's tights on while Ronja screamed, put her in the snowsuit and carried her to the car. She put the glittery dress in a bag “for later.” In daycare, Ronja clung to Julia's leg and cried. Julia arrived late to the conference, red-eyed and feeling like she had failed.

## What Ben did differently

Ben's mornings weren't better, just different. He had developed a method he called “distraction”: While Ronja was putting on a shoe, he told a story about a bear that had lost its socks. That worked until Ronja realized what he was doing. “Dad, you're only talking so I can get dressed!” she said indignantly one morning and took off her shoe again.

At the weekend, the two of them sat at the breakfast table, perplexed, while Ronja, for once in a glittery dress over a wool sweater, peacefully ate her sandwich. “She doesn’t want to go to daycare naked,” Ben said. “She wants to decide.”

“But she can’t decide whether to wear a summer dress when it’s frosty,” said Julia.

“No,” Ben said. "But maybe she doesn't have to. Maybe it's enough if she's allowed to decide something."

## The closet with two options

On Sunday evening they rearranged Ronja's closet together with her. The summer clothes went into a box in the attic, “they are now sleeping until summer,” explained Julia. What was left were things that were suitable for the winter. And every evening they put two outfits together on the chair: for example, the red pants with the sweater with the fox, or the jeans with the striped sweater. Ronja was allowed to choose.

They found a compromise for the glittery dress: Ronja was allowed to wear it, but over the tights and under the sweater, “like a secret.” Ronja thought the idea of ​​the secret was great.

On Monday morning, Ronja stood in front of the chair, looked long and hard at both options, as if she were deciding the future of the world, and chose the fox sweater. Then she put it on, backwards, with the fox facing backwards.

Julia opened her mouth. Then she closed it again. The fox pointed backwards. So what? Ronja was dressed, she was warm, and she was incredibly proud.

## Time as a gift

The second thing that changed was time. Julia did the math: If Ronja had to get dressed herself, it would take her about fifteen minutes. When Julia put them on, despite resistance, it took twenty minutes and ended in tears. So she got up fifteen minutes earlier and planned her dressing time more generously.

“I realized that the real opponent wasn’t Ronja, but the clock,” Julia later told her friend. "If I have time, I can watch her fight with the sleeve. If I don't have time, I take the sleeve away from her and then she fights with me."

For days when things had to be done really quickly, they had an agreement: “Today is a quick day, today I’ll help you.” Ronja usually accepted this because quick days were rare and because they were announced in advance.

## A conversation with the teacher

A few weeks after the change, the teacher asked Ms. Okafor when we picked her up: "What have you changed at home? Ronja arrives much more relaxed." Julia talked about the chair with the two outfits and the fox looking behind. Ms. Okafor laughed. "We do it in a similar way at daycare. When they go out, the children are allowed to choose between mud pants and rain pants, but not whether they want to wear pants."

She also said that there were a lot of children in Ronja's group in the same phase. "At three, they want to take the world into their own hands. If you give them small areas in which they can really decide, they fight less for the big ones."

Julia found this sentence so fitting that she wrote it on a piece of paper and hung it on the refrigerator. Ben added below in scrawled writing: “And if they do fight: take a deep breath.” Both sentences were still hanging there long after Ronja went to school.

Of course there were still difficult mornings. Once Ronja insisted on wearing her diving goggles to daycare, another time she just wanted socks in the same color as her breakfast porridge. But these things were small, and Julia had learned to simply not fight the small battles.

## What happened to the fox

In the spring, when the summer dresses came back from the attic, there was a small party. Ronja put on the glittering dress, this time officially, with no secret underneath, because it was twenty degrees outside. She spun in the hallway until she felt dizzy.

The fox sweater was still on the chair. Ronja looked at him and said: "The fox has been looking backwards all winter. Now he can move forward." She folded the sweater neatly, with the fox on top, and carried it into the attic box herself.

## For your everyday life

The need to make decisions and do things yourself is part of the normal development of small children. It's exhausting, but it's also a sign that a child is discovering his or her own effectiveness. What helped Ronja's family:

- **Gives a real choice between two options.** “Do you want the red pants or the blue pants?” gives the child freedom of choice without giving up control over the weather and occasion.
- **Tidy up the closet according to the season.** What isn't in the closet can't become a point of contention. Things that don’t fit the season are allowed to “sleep”.
- **Plan time for DIY.** Toddlers take significantly longer to get dressed. If you take this time into account from the outset, you avoid fighting against the clock.
- **Leave imperfections.** A sweater worn backwards or two different socks won't hurt anyone. The pride of having achieved it yourself is more important.
- **Find compromises with imagination.** The favorite piece as a “secret” under the warm sweater, the sunglasses in autumn, the princess hat over the cap: there are often creative solutions.
- **Announces exceptions.** On days when things have to be done really quickly, an announcement the evening before helps: “Tomorrow is a quick day, I’ll help you.”

### Understanding the autonomy phase

What was once called the defiance phase is now mostly called the autonomy phase by experts. Many children go through it between the ages of two and four. They discover that they have a will of their own and can make a difference, but they are still barely able to regulate their disappointment and frustration. When your own plan, glitter dress in the frost, fails because of reality, it ends in tears and screams. This is tiring, but a healthy developmental step. The fewer fights over small things, the more strength remains for the few things that parents really need to stick with.

### Clothing that makes independence easier

Some things can be made easier with the right clothing. A three-year-old child can often handle pants with elastic instead of buttons, shoes with Velcro fasteners and jackets with large zippers on their own. A popular trick for jackets: The jacket lies open on the floor with the hood facing the child's feet. He puts both arms into the sleeves and swings the jacket over his head and it's on. To ensure that the shoes end up on the right foot, you can cut a sticker in half and stick one half into each shoe. If the shoes are right next to each other, the whole picture emerges.

### Is my child warm enough?

Children freeze differently than adults, and hands and feet often feel cool even when the child is warm. Therefore, it is better to feel your neck: if it is warm and dry, the clothing fits. If it's sweaty, it's too much. Several thin layers, the onion principle, are easier to adapt depending on the weather and movement than a thick sweater.

### To talk to your children

When you're choosing it in the evening, ask: What do you like about this sweater? What things are your favorite things? Children who are allowed to talk about their clothes feel that their wishes are taken seriously, even if not every wish can be fulfilled every day.

And feel free to tell them which piece of clothing you liked best as a child. Children love stories like this, and they show that their parents once had a glittery dress or a favorite sweater that they didn't want to part with.
