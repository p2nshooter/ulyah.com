“I’m not your babysitter!”

The door slammed so loudly that a picture frame hung askew in the hallway. Jonas Hartmann, twelve years old, sat on the bed in his room, his fists clenched, fighting back tears that he felt far too old for. His parents, Claudia and Peter, stood outside in the hallway, looking at each other perplexed. Behind them, at the kitchen door, stood the twins Lotta and Lena, seven years old, their eyes wide.

It had started innocently enough. Claudia had asked whether Jonas could go to the playground with the twins again after school because she had a conference call herself. It was the third time this week.

## How it came to this

When the twins were born, Jonas was five. He was happy, admired the babies, held the bottles. And because his parents were completely overwhelmed with two infants, they often praised him: "You're such a great big brother. You help us so much."

Jonas was happy to help. He read to the twins when they were three. He taught them to ride bikes when they were five. He picked them up from after-school care since they started school. And at some point, without anyone consciously deciding, helping became a duty. When Mom worked, Jonas looked after him. When Dad went shopping, Jonas looked after him. If the twins argued, Jonas was supposed to mediate.

And Jonas himself? He gave up his soccer practice last year because it clashed with the twins' pick-up times. His friends met at the skate park after school, but he usually didn't have time. And when he complained, it was said: “Oh, you’re the big one.”

## The conversation in the evening

That evening Peter knocked on Jonas' door. Jonas didn't say anything, but he let him in. Peter sat down in the desk chair and was silent for a while. Then he asked, “How long have you been feeling this way?”

Jonas shrugged his shoulders. Then it bubbled out of him. That he never has time for himself. That his friends stopped asking him because he always said no anyway. That he misses football. That the twins annoyed him when he had to supervise them all the time, even though he actually liked them. And that he felt bad for thinking all that.

Peter listened without interrupting. When Jonas was finished, he said, "You're right. We've made it far too easy for you to help us. And we've forgotten that you're still a child yourself."

Jonas looked up in surprise. He had expected contradiction, an explanation as to why there was no other way. Not with that.

## The family plan

At the weekend, the family sat down at the kitchen table, all five of them. Claudia had drawn up a big weekly plan. “We’re now looking together at who needs what and when,” she said.

It turned out that many things could be different. Claudia was able to schedule her telephone conferences in the morning on two days. Peter was able to finish work early on Thursdays. The twins were old enough to stay longer at the after-school care center twice a week, and there was a theater group there anyway that they found exciting. For one afternoon a week, they asked their neighbor, Ms. Demir, a retired teacher who was happy to have company.

And Jonas? He was allowed to play football again. Tuesdays and Thursdays, without exception. “If someone asks if you can look after the twins, you can say no,” Claudia explained. “And if you say yes, we’ll pay you for it, like a real babysitter.”

Jonas laughed for the first time this weekend. “Five euros an hour?” – “Three,” said Peter. “Four,” said Jonas. They agreed on four.

## What the twins learned

The change was not easy for Lotta and Lena. They missed that Jonas was always there. In the first few weeks they argued with each other more often because there was no longer any big brother to mediate. Claudia and Peter had to intervene again themselves, which was exhausting, but also showed how much Jonas had carried so far.

Over time, the twins learned to resolve their disputes alone more often. And they learned something else: When Jonas played with them now, it was because he wanted to, not because he had to. This felt different. When he played football with them in the garden on Sundays, he laughed, ran, and let them score goals. Before, he had just watched them, bored.

## A relapse into old patterns

In the spring, Peter got sick for three weeks and Claudia was left alone with everything. On a particularly busy day, the old question slipped out: "Jonas, can you pick up the girls from after-school care today? And maybe do homework with them?"

Jonas had training. He looked at his mother for a long time. She looked tired and he could see her own irritation at her question. “I can pick it up,” he finally said. "No homework. I'll go to training afterwards, you promised."

Claudia took a breath. "You're right. Thanks for picking me up. I'll do the homework with them tonight." She later called Ms. Demir, who was happy to step in and gave Jonas the four euros to pick it up that evening, even though he didn't want to accept it at first.

This moment, Claudia later said, showed her how easy it is to fall back into old patterns when things get tight. And that her son had now learned to set friendly but clear boundaries. That, she thought, was perhaps the most valuable thing he had taken away from this whole story.

## A year later

A year later, Jonas played in the team again as a striker. At the season finale, the twins stood on the sidelines and screamed their hearts out. They had painted a poster: “JONAS IS THE BEST BROTHER”. When he scored a goal, they ran onto the field and had to be sent back by the referee.

After the game, Jonas hugged his sisters, sweaty and happy. Claudia watched the three of them and thought about the day the door slammed. She was glad her son had raised his voice back then. Otherwise they might never have realized how much they had charged him.

## For your everyday life

Older siblings are often happy to help, and a certain amount of responsibility can be good for children. When helping becomes a permanent duty, older children quickly feel overwhelmed and deprived of their own childhood. What helped the Hartmann family:

- **Pay attention to balance.** Ask yourself honestly: How often does the older child take on tasks that are actually adult tasks? Do you still have enough time for your own hobbies and friends?
- **Listen when a child complains.** A tantrum is sometimes the first clear indication that something has been wrong for a while. Listening without immediately justifying opens up the conversation.
- **Plan together.** A family plan that everyone works on often shows that care can be distributed differently: after-school care, neighbors, grandparents, more flexible working hours.
- **Allows no.** If the older child is babysitting a sibling, that should be a request they can refuse, not a given.
- **Visibly recognizes help.** A small payment, a thank you or a privilege shows that help is not an obligation, but something special.
- **Let older children be children.** They are not co-parents. Resolving conflicts between younger siblings remains primarily the responsibility of adults.

### When are children allowed to pay attention?

There is no fixed minimum age for looking after siblings in Germany. The responsibility remains with the parents: They have to assess whether their child is mature enough, how long they can cope on their own and what should happen in an emergency. Many family education centers and aid organizations offer babysitting courses, often from around the age of twelve or thirteen, in which young people learn first aid for children, game ideas and how to deal with difficult situations. Start with short times, leave telephone numbers clearly visible and discuss beforehand what is allowed and what is not.

### When children grow up too early

Experts speak of parentification when children, over a long period of time, take on tasks or worries that actually belong to their parents, be it looking after younger siblings or comforting a stressed parent. Signs may include a child feeling guilty if they take up time for themselves, rarely play with their peers, or appear conspicuously sensible and mature. This can easily happen, especially in families with illness, separation or a heavy workload, without anyone wanting it. If you recognize this in your child, consciously relieve him or her and, if necessary, get support from a family counseling center.

### Time just for the big one

Older siblings often receive less undivided attention than the little ones. A fixed appointment just with the older child, such as breakfast at the bakery on Saturday or a visit to the stadium together, shows that you are not just a helper, but important in your own right.

### To talk to your children

Asks the oldest child: When do you like to help? And when does it feel like too much? Ask the younger ones: What do you like to do with your big brother or sister if it's voluntary?

If your older child says something is too much for him, take it as a sign of trust and not as ingratitude. It shows that you dare to be honest. Thank you for your openness and arrange an appointment to see together whether the new distribution works.
