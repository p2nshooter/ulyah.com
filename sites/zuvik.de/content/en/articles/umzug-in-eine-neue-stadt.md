Hannes found out about it on a Sunday in March, over lunch. His mother Martina put down the fork, looked at his father, then at him and his little sister Pia, and said: "We have to tell you something. We're moving to Frankfurt in the summer."

Pia, six years old, immediately asked whether there was a zoo in Frankfurt. Hannes, ten, said nothing. He got up, went to his room and closed the door. Through the window he saw the stream behind the house, the pasture with Farmer Schmitz's cows and the soccer field on the outskirts of town where he trained with his team every Tuesday and Thursday.

He had spent his entire life in this village in the Eifel. He knew every path, every tree, every dog. And he had no plans to live anywhere else.

## Why

Martina had gotten a job as an engineer at a company in Frankfurt, a position that she had been working towards for a long time. The company she had previously worked for in the district town closed at the end of the year. Hannes' father Thomas was a carpenter and had found a workshop in Frankfurt that wanted to hire him. It was a sensible decision. But for Hannes it felt like a judgment.

In the evening Thomas came into his room and sat on the floor next to the bed. “You’re mad,” he stated.

“You didn’t even ask me,” said Hannes.

Thomas nodded slowly. "That's true. Mom and I had to make the decision because it was about our work. It's sometimes the case that parents have to decide things that also affect the children." He paused. "But you're allowed to be angry about it. And you're allowed to be sad. And we'll listen to you."

Hannes turned to the wall. But he didn't send his father out.

## The weeks before the move

In the months leading up to the summer, Hannes was often angry. He argued with Pia, who was looking forward to Frankfurt because there was a zoo there. He argued with his parents over small things. And he spent every free minute with his best friend Felix, with whom he had been friends since kindergarten. They built dams in the creek, played soccer until dark, and didn't talk about the move.

Martina and Thomas tried to involve Hannes wherever possible. One weekend in May they went to Frankfurt together and looked at the apartment they had rented: four rooms on the third floor of an old building in Bornheim, with a balcony and a view of a backyard with a large chestnut tree. Hannes was allowed to choose his room and choose the color of the walls. He chose dark green, like the forests in the Eifel.

They walked through the neighborhood, found a bakery, a playground, a park. And Thomas had found a nearby football club online where they could watch the U11s training on Saturday mornings. The trainer, a tall man with a gray beard, shook Hannes' hand and said: "If you come in the summer, you are very welcome." Hannes nodded without smiling. But on the drive back he asked in which position the team needed someone.

## The farewell

A week before the move, the family celebrated a farewell party in the garden. The neighbors came, the friends from school, the team from the soccer club. Farmer Schmitz brought a case of apple juice. Felix's mother had baked a cake that said "See you soon, Hannes" in icing.

Hannes was afraid of the festival. But it turned out nice. His team gave him a jersey that everyone signed. His teacher gave him a notebook in which every child in the class had written or drawn something. And Felix gave him a small package that he was only supposed to open in Frankfurt.

On the last evening, Hannes and Felix sat by the stream with their feet in the cold water. “We can talk on the phone,” said Felix. "And write. And you come here on vacation." Hannes nodded. They both knew it wouldn't be the same. Nobody said it.

## The first few weeks

The first few weeks in Frankfurt were difficult. The apartment smelled strange, the streets were loud, and at night Hannes heard the tram instead of the crickets. School started after the summer holidays, and Hannes was the new kid in a class where everyone had known each other for four years. During the first break he stood alone at the edge of the schoolyard. In the second too.

At home he opened Felix's package. Inside was a stone from the stream, smooth and gray, with a white stripe down the middle, and a note: "So you don't forget the stream." Hannes put the stone on his bedside table. That evening he cried for the first time since moving, and Martina sat by his bed for a long time.

“It’s okay that it hurts,” she said. "It shows how important home was to you. And at some point this will become a home too. Not right away. But at some point."

After three days, Pia, on the other hand, had a best friend, a girl from the house across the street who loved painting horses just as much as she did. Hannes thought that was unfair.

## The first training

On the second Tuesday after the holidays, Hannes went to the football club's training for the first time. He had had a stomach ache all week because of it. He put on his old team's jersey, with the signatures on it, as a kind of armor.

The training was different than in the Eifel. The pitch was made of artificial turf, the boys were faster and louder, and Hannes didn't know any names. But when he played a deep pass on the final play that turned into a goal, a boy with curls and braces high-fived him. “Awesome pass,” said the boy. "I'm Tarik. You're the new one in 5c, right? I'm in 5a."

On the way home, Hannes told his father something for the first time in weeks without being asked. About Tarik, about the goal, about the coach with the gray beard who said he had “a good eye.”

## Two homes

It took months until Hannes really felt like he had arrived. Tarik became his friend, and through Tarik he met other children. He found a connection in class when the teacher asked him to talk about the Eifel in geography class, about the maars and the volcanoes that have been dormant for thousands of years, and suddenly everyone wanted to know whether he had ever stood in a volcano. (He did, so to speak.)

He spoke to Felix on the phone every Sunday, and during the fall holidays he went back to the village for a week and slept with Felix. It was beautiful and strange at the same time. The stream was still there, and so were the cows. But Felix now had new friends in the team, and Hannes talked about Frankfurt, about the tram and about Tarik, and realized that he was looking forward to the return journey.

When he was back in Frankfurt, he placed Felix's stone on the window sill in his dark green room, from where you could see the chestnut tree in the backyard. “I have two homes now,” he told his mother. “Is that weird?”

“No,” said Martina. “That’s lucky.”

## For your everyday life

For children, moving to a new city often means losing friends, familiar places and routines. Anger, sadness and withdrawal are normal reactions. Children need time, understanding and opportunities to say goodbye and discover new things. What helped Hannes' family:

- **Inform early and honestly.** Children should find out in good time why the family is moving so that they can prepare for it.
- **Allow feelings.** Anger and sadness about moving are justified. Children need to feel like they can express these feelings and be heard.
- **Let children have a say in where it goes.** Choosing their own room, choosing the color of the walls or looking at leisure activities gives children back some control.
- **Consciously say goodbye.** A farewell party, mementos and time with your best friends help you process the change.
- **Take something familiar with you.** A hobby like football, an instrument or a club can be a bridge to new friendships in the new place.
- **Keep in touch with old friends.** Phone calls, letters and visits during the holidays help not to experience the farewell as final.
- **Be patient.** Arriving can take months, and siblings often take different amounts of time.

### Prepare well for the change to school

A move to another federal state often means a change to a different school system. Curricula, foreign language sequence and sometimes even the number of years of primary school differ. Register your child at the new school early, bring certificates and documents with you and ask during the admission interview where there might be differences in the material. A short conversation with the new class teacher in the first few weeks helps to identify gaps early. Many teachers also ask a child from the class to act as a sponsor and look after the new child.

### The moving day itself

On moving day there is little for children to do and a lot to lose, such as their favorite cuddly toy in one of the hundred boxes. Many families leave younger children with grandparents or friends on this day. Pack a box with the most important things for the first night and first set up the children's room in the new apartment with familiar bed linen and favorite things in their usual place.

### To talk to your children

- What would you miss most if we moved?
- What could help you make friends in a new place?
- What would you definitely take with you from your current home?
- Can you have two homes? What makes a place a home?
