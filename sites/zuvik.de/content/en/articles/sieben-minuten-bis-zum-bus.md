The bus leaves at 7:23 a.m. Everyone in the Brandt family knew this, even three-year-old Jonas, who hadn't yet taken the bus. Nevertheless, Sandra Brandt stood in the hallway almost every morning at 7:16 a.m., a shoe in her hand, her voice already two tones too high, and called up the stairs: "Lea! Where's your jacket? We still have seven minutes!"

Lea was eight and wore the jacket most of the time, just not where her mother was looking for it. Or she wasn't wearing them because she was still in her pajamas sitting by the window watching a squirrel. Or she had the jacket on but no socks. There were countless ways a morning could go wrong, and the Brandt family seemed to try them all.

## The morning everyone cried

The low point came on a Tuesday in February. It was still dark outside, Jonas had coughed twice during the night, and Marc, the father, had to leave early because an accident had been reported on the motorway. Sandra stood alone between the coffee machine and the children's room.

At 7:14 a.m. she noticed that Lea needed her sports gear. At 7:17 a.m. she found it in the washing machine, wet. At 7:19 a.m. Lea cried because she wasn't allowed to do gymnastics without gym equipment. At 7:20 a.m. Jonas cried because Leah was crying. And at 7:21 a.m., when Sandra opened the front door and just saw the bus turn the corner, she cried too.

She drove Lea to school in the car, in silence. At the traffic light in front of the schoolyard, Lea said quietly: “Mom, I don’t like mornings.” Sandra looked at her in the rearview mirror and answered honestly, “Me neither, darling.”

## A piece of paper on the fridge

In the evening Sandra and Marc sat at the kitchen table. The children were sleeping. “We do the same thing every morning,” Marc said, “and it still surprises us every time.” He was right. The process was always the same: get up, get dressed, have breakfast, brush your teeth, shoes, jacket, satchel. But this process wasn't written anywhere. He only existed in Sandra's head, and from there he was shouted loudly into the hallway.

On Saturday they picked up a large sheet of paper and colored pencils. “We’re painting your morning now,” Sandra explained. Lea was there straight away. She drew a bed with a rising sun, a wardrobe, a cereal bowl, a toothbrush with too much toothpaste, a pair of sneakers and finally a yellow bus. Jonas drew a circle and explained that it was his teddy bear and that he had to come with him too.

They stuck a small clothespin next to each picture. The idea was simple: whoever completed a step would clip the clip to the other side of the sheet. This meant everyone could see where they were standing without an adult having to announce it.

## The evening decides the morning

While painting, Lea noticed something. “Mom, you can do half of it in the evening.” She was right. Clothes could be left on the chair in the evening, the lunch box in the fridge, the satchel packed and leaning against the door, the sports equipment checked the evening before.

This is how a second, smaller sheet was created: the evening plan. Just three pictures: a chair with clothes, a satchel on the door, a calendar with a sneaker as a reminder to check whether there is sports tomorrow.

The first few days were bumpy. Lea forgot the clips, Jonas clipped them all at once and was very proud. But something had changed: When Sandra asked "Where are you on your schedule?", it was a different question than "Why aren't you still dressed?" The first question made Lea look, the second made her defend herself.

## Ten minutes earlier, but different

The second change was more uncomfortable. Sandra set her own alarm ten minutes earlier. Not to get more done, but to have a quiet coffee before the children woke up. “I noticed that I had woken up feeling rushed,” she later told her sister. “And children notice that immediately.”

Marc took over the wake-up call on his late days. He sat on the edge of Lea's bed for a minute before turning on the light and asked what she had dreamed. It took a minute and saved, it seemed, ten.

After three weeks something happened that no one expected. It was 7:15 a.m., Lea was ready in the hallway and Sandra was looking for her own car keys. Lea looked at the plan, then at her mother, and said in a very serious voice, “Mom, where are you on your plan?”

Sandra laughed so hard that they almost missed the bus. Almost.

## What Jonas contributed

It was Marc's idea that the plan also applied to Jonas, even though he wasn't taking the bus yet. “He wants everything Lea has,” he said. So Jonas got his own, much shorter plan with just four pictures: bed, pants, bread, teddy. It took him about as long to complete each step as it took Lea to complete her entire morning, but he was immensely proud of each peg he changed.

This had a side effect that no one expected. Lea suddenly felt like the big one who showed how it was done. When Jonas dawdled, she didn't call for her mother, but went to him and asked, just like Sandra: "Where are you on your plan?" Sometimes she helped him into his pants. Sometimes, to be honest, the two of them argued about who had the better bracket.

## The mornings that still went wrong

It would be nice to say that every morning since then has been quiet. It wasn't like that. On days when a child got sick, when the heating went out or when Lea woke up in a bad mood, even the best plan only helped to a limited extent. The difference was different: these mornings were now exceptions. In the past, chaos had been the norm and the quiet days the exception. Now it was the other way around, and that was what made the difficult mornings more bearable. On days like this, Sandra no longer said, “Nothing is working for us,” but rather, “Today is a difficult morning.” It sounded smaller, and it was smaller too.

## For your everyday life

Mornings are the most stressful time of the day for many families: little time, tired people, fixed departure times. A few ideas that have helped the Brandt family and that you can try:

- **Makes the process visible.** A painted or photographed morning plan at child level gives children orientation, even for those who cannot yet read. Let the children help shape things; They take what they have painted themselves more seriously.
- **Ask about the plan instead of the failure.** “What’s next on your plan?” invites you to act. “Why aren’t you ready yet?” invites you to justify yourself.
- **Postpone what's possible to the evening.** Put out your clothes, pack your satchel, check appointments for the next day: Every task that is completed in the evening is missing as a point of contention in the morning.
- **Plan a buffer, even for yourself.** Ten unhurried minutes before the kids wake up often changes the mood of the entire morning.
- **Wakes up gently.** A minute on the edge of the bed, a conversation about dreams, light only afterwards: For many children, the transition from sleep to the day is easier if it is not abrupt.
- **Expect setbacks.** New routines usually take a few weeks to gel. A bad morning doesn't mean the plan won't work.

### The morning begins the evening before

Many morning struggles stem from not enough sleep. As a guide, sleep medical societies recommend around 10 to 13 hours of sleep per day for children aged three to five, including afternoon naps, around 9 to 12 hours for children aged six to twelve and 8 to 10 hours for teenagers. As a primary school child, anyone who has to get up at 6:45 a.m. needs a bedtime that is not too late. Calculate back from your wake-up time. It often turns out that a quieter morning starts with the lights going out half an hour earlier.

### Breakfast without the rush

Not every child is hungry in the morning, and not every family has time for a long breakfast. What can be prepared the night before is helpful: oatmeal that has soaked overnight in the fridge with milk or yoghurt, sliced ​​fruit in a can, bread that just needs to be topped. Children who cannot eat anything early can take a slightly larger lunch with them. More important than a full plate is a quiet moment at the table, even if just for five minutes.

### When your stomach hurts in the morning

If a child repeatedly complains of stomach ache or nausea in the morning, it's worth taking a closer look. Sometimes it's just hunger, being in a hurry or not getting enough sleep. Sometimes it's worries, for example about a class test, about a particular child or about physical education class. Pay attention to whether the symptoms disappear on weekends and holidays and talk about them in a quiet moment. If the symptoms persist, you should be examined by a pediatrician.

### To talk to your children

On a quiet afternoon, ask your children: What is the hardest thing for you in the morning? What would help you? Children often have surprisingly practical answers, like Lea, who noticed that half of the morning can be done in the evening.

Write down the answers and hang them next to your morning plan. After a few weeks you can look together to see what has changed and adjust the plan. A morning plan can grow with you, just like the children it is made for.
