On Saturday, the first day of the autumn holidays, Henrik looked out of the kitchen window of the old apartment in Hamburg-Eimsbüttel and said: “It’s definitely going away soon.” A fine, gray rain was falling outside, blurring the roofs opposite. The weather app showed ten clouds with drops for the next ten days.

His wife Lena, a midwife at the hospital, was on duty during the holidays, like every fall. Henrik had taken a two-week vacation to be with the children. He was looking forward to it: Hagenbeck's zoo, bike rides on the Alster, maybe a trip to the Baltic Sea. None of this was left in the room now.

Smilla, six, and Jannes, four, were still sitting on the sofa in their pajamas and asked for the first time that morning: “Dad, what are we doing today?”

## The first day

On the first day it was fine. They built a marble run across the hallway out of toilet paper rolls and tape. They baked waffles and ate them with applesauce. They played Memory three times and Uno once, with Jannes interpreting the rules as he saw fit. In the afternoon they watched a film. At half past seven the children were asleep and Henrik sat on the sofa and thought: Nine more days.

On the second day, the children had already finished all the games by ten in the morning. Smilla asked for the tablet. Henrik gave in for half an hour, which turned into an hour and then an hour and a half because he could finally do the laundry in that time. When he took the tablet away, Jannes screamed as if his arm had been taken off. Smilla sulked for the rest of the morning.

## The third day

On the third day everything went wrong. The children started arguing at seven in the morning. Who? About everything. About the blue cup, about the place at the window, about the question of who was allowed into the bathroom first. Jannes threw a building block at Smilla and hit the vase on the windowsill. Smilla cried because she didn't feel like going inside anymore. Jannes cried because Smilla cried. Henrik stood in the kitchen between the shards of the vase and noticed how his patience was breaking.

“That’s enough!” he shouted, louder than he wanted. Both children fell silent and looked at him in shock.

Henrik took a deep breath. Then he went into the hallway, opened the closet and took out three pairs of rubber boots, two pairs of mud pants and his old rain jacket. “Get dressed,” he said. “We’re going out.”

“But it’s raining,” said Smilla.

“Exactly,” said Henrik.

## Puddles are for jumping

It turned out to be one of the best decisions of his vacation. Down on the street the children were still hesitating, as if the rain was hurting them. Then Jannes stepped into the first puddle and the water splashed up to his chin. He squealed with pleasure. A minute later both children were jumping from puddle to puddle, laughing loudly and wet from head to toe.

They went to the park by the pond, which was almost empty that morning. Only an older gentleman with a dachshund in a yellow raincoat met them. Under the chestnut trees they gathered the shiny brown fruits until Smilla's pockets bulged. They watched as the rain made small rings on the water of the pond. Jannes discovered a snail on a stone and wanted to call it Schnecki and take it with him. After a lengthy negotiation, Schnecki was allowed to stay where she was.

When they got home after two hours, they were soaked, cold and happy. Henrik drew them a warm bath and made cocoa. And in the afternoon, for the first time in days, the children played peacefully with each other: they used the chestnuts to build animals out of matches and toothpicks, a whole herd.

## A plan for rainy weather

That evening, Henrik told Lena about the day on the phone. Lena laughed. "You know what my grandma always said? There's no such thing as bad weather, just wrong clothing." And then she added something that made Henrik think: "The children don't need a program every day. They need a rhythm. And they can be bored sometimes."

The next morning, Henrik and the children sat down at the kitchen table and made a plan. Not for every hour, but for every day. Get out in the morning, no matter what the weather. Lunch and a quiet hour in which everyone was allowed to play or read on their own. A joint project in the afternoon. The tablet was only available in the evening, twenty minutes, with an hourglass.

The quiet hour was difficult at first. “I’m bored,” Smilla said after five minutes on the first day. Henrik said, "That's fine. Feel free to get bored for a bit." Smilla looked at him indignantly. But after a while she took out her pencils and started drawing a snail. Then another. By the end of the week she had drawn a whole notebook about Schnecki, with stories that she dictated to Henrik.

## The rain researchers

The children soon developed the joint projects in the afternoon themselves. Smilla wanted to know how much it actually rained. So they cut open a plastic bottle, stuck a ruler to it and put it on the balcony. Every morning they counted how many millimeters it had rained and Smilla wrote it down in a table. At the end of the holidays it was forty-six millimeters. “That’s the equivalent of half a bathtub,” said Smilla, which wasn’t true, but Henrik accepted it.

One day they took the bus to the Elbe to Övelgönne and watched the gray and huge container ships glide past in the rain. Jannes waved to each ship. Another day they went to the library and borrowed as many books as they could carry. And one particularly gray afternoon, Henrik and the children built a den out of blankets, chairs and clothespins in the living room, where they sat with flashlights and told stories while the rain drummed against the windows outside.

## The last day

On the last Sunday of the holidays it stopped raining. The sun came out, the streets were shining, and Henrik suggested we finally go to the zoo. Smilla and Jannes looked at each other. “Can’t we go to the park by the pond again?” asked Smilla. “See if Schnecki is still there.”

Schnecki was no longer there. But on the way back they found a puddle the size of a small lake and jumped in until their rubber boots were full of water.

When Lena came home that evening, she found her family on the sofa, all three wrapped in blankets. Smilla showed her the rain table and the snail book. Jannes talked about the container ships. “And,” Lena Henrik later asked quietly, “how were your holidays?” Henrik thought about it. “Wet,” he said. “And surprisingly beautiful.”

## For your everyday life

Long periods of rain, especially during the holidays, can push families to their limits. Children don't move enough, the apartment becomes cramped, arguments increase. Rain is often not a problem for children, but rather an adventure - provided the adults allow it. What helped Henrik:

- **Go outside anyway.** With mud pants, rubber boots, and a rain jacket, kids can play outside in almost any weather. Exercising in the fresh air relieves tension and often improves your mood suddenly.
- **Finding a simple daily rhythm.** No timetable, but a rough structure: outside, quiet time, joint project. Children feel safer when they know what to expect.
- **Allow boredom.** When children are bored, there is often room for their own ideas. It's not the parent's job to fill every minute.
- **Clearly limit screen time.** A fixed time and a visible end, such as with an hourglass, avoid discussions and temper tantrums when switching off.
- **Develop projects from everyday life.** A rain gauge, a herd of chestnuts, a notebook about a snail: the best ideas often come from the children themselves.
- **Use free places.** Libraries, parks, riverside paths and bus rides are good excursion destinations even in bad weather and are easy on your vacation budget.

### Rain gear that really helps

To have fun outside, the clothes have to be right. Mud pants with straps that reach over the chest and a waterproof jacket with a hood have proven useful. Rubber boots can be one size larger so that warm socks can fit in them. Underneath, wool or fleece keeps you warm better than cotton, which gets wet and stays wet. Pack a change of socks and a towel for longer trips, and plan on having a warm bath when you get home. Used mud clothes can often be found cheaply at children's flea markets because children outgrow them faster than they wear them out.

### Small research projects for rainy days

Rain is a wonderful reason for research. In addition to a rain gauge like Smilla's, you can draw the outline of a puddle on the sidewalk with chalk and see how much smaller it has become the next day. A simple water filter can be built from a cut open plastic bottle, cotton wool, sand and gravel, which makes cloudy puddle water visibly clearer. Of course you shouldn't drink it. And after a heavy shower, earthworms come to the surface, which can be easily observed with a magnifying glass before they are carefully allowed to return to the bed.

### To talk to your children

- What do you enjoy most outside when it's raining?
- What do you do when you're bored - and is boredom actually a bad thing?
- What project would you like to do with us on a rainy day?
- Why do we argue more often when we're indoors for a long time?
