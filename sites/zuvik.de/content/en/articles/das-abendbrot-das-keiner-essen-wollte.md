“What’s that green thing in there?”

The question came every evening, and it always came from Deniz. He was six, and for him every green piece on the plate was a personal attack. His sister Elif, ten, was more subtle. She didn't ask, she pushed. With the fork, everything she didn't like went to the edge of the plate until there was a small wall of zucchini, peppers and onions.

Ayşe Kaya stood in the kitchen of her apartment in Dortmund for an hour every evening after work. She liked to cook, actually. Her mother's dishes, lentil soup, stuffed peppers, bulgur with tomatoes, but also pasta, pancakes and the vegetable curry that she discovered on vacation. And every evening she would sit at the table and watch her children dissect the food.

## An evening like many

It was particularly bad on a Thursday in October. There was vegetable casserole. Deniz crossed his arms. Elif ate three forkfuls of potatoes and declared she was full. Her husband Tobias tried humor (“Whoever eats up gets to decide the weather tomorrow”), then with severity (“Two bites of everything, no other dessert”), and finally, when Deniz started to cry, with resignation.

Later, while washing dishes, Ayşe said: “I feel like I’m cooking for the trash can.” Tobias nodded. "And we argue every night. Dinner is the only hour we're all together, and we spend it negotiating over zucchini."

That sat. Because dinner was actually exactly that: the only time of the day when the whole family sat at the same table. And it had become a battlefield.

## What the pediatrician said

At Deniz's next checkup, Ayşe asked the pediatrician if she had anything to worry about. The doctor looked at the growth curve: Deniz grew normally, gained weight normally, and was fit. “A lot of kids this age are picky,” she said. "This is often a phase. More important than each individual plate is that there are no fights at the table. Offer without pushing. And let the children participate."

Join in. Ayşe thought about it. Her children only saw the food when it was ready on the table, an alien object that they knew nothing about. As a child, she helped her mother fill the peppers and ate the filling before it was in the oven.

## The cooking evening

On Saturday, Ayşe explained: "From now on there will be a children's cooking evening. Every Wednesday. You choose what is available, you buy with us, you cook. I only help if you ask."

Elif was skeptical, Deniz was enthusiastic. For the first Wednesday, Deniz chose pancakes. “Just pancakes?” Ayşe asked. “Just pancakes,” he confirmed. She took a deep breath and said, "All right. But there will also be a bowl of something raw on the table, whatever you choose."

In the supermarket the children stood in front of the vegetable shelf. Elif took a cucumber. Deniz, after thinking about it for a long time, chose a yellow pepper “because it looks like the sun”. At home, Elif cut the cucumber into sticks and Deniz cut the pepper into crooked strips, under supervision and with a child-sized knife.

In the evening Deniz ate four pancakes. And three strips of pepper. Self-cut ones. Ayşe didn't say a word about it, although she was cheering inside.

## The new rule at the table

She also agreed on a new table rule with Tobias, which was actually an anti-rule: Nobody has to eat anything. But everyone stays seated until most are finished, and no one comments on anyone else's food. Instead of talking about plates, they talked about the day. Everyone told a good thing and a stupid thing.

The first few evenings were strangely quiet. Deniz tried a piece of carrot from the stew without anyone asking him to. He grimaced and put it back. Nobody said anything. Two weeks later he ate the carrots, not all of them, but some.

Elif developed ambition at the children's cooking evening. She wanted to learn her grandmother's lentil dish and called Grandma Gül via video. She showed her how to slowly sauté onions and also said that she didn't like lentils as a child either. “Until I cooked them myself,” she said, laughing.

Dinner wasn't perfect. There were still evenings with walls of plates and sighs. But it was what it was supposed to be again: the one hour in which everyone is together.

## A setback and a realization

In December, when the whole family had a cold, the children's cooking evening was canceled for three weeks in a row. The old patterns promptly returned. Deniz asked again about the green one, Elif again built walls at the edge of the plate, and Tobias heard himself say again, to his own annoyance: “Just two more bites.”

Ayşe noticed it first. “We’re slipping backwards,” she said one evening. But this time they knew what had helped. They decided not to have a big debate and simply resumed Wednesday. Elif chose lentil soup according to grandma's recipe, Deniz insisted on cucumber sticks, and because everyone was still sniffly, the soup tasted particularly good.

The insight that Ayşe took away from these weeks was simple: it wasn't a single trick that helped, but the attitude behind it. Their children ate more not because they were cooking, but because food was no longer a place of struggle for control. When that attitude was lost, the fights returned, no matter who was at the stove.

Elif summed it up in her own way when her friend came to dinner and asked if you had to eat the broccoli. “With us you don’t have to do anything,” she explained. “But you’re just missing something.”

## Elif's recipe booklet

In the new year, Elif created a recipe book. On the first page she wrote Grandma Gül's lentil soup, with a drawing of the onions that need to be “let golden very slowly.” On the second came Deniz's pancakes, dictated by Deniz himself and with the note “yellow peppers because of the sun”. Little by little the magazine filled up: the vegetable curry, a pasta casserole, Tobias' scrambled eggs. When visitors came, Elif would take out the booklet and sometimes the guest would be allowed to choose a dish for the next children's cooking evening.

## For your everyday life

Picky eating behavior is common among children of kindergarten and elementary school age. If your child is growing normally and is healthy, small changes often help more than pressure. Ideas from Ayşe's kitchen:

- **Take the pressure off the plate.** Coercion, reward with dessert or discussions about “two bites” often turn eating into a power struggle. Offer without pushing and let your child decide how much to take.
- **Get the kids in the kitchen.** Children are more likely to try what they have chosen, washed, cut or stirred themselves. Even three-year-olds can wash vegetables or pick lettuce.
- **Always offer something familiar.** If there is bread, pasta or potatoes next to the new dish, no one will be hungry and the new thing will seem less threatening.
- **Repeated without comment.** Many children need a food on the table numerous times before they try it. This is normal and not a sign of stubbornness.
- **Make the conversation the focus.** Questions like “What was good today, what was stupid?” draw attention from the plate to the interaction.
- **Get advice if you are concerned.** If a child is losing weight, accepts noticeably few foods, or eating causes great anxiety, talk to your pediatrician.

### What's behind picky eating

The fact that children view something new on their plate with suspicion is not a parenting error. Many children go through a phase between the ages of two and six in which they reject unfamiliar foods. Experts speak of neophobia, the fear of new things, and suspect that this is an old protective mechanism: If you don't put everything in your mouth as a small child, you are less likely to poison yourself. In addition, many children perceive bitter substances more intensely than adults. Vegetables like Brussels sprouts and broccoli actually taste different to them than to their parents. Patience and frequent, relaxed offerings therefore work better than persuasion.

### Kitchen tasks by age

| Dude | What children can do well |
|---|---|
| 2 to 3 years | Wash vegetables, pick lettuce, stir, put ingredients in a bowl |
| 4 to 5 years | cutting soft foods with a children's knife, kneading dough, setting the table |
| 6 to 8 years | Read out simple recipes, weigh out the ingredients, stir them on the stove with supervision |
| 9 to 12 years | cook simple dishes largely independently, write shopping lists |

Stay close to knives, hot pots, and the oven until you are sure your child can handle them.

### Phrases that we have gotten used to

Ayşe and Tobias noticed how often they had used words to put pressure on them. They gradually replaced “Just one more bite”, “At least try it” or “Other children would be happy” with sentences that gave freedom: “You don’t have to eat it.” “You can just smell it for now.” “How does this feel, crunchy or soft?” For many children, touching and smelling is a first step before they dare to try something out.

### Share the weekly plan

A simple help for busy parents is a weekly plan on the fridge in which each family member writes down a dish. This way everyone knows that their favorite meal is coming soon and the question “What’s for today?” loses her fear. For Ayşe, the plan had another advantage: she no longer had to make decisions alone every evening.

### To talk to your children

Ask: What meal would you like to cook yourself? And which vegetables look funniest to you? Sometimes a new favorite food starts with a pepper that looks like the sun.
