Mira came to 5b on a Monday in October, six weeks into the school year, when the groups in the class had already found each other. Ms. Petersen, the class teacher, introduced her: "This is Mira. She moved with her family from Regensburg to Rostock. Please help her find her way."

Mira stood at the front of the table, a girl with thick brown braids, a knitted deer-print jacket, and a backpack with a small pretzel-shaped pendant dangling from it. “Hello,” she said. A few children giggled.

Clara sat in the third row next to her best friend Lotta. She didn't giggle. But she looked over at Lotta, and Lotta grimaced, and Clara smiled back. It was just a small smile. She thought nothing of it.

## A sticker

In the first few weeks, Mira mostly sat alone on the wall at the edge of the schoolyard during breaks and ate her bread. A few times she tried to join groups, but then the conversation stopped or the others turned away. When she said something in class with her soft accent, someone in the back row would quietly imitate her.

The class had a group chat that included everyone except Mira because no one had thought to add her. Or because someone consciously didn't think of it. On a Wednesday evening, Jannik, one of the loudest boys in the class, sent a sticker into the chat. It was a photo of Mira, taken secretly in the schoolyard, with a pair of drawn leather trousers and the text “Hello, I'm the new one”. Underneath he had written: “Who wants to go to the mountain pasture with the cow?”

Reactions came within minutes. Laughing smileys, cow emojis, a “hahaha”. Clara lay on her bed and watched the news. She didn't really find the sticker funny. But everyone responded. If she didn't send anything, it would be noticed. She tapped the laughing smiley and put the phone away.

## What the mother sees

That evening, Clara's cell phone was on the kitchen table while Clara took a shower. Her mother Ines was clearing the table when the screen lit up. A new message in the class chat, as a preview: “Tomorrow I’ll bring a cowbell for the new one 😂”. Above that, still visible, is the sticker.

Ines didn't pick up the cell phone. She didn't read any further. But she had seen enough.

When Clara came into the kitchen in her pajamas, her mother was sitting at the table with two cups of tea in front of her. “Sit down for a moment,” said Ines. "I just accidentally saw a message on your phone. From the class chat. I don't want to pry. But I want you to tell me what's going on."

Clara blushed. She spoke, hesitantly, about Mira, about the sticker, about the cowbell. “I didn’t write anything,” she said. “Just a smiley face.”

"And how do you think Mira would feel if she could see this chat?"

Clara didn't answer. She looked at her cup.

## A smiley isn't nothing

That evening, Ines told Clara something that she had never told her before. That she herself was new in the seventh grade after her family moved from Saxony to Rostock a few years after the fall of the Wall. That the others had aped her because of her dialect. That she had sat on the toilet during breaks for a whole year so that no one would see that she was alone.

“The worst weren’t the ones who were mean,” said Ines. "There were two or three. The worst were the others who laughed along. Because I thought: If everyone is laughing, then maybe there's something wrong with me."

Clara was silent for a long time. “I only laughed because everyone laughed,” she finally said quietly.

“I know,” said Ines. "That's usually the case. But for Mira it doesn't make any difference."

She didn't force Clara to do anything. She just said, "Think about what you want to do. And if you need help, I'm here."

## On the wall

The next day Jannik actually brought a small cowbell with him and rang it when Mira came into the classroom in the morning. A few laughed. Mira sat down in her seat, put her head on the table and only raised it again when Mrs. Petersen came in.

During the long break, Clara saw Mira sitting on the wall again. Lotta pulled her towards the other girls who were standing at the basketball hoop. Clara stopped. “You go ahead,” she said. Lotta looked at her in surprise, shrugged her shoulders and left.

Clara went to the wall. Her heart was pounding as hard as if she were giving a presentation to the entire class. She sat next to Mira, not too close. Mira looked at her suspiciously.

“What are you drawing?” asked Clara. Mira had a small pad on her knees on which she was scribbling with a ballpoint pen.

Mira hesitated. Then she turned the block over. On it was a manga-style drawing of a girl with wide eyes and a sword, so well drawn that Clara opened and closed her mouth. “That’s awesome,” she finally said. “Did you think of this yourself?”

Mira nodded. And then, for the first time in six weeks, she told someone at the new school something about herself.

## The hard part

Talking to Mira was the easier part. The more difficult one came next. Lotta was offended because Clara had left her standing during the break. Someone wrote in the class chat: “Clara has a new best friend now, muuuh.” Clara read the message and felt her face heat up. For a moment she wished she had just gone to the basketball hoop.

That evening she showed her mother the message. Ines read it and took Clara in her arms. “Now you know a little bit how Mira feels,” she said. “And you have something Mira didn’t have: you.”

They discussed together what Clara could do. Clara didn't want to tell on Jannik. But she didn't want it to continue either. In the end, she wrote a short email to Ms. Petersen, with the support of her mother, without naming names: that there were messages in the class chat about a classmate that were hurtful and that she was worried.

Ms. Petersen responded quickly. The next week in class council there was a conversation about group chats, about photos that you can't take or send without permission, and about what it feels like to be new. Ms. Petersen did not name any names. But Jannik sat there with his head bowed the whole hour and the sticker disappeared from the chat.

## In spring

In the spring, Mira no longer sat alone on the wall. Clara usually sat next to her, sometimes two girls from the parallel class who also drew manga. After a few weeks, Lotta got along with Clara again, and one day she too sat on the wall and let Mira show her how to draw big eyes.

Mira still said “Hello”. But now a few others were saying it too, and it no longer sounded mocking, but rather like a greeting that belonged to the class.

## For your everyday life

Exclusion often begins quietly: a giggle, a turning away, a laughing smiley in the group chat. Many children take part without any bad intentions, simply because everyone else does it and because they are afraid of becoming a target themselves. What helped Clara's family:

- **Approach calmly, do not punish.** A conversation that asks questions rather than condemns makes it easier for children to talk honestly and think about their own behavior.
- **Let the gaze change.** The question “How would that feel for you?” helps children understand the impact of small gestures.
- **Share your own experiences.** When parents tell how they have experienced exclusion themselves, the topic becomes tangible for children.
- **Appreciate courage.** Going against the group is difficult and can have consequences. Children need support when they choose the right thing.
- **Involve the school.** Teachers should be aware of bullying in class chats. A message can draw attention to a problem even without a name.
- **Talk about digital rules.** Taking and distributing photos of others without their permission is not only hurtful, but it also violates their rights. Children should know this.

### The right to your own image

Many children don't know that they can't simply distribute photos of others. In Germany, everyone has a right to their own image: Anyone who sends or publishes photos or videos of others without their consent is violating this right. If the images are additionally edited to ridicule someone, it can be an insult. From the age of 14, young people in Germany are of criminal responsibility and can also be held legally responsible for such acts. It's worth discussing this with children before they hold their first cell phone in their hands.

### Make it easier for new children to get started

Parents can also help new children arrive more quickly. Ask the parent group whether the new child is already in the class chat. Invite them to play or to a child's birthday party. And encourage your child to talk to the new person during the break. A single friendly child is often enough for a new one to no longer feel alone.

### To talk to your children

- Is there someone in your class who is often alone? Why do you think that is?
- What would you do if someone made fun of another child in the class chat?
- Is a smiling smiley the same as writing something mean yourself?
- How did you feel when you were somewhere new? What would have helped you back then?
