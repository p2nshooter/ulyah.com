At half past two in the afternoon in the Brinkmann family's house in Bielefeld-Schildesche there was always a tension in the air that you could almost feel. Leonie, eight years old, third grade, sat at the kitchen table in front of her math notebook. Her mother, Katja, sat next to her with a pencil and an eraser in her hand. The soup for dinner was simmering on the stove. Leonie's little brother Finn built a tower in the living room with a loud noise.

“Seven times eight?” asked Katja.

Leonie stared at the paper. “Fifty-four.”

"No. Think again."

“Fifty-five?”

Katja exhaled audibly. "Leonie. We practiced this yesterday. Seven times eight."

Leonie burst into tears, pushed the notebook away and shouted: “I’m too stupid for math!” Then she ran into her room and slammed the door. It was five past three.

## The same every day

It had been like this for weeks. Leonie came home from school at quarter past one, had lunch and then sat down to do her homework. And every day it became a fight that sometimes lasted until the evening. Katja worked as an administrative employee for the city in the mornings and kept the afternoons free to be there for the children. But these afternoons no longer felt like time for the children. They felt like a battlefield.

Katja had been a good student herself. She didn't understand why Leonie forgot things that she had known the day before. She didn't understand why her daughter collapsed at the slightest mistake. And she didn't understand why she herself lost patience so quickly. In the evenings, when the children were sleeping, she was ashamed of her tone. The next afternoon it happened again.

Her husband Daniel worked shifts as a bus driver and mostly only heard about the homework struggles through stories. “Just let her do it alone,” he suggested. Katja shook her head. "Then she does everything wrong. And then the teacher thinks we don't care."

## A conversation at school

At the parent-teacher conference in November, Katja sat on a small chair in front of Ms. Akgül, Leonie's class teacher. She had prepared for the interview and brought a list with her: the math problems that Leonie couldn't do, the dictations with many mistakes, and the question of whether Leonie might need tutoring.

Ms. Akgül listened and then leafed through Leonie's notebook. "Can I ask you something? How long has Leonie been sitting on her homework?"

Katja thought about it. "Sometimes an hour. Sometimes two. With breaks."

Ms. Akgül nodded slowly. "In third grade, homework is scheduled so that a child can complete it in about forty-five minutes. If it takes longer, I want to know. Then it's too much or too hard, and I have to adjust that. Not you."

Then she pointed to a page in the notebook. "And one more thing. I don't see any mistakes here. And not here either. But Leonie makes mistakes in class, like all children. I assume that they erase at home until everything is right?"

Katja blushed. She nodded.

“I understand that,” said Ms. Akgül kindly. "But for me, Leonie's mistakes are important. They show me what she hasn't yet understood. If everything is right, I think she can do it. And Leonie learns that mistakes are something bad that you have to hide."

## What Leonie says about it

The evening after the parent-teacher conference, Katja sat down on the bed with Leonie. “I was with Ms. Akgül today,” she said. “And I think I did some things wrong with my homework.”

Leonie looked at her in surprise. That her mother was doing something wrong was a new idea.

“How do you actually feel when we do homework?” asked Katja.

Leonie tugged at her blanket. “Like I’m writing an exam,” she finally said. "And you're the teacher. But a strict one. And when I do something wrong, you look like that." She made a face that Katja recognized immediately: knitted eyebrows, thin lips. It was her own face.

“And what would help you?”

Leonie thought. "If I'm allowed to play first. And if you're not sitting right next to me. And if I'm allowed to say when I need help."

## New rules

Katja and Daniel sat down with Leonie at the weekend and agreed on new rules. They wrote them on a piece of paper and hung it on the refrigerator.

After school there was lunch and then an hour break. Leonie was allowed to play, run around outside or just lie on the sofa. Homework time began at half past three, at Leonie's own desk in her room instead of in the noisy kitchen. Katja was nearby, in the hallway or in the kitchen, but she wasn't sitting next to them. When Leonie needed help, she came to her or called out.

There was an egg timer on the desk set to forty-five minutes. When she rang the bell, it was over, regardless of whether everything was ready or not. Katja then wrote a short sentence for Ms. Akgül in the homework book: “Leonie worked for 45 minutes and didn’t complete task 4.”

And the most important thing: it was no longer erased. Errors remained. Katja looked at the homework at the end to see if everything had been done, but she didn't correct it.

## The first few weeks

It wasn't easy right away. On the first day, after five minutes, Leonie called out that she didn't understand task two. Katja went to her and resisted the urge to say the solution. Instead, she asked, "What's in the assignment? Read it to me." Leonie read. As she read it, she realized what was meant. “Oh,” she said, and Katja went out again.

On the third day the egg timer rang and Leonie had only done half of it. Katja wrote the note in the notebook with a sinking feeling in her stomach. Two days later, Ms. Akgül replied: "Thank you for the feedback. I saw that the word problems are still difficult for Leonie. We'll practice this in the support lesson."

And after three weeks something happened that Katja hadn't expected. Leonie came out of her room, magazine in hand, and said: “Mom, I made a mistake, but now I know where.” She pointed to a bill that she had crossed out and rewritten. Seven times eight, fifty-six. “I remembered it at seven times seven plus seven,” she explained proudly.

## Afternoons without war

The afternoons in the Brinkmanns' house became quieter. Not every day, there were still bad days when Leonie was tired or the tasks were simply too difficult. But the tears became less frequent, and so did the slammed doors.

Katja realized that she liked the afternoons again. After homework time, they sometimes went to the playground with Finn or baked something. And sometimes, without it being a school topic, Leonie would ask while baking: “Mom, if we have three trays and eight cookies on each tray, how many are there?” And then calculated it myself.

## For your everyday life

Homework is a source of conflict in many families. Parents want to help, children feel controlled, and in the end everyone is exhausted. Homework in primary school is primarily intended to show what a child can do on their own. What helped Leonie's family:

- **Keep an eye on the time.** Many states and schools provide guidelines for the length of homework. If a child regularly takes significantly longer, the teacher should know.
- **Leave mistakes.** Corrected homework obscures what a child has not yet understood. Errors are valuable information for teachers.
- **Break first, then work.** After a long morning at school, many children need exercise, food and rest before they can concentrate again.
- **Create a quiet workspace.** A fixed space, away from the kitchen and siblings, helps with concentration.
- **Be close, but don't sit next to them.** Being available when the child needs help provides security without being controlling.
- **Help with questions instead of solutions.** “What does the assignment say?” or “How did you get started?” promote your own thinking more than predetermined answers.
- **Keep in touch with the teacher.** A short note in the homework book can help you find the right amount and the right level of difficulty together.

### Calculation strategies instead of cramming

Leonie's trick with seven times seven plus seven is no coincidence. Many primary schools today teach the multiplication tables using so-called core tasks: Anyone who can do one, two, five and ten confidently can derive the remaining tasks from them. Seven times nine is seven times ten minus seven. There are also exchange tasks, because eight times seven is the same as seven times eight, and neighboring tasks like Leonie's. Ask your child how they do math in school and practice the same way. Two different paths, one at school and one at home, confuse rather than help.

### When learning is constantly difficult

Despite practice and good support, some children have great difficulty reading, spelling or arithmetic over a long period of time. This could be due to a reading or writing weakness or a weakness in arithmetic. Talk to the teacher about it; School psychology counseling centers and specialists can carry out an assessment. In many federal states, if difficulties are identified, there is compensation for disadvantages, such as more time for class tests.

### To talk to your children

- How do you feel when we do homework together?
- Where and when can you concentrate best?
- What is bad about a mistake for you and what can you learn from a mistake?
- How would you like us to help you if you get stuck?
