"Mom! Theo took a cookie even though you said only after dinner!"

"Mom! Theo said 'stupid'!"

"Mom! Theo is rocking on the chair!"

Melanie Bauer stood in the kitchen of her apartment in Cologne-Ehrenfeld and closed her eyes for a moment. It was the seventh time that afternoon that her daughter Clara, seven years old, ratted out her brother Theo, five years old. And it was only half past three.

## The little policewoman

Clara was a child who loved rules. She always knew exactly what was allowed and what wasn't, and she found it unbearable when someone didn't comply. At school she was popular with the teachers because she was reliable and tidy. Less so with the other children because she reported every rule violation.

At home, Theo was their main suspect. Theo was a happy, wild boy who viewed rules rather than suggestions. He grabbed cookies, climbed cupboards, and said words he picked up in kindergarten. And every time Clara stood in the door and reported it.

Melanie and her husband Jan didn't really know how to react. When they admonished Theo, Clara felt vindicated and snitched more. When they told Clara not to gossip, Clara was offended: “But Theo did something forbidden!” And Theo, who was getting caught more and more often, became angry with his sister. The two of them were now arguing almost non-stop.

## The day at the pond

One Saturday in spring the family drove to the city forest. There was a small pond with ducks and the children were allowed to play on the bank while Melanie and Jan sat on a bench. “Stay where we see you,” called Jan.

After a while Clara came running. Melanie sighed inwardly and awaited the next report about Theo and his transgressions. But Clara was pale. "Mom, Theo went onto the jetty. Right at the front. And the jetty is broken."

Jan jumped up. At the other end of the pond, Theo stood on an old wooden pier, half of it rotten, leaning over the water to feed a duck. Jan ran and got him back before anything happened. Theo was confused, then cried because he was scared.

When they were all back on the bench, Melanie took Clara in her arms. “You were right to come and get us,” she said. “That was important.”

## The conversation about two types of reports

In the evening, after the children had bathed, Melanie and Jan sat down on the sofa with Clara. Theo was already in bed. "We want to talk to you about today," said Jan. "You came to get us when Theo was on the broken jetty. That was right and important."

Clara nodded proudly. Then she asked, "And if he takes cookies? Does that matter too?"

Melanie and Jan looked at each other. This was exactly the question they were hoping for. “Let’s think about it,” Melanie said. “What happens if Theo takes a cookie?” Clara thought about it. “Then he might not be hungry when he eats.” – “Is that dangerous?” - "No." – “And what if Theo stands on the broken jetty?” – “Then he can fall into the water and drown.”

Jan took a piece of paper and drew two columns. About one he wrote “Snitching” and about the other “Get help”. “Snitching is when you tell an adult something to get the other person in trouble,” he explained. “Getting help is when you tell an adult something because someone is in danger or hurting or hurting someone else.”

## The list on the fridge

Together they collected examples. Under “get help” came: Someone is hurt. Someone is doing something dangerous. Someone hurts someone else. Someone has disappeared. Under “snitches” came: Someone takes a cookie. Someone says a bad word. Someone is rocking in the chair. Someone didn't clean their room.

Clara found a third category that her parents hadn't thought of: "And what if Theo takes away my toys?" Jan thought about it. "Then you try to work it out with him first. You tell him you don't want this. And if he doesn't stop, you can ask us for help so we can help you solve this."

Melanie hung the list on the refrigerator. In the following weeks, when Clara came with a report, she would only ask: “Is this petting or getting help?” Clara sometimes looked at the list, sometimes she already knew it. And sometimes she would turn around and leave without saying anything.

## What happened to Theo

Interestingly, Theo also changed. Because he was no longer caught and reprimanded for every little thing, he felt less watched. He argued less with Clara. And one day, when Clara hit her knee while riding her bike, it was Theo who ran into the house and shouted: "Mom, Clara's bleeding! This is the time to get help!"

Clara, sitting on the sidewalk with teary eyes, laughed despite the pain.

## A word to the school

Melanie also told Clara's teacher about the list at a parent-teacher meeting. The teacher was interested. “We have the same topic in class,” she said. “Some children report everything, others don’t even dare to say when they are being bullied because they are afraid of being seen as a bully.” She adopted the two column idea for her class. And Clara was very proud when the teacher explained where the idea came from.

## On the climbing frame

A few weeks later, the list was put to the test, this time with no parents around. During recess, Clara saw a fourth-grade boy repeatedly pushing a first-grader off the climbing frame. The little one cried, but no one intervened. A few children from Clara's class were standing nearby. “Just don’t snitch,” said one of them, “otherwise you’ll be the snitch.”

Clara hesitated. Then she thought about the two cracks on the refrigerator. Someone hurts someone else. That was clearly under “get help.” She ran to the break supervisor and told them what she had seen. The teacher went over immediately and the pushing stopped.

In the afternoon Clara told her at home, still a little unsure. “The others thought it was stupid.” Jan sat down next to her. “And the little one?” Clara thought about it. "He said thank you. His name is Mats." – “Then you did exactly the right thing. Even if it took courage.”

## For your everyday life

Many children of kindergarten and elementary school age report every rule violation by others to adults. This often has to do with a strong sense of justice. At the same time, it is important that children learn that in dangerous situations they should always seek help without fear of being seen as a snitch. What helped the Bauer family:

- **Distinguishes between sneaking and getting help.** Sneaking is about getting someone into trouble. Getting help is necessary when someone is in danger, is injured or is about to be injured.
- **Explicitly praises getting help.** Children should know that it is always right to get adults if something dangerous happens. This also applies when it comes to yourself.
- **Ask instead of reacting immediately.** “Is this petting or getting help?” helps children to assess for themselves whether an adult is necessary.
- **Encourages children to sort out small things themselves.** “Tell Theo you don’t want to do that” strengthens the ability to resolve conflicts.
- **Pay attention to the other child.** A sibling who is constantly reported on often feels like they are being watched and reacts with defiance. Less control over small things can promote peace.
- **Make it clear: If you have secrets that cause fear, you should always get help.** Children need to know that they can tell adults anything that will harm them or others, even if someone has asked them not to.

### Why children gossip so often

There are usually good reasons why children of kindergarten and early elementary school age report every violation of the rules. At this age, many children take rules very literally and perceive any exceptions as unfair. When reporting, some are also looking for confirmation that they are doing everything right, or for some attention from their parents, especially if a sibling gets a lot of it. Anyone who knows these reasons can react more calmly: the child is not being sneaky, but is practicing how rules and justice work.

### Good and bad secrets

Closely related to snitching is the question of secrets. Many families and daycare centers therefore speak of two types: **Good secrets** bring joy, such as a birthday present or a surprise, and are revealed at some point. **Bad secrets** make you scared, sick to your stomach or feel strange. Children can and should always tell secrets like this to a trusted adult, even if someone has said they aren't allowed to. This distinction protects children because it gives them a simple rule for difficult situations.

### When no one listens

Also tell your children what they can do if an adult doesn't respond: say it again, tell another adult, don't give up. Older children can also contact the number against grief, the children's and youth hotline, free of charge and anonymously on 116 111.

### To talk to your children

Draw two columns together on a sheet: Snitch and get help. Collect examples from your everyday life. Also ask: Is there anything you haven't dared to tell yet?

Praise your child even if he or she only dares to tell something later. Some children need days before they can talk about an unpleasant experience. The important thing is not why it took so long, but rather that it was said now.
