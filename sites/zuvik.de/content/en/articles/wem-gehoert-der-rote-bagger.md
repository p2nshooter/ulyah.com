The red excavator was not the most expensive toy in the Neumann family's children's room. It wasn't even the newest. It had a broken shovel tip, a scratch on the side and a sticker that no one could decipher. But it was, without a doubt, the most sought-after object in the world. At least for Felix, five years, and Anton, three years.

“Mine!” Anton shouted, clutching the excavator with both arms. “I had it first!” Felix shouted and pulled on the shovel. The shovel gave way, not completely, but audibly. Both boys froze. Then they started howling at the same time.

## The Now Sharing Method

Her mother Katharina came out of the kitchen where she was cutting onions, her hands still wet. She knew this scene. She knew them several times a day. And she had a standard answer that she picked up from her own mother: "Now it's time to share! Felix, you're the big one, give it to Anton."

Felix handed over the excavator, looking as if his heart had been ripped out. Anton held the excavator, triumphant, but didn't play with it. He just held him. And Felix sat down in a corner and hardly said a word the whole evening.

In the evening Katharina told her husband Robert about it. “I feel like we’re doing something wrong by sharing,” she said. "Felix always has to give in because he's the big one. And Anton learns that if he screams loud enough, he gets everything."

## What the sandbox showed

On Saturday, Robert observed a scene on the playground that gave him pause. A girl of maybe four years old was playing with a bucket, a boy wanted it. The girl's mother didn't come running over. She just said from the edge of the sandpit: "Paula, Jonas would like the bucket too. Will you tell him when you're ready?" Paula thought about it and said, “When my cake is ready.” Jonas waited, meanwhile digging with his hands. Three minutes later Paula pushed the bucket towards him.

Robert spoke to his mother. “How do you do that?” She laughed. "I don't force her to share anymore. She can finish playing. But she has to tell the other person when. Adults don't immediately give away their cell phone just because someone else wants it."

Robert laughed along. But on the way home he thought about it for a long time.

## Take turns instead of sharing

At home, Katharina and Robert discussed a new rule: Anyone who plays with a toy can finish the game. If you have to wait, you can ask: “Will you tell me when you’ll be ready?” And if it takes too long, an egg timer can help.

The egg timer was an old thing shaped like a tomato that ticked and rang at the end. Katharina set it to ten minutes if an argument escalated. Whoever had the excavator was allowed to play with it for ten minutes. Then the bell rang and it was the other person's turn.

The boys found the tomato fascinating. Anton, who didn't yet know how long ten minutes was, sometimes sat next to his brother and stared at the clock as if he could speed it up with his eyes. Felix, in turn, began to make good use of the time. He played with more concentration when he knew it was limited.

## The special things

The second rule came from Felix himself. One evening, when he was angry because Anton had messed up his picture book collection, he shouted: “Some things are just mine!”

Katharina wanted to object at first. Then she paused. Wasn't he right? She didn't share everything herself. Her favorite mug, her diary, her headphones.

So each boy got his own box in which his “special things” lay. What was in the box didn't have to be shared. For Felix these were his picture books and a stuffed shark. For Anton, a security blanket and, to everyone's surprise, an ordinary wooden spoon that he had stolen from the kitchen. Everything else in the children's room belonged to everyone, and that meant an egg timer and taking turns.

## When visitors come

Visits were a particular challenge. When Felix's kindergarten friend Emma came to play, Anton immediately wanted to play, and Emma wanted, of all things, the red excavator. Felix looked to his mother for help.

Katharina had spoken to Felix beforehand. "When Emma comes, the special things in your box will be safe. You'll play everything else together." That morning Felix had put his plush shark and two favorite books in the box and put the box on the cupboard. The excavator wasn't there. Felix thought about it for a long time and then decided against it because he knew that Emma liked Bagger.

Now the temptation was great to change my mind. But he remembered the agreement and said to Emma: "You can dredge. But tell me when you're done." Emma, ​​who didn't know such rules from home, looked at him in surprise and then said: "When the sandcastle is finished." That was enough for Felix.

During this time, Anton was given his own task: he was allowed to guard the tomato egg timer. He did this with so much seriousness that he completely forgot that he wanted the excavator.

## A dispute that they solved alone

A few weeks later, Katharina first heard loud nagging from the children's room, then suddenly silence. She crept to the door. Felix and Anton were sitting on the carpet, with the red excavator and a blue dump truck between them.

“You dig, I tip,” Felix was explaining. “Then we’ll swap.” Anthony nodded. They built a construction site out of building blocks where the excavator loaded the blocks and the dump truck took them away. When Anton wanted the excavator after a while, Felix said: "In a moment. When the house is finished." And Anton waited.

Katharina quietly went back into the kitchen without saying anything. She later praised the two not for sharing, but for their construction work. “That was a really big house that you built together.” Felix beamed. "Anton tipped. He's really good at it."

The red excavator no longer has a shovel. It's still on the shelf.

## For your everyday life

Fighting over toys is part of everyday life with little siblings. Young children learn to share gradually, and forced sharing often leads to resentment rather than generosity. What helped the Neumann family:

- **Take turns instead of sharing immediately.** Whoever has a toy can finish playing. If you have to wait, you can ask when it will be your turn. This is often fairer for children than immediate handover.
- **Makes time visible.** An egg timer, hourglass or song provides a reliable limit for young children who cannot yet judge time.
- **Allowed “special things.”** Each child is allowed a few things that they don’t have to share. If you are sure that something belongs to you, it is often easier to share the rest.
- **Don't always let the older child give in.** "You're the big one" is often unfair to older siblings. The same rules for everyone create more peace.
- **Give space before intervening.** Children resolve many small disputes on their own if adults don't immediately intervene. Intervention is necessary when someone is hurting or a child is permanently inferior.
- **Praise the cooperation.** Instead of “It’s great that you shared,” a comment about playing together often has a stronger effect: “You built something big together.”

### Why sharing is so hard

That a three-year-old clutches an excavator with both arms and shouts “Mine!” calling is not a sign of selfishness. Around the age of two, children discover that they are their own person, and “mine” is part of that discovery. They only gradually learn to put themselves in other people's shoes, postpone wishes and estimate time, usually during their kindergarten years. For many children, voluntary, generous sharing only becomes more apparent in preschool and primary school age. If you know this, you can demand less from three-year-olds and be happy about every little step.

### Phrases that help with taking turns

Children need words to negotiate. So practice a few simple sentences, preferably playfully with cuddly toys or hand puppets: “Will you tell me when you’re finished?” – “It’s your turn when my tower is finished.” – “Do you want to play?” – “I just want to do this alone.” When these sentences are practiced in play, children come up with them more easily in arguments. And when a child uses them, it's worth a word of appreciation.

### If something breaks

Toys that are fought over sometimes break, like the shovel of the red excavator. Children then often feel guilty or blame the sibling. It's helpful to distinguish between intention and accident: "You both pulled, and then it happened. It wasn't intentional." Try to repair it together or think about what the toy can do now. An excavator without a bucket might make a great tow truck.

### Buying twice rarely helps

Some parents try to avoid arguments by buying everything twice. This only helps for a short time, because usually one of the two copies is suddenly the better one. Fewer, easily accessible toys and clear rules for taking turns bring more peace and quiet in the long run.

### To talk to your children

Ask your children: What things are so important to you that you don't want to share them? And what things are more fun with two people? You can write or draw a list together that will help in the next argument.

Hang the drawn list where the games are played, for example next to the toy shelf. Then even small children who don't read yet can look at the pictures to see what is special and what belongs to everyone.
