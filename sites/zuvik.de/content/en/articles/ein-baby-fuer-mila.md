Mila had been looking forward to the baby for months. She had put her hand on her mother Nadine's round stomach and felt it kick. She had suggested names, “Unicorn” and “French Fries” and, after much thought, “Paula.” She had painted a picture for the baby's room and explained to her teddy that he would soon no longer be the youngest in the family.

Then Paul came. Not Paula, but Paul, and that was the first disappointment. The second quickly followed: Paul couldn't do anything. He couldn't play, couldn't laugh, couldn't even look properly. He screamed, drank, slept and screamed again. And all the adults, absolutely everyone, thought he was wonderful.

## The visitors and the little word “also”

In the first few weeks, many visitors came to the Krüger family's apartment in Nuremberg. Grandma and Grandpa, Aunt Lisa, the neighbor, Dad's colleagues. Everyone brought gifts for Paul: rompers, rattles, a cuddly toy. Some brought something for Mila, others didn't. And when greeting us, almost everyone first said: “Where is the little one?” Only then, sometimes, did they think of Mila. "Oh, and you're a big sister now! Are you so happy too?"

Mila wasn't happy. She didn't say anything either. But Nadine noticed that Mila was starting to change. The four-year-old, who had been sober for a year, peed her pants again. She no longer wanted to eat herself, but wanted to be fed. She suddenly spoke in a high-pitched baby voice. And one evening when Nadine was breastfeeding Paul, Mila stood in front of her and said loudly: “Take him back to the hospital.”

## An evening with tears

Nadine's husband Tim was on a business trip that evening. Nadine was exhausted, hadn't slept through the night in weeks, and Mila's sentence hit her right in the heart. “You don’t say things like that!” she snapped at Mila. Mila ran into her room and slammed the door. Then Paul cried, and Nadine cried too.

Later, when Paul was sleeping, Nadine sat on the edge of Mila's bed. Mila was hiding under the blanket. "Are you still awake?" A quiet “yes”. Nadine was silent for a while. Then she said something she had thought about beforehand: "You know, sometimes Paul annoys me too. When he screams at night and I'm so tired."

The ceiling moved. Mila's face appeared. "Real?" - "Really. Babies are pretty tiring." Mila thought. “Do you still love me?” Nadine took her in her arms. "I love you just as much as I did before. It will never lessen."

Mila cried a little more, but differently. Relieved.

## The Mila period

The next day, Nadine called her midwife, who came for follow-up care. The midwife listened and smiled. “What Mila does is very common,” she said. "Many children revert to previous behaviors after the birth of a sibling. They see that the baby is getting attention with baby behavior and try it themselves. This usually returns when they realize that they are keeping their own space."

She suggested giving Mila a set time every day that was just hers. Not a long one, but a reliable one. When Tim returned, they sat down together and invented “Mila time”: every afternoon, when Paul took his nap, half an hour was just for Mila. She was allowed to decide what they did: do puzzles, paint, read aloud, have fun. No cell phone, no household, no baby.

Mila was suspicious. On the first day she asked three times if Paul would wake up. On the second day she chose a board game and won. On the third day, even before her nap, she stood next to her mother and waited.

## The big sister gets a task

The second idea came from Tim. He noticed that Mila never touched Paul. She looked at him, often for a long time, but she didn't touch him. “Maybe she’s afraid of doing something wrong,” he said.

So Mila was given a task that only she could do: She was allowed to apply baby cream to Paul's feet after bathing. Tim showed her, very slowly. Mila held the little foot in her hand and spread the cream with great seriousness. Paul struggled. Mila giggled. “He’s ticklish!”

From then on, applying lotion to her feet was Mila’s job. She reminded her parents when they forgot. When visitors came, she demonstrated it. And the visitors, to whom Nadine had previously given a little hint, said: "Wow, you can do that? You're a real big sister!"

## Paul laughs

The real turning point came a few weeks later. Mila squatted in front of Paul's rocker and made faces. Suddenly Paul grimaced and then laughed. A real, gurgling baby laugh, the first in his life. And he laughed not at Mom, not at Dad, but at Mila.

Mila ran through the apartment. "He laughed at me! Me first!" She told everyone, her grandma on the phone, the kindergarten teacher, the neighbor in the stairwell. The wetting had long since stopped. The baby's voice only came when she sang something to Paul.

Mila was still jealous sometimes. When Paul knocked her tower over when he started crawling, or when he sat on Dad's lap where she wanted to sit. But that was a different kind of jealousy. One that is known between siblings. No longer the fear of being replaced.

## A year later

For Paul's first birthday, Mila was allowed to blow out the candle because Paul couldn't do it yet. She helped him unpack the presents and explained to each guest which toys Paul already knew and which ones he didn't. When Grandma Gisela asked if she remembered that she once wanted to take him back to the hospital, Mila blushed. “I was still little then,” she said with dignity.

That evening, Nadine thought for a long time about the past year. Mila time still existed, even if it was sometimes canceled when Paul was sick. Then they caught up with her the next day, because Mila was strict about making sure that no Mila time was lost. Nadine was happy about that. It showed her how important that half hour was to her daughter and how good it had been to introduce her so early.

## For your everyday life

The birth of a sibling is a big change for many children. Jealousy, anger, or regression to previous behaviors are common and usually temporary. What helped Mila's family:

- **Allows mixed emotions.** When a child says they want to give the baby back, they are usually expressing fear, not malice. Acknowledging feelings (“The baby is pretty tiring, isn’t it?”) helps more than blame.
- **Reaffirm your love explicitly.** Children need a clear message that they are loved just as much as before. Say it, even if it seems self-evident.
- **Creates a fixed exclusive time.** A reliable half hour per day, just for the older child, without a baby and without a cell phone, provides security.
- **Give your own task.** Small, age-appropriate tasks related to the baby, such as applying lotion to the feet, getting a diaper or singing a song, make the older child feel proud.
- **Ask visitors to pay attention to the older child.** A quick note is often enough: greet the older child first, perhaps bring a little something for both of them.
- **Reacts calmly to regressions.** Wetting or baby talk usually occurs when the child feels safe. If regression persists for a long time or if you are worried, talk to your pediatrician.

### Prepare before birth

A lot of things can be done easier before the baby is here. Read picture books about babies and siblings together, look at photos from when the older child was a baby and tell them what it was like back then. Plan carefully who will look after the older child during the birth and explain this to them beforehand. If possible, big changes such as moving to a big bed or starting daycare should not fall exactly in the weeks surrounding the birth. And don't promise a playmate: a newborn can't play for months, and the disappointment is even greater.

### How much jealousy is normal?

Anger, withdrawal, baby talk, more defiance or even excessive tenderness towards the baby are common reactions. It is different if the older child hurts the baby, for example pinches or pushes it. Then it's important to protect the baby and not leave them unattended without embarrassing the big child. A sentence like “You can be angry, but you can’t hurt Paul” separates the feeling from the behavior. At the same time, offer other ways to get rid of anger: punch a pillow, stomp, scold loudly.

### When parents are exhausted

The first few months with a baby and an older child are exhausting. Accept help from family, friends and neighbors with cooking, shopping or picking up. The midwife is an important contact person during the postpartum period, including for questions about the older child. Under certain conditions, the health insurance company will cover household help after the birth; ask your cashier. And in many places there are early help services that support families with small children.

### To talk to your children

Asks the older child: What's nice about being a big sibling? And what's stupid? Both answers can be. Also tells what it was like when she was a baby herself. Many children like to hear that they too were once so small and so tiring.

A shared photo album with pictures from both babyhoods can help. Children like to compare who had more hair or screamed louder, and feel that they were cared for just as much as their little sibling is now.
