“Something that is older than your parents,” Ms. Kühn said and wrote it on the board. “Bring it with you and tell what it has to do with your family.”

Jonas, ten years old, fourth grade at a primary school in Kassel, looked through the options on the way home. His mother's cell phone: too new. The toaster: ugly, and he didn't know how old it was. The cuckoo clock in the hallway: too heavy for the satchel. At the dinner table he asked his mother Sabine. She thought for a moment and then said: "Ask Grandpa Werner. He saves everything."

## An assignment from school

Grandpa Werner lived two streets away in a terraced house with a garden where he grew tomatoes and talked to the blackbirds. He had lived alone since Grandma Helga died three years ago. Jonas often visited him after school; they played mill or repaired bicycles in the basement. But they rarely talked about the past. Grandpa Werner wasn't a man of many words.

When Jonas told him about the homework, Grandpa Werner nodded slowly, got up and disappeared into the bedroom. Jonas heard a cupboard door squeak, then the rustling of paper. Grandpa came back with a thick book, bound in brown linen, with chips at the corners. “My mother put this on,” he said. “I was your age then.”

He placed the album on the kitchen table, wiped the cover with his sleeve and pushed it towards Jonas. "Careful. The sides are sensitive."

## Black cardboard pages

Jonas had never seen a photo album like this. The pages were made of black cardboard, and there was thin, crackling paper between them, making the images blurry. The photos themselves were small, black and white, with jagged edges, and were housed in tiny corners of paper. Below it, in white ink and in a curved font that Jonas could barely read, was what was shown in the pictures.

“Werner with the new bike, Easter 1962.” A skinny boy with a crew cut and shorts, standing proudly next to a bike that looked way too big for him. “That’s you?” Jonas asked incredulously. Grandpa Werner laughed. "The bike had three owners before. I had to screw wooden blocks to the pedals so I could get to it."

They continued browsing. The flood on the Fulda, which filled the cellar. Confirmation, Grandpa in his first suit that was too big for him in the shoulders. The first day of the apprenticeship as a toolmaker, a group photo in front of a factory hall with many serious young men. A moped, a tent camp on the Edersee, a dance evening in 1971. "And then," said Grandpa Werner, tapping on a picture in which a young woman with her hair pinned up laughed at the camera, "that's when I met your grandma. She stepped on my foot and didn't even apologize."

Jonas looked at his grandfather. He had never heard him talk so much. And he had never seen him smile like that.

## The empty corner

There was a gap on a page from 1968. Four corners of paper were still stuck to the box, but the photo between them was missing. Beneath it, written in white ink, was just one name: “Klaus.”

“Who is Klaus?” asked Jonas.

Grandpa Werner didn't answer immediately. He took off his glasses, cleaned them laboriously with his handkerchief and put them back on. The kitchen clock was ticking. A blackbird scolded outside. Jonas realized he had asked the wrong thing, but he didn't know what.

“Klaus was my brother,” Grandpa Werner finally said. “My big brother.”

Jonas didn't know that Grandpa had a brother. Nobody had ever told him about it. “Where is he now?”

"He died. At nineteen. A motorcycle accident on the country road to Baunatal." Grandpa Werner looked at the empty space in the album. "My mother took the photo out afterward. She couldn't look at it anymore. And then we didn't talk about him anymore. That's how it was back then. People didn't talk about it."

## Uncle Klaus

Jonas sat very still. He thought about what it would be like if his own sister Marie were suddenly no longer there and then no one would say her name anymore. The thought made a lump in his throat.

“What was he like?” he asked quietly.

And Grandpa Werner told us. That Klaus had taught him to whistle on two fingers. That he collected Elvis records and combed his hair back with pomade, much to his father's annoyance. That he once spent a whole Sunday building a kite with Werner, which then got stuck in a poplar tree when it was first launched. That he had defended Werner when the big boys from the next street wanted to beat him up. “He was loud and funny and a little careless,” said Grandpa Werner. “And I admired him like no other person.”

When Sabine came to pick Jonas up that evening, the two of them were still sitting at the kitchen table. Jonas excitedly told her about Klaus. Sabine looked at her father in surprise. "Dad, I knew you had a brother. But you never talked about him. Not once."

“You never asked,” said Grandpa Werner. And after a pause: "And I never offered it. Maybe that was a mistake."

## The digital album

Over the next few weeks, Jonas spent even more afternoons with Grandpa Werner than usual. They had made a plan: They wanted to photograph the album so that it wouldn't get lost. Jonas carefully placed each page under the kitchen lamp and took photos of the pictures with his mother's old cell phone. Grandpa Werner dictated what was shown in the photos and Jonas typed it with two fingers. Who was that? Where was that? What happened that day?

Sometimes Grandpa Werner didn't know exactly, then he called his cousin Ilse in Göttingen, who had an even better memory than he. Ilse told about Klaus how he had climbed onto the stage at the shooting festival and sang a song, completely wrong and very loud. Grandpa Werner laughed on the phone until tears came to his eyes, and Jonas wasn't sure whether they were tears of laughter or others. Maybe both.

The evenings became a file with one hundred and twelve pictures and many stories. Sabine printed them out and had them bound. A new album, with white pages and large pictures, with Grandpa Werner's memories in Jonas' words underneath.

## The lecture

On the day of the presentation, Jonas stood in front of his class, the old album in both hands. He talked about the bicycle with the wooden blocks, about the flood, about the dance evening where Grandma Helga stepped on Grandpa's foot. The class laughed. Then he told me about Klaus. About the empty corner, about the dragon in the poplar and about the silence that had lasted so long.

It was very quiet in the classroom when he finished. Then Paula spoke up from the front row. "My grandma also had a sister who died. But I only know that because I once saw a grave." Ms. Kühn nodded slowly. “Maybe you’ll ask at home,” she said. “Sometimes adults wait for someone to ask.”

When Jonas came to Grandpa Werner's in the afternoon, there was an envelope on the kitchen table. Inside was a small black-and-white photo with jagged edges: a young man with slicked-back hair, leaning against a motorcycle, grinning. “I found it in the dresser, in my mother’s papers,” said Grandpa Werner. "She didn't throw it away. She just put it away."

Together they put the photo back into the four corners of the paper. The gap was closed. And Grandpa Werner said, more to himself than to Jonas: "So. Now he's back at it."

## For your everyday life

Old photos are more than just keepsakes. They are a door to conversations that might otherwise never happen - about childhood, about love, about loss. Older people in particular, who grew up in a time when painful things were kept silent, often only open up when someone asks with genuine interest. What helped Jonas and his grandpa:

- **Start with a specific reason.** A school project, a birthday, a move: an external reason makes it easier to get the albums out of the closet without it feeling like an interrogation.
- **Ask open-ended questions.** “What was it like back then?” or “What happened that day?” lead further than questions that can be answered with yes or no.
- **Endure silence.** When a question touches on a sore point, the answer sometimes takes time. Don't push, but don't change the subject immediately either.
- **Make something of it together.** Take photos or scan them and label them with memories: This protects the pictures and gives children and grandparents a joint project in which everyone can contribute something.
- **Also allow the sad stories.** Children can deal with loss and grief when adults tell them honestly and calmly. Confidence protects you less than you think.
- **Include other relatives.** Cousins, aunts, old neighbors: Everyone remembers different details, and often the complete picture only emerges through exchange.

### To talk to your children

- Why do you think Grandpa Werner didn't talk about Klaus for so long?
- Are there any photos of our family that you would like to look at with us?
- Who in our family would you like to ask what it was like in the old days - and what would you ask?
- How does it feel when someone says the name of someone you miss?
