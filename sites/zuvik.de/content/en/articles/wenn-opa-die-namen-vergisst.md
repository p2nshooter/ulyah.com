Nele first noticed it on her eighth birthday. Like every year, Grandpa Günther came with a gift wrapped in newspaper because he thought wrapping paper was a waste. He hugged her and said: “All the best, Susanne!”

Susanne was Nele's mother.

Everyone laughed. Grandpa laughed too, a little embarrassed, and corrected himself. "Nele, of course. You look so similar." Nele thought that was funny. But her mother became quiet, and for the rest of the afternoon she watched Grandpa with a look that Nele didn't understand.

## Small characters

More things like this happened in the following months. Grandpa forgot that he had already eaten. He called three times a day and told the same story. He put the milk in the cupboard and the salt in the fridge. And once, on a Saturday morning, the baker from his neighborhood in Bielefeld called Nele's mother: Grandpa was standing in front of her shop and didn't know how to get home.

After several visits to the doctor, the adults had a diagnosis: dementia. Nele's parents, Susanne and Christoph, sat in the kitchen for a long time one evening and talked quietly. Nele heard the word “care” and the word “getting worse” through the door.

The next morning she asked, “Is Grandpa sick?”

## How to explain it to a child

Susanne had prepared for this question. The doctor had advised her to be honest, in simple words, without causing fear, but also without sugar-coating. She sat down with Nele at the breakfast table.

"Yes, Grandpa is sick. The illness is called dementia. It is in his head, in the brain. The brain is like a large archive in which all memories are stored. With Grandpa, things are slowly getting mixed up in this archive. He can no longer find some memories, he mixes up some."

Nele thought. “That’s why he called me Susan?” - "Yes. In his archive, you and I are very close to each other. And sometimes he reaches for the wrong drawer."

“Will he be okay?” Susanne took a deep breath. "No. The illness is slowly getting worse. Grandpa is forgotten more as time goes on. But he is still Grandpa. And he loves you, even if he doesn't know your name."

Nele cried a little. Then she asked something Susanne hadn’t expected: “Is it contagious?” Susanne took her in her arms. "No. Not at all. You can visit and hug him as often as you want."

## What Nele could do

Nele wanted to do something. She was a child who wanted to solve problems. So the family thought together how they could help Grandpa.

The first idea came from Nele herself: she painted signs for Grandpa. “Milk” on the refrigerator, “plates” on the kitchen cupboard, “bathroom” on the bathroom door. The signs actually helped a little, and Grandpa was touched. “My granddaughter, the sign painter,” he said proudly to each visitor.

The second idea came from Christoph: a photo album with names. On each page they stuck a large photo of a family member and wrote underneath the name and one thing that connected Grandpa with that person. “Nele, your granddaughter, who plays don’t-be-annoyed with you.” Grandpa often leafed through it, especially on days when he was unsure.

## The days when Grandpa didn't recognize her

About a year later, the day Nele had been dreading came. Grandpa had now moved into a shared apartment for people with dementia, not far from Nele's school. When Nele visited him, he looked at her kindly and asked: “And who are you, young lady?”

Nele froze. Her mother squeezed her hand. "This is Nele, Dad. Your granddaughter." Grandpa nodded politely, but Nele saw that he didn't recognize her.

On the way home, Nele cried in the car. “He doesn’t know who I am anymore.” Susanne stopped at the side of the road and turned around. "I know it hurts. Me too. But do you know what the nurse told me? When Grandpa no longer recognizes you by name, sometimes he still recognizes you by heart. He feels that you are someone who is good for him. Did you see how he smiled when you showed him the picture?"

Nele nodded. Grandpa had smiled.

## New ways to be together

During the next visits, Nele tried new things. She played the game with Grandpa that he had taught her, Don't get angry. He sometimes forgot the rules, but he was happy every time he threw out a piece. She sang him songs that Susanne had taught her, old songs from Grandpa's youth. Something amazing happened during the songs: Grandpa sang along. He knew every word, even if he didn't remember what day it was.

“The music often stays the longest,” explained the nurse. “And the feelings.”

Nele learned that she didn't have to correct Grandpa. When he called her Susan, sometimes she would just say “Yes, Dad” and laugh with him. When he talked about the past as if it were today, she listened. It no longer mattered whether he knew who she was. What was important was that they had a good hour together.

## What Nele took with her

A year later, Nele was supposed to give a presentation at school about someone important to her. She chose Grandpa Günther. She told the class that he used to be a carpenter and had shown her how to make a bird box. That he now had a disease called dementia and forgot a lot of things. And that he could still sing all the verses of “Go out, my heart, and look for joy.”

A boy from her class spoke up. His grandmother had the same illness, he said, and he never dared to talk about it. After class the two talked to each other for a long time. They exchanged tips: that songs help, that photos help, that you don't have to say who you are every time.

In the evening Nele told her mother about it. “I thought it was just us,” she said. Susanne stroked her hair. "Many families have that. People rarely talk about it." Nele nodded thoughtfully. Then she said, "I'm talking about it. Grandpa would want that." And Susanne was very proud of her daughter.

## For your everyday life

When grandparents develop dementia, it affects the entire family, including the children. Children sense when something changes and need honest, age-appropriate explanations. What helped Nele’s family:

- **Explained honestly and simply.** Children understand more than adults often think. Images like the “archive in the head” help to make the illness tangible. Also says that the disease is not contagious and that it is no one's fault.
- **Prepares for change.** Children can cope better when they know that grandparents may not recognize them than if they are unprepared.
- **Give kids something to do.** Paint signs, create a photo album, sing songs: Children feel less helpless when they have something to contribute.
- **Seek new forms of closeness.** Music, touch, simple games and looking at photos together often still work when conversations become more difficult.
- **Allows grief.** It is sad when a loved one changes. Adults can also show that they are sad in front of children.
- **Get support.** Advice centers and relatives groups for families with dementia patients offer information and relief, including material that is specifically aimed at children.

### Make visits good

Short, quiet visits are often nicer than long and strenuous ones. Many people with dementia are clearer in the morning and become more restless in the evening. When greeting someone, simply introduce yourself by name instead of asking “Do you know who I am?” This question puts you under pressure and makes you sad if there is no answer. Bring something you can do together: a photo album, a simple game, a song. And say goodbye in a friendly manner, even if the visit could soon be forgotten. The good feeling often lasts longer than the memory.

### When grandpa says something strange

People with dementia repeat questions, mix up times and people, or are sometimes suspicious, such as when they think someone has stolen their glasses. Children are frightened by this. Explain to them that arguing or improving doesn't help because the illness changes the memory, not the will. It's better to respond to the feeling: "You're looking for your glasses, that's annoying. Come on, we'll look together." This way the sick person feels taken seriously and the situation usually calms down on its own.

### Help for the whole family

Caring for someone with dementia is stressful, and parents are often there for their children and their own parents at the same time. You don't have to do this alone. Care support centers and advice centers help with questions about the level of care, respite offers and living arrangements. The German Alzheimer Society offers a helpline and information, including material aimed at children and young people. Family groups give you the opportunity to exchange ideas with people who are experiencing similar things.

### To talk to your children

Asks: What did you notice about grandpa or grandma? What are you worried about? What would you like to do with them while you can? Children often have creative ideas about how they can create closeness.

Sometimes children don't want to visit their grandparents for a while because it makes them too sad. That's okay too. A painted picture, a voice message or a song that you record and bring with you maintains the connection until your child is ready again.
