“Everyone has one,” Noor said. "Really everyone. Except me and Leander, and Leander doesn't count because his parents are against everything."

It was the last summer before fifth grade. Noor, ten years old, would be moving to high school after the holidays, taking the bus instead of walking to elementary school, and she was convinced that she wouldn't make any friends there without a smartphone. She stood in the kitchen of the apartment in Augsburg, her arms crossed, and looked at her parents as if they were the last people in the world who didn't understand.

Her father Karim, a teacher at a vocational school, sighed. Her mother, Julia, who worked from home as a graphic designer, looked up from her laptop. They had had this conversation many times before. Until now they had always said: later. Now was later.

## What parents fear

In the evening, after Noor was in bed, Karim and Julia sat on the balcony and talked. Julia had concerns. She had read about how much time children spent on smartphones, she had heard about group chats where children were bullied, about videos that no 10-year-old should see, and about strangers contacting children through games. “She’s still so small,” she said.

Karim understood the worries. Every day at his vocational school he saw young people who hardly looked up from their cell phones. But he also saw something else. “If we forbid her completely, she won’t learn to deal with it,” he said. "Then she'll get one at thirteen and have no idea. I'd rather she learn it now, while we're still at it."

They decided on a smartphone. But not just like that. They wanted to decide beforehand with Noor how it should be used.

## The contract

Julia found templates for a so-called media usage agreement on the Internet, for example from the EU initiative klicksafe. In it, parents and children were able to set rules together, and not only obligations for the children, but also for the parents. They printed out a template, sat with Noor at the kitchen table one Sunday afternoon and went through it point by point.

It was a long negotiation. Noor wanted three hours of screen time a day, his parents wanted one. They agreed on one hour on school days, two on weekends, not counting homework and calls to grandma. The cell phone would be charged in the kitchen at night, not in the children's room. While we were eating, everyone's cell phones were in a basket on the counter, including the parents'. Noor had suggested this rule with a triumphant look at her father, who liked to check football scores over dinner.

Apps were talked about for a particularly long time. The parents set up parental controls and Noor was only allowed to install new apps together with them. They explained to her that many social networks and messengers have a minimum age that she had not yet reached, and that once photos are sent, they can never be fully retrieved. She was only allowed to send photos of other children with their permission.

And then came the point that Karim found most important. He wrote it himself in the contract, in thick pen: "If Noor sees or gets something that scares her, confuses her or seems strange, she can always come to us. She won't get in trouble for it and her cell phone won't be taken away from her."

“Really not?” Noor asked suspiciously. “Not even if I did something wrong?”

“Really not,” said Karim. "We can then think about what we should do together. But only if you tell us."

All three signed. The contract went on the bulletin board in the kitchen.

## The first few weeks

The smartphone was a used device from Julia's sister, with a case that had a picture of a fox on it. Noor loved it from the first moment. She set up a background image, took photos of the neighbor's cat every day and wrote long messages with lots of hearts to her grandma in Damascus, which brought grandma Samira to tears.

Not everything went according to contract. In the second week, Julia found the cell phone under Noor's pillow at half past ten in the evening. Instead of an argument, there was a conversation, and Noor was only allowed to use the cell phone for calls for a day, as stipulated in the contract. And Karim was caught twice at dinner by Noor secretly checking his phone under the table. He then had to do the dishes alone, as stipulated in the contract.

At school, Noor noticed that the smartphone made less of a difference than she had thought. The other children weren't suddenly her friends just because she now had one too. She found her friend in the theater group, where she started talking to a girl named Emilia about a book.

## The message in the night

In November, on a Tuesday evening, Noor got a message forwarded through a class chat from a number she didn't recognize. It was a picture of a doll with empty eyes and underneath a long text: If you don't send this message to twenty people, someone will be at your bedside at midnight. There was even a story about a girl who supposedly had this happen to her.

Noor read the message in the kitchen just before she had to put the phone down to charge. She turned pale. She considered sending the message further, just to be sure. But she knew that would be mean because then twenty other children would be scared too. She put down the phone and went to bed. And lay there, eyes open, listening to every sound.

At eleven o'clock she got up again and went to her parents in the living room, her face crying. “I have to show you something,” she said. “But you said I wouldn’t get in trouble.”

## What happened next

Karim read the message. He put down the cell phone and took Noor in his arms. “You were right to come to us,” he said. “That’s exactly why we agreed to this.”

Julia explained to her what a chain letter is. That someone would come up with something like this to scare people and that the stories in it are made up. That no one would come, not at midnight or at any other time. That it was right not to forward the message. “You broke the chain,” Julia said. “You saved twenty children from being afraid tonight.”

They deleted the message together. Noor was allowed to sleep in her parents' bed that night. And the next day Julia wrote to the class teacher, who brought up the topic in class without saying who the tip came from. It turned out that five other children had received the same message and had not slept. They sent two of them on. Neither had told their parents.

Hearing this, Noor was silent. Then she said, “Maybe they didn’t have a contract.”

## For your everyday life

The first smartphone is a big step for many children - and often associated with worries for parents. A complete ban will not protect children in the long term because they will have to learn how to use it at some point anyway. Clear agreements, technical protective measures and, above all, trust help more. What helped Noor's family:

- **Set rules together.** A media usage agreement, for which there are free templates on the Internet, makes agreements binding. When children negotiate, they are more likely to accept the rules.
- **Set rules for parents too.** Children take rules more seriously when adults also follow them, for example when it comes to banning cell phones at the dinner table.
- **Charge your cell phone outside the children's room at night.** This protects sleep and prevents secret surfing.
- **Use parental control settings.** Age ratings, time limits and the joint installation of new apps give children a protected environment.
- **Talk about dangers without causing fear.** Chain letters, strangers in chats, photos without permission: Children should know what they can encounter and that they are not alone.
- **Promise that honesty won't get you in trouble.** Children only talk about unpleasant experiences if they don't have to worry about their cell phone being taken away.

### When chats turn into bullying

Chain letters are unpleasant, cyberbullying is more serious: insults, embarrassing photos, exclusion from groups or fake profiles. Signs can be that a child withdraws, suddenly avoids the cell phone or, on the contrary, constantly controls it nervously, sleeps poorly or no longer wants to go to school. If your child is affected, secure evidence with screenshots, block the senders, report the content in the app and inform the school. Don't respond to the attacks. There is also support outside the family, for example through the number against grief on 116 111 or through counseling services in which young people help other young people.

### Trust instead of secret control

Some parents consider reading their children's messages secretly. This may be reassuring in the short term, but it destroys the very trust that children need to come forward in an emergency. Open agreements are better: what settings are there, why, and when will they be relaxed? With every year that a child uses their cell phone responsibly, they are allowed more freedom.

### To talk to your children

- What do you want to use a smartphone for and what would you rather not use it for?
- Which rules do you think make sense and which should also apply to us parents?
- What would you do if you received a message that scared you?
- Why is it important to only share photos of others with their permission?
- When should we review the contract together and what would have to happen to give you more freedom?
- Who else could you confide in if something unpleasant happens to you online?
