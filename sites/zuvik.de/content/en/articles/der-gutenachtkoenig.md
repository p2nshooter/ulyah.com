The first appearance usually came at 7:52 p.m. The door to the living room opened a crack and a small face poked through. "I'm thirsty." Katrin Lehmann got up, got a glass of water, took Luis back to bed and covered him up. At 8:05 p.m.: “I have to pee.” At 8:20 p.m.: “There was a noise.” At 8:41 p.m.: “I can’t sleep.” And at 9:10 p.m., when Katrin and her wife Jana were finally sitting on the sofa, Luis stood in the door again, this time with tears: “Something lives in my closet.”

Luis was five, and since moving into the new apartment in Leipzig, falling asleep had become an entire evening routine. He slept like a log in the old apartment. There was a closet with a door that never fully closed, a street lamp whose light cast patterns on the ceiling through the curtains, and sounds he didn't recognize.

## The evenings when everyone lost

Katrin and Jana had tried everything. They had scolded Luis, which made him cry and made the evening even longer. They had laid with him until he slept, and had fallen asleep themselves, dressed, with contact lenses in their eyes. They had promised him that there were no monsters, which he didn't believe. And, on one particularly tired evening, they had said, "If you come out again, there will be no television tomorrow," which resulted in Luis not coming out, but crying quietly into his pillow for an hour.

“We do something different every evening,” Jana stated as they sat exhausted at the kitchen table again at half past nine. "Sometimes we're strict, sometimes we're soft, sometimes we're with him, sometimes we're not. How is he supposed to know what counts?"

She was right. The only thing that was reliably the same every night was the chaos.

## A ritual with a crown

Of all people, Luis himself provided the impetus. While tidying up, he found a cardboard crown from the child's last birthday party. He put it on and declared: “I am the king of the nursery.”

Katrin had an idea. "Do you know what a king does every night? He checks his kingdom before he sleeps." Luis' eyes lit up.

This is how the Bedtime King ritual came into being. Every evening, after brushing his teeth, Luis put the crown on and made his rounds with a flashlight. He shone his light into the closet (empty), under the bed (a sock, a Lego brick, nothing else), behind the curtain (the lantern, “it looks after the street”). He then declared his kingdom safe, placed the crown on the bedside table and crawled into bed.

Then came the second part: always the same book, always the same song, always the same three questions. What was nice today? What was stupid? What are you looking forward to tomorrow?

## The cup of water and the magic formula

The excuses remained. Katrin and Jana now knew them all and decided to get ahead of them. Next to the bed came a cup with a lid and water. Before the tour, Luis went to the toilet again, naturally as part of the ritual. And for the noises they were given a formula that Jana had come up with: “This is the apartment, it also falls asleep.” If Luis heard a crack, he was allowed to recite it quietly.

But the most important thing was an agreement that they discussed with Luis in the afternoon, not in the evening when everyone was tired. “We’ll come back and check on you when you’re already in bed,” Katrin promised. "You don't have to come out. We're coming."

They kept their word. Five minutes after the goodnight kiss, one of them opened the door quietly, stroked his head, said “It’s all good, King” and left again. After ten minutes again. The distances increased over time. And on many evenings, Luis was already asleep on his second visit.

## The nights that remained difficult

There were setbacks. When Luis had a middle ear infection, the illness threw everything into disarray, and for a few nights he went back to sleeping in bed with his mother. When a child in kindergarten talked about ghosts, the fear of the closet came back. And on evenings when he hadn't been outside enough during the day, he simply wasn't tired.

But Katrin and Jana had learned something: a bad evening wasn't proof that the ritual had failed. He was a bad evening. The next day the crown reappeared, the tour was made again, and the kingdom was declared safe again.

## When the grandparents came to visit

A test came in the spring when Jana's parents looked after Luis for a weekend while his mothers danced at a wedding. Grandpa Werner didn't think much of rituals. “We used to just go to bed,” he said on the phone, and Jana could almost hear him rolling his eyes.

Nevertheless, Katrin wrote down the process on a piece of paper: brushing teeth, toilet, crown, tour with flashlight, the book about the little bear, the song about the moon, three questions, kiss goodnight, check again after five minutes. Grandpa Werner read the note with a frown.

On Sunday evening, when the two women returned, Grandpa Werner was sitting contentedly in the kitchen. “He explained everything to me,” he reported. "I had to wear the crown because I was the guest king. We shone into the closet together. On the third look he was already asleep." He was silent for a moment, then added: "Your mother would have needed something like that for you too, Jana. You also had a ghost every night as a child."

Jana laughed. She didn't even remember that. But it comforted them that Luis' fears weren't the result of anything they'd done wrong. They were simply part of growing up, just as they had already been part of their own growing up.

## What the crown actually meant

Months later, when Luis now almost always fell asleep without interruption, Jana asked him during the tour: “Do you still think that something lives in the closet?” Luis shone his light into it, thought about it and said: "No. But I'll look anyway. A king has to look."

Jana understood at that moment what the ritual was for him. It was never about convincing him that monsters don't exist. It was about him being able to do something about his fear. And that he could count on the same thing happening every night, in the same order, with the same people who had promised to check on him again.

## For your everyday life

Difficulty falling asleep is one of the most common concerns for parents of young children. They are particularly common after changes such as moving, a new sibling or starting daycare. What helped Luis' family:

- **A fixed evening ritual.** Always the same order, such as brushing your teeth, going to the toilet, a short ritual, a book, a song, a conversation, gives children security. Less is often more: a ritual that lasts twenty minutes can be maintained.
- **Take fears seriously, don't explain them away.** “There are no monsters” doesn't convince many children. It is often more helpful to give the child something in his hand: a flashlight, a patrol, a formula, a cuddly toy that pays attention.
- **Preempt the excuses.** Water within reach, going to the bathroom before bed, a small night light: What children need should be there before they have to ask for it.
- **Promise to check on the child and keep the promise.** Children who know their parents are coming again don't have to get up to make sure themselves.
- **Discuss rules during the day, not in the evening.** Everyone is tired in the evening. New agreements can be explained in peace in the afternoon.
- **Pay attention to day and exercise.** Lots of time outside, exercise and a quiet hour before bed without a screen make it easier for many children to fall asleep.
- **Get advice if problems persist.** If a child hardly sleeps for weeks, wakes up very often at night, snores or is noticeably exhausted during the day, talk to your pediatrician.

### Nightmare or night terror?

Not all nighttime crying is the same. After a **nightmare**, a child usually wakes up in the second half of the night, can remember the dream and can be comforted. Closeness, a light and a few calm words help here. The **night terror**, in technical language Pavor nocturnus, usually occurs in the first few hours after falling asleep: the child screams, perhaps sits in bed with his eyes open, but is not awake at all and does not recognize his parents. In the morning it doesn't remember anything. Waking up usually doesn't help in this case. It is better to remain calm and make sure that the child does not get injured. Night terrors are common in toddlers and kindergarten children and usually go away on their own. If they occur very frequently, talk to your pediatrician.

### A room to fall asleep in

The room itself can also help. Many children sleep better when it is quiet, cool and dark. A blackout curtain keeps out streetlights like the one outside Luis' window, and a dim nightlight with a warm glow is enough for orientation. Cupboards whose doors do not close can be closed securely with a small magnet or a hook. Sometimes it's just that one crack that sets the imagination going.

### To talk to your children

Ask in the afternoon, not in the evening: What scares you when you fall asleep? What would help you feel safe? Let your child have a say in what their evening ritual looks like. A king who has invented his own ritual rules it with pride.
