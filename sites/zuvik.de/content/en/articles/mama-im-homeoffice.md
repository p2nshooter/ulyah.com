"Mom, look! I built a kite!"

Elias, six years old, stood in the doorway of the small study, holding a structure made of toilet paper rolls, wool threads and a lot of adhesive tape. Behind him, on the screen of Yasemin's laptop, eight faces could be seen, in small tiles, all now looking at Elias.

Yasemin, who had just been presenting her department's quarterly figures, felt the blood rush to her cheeks. “Elias, darling, I’m in a meeting right now,” she said quietly, with a forced smile at the camera. “Show it to me now, will you?”

Elias didn't move. “But he can fly,” he said. "Really."

One of the colleagues laughed. Yasemin didn't know if he meant it in a friendly way. She stood up, led Elias out gently but firmly and closed the door. As she sat back down, she heard him crying outside.

## There and yet not there

Yasemin has been working primarily from home for two years, as a project manager at an insurance company in Bonn. She had wanted it that way. No more commuting, more time for the family, the opportunity to pick Elias up from kindergarten at lunchtime. Her husband Daniel worked shifts as a paramedic and was often home at irregular times.

But since Elias started school and finished at half past twelve, the model no longer worked. He came home at lunchtime and Yasemin was there. And yet not there. She was sitting behind a door, talking on the phone, typing, thinking about tables and appointments. This was difficult for Elias to understand. When mom is home, mom is there for me, right?

On days like this, Yasemin felt torn. At work she felt like she wasn't concentrating enough. With Elias she had the feeling that she was constantly sending him away. In the evening she was exhausted and still had the impression that she had not done justice to anyone.

## A conversation after the dragon

The evening after the video conference, she sat down with Elias on the carpet in his room. The dragon lay next to him, one wing was snapped off. “I’m sorry I sent you out earlier,” she said. “I want to see your dragon properly now.”

Elias showed it to her, at first hesitantly, then more and more enthusiastically. The dragon had a name, Firefang, and it could fly if you swung it around by the wool above its head. Yasemin admired him extensively.

Then she asked: “Elias, how do you feel when I’m in the study and the door is closed?”

Elijah thought. “Stupid,” he said. "I never know if I'll be allowed in. Sometimes you say yes, and sometimes you say no. And then you're angry."

“I’m not angry,” said Yasemin. Then she paused. "Well. Sometimes I get stressed. And that might look like evil."

## The traffic light

That evening, when Daniel came home from shift, the three of them sat at the kitchen table and thought. The problem wasn't that Elias was bothersome. The problem was that he had no way of knowing when he was being disruptive. To him, the closed door always looked the same.

Daniel had the idea of ​​the traffic light. He cut three circles out of a piece of cardboard, Elias painted them red, yellow and green, and they attached them to a ribbon that hung on the study doorknob. A cardboard arrow attached with a clothespin indicated the current color.

Green meant: Come in, mom has time for you. Yellow meant: Mom is working, but you can knock if it's important and ask a quick question. Red meant: Mom is in a meeting or a phone call, please don't come in. "Except," said Daniel, "in an emergency. If you hurt yourself or something bad happened, you're always allowed in. Even when the light is red."

“What is an emergency?” Elias asked.

They made a list. Blood was an emergency. Fire was an emergency. If someone Elias didn't know rang the doorbell, it was an emergency. A finished kite was not an emergency. “You’ll show me this when the traffic light is green again,” said Yasemin. “And then I’ll take a good look at him.”

## The quarter hour

The traffic light alone wasn't enough. Elias also needed to know when the traffic light would turn green. So they introduced a second rule: the Elias quarter hour. Every day at three o'clock, no matter what was on Yasemin's work list, she turned the traffic light green, closed the laptop and spent fifteen minutes just with Elias. They played Uno, built with Lego, walked around the block or ate an apple together on the balcony. Elias was allowed to decide what they did.

Yasemin entered the quarter hour into her work calendar as “Appointment E.” so that no one scheduled a meeting for it. At first she thought it was strange to write her own son on a calendar. Then she realized how good it felt.

For the other hours, Elias was given a box of things he could do alone: ​​coloring books, audio books, craft supplies, a five-hundred-piece puzzle that he worked on for weeks. Daniel set up playdates with friends on days when he had an early shift and was home in the afternoon. And on Tuesdays and Thursdays, Elias went to childcare after school, where he could play with other children in the garden.

## The day with the knee

Three weeks after the traffic lights were introduced, Yasemin had an important conversation with management. The traffic light was red. After twenty minutes the door flew open and Elias stood there with tears on his face and a bleeding knee. He had tripped over a flower pot on the balcony.

Yasemin apologized, turned off the camera and microphone and took care of Elias. She washed his knee, put a plaster on it and held him in her arms until he calmed down. Then she turned the camera back on, with Elias on her lap. “Sorry,” she said. “An emergency.”

The manager, a man in his late fifties whom Yasemin had previously only known as strict and formal, leaned forward. "What happened, young man?" he asked. Elias talked in detail about the flower pot. The manager nodded seriously. “I have three grandchildren,” he then said. "The littlest one fell off the climbing frame last week. Plasters with dinosaurs on them help best."

Elias beamed. Yasemin almost laughed. The conversation continued, and Elias sat quietly on her lap until, after five minutes, he got bored and walked out. At the door he turned around and turned the traffic light arrow back to red.

## For your everyday life

Working from home can give families more flexibility and time together. However, it is often confusing for children when a parent is at home but not available. Clear signals, reliable times together and good alternatives help everyone. What helped Elias' family:

- **Make availability visible.** A traffic light or sign on the door shows children when they are allowed to disturb and when they are not. This gives security instead of uncertainty.
- **Clearly define emergencies.** Children should know exactly when they can come. This takes away their fear of not being able to disturb people in an emergency.
- **Plan fixed times together.** A reliable quarter of an hour a day that is on the calendar is often more valuable for children than many interrupted minutes.
- **Offer alternatives.** A box with activity materials, playdates or childcare offers bridge the working hours.
- **Plan realistically.** With an elementary school child at home, not every working day is equally productive. Important meetings can sometimes be scheduled for times when the child is being cared for.
- **Speak openly with the employer.** Many superiors and colleagues have children of their own and are more understanding than you might think.

### Divide the working day wisely

Not every job requires the same level of concentration. Many parents who work from home schedule tasks that require full attention during times when the children are at school, daycare or day care, and do emails or routine work when the children are playing at home. Important meetings can often be arranged so that a second adult is there. Having lunch together without a laptop at the table gives the day a center that children can rely on, and the break is good for adults too.

### When the screen cracks

Some days you can't make an important phone call without a radio play, children's program or tablet. That's not a failure. The only thing that's helpful is to use this time consciously rather than out of embarrassment: it's better to have a previously agreed sequence than an open ending. Radio plays and audio books are a good alternative because children can paint, build or do puzzles.

### When the child is sick

A sick child and a full working day hardly fit together, even when working from home. Parents with statutory health insurance can take child sick days to care for a sick child under the age of twelve and receive child sickness benefit from the health insurance company. You can find out how many days there are and what you need to pay attention to from your health insurance company. A child who is sick at home needs peace and care, not a mother or father giving presentations on the side.

### To talk to your children

- How does it feel when I'm at home but have to work?
- What would be a real emergency for you where you can always bother me?
- What would you most like to do in our fifteen minutes together?
- What do you like to do alone when I don't have time?
