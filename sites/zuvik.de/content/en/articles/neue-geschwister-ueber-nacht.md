Sophie was ten when her life changed in a single moving van. For five years she lived with her mother Anja in a three-room apartment in Hamburg-Barmbek, just the two of them. They had their rituals: pizza on Friday, TV show night on Saturday, breakfast in bed on Sunday. Sophie saw her father every other weekend.

Then Anja met Thomas. Thomas was nice, Sophie had to admit that. He repaired her bike, told funny stories and cooked better than her mother. But Thomas also had two sons: Leon, twelve, and Mats, eight. And after a year, the adults decided they would all move in together. In a terraced house on the outskirts of town, with a garden, “so that everyone has enough space”.

## Enough space

Enough space was relative. There were four upstairs rooms in the terraced house, and Sophie didn't have to share one of them with anyone, that was a promise. But she shared the bathroom with two boys who threw their towels on the floor and left the toothpaste on the table. The kitchen was suddenly loud. There were five people sitting at the dinner table, and two of them were constantly talking about football and video games, which Sophie didn't understand.

Friday pizza was still available, but now they had to agree on a type, and Leon always wanted salami and Mats didn't want mushrooms. There was no longer a series night because the television was in the living room where the boys were playing. And breakfast in bed on Sunday? Nobody thought about that anymore.

Sophie pulled away. She came home from school, went to her room and didn't come out until dinner. She said little while eating. When Thomas asked her something, she answered in monosyllables.

## Leon isn't happy either

What Sophie didn't know: Leon wasn't doing much better. He had lost his old room, his friends from the old street, his usual route to school. And he suddenly had a stepsister who, he felt, treated him like air. Mats, the youngest, seemed to settle in the quickest, but he also sometimes cried in the evenings when he thought about his mother, with whom he only saw every other weekend.

Anja and Thomas naturally noticed the tensions. They had read books about blended families and knew that growing together takes time. But knowing and experiencing are two different things. “I feel like I’ve taken away my daughter’s home,” Anja said one evening. Thomas took her hand. "And I give my sons theirs. We have to help them find a new one. Together."

## What only belongs to Mom and Sophie

The first step was that Anja understood Sophie's withdrawal not as rejection, but as sadness. Sophie mourned the loss of life as a couple. So Anja created an island of them. Every Sunday morning there was breakfast in bed again, just for the two of them. During this time, Thomas went to the bakery with his sons and had breakfast with them in the kitchen. “It’s not rude,” he told the boys. “You only have time with me.”

In fact, Thomas had his own ritual with Leon and Mats: on Wednesday evenings the three of them went to the swimming pool. That remained. The new family didn't have to do everything together. There were allowed to be islands where the old families continued to live.

## A joint project

The second step came rather by chance. There was an old, dilapidated shed in the garden of the terraced house. Thomas wanted to tear it down. Mats, the youngest, had another idea: "Can we make this a treehouse? A ground treehouse?"

The adults laughed, but the idea stuck. Thomas said, "If you three plan this together, I'll help build it." Leon and Sophie really looked at each other for the first time. Leon shrugged. Sophie too. But the next day the three of them sat at the kitchen table with paper and pencil.

It turned out that Sophie was good at drawing and Leon was good at arithmetic. Mats had the craziest ideas, a slide, a bell, a secret compartment. They argued about the color (Sophie wanted turquoise, Leon black, they agreed on dark green) and the rules (no adults without an invitation). They spent three weekends building with Thomas, sawing, screwing and painting. Anja brought lemonade and otherwise stayed out of it.

## The evening in the shed

When the shed was finished, the children hung a sign on the door: “Club of Five (but only three).” It wasn't entirely clear what that meant, but it was her thing. On the first evening they were allowed to sit there with flashlights and eat chips.

Anja and Thomas were sitting on the terrace and heard laughter through the wooden walls. Then, after a while, Leon's voice: "Sophie, what's your dad like?" And Sophie, who usually never spoke about her father, told us. And Leon talked about his mother. And Mats said that he was sometimes afraid that Dad and Anja could also split up.

The adults looked at each other. They had no idea how much their children were affected. But they were glad the children had found a place where they could tell each other.

## Not siblings, but family

It took a long time for the terraced house to feel like a home for everyone. There were arguments about the bathroom, jealousy when Thomas praised Sophie or Anja cuddled with Mats, difficult weekends when the children were with their other parents and came back with mixed feelings.

But on Sophie's eleventh birthday, Leon showed up at her door in the morning with a poorly wrapped present: a drawing pad and a set of expensive pens. “I shared it with Mats,” he murmured. “So you can continue planning the club.”

Sophie hugged him, a little awkwardly. They didn't call each other siblings. But they were a family, a new kind of family that they invented together.

## The first Christmas

The biggest test came in December. Sophie was supposed to spend Christmas Eve with her father, Leon and Mats were supposed to spend the first holiday with their mother. When should the new family even celebrate? Anja suggested December 23rd. "Our own Christmas. With everything that goes with it."

The children were skeptical at first. “Christmas on the wrong day?” asked Mats. But then the Club of Five took over the planning. Sophie painted place cards, Leon put together a playlist that alternated between football anthems and Christmas songs, and Mats insisted that everyone find a present under the tree that couldn't cost more than five euros. In the evening the five of them sat around the table and Thomas gave a short speech that he had to start over three times because the children were laughing.

“That was the best fake Christmas in the world,” said Mats as he went to bed. And the next year no one asked if they could celebrate on the 23rd. It had just become her day.

## For your everyday life

When two families move together, a lot changes for all children at once: apartment, habits, attention from parents. Growing together takes time, often years. What helped Sophie's new family:

- **Allows mourning for the old.** Children also lose something when they move in together: rituals, spaces, exclusive time with one parent. Their withdrawal is often sadness, not rejection.
- **Preserves islands of the old family.** Fixed times in which each child is alone with their biological parent provide security. Not everything has to happen together.
- **Provide your own space.** Having your own room or at least your own area that no one enters without permission is particularly important for many children in blended families.
- **Enables collaborative projects.** Something that children plan and create together often bonds more than any forced family program.
- **Don't force feelings.** Step-siblings don't have to immediately like each other or call each other siblings. Respect in everyday life is a good start.
- **Talk about fears.** Children from separated families are sometimes afraid that their new family will break up too. Expressing this fear and taking it seriously can provide relief.
- **Get support.** Many family counseling centers offer advice and groups specifically for blended families.

### The role of the stepparent

Many experts advise that a stepparent initially behaves more like a friendly, reliable adult, rather than an additional guardian. Relationship comes before education: shared experiences, interest in the child's hobbies, help with little things. At first, rules and consequences remain primarily the responsibility of the birth parent. As a couple, you discuss common house rules in advance, such as bathroom or screen time, and then implement them together. This way, children don’t feel patronized by someone “new”.

### Arrangements with the other parents

Children in blended families often commute between two households. The calmer the handovers are, the easier it is for them to make the change. A common calendar for weekends, vacations and public holidays prevents misunderstandings. And one rule applies to all adults: Don't talk badly about the other parent in front of the children. Otherwise, children get into loyalty conflicts and feel like they have to choose between people who love them.

### Patience with time

Experts assume that it usually takes several years for a patchwork family to grow together. Setbacks, such as after a vacation with the other parent or during puberty, are part of it and do not mean that you have failed.

### To talk to your children

Ask each child individually: What do you miss from before? What's good in the new family? And what do you want to make it feel more like home?

And as a couple, ask yourself regularly: What is going well and what is bothering us right now? A patchwork family thrives on the adults keeping in touch with each other, especially on difficult days.
