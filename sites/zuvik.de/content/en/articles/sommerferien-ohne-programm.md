“Lea is flying to Mallorca,” said Elif on the last day of school before the summer holidays. "Jonas is going to Denmark. And Mia is going to a riding stable in Bavaria for two whole weeks." She threw her satchel into the corner of the hallway. “And we don’t do anything.”

Her mother Nadine stood in the kitchen of the apartment in Nuremberg-Gostenhof and peeled potatoes. She had been dreading this moment. In May, the family car had broken down, the repairs would have cost more than the car was worth, and the money that had been saved for a vacation on the North Sea had gone into a used station wagon. Her husband Murat worked as a warehouse manager for a shipping company and had three weeks of vacation, but there wasn't enough for a trip this year.

“We don’t do nothing,” said Nadine. “We’re on vacation at home.”

Elif rolled his eyes. She was ten and knew exactly what that meant.

## Six weeks

Little Can, seven, took the news more calmly. He just asked: “Then can I go to the outdoor pool every day?” But Elif remained disgruntled. She sulked for the first two days of vacation, lying on her bed, reading comics she already knew, and sending messages to her friends that rarely got answered because everyone was at the beach or on the highway somewhere.

During the first three weeks of vacation, Nadine worked part-time as an administrative assistant in a doctor's office; After that she had vacation and Murat took over his three weeks. They had enrolled the children in the city's holiday program for the mornings and for a few days on which there were excursions. But the rest of the six weeks lay before them, unplanned, like a big, blank piece of paper.

On the third evening, over dinner on the small balcony, Murat said: "I have an idea. We'll make a list. Everyone writes down what they really want to do during these holidays. It shouldn't cost anything or almost nothing. And then we'll do as much of it as we can manage."

## The list on the fridge

Elif didn't want to take part at first. But when Can started dictating his wishes, she couldn't resist. Can wanted to go to the outdoor pool every day, sleep in a tent one night, build a dam in the stream and find out how many scoops of ice cream he could make. Murat finally wanted to take the children to see the castle, which he had driven past every day for twelve years without ever going inside. Nadine wanted to spend an evening watching stars and making pizza in her own oven with homemade dough.

Elif thought for a long time. Then she wrote, in neat handwriting: "Write a book. Ride a bike without a fixed destination. Stay up as late as I want."

The list went on the fridge. Next to it, Nadine hung a large sheet of paper with six columns, one for each holiday week. “It’s not a timetable,” she explained. "This is a logbook. We write down what we experienced here."

## The outdoor pool and the castle

The municipal outdoor swimming pool was twenty minutes away by bike. In the first week, Nadine and the children went there almost every afternoon after work. With the family ticket for the season, each visit cost little more than an ice cream. Can did his seahorse this week and then stood in front of the lifeguard as proudly as if he had won a gold medal.

On the first Saturday they visited the imperial castle. Murat had previously borrowed a book about the history of Nuremberg from the city library and on the way up he talked about emperors, knights and the deep well that reached almost fifty meters into the rock. In the well house, a guide poured water into the shaft, and it seemed like an eternity before it splashed at the bottom. Can covered his ears, Elif was amazed. That evening she wrote in the logbook: "The well is as deep as a skyscraper. Dad knew everything except why the knights didn't have toothbrushes."

## The night in the garden

Nadine's parents, Grandma Hilde and Grandpa Gerd, owned an allotment on the outskirts of town, with an arbor, an apple tree and a meadow big enough for a tent. In the second week, Murat and the children put up the old family tent that had been in the basement for years and was missing a pole. They replaced it with a broom handle.

The evening was unforgettable. Grandpa Gerd grilled sausages and corn on the cob, Grandma Hilde brought potato salad. When it got dark, everyone lay on blankets on the grass and looked up at the sky. It was the week when the Perseids were at their peak, and Nadine had looked online to find out when the most shooting stars could be seen. They numbered seventeen. At each one, someone was allowed to make a wish, loudly or quietly. Can wanted a dog out loud. Elif quietly wished for something that she wouldn't tell anyone.

That night, Elif, Can and Murat slept in the tent, on sleeping pads that smelled like a cellar. At three in the morning, Can woke everyone up because he urgently needed to go to the bathroom. At six o'clock they woke up the blackbirds. It was, Elif thought, the best hotel in the world.

## Cycling without a destination

In the fourth week, Murat had vacation and he fulfilled Elif's wish: cycling without a fixed destination. They set off, just the two of them, and at each intersection Elif was allowed to decide whether to go left, right or straight. They ended up on the banks of the Pegnitz, then in a small forest, then at a bakery in a suburb they had never heard of, where they had the best streusel snails Elif had ever eaten. They came home after four hours with sunburn on their noses and mosquitoes on their legs.

“We could have gotten lost,” said Elif in the evening. “We did too,” said Murat. “That was the best thing.”

After that they set off almost every week. And between trips, Elif often sat on the balcony and wrote in a thick notebook. She didn't show anyone what she wrote.

## The book

On the last weekend of the holidays, Elif put the magazine on the breakfast table for her parents. On the envelope it said in colorful letters: "The Summer We Went Nowhere. From Elif." Inside were twenty-three pages full of stories and drawings: the deep well, the shooting stars, the tent with the broomstick, Can's seahorse, the sprinkle snails.

Nadine read the magazine twice. The second time she had to wipe her eyes.

On the first day of school, the children in class talked about their vacation. Lea showed photos of Mallorca, Jonas of Danish dunes. When it was Elif's turn, she hesitated for a moment. Then she took her notebook out of her satchel and read the story about the night in the garden. When she was finished, Mia, who had been at the riding stables for two weeks, asked: "Where is the garden? Can you go there too?"

## Next summer

The following year there was enough money again for a week on the North Sea. When the family wrote the new holiday list, at the top, in Elif's handwriting, was: "Tent night in Grandpa's garden. With Mia." Murat asked if she really wanted to do that since they were going to the sea. Elif looked at him as if he had said something very stupid. “One thing is vacation, Dad,” she explained. “The other thing is tradition.”

## For your everyday life

Not every family can travel during the summer holidays, and not everyone wants to. It can still be difficult for children when everyone else talks about plane rides and beach vacations. At the same time, holidays at home offer a special opportunity: time without pressure to rediscover your own surroundings. What helped Elif's family:

- **Take disappointment seriously.** It's okay to be sad when others move away. Children need understanding first before they are open to alternatives.
- **Write a wish list together.** When each family member contributes their own ideas, the holidays become a shared project rather than a substitute program.
- **Discover your own city as a travel destination.** Castles, museums, parks and riverside paths that you have driven past for years are suddenly adventures.
- **Take advantage of city offers.** Many cities and municipalities offer inexpensive holiday programs, holiday passes or summer reading clubs in libraries.
- **Plan small adventures.** A night in a tent, a bike ride without a destination or an evening under the stars cost little and will be remembered for a long time.
- **Record experiences.** A holiday logbook or a self-written notebook helps children to become aware of their own experiences and to talk about them proudly.

### Support when money is tight

Many families don't know what help is available for leisure and holidays. Families who receive citizen's benefit, child allowance, housing benefit or social assistance can receive, among other things, subsidies for trips to school and daycare as well as a monthly amount for sports clubs, music schools or leisure activities through the education and participation package. Many cities issue a holiday pass in the summer, with which swimming pools, museums and events are cheaper or free. Church communities, youth associations and municipalities also offer holiday camps, for which there are often discounts. Ask at the town hall, the youth welfare office or at school what is available in your area.

### Boredom is allowed

Holidays at home also mean hours in which nothing happens. Children don't have to have a program every day. It is precisely in long, empty afternoons that ideas like Elif's book arise. Parents can endure boredom instead of immediately filling it with suggestions.

### To talk to your children

- What would you put on a vacation list if it didn't cost anything?
- What place in our city don't you know yet, even though it's very close?
- How does it feel when everyone else talks about their travels? What can you say then?
- What makes a really good holiday day for you?
