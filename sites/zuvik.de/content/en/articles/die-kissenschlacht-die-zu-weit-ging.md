As always, it started with laughter. Paul, nine, threw a pillow at his sister Greta, six. Greta squealed with pleasure and threw back. Soon all the cushions on the sofa flew through the living room of the Weber family in Freiburg, then the blanket, then the stuffed animals. The two rolled on the carpet, tickled each other, fought, laughed so loudly that the neighbor's dog barked.

And then, as always, things tipped over. Paul pressed a pillow on Greta's face, just briefly, during the game. Greta couldn't breathe, kicked, screamed, and when Paul let go, she cried. Not because of laughter. Correct.

Her father, Martin, came rushing out of the study. "What's going on here?" Greta sobbed. Paul stood there with a red face and a guilty conscience. “We were just playing,” he said. “She laughed too.”

## Fighting forbidden?

The pillow fights almost always ended like this. Sometimes someone hit their head, sometimes tickling turned into pinching, sometimes Greta was simply too small for Paul's strength. Martin and his wife Sabine had often talked about banning romping completely.

“It always ends in tears,” said Sabine that evening. Martin was unsure. "But they love it. And I wrestled with my brother as a kid too. I think that's part of it."

The next week they asked the director of Greta's kindergarten, Ms. Brenner, who had been working with children for twenty years. “Routing and running around are important for many children,” said Ms. Brenner. "They learn to assess their strength, pay attention to others and sense boundaries. But for this they need clear rules. In our exercise room there is a stop word. When a child says it, everyone stops immediately. Always."

## The stop word

At the weekend, Martin and Sabine sat down with both children. “We want you to keep raging,” Martin said. "But in such a way that no one cries. We need rules for that. And you can choose them too."

The first rule was the stop word. The children discussed for a long time which word it should be. “Stop” was too boring, Paul thought. “Break” was said too often by mistake. Greta suggested “pineapple” and everyone laughed. So “pineapple” became the stop word. If you shout “pineapple”, everyone will stop immediately. Without discussion, without “just one more time”.

The second rule: Nothing on the face, nothing on the neck. Pillow on the body only.

Greta suggested the third rule: “If someone cries, it’s over for the day.” Paul protested, but Martin liked the rule. “If someone cries, it’s no longer a game,” he said.

## The first test

The first test came the next evening. The pillow fight started, got wild, and at one point Paul was on top of Greta, tickling her while she couldn't breathe from laughing. “Pineapple!” gasped Greta.

Paul paused. For a moment it looked like he wanted to continue. Then he rolled to the side. Greta sat up, gasped and grinned. "Okay. Go ahead."

Martin had been watching from the door and held his breath. It had worked. And, what he didn't expect: Because Greta knew that she could stop at any time, she became braver. She now dared to do things that she had previously avoided. And Paul, who knew he had to stop at “Pineapple,” was able to let off steam in a more relaxed manner without always having to judge for himself when it was too much.

## Paul also needs a stop word

A few weeks later there was a surprise. During a particularly wild round, Paul suddenly shouted “Pineapple!” Greta stopped and looked at him in surprise. “You’re the strong one!”

Paul rubbed his ear. "You elbowed me in the ear. That hurts even big ones." Greta looked at him for a long time. Then she said, “Sorry.” And then, after a pause: “I didn’t know grown-ups could say pineapple.”

Sabine, who was told this at dinner, found this moment almost more important than any other. Greta had learned that her big brother was also vulnerable. And Paul had learned that saying “pineapple” wasn’t a weakness.

## A conversation about strength

After the ear incident, Martin took the opportunity to have a conversation he had wanted to have for a long time. On a quiet Sunday afternoon he sat down with Paul on the steps in front of the house. “You’ve gotten pretty strong,” he said. Paul grinned flattered.

“Do you know what the hardest thing about being strong is?” Martin asked. Paul shook his head. "That you always have to be careful how much of it you use. When you romp with Greta, it's like playing with a smaller dog. You only have to use part of your strength at a time so that it's fun for both of you."

Paul thought about it. “How much?” – “About enough for Greta to win sometimes,” said Martin. Paul grimaced. “But then I’ll let them win.” – “Sometimes. And sometimes you win. A game in which the same person always wins eventually stops being fun for the other person.”

In the following weeks, Sabine observed that Paul used his strength more consciously when he raged. He didn't let Greta win on purpose; she would have noticed and resented it. But he no longer sat on her with all his weight. Greta, in turn, began to develop strategies with which she could outsmart her big brother. This didn't make the pillow fights more boring, but rather more exciting.

## The Tobe corner

Over time, the family set up a real play area. In the corner of the child's room were two old mattresses, a pile of pillows and a mat. Fighting was allowed here, but no longer in the living room, where the coffee table with its hard edges was. Martin also sometimes raged along. Then the same rules applied to him, and both children insisted that he respect “Pineapple.”

There were still tears, of course. Children fall, bump into each other, are tired. But the tears had become less frequent. And they no longer ended in accusations, but mostly in an apology and a short pause before the next pillow flew.

## Pineapple in kindergarten

Greta proudly talked about her new stop word in kindergarten. Ms. Brenner laughed when she heard about it and asked the group if they wanted to try it. Since then, sometimes fifteen children in the exercise room shout “Pineapple!” at the same time, mostly because it’s funny, but sometimes because it’s too much for someone. And Greta explains to the newbies with a serious expression that everyone really has to stop eating pineapples. “Even the big ones.”

## For your everyday life

Fighting and running wild are a joy and an important experience for many children. They learn to assess their strength, read other people's signals and be considerate. Clear rules help to achieve this. What helped the Weber family:

- **Agree on a stop word.** A word that everyone stops immediately gives every child security. If children choose it themselves, they are more likely to stick to it.
- **Set clear boundaries.** For example: nothing on the face, nothing on the neck, no kicking, no biting. A few rules that everyone knows work better than many.
- **Creates a safe place.** A play area with mattresses and pillows, away from hard edges, reduces injuries.
- **Be careful of unequal strength.** If siblings are far apart in age, the younger child needs special protection. The stop word helps, but does not replace the adult's attention.
- **Ends the game if there are tears.** If someone cries, it's no longer a game. A break helps everyone calm down.
- **Have a go yourself.** When parents play along and stick to the rules, children learn by example.

### Game or serious?

Not every fight is an argument. You can recognize playful fighting by a few signs: The children laugh, the roles change, sometimes one is on top, sometimes the other, and both come back voluntarily after a break. Often the stronger child holds back a little without anyone asking. Things get serious when faces are tense, the same person is always on the bottom, someone wants to get away and isn't allowed to, or laughter turns into crying. Then it's time to intervene calmly.

### When rage turns into anger

Sometimes the mood changes so quickly that no one knows how it happened. Then separate the children without accusations, give everyone a moment to breathe and only then listen to both sides. Questions like “What happened?” and “What do you need now?” help more than finding someone to blame. Often a glass of water and five minutes of peace are enough for both of them to want to play together again.

### Movement belongs in every day

Children who sit a lot often have a great need to move, which then discharges itself in the living room. The World Health Organization recommends that school-age children and adolescents get an average of at least 60 minutes of exercise a day, ranging from moderate to strenuous. A walk or bike ride to school, time on the playground or a sports club often make running around at home more relaxing.

### To talk to your children

Ask: What do you enjoy most while playing around? When does it get too much for you? And which word do you want as a stop word? Rules invented together are taken more seriously than rules imposed from above.

And feel free to talk about your own pillow fights from the past. Children like to hear that their parents also raged, argued and made up as children. That doesn't make the rules any less serious, but it does make them much more likeable.
