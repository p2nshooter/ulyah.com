The Schulze family in Münster had everything twice. Two high chairs, two child seats, two bicycles in the same color, two pairs of rubber boots in size 31. Ben and Tim were identical twins, eight years old, and anyone who didn't know them well constantly confused them. Their grandmother called them “the boys,” their teacher “the Schulzes,” and the neighbors mostly just called them “the twins.”

Her parents, Daniela and Frank, had always found it practical to treat both of them equally. Same clothes, there was no argument. Equal gifts, that was fair. Same hobbies, that saved travel. For three years both played in the same football club, on the same team, in similar positions.

## The sentence in the car

One Thursday, on the way to training, Tim suddenly said from the back seat: “I don’t want to play football anymore.”

Daniela looked in the rearview mirror. Ben looked at Tim in surprise. “Why not?” asked Daniela. Tim shrugged. "I don't like it. I never liked it."

“But you’ve been playing for three years,” said Daniela. Tim was silent. Then he said very quietly, “I only joined because Ben wanted to.”

Ben immediately protested. "That's not true! You scored goals too!" And then the two argued until they arrived at the sports field. Tim got out, put on his shoes and played along. But Daniela saw him standing on the sidelines all evening, his hands in his pockets when the ball wasn't near him.

## The question no one had asked

That evening Daniela told her husband about it. Frank was surprised. “He never said anything.” – “We never asked him either,” replied Daniela. "We always both asked. And Ben always answers first."

They thought about it and realized how often this was the case. When they asked what the boys wanted for their birthday, Ben would say three wishes and Tim would say "Same." When it came to vacation, Ben wanted to go to the sea, and Tim nodded. Ben was the louder, the faster. Tim was the quieter one. And in the “the boys” package, Tim somehow disappeared.

## Separate time

Daniela and Frank decided to try something out. Each boy was given an afternoon alone with a parent once a week. Alternately, sometimes with mom, sometimes with dad. The boy was allowed to decide what they did.

Ben's first afternoon with Frank was, unsurprisingly, soccer in the park. Tim's first afternoon with Daniela took them to the city library. Tim ran purposefully to a shelf that Daniela didn't even recognize: books about insects. He took out a thick volume of photos of beetles and leafed through it, and as he leafed through he began to talk. Of stag beetles, which only live for a few weeks as adults. Of ants that carry many times their own weight. Of praying mantises.

Daniela listened and was amazed. How did he know all this? “From the school library,” said Tim. “At recess, when Ben plays soccer.”

## Own ways

After that afternoon several things changed. Tim was allowed to quit football. Instead, Daniela enrolled him in a children's conservation group that met every Saturday morning on the outskirts of town and observed insects, plants and birds. Tim came back from the first meeting with muddy pants and shining eyes.

Ben was offended at first. “Now I play alone,” he said over dinner. Frank nodded. "Yeah. And you can tell Tim what it was like at the next game. And he'll tell you what he found." Ben grimaced, but didn't say anything else.

The clothes were no longer bought straight away. Ben wanted jerseys, Tim wanted a sweater with a bug on it. For the first time at Christmas, the grandmother gave two different gifts after Daniela asked her: a football for Ben and a magnifying glass with a built-in light for Tim.

## The jealousy came later

Not everything went smoothly. A few weeks later, Tim received a lot of praise at school for his insect collection. His teacher asked him to talk about it in front of the class. Ben, who had always been the better known of the two, suddenly became quiet and sullen.

That evening, when he went to bed, he said to Frank: “Everyone thinks Tim is great now.” Frank sat on the edge of the bed. “And how does that make you feel?” Ben hesitated. "Funny. We always used to be great together."

Frank understood what he meant. For Ben, the twin package had also been a protection. Now he too had to learn to be alone. "You know," Frank said, "Tim's great with bugs now and you're great with football. And together you're still brothers. It doesn't stop."

## What the teacher noticed

At the parent-teacher conference in the fall, the class teacher, Ms. Yıldız, said something that stuck with Daniela for a long time. “Tim has been contacting us a lot more often in the last few weeks,” she said. "He used to wait for Ben to answer and then nod. Now he has his own answers. Sometimes completely different than his brother."

She suggested that the two of them be seated at different group tables in the new school year, not as a separation, but so that everyone could find their own place. Daniela was unsure at first. She had always thought the twins needed each other for support. But when she asked the boys, they both surprised her. Ben thought it was “okay, but we’ll play together at break.” Tim just said: “Then I can finally say something first.”

Frank summed it up that evening: "We've been raising twins for eight years. Now we're starting to raise two sons." Daniela had to laugh, but she knew what he meant. The two were not halves of a whole. They were two whole children who happened to share their birthday on the same day.

## The beetle on the football field

In the summer, the whole family sat on the sidelines as Ben's team finished their season. Tim had his insect book with him, but this time he was watching the game. When Ben scored a goal, Tim was the first to jump up and cheer.

At halftime, Ben came running to the sideline, sweating. “Tim, look!” He had something in the cup of his hand. A small, shiny green beetle crawled across his fingers. "I found it on the goal post. What is it?" – “A rose beetle,” said Tim, immediately in his element. "They're completely harmless. They like flowers."

The brothers put their heads together. Daniela watched them and thought that she had never seen the two of them as connected as they were now that they were allowed to be different.

## Two cakes

On her ninth birthday there were two cakes for the first time. Ben had wanted a cake shaped like a soccer field, with green icing and two waffle goals. Tim wanted a ladybug, red with black chocolate dots. The guests sang “Happy Birthday” twice, once for Ben, once for Tim, and everyone blew out their own candles. The two then exchanged a piece, without anyone suggesting it. “That’s fair,” Ben told the guests. Tim nodded and had icing up to his ears.

## For your everyday life

Whether twins or siblings with a small age gap, when children are often treated as a unit, one of them can easily fade into the background. Every child needs the feeling of being seen as their own person. What helped the Schulze family:

- **Ask each child individually.** Questions to “the children” are often answered by the louder child. When asked individually, quieter children are more likely to express their own wishes.
- **Creates time for two.** Regular time alone with one parent, in which the child determines what happens, shows interests that get lost in everyday life.
- **Allows different hobbies.** What's practical doesn't have to suit both people. Own interests strengthen self-confidence.
- **Abandons equal treatment at all costs.** Fair does not mean equal. Different gifts or clothing can be fairer than identical ones.
- **Take jealousy seriously.** When one child suddenly gets more attention, the other sometimes feels neglected. Talking about it helps both of them.
- **Use names instead of collective nouns.** “Ben and Tim” instead of “the boys” is a small change with a big impact.

### One class or two?

There is no general answer to whether twins should go to the same daycare group or school class. Some twins give each other security, especially in the beginning. Others constantly compare themselves, compete or, like Tim, let others speak for themselves. Talk to the teachers your children experience in everyday life and ask the children themselves. A decision doesn't have to last forever: What was right in the first grade may look different in the third. Separate tables or groups also help within a class so that each child can make their own friendships.

### When others compare

Twins are constantly compared: who is taller, who is faster, who reads better? This often happens thoughtlessly, through relatives, neighbors or other parents. You can counteract in a friendly way, for example with a sentence like “The two are very different, and that’s a good thing.” At home, it helps to measure each child based on their own progress, not the other. Praise that refers to the effort, “You worked on this picture for a long time,” does not compare and is still strengthening.

### Exchange with other families

In many places there are groups and associations for parents of twins and multiples. There you can discuss what other families have decided on clothing, hobbies, daycare and school. The experience of parents whose twins are older can help them to view their own questions more calmly.

### To talk to your children

Ask each child individually: What do you like to do most? What do you like about your brother or sister, and what is completely different about you? The answers often show how different siblings are who look the same from the outside.
