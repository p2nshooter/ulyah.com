It started on December 28th. Somewhere in the backyard of the apartment building in Berlin-Neukölln, a firecracker exploded, loud as a gunshot, and the echo echoed back and forth between the house walls. Noah, five years old, dropped his spoon, ran out of the kitchen and disappeared into his room. When his mother Svenja followed him, she found him under the bed, his hands on his ears, curled up like a hedgehog.

“It’s just a blast,” Svenja said quietly and lay down on the floor so he could see her face. "Outside. Very far away from us."

Noah shook his head. He didn't come out again until it had been quiet for half an hour.

## The memory of last year

Svenja and her husband Thomas knew what that meant. Last year they celebrated New Year's Eve with friends in Kreuzberg, in an apartment on the fifth floor with a view over the rooftops. At midnight everyone was standing at the window to watch the fireworks and Thomas was holding Noah in his arms. When the first rockets exploded, Noah was so shocked that he could no longer calm down. He screamed until he was hoarse and ended up hiding under his friends' guest bed. They had driven home at half past twelve, through streets full of smoke and noise, with a shaking child in the back seat.

Noah's big sister Marie, nine, had a different experience. For her, New Year's Eve was the greatest thing: staying up late, the lights, the colorful balls in the sky. “This year I want to stay awake until midnight,” she announced in November. "And watch rockets. And not go home early because of Noah."

Svenja and Thomas were faced with a dilemma. Two children, two completely different wishes, one single night.

## What lies behind the fear

On the evening of December 28th, Svenja sat down on the sofa with Noah and asked him what actually scared him about the firecrackers. Noah thought for a long time. “That it’s so loud,” he said. "And that you don't know when it's coming. And that it might break you."

“Break what?”

"The house. Or us."

Svenja understood that Noah wasn't just afraid of noise. He didn't know what a firecracker was, how it worked and why people lit it. For him it was an unpredictable danger that came out of nowhere.

The next day they borrowed a picture book about New Year's Eve from the city library, in which a little bear was afraid of the fireworks. At the kitchen table, Thomas explained to Noah with paper and pens what's in a rocket: a bit of powder that burns and little beads that glow with colors when they get hot. He explained that the banging was just air dispersing very quickly. “It’s loud, but it can’t break our house,” Thomas said. “And neither do we when we’re inside.”

## A plan for two children

Together with Marie and Noah, Svenja and Thomas made a plan for New Year's Eve. This year they would celebrate at home, in their own apartment, where Noah knew his way around and had a safe place to retreat. They would invite friends, a family friend from the house whose children were about the same age as Marie and Noah.

And they would celebrate midnight twice.

“Twice?” Marie asked suspiciously.

“Once at seven in the evening,” Svenja explained. "It's children's midnight. We all count backwards together, toast with children's punch and make sparklers on the balcony. Then Noah goes to bed. And you can stay up until the real midnight and watch the fireworks with Dad at the window."

Marie thought about it. “What if Noah wakes up at midnight?”

“Then mom is with him,” said Thomas. "And if he wants, he can go to the window too. But only if he wants to."

Noah also got a pair of hearing protection, real ones, with big blue earplugs like the ones construction workers wear. He tried it out immediately and ran around the whole apartment with it. “All I can hear is my heart,” he said enthusiastically.

## The New Year's Eve

On December 31st it was already loud in Neukölln from midday onwards. Noah winced every time, but he stopped running under the bed. When it got too much for him, he put on his hearing protection and continued playing with his building blocks as if it was nothing.

In the afternoon, the family came from the third floor, Ayşe and Kemal with their children Defne and Emir. They played games, ate raclette, and at half past six there was wax casting. Instead of lead, which hasn't been allowed to be sold for years, they melted leftover wax in an old spoon over a candle and poured it into a bowl of cold water. Noah's figure looked like a dragon. “This means that you will be very courageous in the new year,” said Ayşe, who performed the interpretation with great seriousness.

At seven o'clock everyone gathered in the living room. Thomas had set the wall clock at two minutes to twelve. The children counted in unison: “Ten, nine, eight…” and when there was zero, everyone shouted “Happy New Year!” and toasted with children's punch. Then they went out onto the balcony and lit sparklers. Noah held his tightly and drew circles in the darkness. He didn't have any hearing protection on. When a rocket whistled somewhere, he looked up, hesitated - and continued painting.

## At midnight

Noah was asleep at eight, his ear defenders next to his pillow. Marie, Defne and Emir held on bravely with board games, popcorn and a movie. At twelve o'clock they stood at the living room window with Thomas and Kemal and the sky over Neukölln exploded with all colors.

Svenja sat in the children's room with Noah. He woke up at two minutes past twelve. He looked at her, confused, and heard the crash from outside. Then he reached for the ear protection and put it on. “Mom,” he said, much too loudly because he could barely hear himself, “I want to look.”

Svenja took him in her arms and carried him into the living room. Marie made room for him at the window. Noah looked up into the sky, his hands on the blue capsules, and saw golden rain, red stars and green orbs that shattered and slowly burned away. He didn't say anything. He just looked.

After five minutes he said: “That’s enough.” Svenja took him back to bed. He immediately fell asleep again.

On New Year's morning, at breakfast, Noah told anyone who would listen that he had seen the fireworks at midnight. Twice in fact: once at seven and once right. “I was a dragon,” he said. “Like wax.”

## For your everyday life

Many children are afraid of the noise on New Year's Eve, especially in cities where there is banging for days. This fear is understandable and not a sign of weakness. With preparation, understanding and a good plan, the turn of the year can still be beautiful for everyone. What helped Noah's family:

- **Want to understand the fear.** Ask what exactly the child is afraid of. Often behind the fear of noise there is also a lack of knowledge about what is actually happening.
- **Explain what fireworks are.** Picture books and simple explanations help children classify the unknown and assess the danger more realistically.
- **Create a safe place.** Having your own home with a familiar retreat often gives anxious children more security than a party at someone else's house.
- **Offer hearing protection.** Ear muffs for children significantly reduce noise and give them the feeling of being able to do something themselves.
- **Celebrate a Children's Midnight.** An early countdown with punch and sparklers lets younger children get in on the festivities without having to stay up until midnight.
- **Don't force it.** A child should be allowed to decide for themselves whether they want to watch the fireworks. Courage grows when you show it voluntarily.
- **Think about siblings.** Older children have their own wishes. A plan that takes both into account prevents one's fear from crowding out the other's joy.

### Safe through New Year's Eve

New Year's Eve fireworks in the F2 category, i.e. rockets and firecrackers, may only be sold to adults in Germany and are usually only lit on December 31st and January 1st. Children aged twelve and over are allowed to use small fireworks in the F1 category, such as firecrackers or sparklers; smaller children can only use them with an adult at their side. Sparklers belong outside, and the wire stays hot for a long time: a bucket of water to put it down helps. Explain to your children that they should never pick up unexploded bombs, even on New Year's morning. And keep your distance from people who set off firecrackers. Very loud bangs at close range can permanently damage hearing, in both children and adults.

### If the fear remains

It's normal for a child to be afraid on New Year's Eve. It is different when the fear of noise determines everyday life, for example when a child panics every time there is a thunderstorm, every vacuum cleaner or every flush of the toilet or avoids places where it could be noisy. Some children are particularly sensitive to sensory stimuli. Then it's worth talking to your pediatrician, who can assess whether further support makes sense.

### To talk to your children

- Are there any noises that scare you? What helps you then?
- How do you think fireworks work?
- What's most important to you on New Year's Eve: staying up late, the lights, or something else entirely?
- What would you have done if you were Noah when he woke up at midnight?
- On New Year's Eve, how can we think about animals that are afraid of noise, for example the neighbor's dog?
- What do you want for the New Year when we toast at seven o'clock?
