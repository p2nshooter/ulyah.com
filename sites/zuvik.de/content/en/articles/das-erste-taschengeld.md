It started at the checkout in the supermarket, as it often does. Ronja, seven years old, stood next to the shopping cart and held up a pack of surprise eggs. "Please, Mom. Just one."

Kerstin shook her head. “Not today, Ronja.” Ronja put the eggs back, took a pack of trading cards instead and looked at her mother with wide eyes. Kerstin shook her head again. Ronja made a curse that lasted all the way to the car.

In the evening, in the apartment in Essen-Rüttenscheid, Kerstin told her husband Jörg about it. “Every purchase is a negotiation,” she said. "I say no, she asks again, I say no again. And sometimes I give in because I'm tired. And then she knows it's worth continuing to ask."

Jörg nodded. “Maybe it’s time for pocket money,” he said. "Then she can decide for herself. And we don't have to negotiate anymore."

## Two euros on Saturday

Over the next few days they read up, talked to parent friends and looked at the recommendations that youth welfare offices publish every year for different age groups. For Ronja's age, two euros a week seemed reasonable to them. Payment was always to be made on Saturday morning, in coins, so that Ronja could touch and count the money.

They also agreed on a few rules, which they explained to Ronja over breakfast. The pocket money belonged to Ronja. She was allowed to decide for herself what she bought with it, as long as it wasn't anything dangerous. There was no advance. And when the money was gone, it was gone until the next Saturday.

“And if I want something really expensive?” asked Ronja.

“Then you have to save,” said Jörg. “For several weeks.”

Ronja nodded seriously. She was given a money box, a small ceramic pig with a slit in the back and a rubber stopper on her stomach, and on the first Saturday Jörg put two one-euro coins in her hand. Ronja looked at them as if they were pieces of gold.

## Ten minutes at the kiosk

The two euros lasted ten minutes. Ronja went with her father to the kiosk on the corner, where there were sweets in large jars that you could buy individually: sour snakes, licorice snails, foamy strawberries, cola bottles. Ronja took a long time to choose, had each piece put into a paper bag and counted the money on the counter. She had already eaten half of it on the way home.

Jörg said nothing. He had agreed with Kerstin not to comment on Ronja's decisions. It was her money.

On Sunday afternoon the three of them walked through the city center with Ronja's little brother Ole. In the window of a toy store stood a plastic horse, a brown mare with a white mane, painted so lifelike that Ronja stopped in front of it as if she were rooted to the spot. There was a price tag underneath: €8.99.

“I want that,” said Ronja. "Please."

“You can buy this with your pocket money,” said Kerstin.

Ronja did the math. She was in first grade and could count up to twenty, albeit slowly. “Two euros every week,” she said. “Then...then it will take a long time.” She looked down at her empty jacket pocket. “And I’ve already spent it all on the candy.”

It was a moment when Kerstin would have liked to have bought her horse. She didn't.

## The waiting

The next few weeks were a test of patience for Ronja and her parents. On the second Saturday, Ronja put both coins in the piggy bank without hesitation. On the third Saturday she stood in front of the kiosk again, money in her fist, struggling with herself. “Just one euro,” she said to her father. “And I’ll save one.” Jörg nodded. She bought two licorice snails and a bottle of cola and put the other euro into the pig.

In the evening she poured the contents of the money box onto her bed and counted. Five euros. Almost four were still missing. She was disappointed. “If I hadn’t bought licorice on Saturday, it would have been more.”

“Yes,” said Kerstin, who was sitting on the edge of the bed. "That's the hard thing about saving. You have to give something up now so you can get something bigger later."

Ronja drew the horse on a piece of paper and hung it next to the money box. Below that she drew nine boxes, one for each euro. She painted five of them brown.

## A fight and a promise

In the fourth week, Ronja clashed with Ole. Ole had colored her favorite book with a thick green felt-tip pen, and Ronja had pushed him so hard that he fell against the shelf and got a dent. Jörg was angry. “There’s no pocket money for that this week,” he said, before he’d even thought.

Ronja burst into tears. Not because of the punishment for pushing, but because of the horse. “Then it will take even longer!”

In the evening Kerstin and Jörg talked about it. “We said the pocket money was hers,” said Kerstin. "If we take it as a punishment now, it will become a reward system. Then it will no longer be about learning about money, but about being good."

Jörg thought. He knew she was right. The next morning he went to Ronja and said: "I was wrong about the pocket money. You'll get it on Saturday. But the pushing wasn't okay and Ole hurt himself. What can we do about that?"

Ronja thought. Then she went to Ole, apologized and helped him put a plaster with dinosaurs on the bump. They erased the book together as best they could. The green spot remained. Ronja decided he looked like a frog.

## Grandma Hildegard's five euros

In the fifth week, Grandma Hildegard came to visit and when she heard that Ronja was saving for a horse, she secretly gave her a five-euro note. Ronja was beside herself with joy. With grandma's money she would have had enough.

But then she did something her parents didn't expect. She held the note in her hand for a while, then said: "I want to buy the horse with my own money. I'll save Grandma's for something else." She folded the bill and put it in a small box on which she wrote “Grandma Money.”

Kerstin looked at Jörg over Ronja's head. Neither of them had taught her that.

## The purchase

In the sixth week, on a Saturday in March, Ronja counted her money for the last time. Nine euros and twenty cents, with a few coins she found in the sofa. She filled in the last box on her picture with brown.

At the toy store, she placed the money on the counter herself, coin by coin, while the saleswoman waited patiently. She got eleven cents back and a small paper bag with the bay mare in it. She held the bag with both hands the whole way home.

The horse was given a name, Caramel, and a place on the bedside table where Ronja looked at him every evening. It wasn't the most expensive toy in her room. But it was the only thing she had waited six weeks for.

A few weeks later, at the checkout in the supermarket, Ronja reached for a pack of surprise eggs. Then she put them back again. “They’re too expensive for that little bit of chocolate,” she said to her mother. Kerstin had to suppress a laugh.

## For your everyday life

Pocket money is one of the first opportunities for children to practice handling money. You learn to make decisions, endure bad purchases, save for things and assess the value of things. It is important that they are allowed to have their own experiences. What helped Ronja's family:

- **Agree on a fixed amount and day.** Regularity gives children planning security. Youth welfare offices publish age-appropriate recommendations that parents can follow.
- **Cash for small children.** Coins can be touched, counted and seen as they decrease. This makes money understandable for younger children.
- **Do not comment on decisions.** Even buying candy quickly is a learning experience. Children notice when they regret something.
- **Don't give an advance.** If you have to wait, you learn patience and planning. An advance takes away exactly that experience.
- **Don't use pocket money as a punishment or reward.** When money is tied to behavior, it's no longer about learning but about obeying. Misconduct should be discussed differently.
- **Make savings goals visible.** A picture of the desired item and a progress bar help children persevere.
- **Discuss monetary gifts.** Families can decide together with the child whether money from grandparents counts as pocket money or is saved separately.

### What children can buy with their money

The fact that Ronja was allowed to buy her horse alone also has a legal basis. Children aged seven and over have limited legal capacity in Germany. According to the so-called pocket money paragraph in the Civil Code, your purchases are valid if you pay for them with money that was given to you for free use, i.e. with pocket money. Larger purchases, installment purchases or contracts such as a cell phone subscription, however, require parental consent. This is a good opportunity for children to talk about the difference between small and large decisions.

### From piggy bank to account

As you get older, your ability to handle money can grow. Many families switch from weekly to monthly pocket money for older primary school children or at the start of secondary school so that children learn to plan over a longer period of time. Banks offer accounts for children and young people that do not allow for overdrafts. Looking at the bank statements together shows where the money is going and prepares you for your own budget.

### To talk to your children

- What would you buy with your pocket money if you were free to choose?
- Have you ever bought something and then regretted it? What did you learn from it?
- What would you like to save on and how long would you wait to do so?
- Why is something sometimes worth more if you have saved for it yourself?
