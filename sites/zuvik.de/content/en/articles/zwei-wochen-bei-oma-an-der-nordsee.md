The summer holidays for Marie, nine, and Ben, six, began like every year with a train ride. Her parents took her to the train station in Göttingen, put her on the train heading north and handed her over to Grandma Inge in Bremen, who picked her up in her old station wagon. Then we went on to Dorum on the North Sea, where grandma lived in a small house behind the dike.

For two weeks. Without mom, without dad. Just grandma, the sea and the wind.

## The Grandma Rules

Things were different with grandma. For breakfast there were rolls with Nutella every day. In the evenings the children were allowed to stay up until it got dark, and in summer it got dark very late on the North Sea. There was no television, but they were allowed to walk barefoot through the mudflats until their feet were black with mud. Grandma thought brushing her teeth was important, but combing her hair was less important. And if Ben was still hungry after dinner, there was a bowl of vanilla pudding with cherries.

Marie called it all “the grandma rules,” and she thought they were great.

There were different rules at home in Göttingen. Nutella only on weekends. Go to bed at eight on school days. TV, yes, but only for an hour. And only sweets after dinner if there was something to celebrate.

## The return

When the children returned after two weeks tanned and with sand in all their pockets, their parents Stefanie and Jonas were extremely happy. The joy of seeing each other lasted until exactly the next morning.

“I want Nutella,” Ben said at the breakfast table. “Today is Monday,” said Stefanie. “Nutella is available on the weekend.” – “Grandma has Nutella every day!” – “But here we are not at Grandma’s.” Ben crossed his arms and didn't eat.

That evening, at eight, Marie didn't want to go to bed. “We were allowed to stay up at Grandma’s until it was dark!” Jonas pointed to the window, where the sun was still high. “It’s still light here at eight. And there’s school tomorrow.” Marie slammed her bedroom door.

It went on like this for a week. Each rule was compared to the grandma rules and found to be unfair. Stefanie, who was Grandma Inge's daughter, became more and more irritable. “Mom spoils her completely,” she said to Jonas. “And we’re the bad guys now.”

## A call to the North Sea

At the weekend Stefanie called her mother. She actually just wanted to ask how she was doing, but then it came pouring out: the Nutella discussions, the slammed doors, the feeling of being pitted against her grandmother.

Grandma Inge listened. Then she laughed. "Do you remember how you came back to your Grandma Frieda's after the holidays? You were allowed to eat fried potatoes with fried eggs every evening and read in bed until you fell asleep. And when you came back, you spent a week explaining to me how much better everything was at Grandma Frieda's."

Stefanie remained silent. She had forgotten it. But now she remembered: Grandma Frieda's fried potatoes, the flashlight under the blanket, the feeling of being something special with Grandma.

“Grandparents are allowed to be different,” said Grandma Inge. "I'm only on it for two weeks a year. You're on it for the other fifty weeks. The children know that. Give them a few days."

## The conversation at the kitchen table

Stefanie and Jonas decided to talk to the children. Not accusatory, but curious. On Sunday afternoon they sat together at the kitchen table.

“We notice that you miss the grandma rules,” said Jonas. “Tell me: What was the best thing about Grandma?” The children told. From the mudflats, from the crabs they had caught in the tidal creek, from the lighthouse they had climbed, from the cherry pudding. And the more they talked, the less it was about rules and more about experiences.

“It’s like a vacation at Grandma’s,” Marie said finally. “And here it’s everyday life.” Stefanie nodded. "Exactly. Different rules apply on vacation. At Grandma's it's vacation. At home we have school and work and we need different rules for that."

Ben wasn't convinced yet. “But can’t you take some of Grandma with you?”

## A little grandma at home

Everyone thought that was a good question. And they found an answer. Once a month there was supposed to be a “grandma evening” at home: that evening the children were allowed to stay up until it got dark, they had pudding with cherries, and everyone sat together and told stories, like grandma did when the wind whistled around the house.

From now on, Grandma Inge called via video every Sunday. She showed the children the mud flats at low tide, the seagulls on the dike, the roses in her garden. And the children showed her their school books, their home-built Lego towers, the wobbly tooth that had finally fallen out.

The Nutella discussions stopped after two weeks. The slammed doors too.

## What Stefanie learned

Stefanie often thought about the conversation with her mother. She had felt that Grandma's rules were competitive, as if her mother was undermining her upbringing. But actually, she now understood, they were a gift. The children experienced a different world with Grandma, a freer, slower one. And they learned that different places can have different rules. That may have even been an important lesson.

The only important thing was that the adults didn't badmouth each other. Grandma Inge never said, “You can do that with me because your parents are too strict.” And Stefanie never said “Grandma spoils you too much”. They both said, “It’s like this at Grandma’s, and it’s like this at home.”

## The letter from Grandma Inge

In the fall a letter came from Dorum addressed to Marie and Ben. Grandma Inge rarely wrote letters; she preferred to make phone calls. But this time the envelope contained two dried starfish, a postcard from the lighthouse and a note in Grandma's rounded handwriting:

"Dear Marie, dear Ben, it's quiet here now. The vacationers are gone and the geese are moving over the dike. I often think of you and the black feet. Your mom told me that there's a grandma evening at home. I think that's wonderful. But remember: your parents make the rules for every day, and that's much harder than my rules for two weeks. Be a little nice to them. See you next summer, your grandma Inge."

Marie read the letter aloud. Ben asked if he could hang the starfish in his room. And Stefanie, who was standing in the kitchen and listening, had to turn around briefly so that the children didn't see how moved she was.

## For your everyday life

Grandparents often have different rules than at home, especially during the holidays. This can lead to conflicts when you return, but it is also an opportunity. What helped Marie's family:

- **Give children time to adjust.** After a vacation with their grandparents, children often need a few days to get used to everyday life again. Protests are normal and usually pass.
- **Distinguishes between vacation and everyday life.** Children can easily understand that different rules apply on vacation than when they are at school.
- **Ask about experiences instead of rules.** When children talk about the holidays, it's usually not about Nutella, but about the feeling of freedom and care.
- **Take something with you into your everyday life.** A ritual from the holidays that is repeated occasionally at home can make the transition easier.
- **Don't talk badly about each other.** Parents and grandparents should not criticize each other's rules in front of their children. Differences are allowed, but not devaluation.
- **Clarify important points beforehand.** Parents and grandparents should clearly discuss what is really important, such as safety, medication or allergies, before the holidays. There can be leeway in everything else.

### Prepare for a vacation with your grandparents

A few things make the weeks with grandma and grandpa a lot easier. Give the health insurance card or a copy along with written authorization so that the grandparents can arrange for medical treatment in an emergency. Write down any allergies, medications with dosage, the telephone number of the pediatrician's practice and your own accessibility. And don't forget the cuddly toy: a familiar object helps against homesickness, which can come even on the best holidays, usually in the evening.

### Safe by the sea and in the mudflats

Walking barefoot through the mudflats is one of the most beautiful experiences on the North Sea. At the same time, the mudflats are not a playground without rules. The tide comes in quicker than many people think, and tidal creeks can fill with water in a short period of time. Stay close to the shore with children, pay attention to the tides and never walk out into the fog. For longer distances, guided mudflat hikes with local guides are the right choice. Swimming is best done in guarded swimming areas, and sun protection is more important than it feels on the windy coast.

### Alone by train

If children travel to their grandparents alone or as siblings, good preparation helps: a connection without tight changes, a seat reservation, a piece of paper with telephone numbers in the jacket pocket and a clear agreement about who is waiting at the platform. Find out from the train in advance about the rules for children traveling alone.

### To talk to your children

Ask about your next vacation with your grandparents: What was the best thing? What new things have you experienced? And which of these would you like to do at home sometimes?

A small holiday diary in which your children draw a picture or write a sentence every evening helps with remembering. At home it often becomes a favorite book that is brought out again and again, especially on gray winter days.
