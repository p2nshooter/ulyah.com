“Mira, we’re leaving now!”

The sentence echoed across the playground on the Elbe, and it did what it always did: nothing. Mira, four years old, sat on top of the climbing frame and acted as if she hadn't heard anything. Her father, Henrik, stood at the edge of the sandpit with the balance bike in his hand and felt the patience draining out of him like sand from a leaky bucket.

"Mira! Now!" Mira climbed a step higher. Henrik put the balance bike down, went to the scaffolding, and lifted her down. And then it came, what the Petersen family called “the volcano”: Mira screamed, kicked, threw herself on the floor, screamed some more. The other parents looked over, some sympathetic, some disapproving. Henrik carried his screaming daughter to the bike trailer and wished he could sink into the sand.

## Every change is a drama

It wasn't just the playground. It was every change. From playing to dinner. From dinner to the bathroom. From the bath to bed. From home to daycare. Even home from daycare, even though Mira cried in the morning because she didn't want to go. Henrik and his partner Sophie felt like their days were full of transitions, and any one of them could end in a volcano.

“She’s just stubborn,” Henrik said one evening. Sophie shook her head. She worked as a teacher, not in Mira's daycare, but with children of the same age. "I don't think she's stubborn. I think she's in the middle of something and pulled out of the middle. Imagine reading an exciting book and someone takes it out of your hand and says, eat now."

Henrik thought. That's exactly how he felt when Sophie called him to do the dishes in the middle of a soccer game.

## The hourglass

The next day Sophie brought a small hourglass, the kind you find in board games. The sand ran through for five minutes. “We will announce any change from now on,” she said. “And Mira gets to turn the hourglass over.”

In the evening they tried it out. Mira built a tower out of wooden blocks in the living room. Sophie sat down next to her, watched the building for a moment and then said calmly: "Your tower is already really high. Dinner is coming soon. Would you like to turn the hourglass over? When the sand is down, we'll go into the kitchen."

Mira turned the hourglass over and watched in fascination as the sand trickled down. She continued building, kept looking at the clock. When the last grain had fallen, she called out: “Done!” And went into the kitchen. Without a volcano.

Henrik stared at Sophie as if she had done magic.

## The last time trick

Of course it wasn't always that easy. The hourglass didn't help on the playground; people forgot it at home anyway. But Henrik discovered another trick, actually by accident. When it was time to leave again, he didn't shout, "We're leaving now," but went to Mira and asked, "What's the last thing you want to do? Slide or swing?"

Mira thought. "Slide." - "Good. Slide once, then we'll go to the trailer."

She slipped. And then, after a moment of hesitation, she walked to the trailer. Not thrilled, but no volcano.

Henrik told Sophie and together they understood what had happened. Mira hadn't felt like anything was being taken away from her. She had decided how the playing ended. She had had a degree.

## The bridge to the next one

The third thing they learned was perhaps the most important: transitions were easier when there was something for Mira to look forward to on the other side. “We’re going home now” was an ending. “We’re going home now and you can throw the noodles in the water” was a start.

So many changes got a small bridge. From the bath to bed: “You get to choose the book.” Home from daycare: “Can you tell me what you played on the way?” From the tablet to dinner, however, it was a particularly tough transition for Mira: “When the episode is finished, we set the table together and you can fold the napkins.”

Sometimes it didn't work. Sometimes Mira was too tired, too hungry, or just in a bad mood, and the volcano would erupt anyway. Then Henrik stayed with her, said "I know you wanted to keep playing, that's annoying" and waited until the wave was over. He had learned that explanations didn't help in that moment. Just staying there helped.

## What Henrik learned about himself

As time passed, Henrik noticed something else that made him a little embarrassed. Many volcanoes had not started with Mira, but with him. When he came out of the office stressed and was already going through the shopping list in his head on the playground, he would shout "We're leaving now" in a tone that left no room for goodbye. On relaxed days, however, he sat down with her on his own, asked her about the last time and built bridges without thinking about it.

“I noticed that their transitions were tied to mine,” he later told a friend who had just become a father himself. "When I'm already three steps further inside, I drag her along. And she defends herself against it, quite rightly."

Since then, he took a moment on his way to the playground to arrive himself. He left the phone in his pocket and watched Mira climb. When it was time to leave, he walked over to her more slowly. Those few minutes didn't change everything. But they made enough of a difference that the ride home ended with songs more often than tears.

## Half a year later

Six months later, Henrik and Mira were sitting on the playground on the Elbe again. Henrik looked at the clock and was about to get up when Mira called from the slide: “Dad, how much more?” – “Slide twice more.” Mira raised two fingers, slid twice, counted out loud and came running to the trailer.

A father on the bench next door, with a screaming toddler in his arms, watched them go with wide eyes. Henrik smiled at him. He knew exactly how the man felt.

## For your everyday life

Many toddlers and kindergarten children have difficulty with transitions. They can become completely engrossed in a game and find it difficult to switch gears abruptly. Ideas from Mira's family:

- **Announces change.** “We're leaving in five minutes” gives children time to mentally prepare. For small children who cannot yet estimate time, an hourglass, a timer or a number (“slide three more times”) helps.
- **Let the child choose the ending.** “What’s the last thing you want to do?” gives children some control. They finish the game themselves instead of being ripped out.
- **Builds a bridge to the next thing.** A small task or anticipation on the other side of the transition, like throwing pasta in the water or picking out the book, makes the transition more attractive.
- **Walk there instead of calling.** If you sit next to the child playing and watch for a moment, you will reach him more easily than someone who calls from the other side of the room.
- **Stick with the tantrum.** When the volcano erupts, explanations are of little help. Remaining calm, naming the feeling (“You are disappointed because you still wanted to play”) and waiting helps many children to regain their composure.
- **Watch for hunger and fatigue.** Transitions just before dinner or after a long day are particularly vulnerable. A small snack or a quieter routine can make a big difference.

### Why switching is so difficult

Switching from one activity to the next requires a lot from the brain: releasing attention, holding back an impulse, adjusting to something new. These skills, often summarized under the term executive functions, develop throughout childhood and mature into young adulthood. A four-year-old child cannot switch gears as easily as a ten-year-old, and a ten-year-old cannot switch as easily as an adult. So the volcano is not disobedience, but often simply excessive demands.

### Drop off and pick up

The transitions at the daycare door are particularly difficult for many children. When dropping off, a short, always the same farewell ritual helps, such as a hug, a wave at the window and handing over to a trusted teacher. Don't sneak away secretly, even if it's tempting: children are more likely to feel abandoned. When picking up, many parents experience the opposite of what they expect: the child who has been happy all day cries or gets angry as soon as he sees mom or dad. Often this is because children have pulled themselves together all day and can finally let go of those closest to them. A snack, rest and time on the way home help more than many questions.

### Exit the screen

When watching TV or on a tablet, switching is easier if you agree in advance how many episodes will be watched and stop at a natural end rather than in the middle of the action. Turn off the automatic playback of the next episode so that the end is really an end.

### To talk to your children

In a quiet moment, ask: How does it feel when you have to stop in the middle of playing? What would help you? Some children want a warning, others want a task, others just want a hug when they leave the game.

You can try out what your child answers at the next change. And if a trick no longer works after a few weeks, that's not a step backwards: children outgrow some help and then need new ones.
