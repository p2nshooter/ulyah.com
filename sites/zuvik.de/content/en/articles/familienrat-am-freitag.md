“I’m sitting in the front!”

"No, me! You were in front yesterday!"

“Not at all, yesterday I was behind because you cheated!”

Khanh stood next to the car in the parking lot in front of the supermarket in Mannheim-Neckarau and listened as his children fought over the passenger seat. Linh, twelve, and Paul, nine, both tugged at the passenger door. Mia, five, was already sitting in her child seat in the back seat and shouted: “I want to be in the front too!”, even though she knew she wasn’t allowed to do that.

Khanh took a deep breath and said what he always said: "Both in the back. Now. And if I hear another word, there will be no tablet tonight."

The children got in the back, their faces like storm clouds. The drive home passed in icy silence.

## A house full of conflicts

It wasn't just the car. The Nguyen family fought over almost everything. Who clears the table, who takes out the trash, who gets to go to the bathroom first, who gets the remote control. How long the tablet can stay on, and why Linh is allowed to stay up later than Paul, and why Paul is allowed to stay up later than Mia.

Khanh and his wife Sabrina, he a software developer and she a physiotherapist, had tried to set rules. They had posted plans on the fridge, threatened punishments and promised rewards. But the rules never lasted long. The children discussed, negotiated, looked for gaps. And parents increasingly felt like referees in a game that never stopped.

"The problem is," Sabrina said one evening, "that the rules come from us. The children don't follow them because they aren't their rules."

She had spoken to a colleague in her practice who had been holding a family council with her family for years. Once a week everyone sat down there and discussed what was going on. Everyone was allowed to suggest something, everyone had a voice. “The children have a say,” the colleague said. "And when they have a say in the decision, they stick to it. Most of the time, anyway."

## The first session

The first family council took place on a Friday evening after dinner, at the large dining table. Sabrina had brought a wooden spoon from the kitchen and explained the rules: Whoever holds the spoon in his hand can talk. Everyone else is listening. Nobody is laughed at. Anyone can suggest topics, even the smallest ones.

Linh rolled his eyes. “This is kindergarten.”

“Then you have experience,” said Paul, and Linh threw a piece of bread at him.

It started chaotically. Mia didn't want to give up the spoon anymore and spent ten minutes talking about an earthworm that she had found in kindergarten. Paul interrupted Linh three times even though he didn't have the spoon. Khanh himself forgot the rule twice and spoke in between, whereupon Mia looked at him sternly and said: "Dad! You don't have the spoon!"

But then, after half an hour, something real came out. Paul, with the spoon in his hand, said quietly: "At dinner it's always Linh who talks. And when I want to tell you something, no one listens."

It was quiet at the table. Sabrina and Khanh looked at each other. They had never noticed.

## What goes on the board

After the first session, Sabrina hung a small board in the hallway. Everyone could write topics for the next family council during the week. Mia, who could not yet write, was allowed to paint. The first week it read: “Who sits in the front of the car” (Linh), “Bedtime” (Linh), “Linh is mean” (Paul) and a painted ice cream with three scoops (Mia).

The second family council was about the car. The children made suggestions one by one. Linh wanted to go by age because she was the oldest. Paul wanted to draw. Mia always wanted her to sit in the front. In the end, they agreed on a plan: Linh sits at the front on even days, Paul sits at the front on odd days. Mia was allowed to be included in the plan as soon as she was old and big enough. Until then, she was allowed to choose the music in the car every Saturday.

The argument over the passenger seat was over from one day to the next. Khanh couldn't believe it.

## Real and fake decisions

Not everything went so well. At the third family council, Linh suggested that she be allowed to stay up until midnight on weekends. Sabrina and Khanh didn't want that. Linh could tell by their faces. “Then this is just fake,” she said angrily. “You act as if we have a say, but in the end you decide.”

Khanh thought about it. She had a point. After the meeting he spoke to Sabrina. “We have to be honest,” he said. "There are things that the children are allowed to decide. And there are things where we have the final say, for example when it comes to health and safety. We should tell them that in advance instead of pretending that everything is negotiable."

At the next family council they explained this. They said that bedtime was an issue that parents needed to have a say in because children needed sleep. But that they are ready to find a compromise. Linh suggested: on Friday and Saturday until half past ten, when she got up in time for breakfast the next morning. The parents agreed for a trial month. Linh stuck to it, almost every time.

And Mia's painted ice cream? After a long negotiation, during which Mia gripped the spoon so tightly that her knuckles turned white, the family decided on ice cream Friday: every Friday after the family council everyone had a scoop of ice cream. One, not three. Mia accepted this with great dignity.

## The storytelling round

The most important thing the family council changed had nothing to do with any rules. After Paul's sentence in the first session, Sabrina and Khanh introduced something new at dinner: a storytelling session. Everyone took turns telling what was good and what was difficult that day, and the others listened until he was finished. Soon they no longer needed the wooden spoon.

Paul blossomed. He talked about his soccer games, about his friend Mehmet, about a teacher who had treated him unfairly. Things he had never told before because he had never had a chance to speak. Linh also noticed that she had more peace when eating when she didn't have to constantly talk back to her brother.

After three months, the family was sitting at the dinner table again on a Friday evening, the board in the hallway was almost empty. It had only one topic written on it, in Paul's scraggly writing: "Can we go camping for the holidays?"

They discussed for an hour about where, when, how long and which tent. In the end everyone voted yes, even Linh, who had initially said that camping was “full of nineties”. Mia clapped, Paul cheered, and Khanh got the ice cream.

## For your everyday life

A family council is a regular meeting at which all family members discuss what is going on together. Children learn to express their opinions, listen to others and find compromises. Parents often experience things that get lost in everyday life. What helped the Nguyen family:

- **Find a fixed date.** A certain day of the week at a certain time makes the family council a habit. Half an hour is usually enough.
- **Agree on clear rules for conversation.** A speaking stick, a spoon or a ball show who is currently speaking. This particularly helps those who are quieter to have their say.
- **Collect topics.** A board or piece of paper in the hallway on which everyone can write or draw their topics during the week ensures that nothing is forgotten.
- **Be honest about what is negotiable.** Children notice when participation in decision-making is only fake. It is better to be open about where parents have the final say and where they do not.
- **Decide on trial compromises.** Trying out new rules for a month and then reviewing them together takes the pressure off decisions.
- **End with something nice.** An ice cream, a game or a film together after the family council ensures that everyone wants to come back.
- **Take the little ones seriously too.** Even preschool children have concerns that matter, even if they are expressed differently.

### Sitting in the front is also a safety issue

When arguing about the passenger seat, it's worth taking a look at the rules. In Germany, children under the age of twelve who are shorter than 150 centimeters need a suitable child seat in every seat, including the front. Baby seats facing against the direction of travel may only be installed at the front if the front passenger airbag is switched off. Traffic experts recommend the back seat as the safest place for children anyway. A family council is welcome to decide on the seating plan, but only within what is safe, and parents are allowed to say exactly that.

### When the family council stalls

Not every session is successful. Sometimes everyone is tired, sometimes the mood changes because a topic is too hot. Then it helps to keep the group short, postpone a difficult topic until the next week or take a break. Many families let the moderation take turns so that the children also lead the process. A short note written by an older child reminds everyone of what was decided. And decisions can be checked: Anything that doesn't work after a month is put back on the board.

### To talk to your children

- Is there anything in our family that you would like to change?
- Which decisions would you like to have a say in?
- When do you feel like no one in our family is really listening to you?
- What would be a good ritual to end a family meeting with?
