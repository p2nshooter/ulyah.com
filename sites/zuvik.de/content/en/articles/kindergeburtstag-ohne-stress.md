“I want to invite everyone,” said Mats, “the whole class.”

He sat at the kitchen table in the apartment in Freiburg-Wiehre, with a piece of paper in front of him on which he wrote names with great care. It was mid-February, his seventh birthday was still three weeks away, but Mats had been planning since Christmas. There were twenty-two names on the piece of paper. Below, in capital letters: PIRATES.

His mother Steffi looked over his shoulder and felt something tighten in her stomach. The apartment had three rooms and a balcony. She worked thirty hours a week in a tax office. And Mats' father, her ex-husband Jan, had been living in Karlsruhe since the separation and would only be able to come over in the afternoon that day.

“Twenty-two children,” she said cautiously. “That’s a lot.”

## The images in your head

Steffi wanted this birthday to be perfect. It was the first birthday after the breakup, and somewhere inside her was the feeling that she owed Mats something. In the evening, she scrolled through photos on the Internet: pirate parties with treasure maps on old paper, cakes in the shape of ships, table decorations with barrels and fishing nets, jute party bags with chocolate gold coins. A mother had built an entire ship out of cardboard boxes in the garden.

She wrote a list. Invitations as messages in a bottle. Treasure hunt in the park with seven stations. Eye patches and headscarves for everyone. A cake shaped like a treasure chest. Sausages, potato salad, muffins, fruit skewers. Party bag bags. A costume for Mats. And, if it rained, a plan B for the apartment.

At the end of the evening the list had three pages. Steffi added up the costs and swallowed. Then she added up the hours it would take her to prepare and swallowed again.

## The night of the message in a bottle

A week before the birthday, Steffi sat at the kitchen table at half past midnight and rolled the twenty-first invitation into a small glass bottle. She had colored the edges of the paper with tea bags and scorched them with a lighter, as instructed in instructions. She burned her fingers twice. Her back hurt and she had to get up at half past six the next morning.

Her sister Katrin called because she saw that Steffi was still online. “What are you doing at this time?” she asked. Steffi talked about the message in a bottle, the treasure hunt, the cake and the party favor bags. Katrin listened and then said: "Steffi, who actually wants all this? Mats or you?"

Steffi wanted to object. But she realized she couldn't answer the question honestly. She hadn't asked Mats if he wanted a message in a bottle. She hadn't asked him if he wanted seven stops on the treasure hunt. She had just assumed that more was better.

## What Mats really wanted

The next morning, at breakfast, she asked him. “Mats, when you think about your birthday, what is the most important thing?”

Mats chewed thoughtfully on his bread. “That Theo and Ida are coming,” he said. "And Leon. And that we play pirates. And that there is chocolate cake. Grandma's, not one from the store."

“And the others in the class?”

Mats shrugged his shoulders. "I can invite them too. But I don't play with them that often. I don't play with Moritz at all, he's always mean." He considered. "Actually, I just want my friends to come. And dad."

Steffi leaned back. Three friends, chocolate cake, playing pirates and dad. That was the whole list.

## A new plan

She sat down with Mats and made a new plan. Together they thought about who he really wanted to invite. There were six children: Theo, Ida, Leon, the twins Emma and Ben from the neighborhood and Hamid, with whom Mats had been friends since daycare. A rule of thumb that Steffi knew from a friend was that you should invite as many children as the birthday child is old. With six guests plus Mats, it was just right.

The children who were not invited should not go away empty-handed. Mats would bring muffins for the whole class the day after the birthday, as was customary at his school. Steffi didn't hand out the invitations at school, but distributed them directly to the parents so that no one felt left out.

They still used the bottles that had already been rolled up for the six guests. Mats placed the remaining fifteen bottles on his windowsill as pirate treasure. Grandma Renate, who lived in Emmendingen and had known the recipe for thirty years, baked the cake. The treasure hunt shrank to three stations in the twins' garden, whose parents were happy to make it available. The costume consisted of a striped shirt, a scarf and an eye patch made of black felt, which Mats cut out himself.

## The birthday

On March 10th the sun was shining in Freiburg and it was cold enough for the children to keep their jackets on. At three o'clock the guests rang the bell, one after the other. Each time Mats opened the door, his eye patch crooked on his nose, and shouted “Ahoy!” The children made pirate hats out of paper plates, ate Grandma Renate's chocolate cake until their mouths were brown, and followed the treasure map through the neighbor's garden. The treasure was a box of chocolate coins, which Hamid found and distributed evenly to everyone without anyone asking him to do so.

Jan came at five. He had brought with him a homemade cannon made from a cardboard roll that could be used to shoot cotton balls, and for half an hour there was a sea battle on the balcony. Steffi and Jan stood next to each other in the kitchen door and watched. It wasn't easy for them to be together, but that afternoon it was okay. Mats beamed when he saw them both together.

The children were picked up at half past five. Steffi put the plates in the dishwasher, wiped chocolate from the tablecloth and sat on the sofa with a cup of tea. She was tired, but in a good way. Mats came and snuggled up to her, his face full of cake crumbs.

“Mom,” he said, “that was the best birthday of all.” And after a pause: “Can we do Pirates again next year?”

## For your everyday life

Children's birthdays are a show of strength for many parents. Photos of lavish celebrations on social media and the desire to offer your own child something special can easily put you under pressure. It often turns out that what children want most is their friends, a little play and a cake that tastes like home. What helped Steffi:

- **Ask the child first.** What is most important to them? Often there are a few, very specific things. Everything else is an addition, not a requirement.
- **Keep the guest list manageable.** A well-known rule of thumb is: as many guests as the child is old. Smaller groups are more relaxed for everyone, including the birthday child.
- **Don't embarrass anyone.** It's better to give invitations directly to parents instead of handing them out in class. A small gesture for everyone, such as muffins the next day at school, avoids disappointment.
- **Accept help.** Grandparents, neighbors or parent friends are often happy to help - with a cake, a garden or an hour of supervision.
- **Plan a simple program.** Two or three games, a meal, a little surprise. Children also need free time to simply play with each other.
- **Check your own standards.** The question “Who am I actually doing this for?” can help sort expectations, especially at times when you already feel under pressure as a parent.

### A drain with air

For primary school-aged children, two to three hours of partying is enough. A rough routine helps without planning the day:

| time | What happens |
|---|---|
| first quarter hour | Arrival, gifts, free play |
| then about 20 minutes | a craft or a quiet game to warm up |
| about 20 to 30 minutes | Cake and drink |
| about 40 minutes | a big game, for example a treasure hunt |
| Rest of the time | free play, dinner or sausages, pick up |

Consciously plan gaps. The most beautiful moments often occur when no program is running.

### Allergies and intolerances

When inviting, ask whether a child is not allowed to eat something, for example because of a nut allergy, celiac disease or for religious reasons. A short sentence on the invitation is enough: “Please let us know if your child is not allowed to eat something.” Write down your parents' phone numbers for the afternoon so that you can reach someone quickly in an emergency.

### Birthday in separate families

When parents live apart, birthdays quickly become a question of who celebrates when. There is no right solution for everyone: some families celebrate together, others celebrate twice. It is important that the child does not have to make a decision and that the agreement is made early. Avoid competition for the bigger gift or the nicer celebration. The most important thing for the child is that both parents are happy for him, just as Mats was happy when he saw mom and dad standing together in the kitchen door.

### To talk to your children

- What is the most important thing for you on your birthday?
- Who do you definitely want to have with you – and why these children in particular?
- How does it feel when you aren't invited to a birthday? What can you do so that no one feels left out?
- Which game or cake is part of a real birthday for you?
- What would you like to give your guests when they say goodbye, and does it have to be something purchased?
- How can we thank everyone who helped with the party, for example Grandma or the neighbors with the garden?
