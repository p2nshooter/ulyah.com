Every Sunday at four o'clock in the afternoon, the tablet rang in the Aydın family's apartment in Stuttgart. Two faces appeared on the screen, a little too close to the camera, with a fig tree in the background: Babaanne and Dede, Yusuf's paternal grandparents, in their garden in Izmir.

"Merhaba, Yusuf! Nasılsın?" Babaanne shouted, and Yusuf, seven years old, replied: “İyiyim, Babaanne!” Then it usually continued in German, or Turkish, or a mixture of both that was only understood in this family.

## Two languages, one family

Yusuf was born in Stuttgart, went to second grade there and spoke German as fluently as his classmates. He spoke Turkish with his father Emre and with his grandparents, but not that well. He often found himself at a loss for words, and when he searched for them he became impatient and switched to German. His mother Laura barely spoke Turkish; she had learned it over the last few years, but only to the point where she understood greetings and simple sentences.

Babaanne and Dede spoke a little German. Dede had worked in Germany for ten years in the 1970s, in a factory in Sindelfingen, and then went back. Babaanne had never learned German.

So the video calls were always a bit chaotic. Babaanne asked something, Yusuf didn't quite understand it, Emre translated, Laura smiled and nodded, and Dede made jokes in German that were so old that Yusuf already knew them by heart.

## The feeling of losing something

One Sunday in the fall, Yusuf was unfocused. He would rather play outside with his friends. When Babaanne asked him in Turkish what he had learned at school, he answered in German, briefly and boredly. Babaanne didn't understand. Emre translated. Babaanne nodded, but Emre saw how sad she looked.

After the call, Emre spoke to Laura. “I’m afraid he’ll lose his speech,” he said. "And losing my parents. They're so far away and he only sees them once a year. To him, they're people on a screen."

Laura understood him. Her own parents lived in Ludwigsburg, half an hour away, and Yusuf saw them every week. For him, these were the real grandparents with whom you went on trips and stayed overnight. The grandparents in Izmir were a mixture of love and strangeness.

## A shared project on the screen

Laura had an idea. “What if the calls weren’t just conversations, but something they did together?” Emre looked at her questioningly.

During the next call, Emre suggested to his father: "Baba, show Yusuf your garden. Tell him about the plants." Dede, who loved his garden, was immediately excited. He carried the phone around the garden and pointed out every plant: the tomatoes, the peppers, the mint, the pomegranate tree, and especially the old fig tree that his own father had planted.

“This tree is older than me,” Dede said in German. “And every August he has hundreds of figs.”

Yusuf was fascinated. He had never eaten a fresh fig, only dried ones from the supermarket. “Can I have a fig tree too?” he asked.

## The offshoot

The next summer the family flew to Izmir. Dede took Yusuf into the garden and together they cut a branch from the old fig tree. Dede showed him how to put the branch in a pot of soil and water it. “If he gets roots, you can take him with you to Germany,” he explained.

The branch got roots. Before they left, Emre went to the agricultural authority in Izmir with Dede and the pot, because plants can only be imported from countries outside the EU with a phytosanitary certificate. An examiner looked at the branch carefully and issued the certificate. At the end of the vacation, the little fig tree traveled to Stuttgart in his hand luggage, wrapped in damp newspaper, and Emre declared it at customs at the airport. At home, Yusuf planted it in a large pot on the balcony.

From then on, the video calls had a new theme. Every Sunday Yusuf showed his Dede how the little fig tree grew. And Dede showed him the big fig tree in Izmir. They compared the leaves and talked about watering, and Yusuf learned words he had never used before: yaprak for leaf, kök for root, sulamak for watering. He liked learning them because he needed them.

## Babaanne's stories

Babaanne also found her way. She began reading a short story to Yusuf every time he called, from an old Turkish children's book she had picked up from the shelf. The stories of Nasreddin Hoca, the clever, mischievous man with the donkey. Emre translated difficult passages, but Yusuf often understood enough to laugh.

Laura ordered the book in a bilingual edition. In the evening she read the German version to Yusuf, and on Sunday he heard the Turkish version of Babaanne. And one day Yusuf retold one of the stories to his grandmother, in Turkish, bumpily and with lots of German words in between. Babaanne laughed and cried at the same time.

## The first fig

Two years after Yusuf planted the branch in the pot, the first fruit appeared on his small fig tree. She was tiny, green and hard, and Yusuf checked on her every morning. In September it became soft and dark. He picked it and held it in front of the camera when Dede called.

Dede leaned so close to the screen that only his eyes were visible. Then he stood up and called Babaanne. The two stood in front of their fig tree in Izmir and looked at the one small fig on the balcony in Stuttgart as if it were a miracle.

“You have to share them,” Dede said in Turkish, and Yusuf understood every word. “The first fruit is always shared.” Yusuf got a knife and cut the fig into four pieces: one for himself, one for dad, one for mom. He placed the fourth in front of the tablet. “This is for you,” he said. Dede laughed and Babaanne wiped her eyes.

Later, Emre ate the fourth piece, vicariously. He said it tasted just like the figs of his childhood. Laura wasn't sure if that could be true, with a tree on a balcony in Stuttgart. But she understood what he meant.

## Visit on the balcony

The following spring, Babaanne and Dede came to Stuttgart for the first time in many years. The first thing Dede wanted to see was not the city, not his grandson's school, but the balcony. He stood in front of the pot for a long time, touched the leaves and nodded contentedly. “Good earth,” he said in German. "But more sun. Figs love sun." Together they moved the pot to the brightest corner.

Laura's parents came to dinner, and for one evening all four grandparents sat at the same table. Laura was worried that the languages ​​wouldn't be enough. But Dede talked about his time in Sindelfingen, Laura's father talked about his apprenticeship in a carpenter's shop, and Yusuf proudly translated whenever someone was looking for a word. Babaanne showed Laura's mother how to fold börek, without words, just with her hands. Yusuf later said it was the best night of the year “because all my grandparents were there.”

## For your everyday life

Many children have grandparents who live far away, in another city or country. Video calls can create closeness if they are more than just mandatory conversations. What helped Yusuf's family:

- **Turn calls into shared experiences.** A common topic, such as a plant, a pet, a craft project or a story, gives conversations content and structure.
- **Choose a good time.** Children are more attentive when they don't want to play or are tired. A fixed, well-chosen appointment helps.
- **Let the grandparents show their world.** Garden, kitchen, neighborhood, photos: Children are often interested in the specific lives of their grandparents.
- **Supports family language in a playful way.** Children learn words that they really need. Joint projects create such occasions. Bilingual books can help.
- **Use visits wisely.** If families rarely see each other, shared memories, such as an offshoot, a homemade gift or photos, are valuable connecting pieces for the time afterwards.
- **Be patient with the language.** Multilingual children mix languages. This is normal and not a sign of weakness.

### Strengthen multilingualism in everyday life

Many multilingual families have good experiences with each parent consistently speaking their own language with the child. Books, radio plays, songs and films in the family language also help, and in many places there are lessons in the native language that schools or clubs offer. It is important that the language is associated with beautiful experiences and not with pressure. If children answer, regardless of the language, that is a success. Don't correct every word, just repeat the sentence correctly in your answer.

### Souvenirs from abroad

A cuttings, seeds or fruit from your grandparents' garden are wonderful memories. However, if they come from a country outside the EU, plants, plant parts and seeds usually require a phytosanitary certificate, and meat and dairy products are generally not allowed to be brought in your luggage. Check the customs website before your return journey so that the souvenir doesn't have to stay at the airport.

### To talk to your children

Ask: What would you like to show your grandparents the next time you call? And what would you like to know about her life? Sometimes one question is enough to start a whole conversation.
