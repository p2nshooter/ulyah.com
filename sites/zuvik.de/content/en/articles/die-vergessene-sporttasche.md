The phone vibrated at 9:47 a.m., in the middle of the team meeting. Paul looked at the display: “Kiel-Wik primary school”. He knew what was coming before he picked up.

"Papa?" Jonte's voice sounded small and guilty. "I forgot my gym bag. And we have third period gym. Can you bring it?"

Paul apologized to his colleagues, went home during his lunch break, picked up the sports bag that was lying in the hallway next to the cloakroom, and brought it to school. He left it in the office, where the secretary, Ms. Jensen, gave him a friendly but meaningful smile. “Again?” she asked. Paul nodded. It was the third time this month.

## A boy with a lot of thoughts

Jonte was nine years old and in third grade. He was a happy, curious boy who could talk for hours about sharks, volcanoes and space rockets. He could remember the names of sixty dinosaurs, but not which day he had exercise. He forgot his lunch box, his sneakers, his reading book, the signed letters from his parents and once even his jacket, in February, when it was minus three degrees.

Paul's wife Wiebke was a nurse and worked early shift. She left the house at 5:30 in the morning, leaving Paul in charge of school mornings. And Paul, he had to admit to himself, wasn't the most organized himself in the morning. He regularly searched for his car keys, missed a dentist appointment twice a year, and rarely managed to think clearly before eight o'clock.

That evening he told Wiebke about the sports bag. She sighed. "Paul, you have to stop putting everything after him. Otherwise he'll never learn."

"And what should I do? Leave him sitting there without his sports clothes?"

“Yes,” said Wiebke. “Exactly that.”

## The argument about saving

Paul found this idea difficult to stomach. He remembered his own school days, a strict sports teacher who exposed children without sports equipment in front of the whole class. He didn't want Jonte to feel like that. He wanted to protect him.

Wiebke saw it differently. “It’s not about punishing him,” she said. "It's about him realizing that his things are his responsibility. As long as you save everything, it's your responsibility. And then he continues to forget because it doesn't make any difference."

They argued that evening, not loudly, but for a long time. In the end, Wiebke proposed a compromise. "Let's do two things at once. We'll help him find a system so he'll forget less. And if he still forgets something, we won't bring it back. But we'll tell him in advance so he's not caught off guard."

Paul agreed. Reluctantly.

## The conversation with Jonte

On Saturday morning, over breakfast, they explained the new rule to Jonte. “From Monday we won’t bring anything to school that you’ve forgotten,” said Wiebke. “Not the sports bag, not the lunch box, not the reading book.”

Jonte looked at her in horror. “But then I have to sit on the bench during sports!”

“Yes,” Paul said. "That can happen. And that's why we're helping you not to forget it."

They asked Jonte why he actually forgot the things. Jonte thought. “There’s so much going on in the morning,” he said. "And I never know what day it is. And when I'm getting dressed, I'm thinking about something else, and then it's already a quarter past seven and Dad shouts, 'We have to go!'" He looked at his father. “And dad is always looking for his key.”

Paul had to laugh. He couldn't argue.

## A system for two forgetful people

They made a packing list together on Saturday afternoon. Jonte painted a picture for each day of the week: on Monday a sneaker for sports, on Wednesday a flute for music, on Thursday a book for going to the library. The list went on the inside of the front door, at Jonte's eye level. Directly below, Paul screwed two hooks onto the wall: one for Jonte's school backpack, one for his own key.

The second rule was more important: the backpack was packed in the evening, not in the morning. After dinner, before Jonte was allowed to watch TV, he looked at his list, packed his backpack and put his sports bag on the hook. Paul didn't check whether everything was in there. He just asked, “Have you looked at your list?”

“And you?” asked Jonte. “What are you packing for the evening?”

Paul thought about it. Then he wrote his own little list and stuck it next to Jonte's: keys, cell phone, lunch box, work ID. “We’re doing this together,” he said.

## Tuesday on the bench

The first week went well. The second week too. On Tuesday of the third week, Paul's cell phone rang at 9:32 a.m. “Kiel-Wik Elementary School.”

"Dad," said Jonte, "I forgot the sneakers. I have the bag, but the shoes are still in the hallway because I wore them at soccer yesterday."

Paul closed his eyes. He saw the sneakers in front of him, in the hallway, two minutes from the car. He saw Jonte in front of him, on the bench in the gym while the others played. He felt everything in him yearning to say: I'm coming.

“Jonte,” he said instead, “you know what we agreed on.”

It was quiet at the other end. Then he heard a soft sniffle. “Okay,” Jonte said. "Bye."

Paul sat at his desk for the rest of the morning feeling bad. He wrote Wiebke a message. She replied from the ward room: "I know that was hard. You did it right. ❤️"

## What Jonte says

When Jonte came home in the afternoon, Paul was waiting anxiously. Jonte threw his backpack in the corner and said: "I was allowed to take part barefoot. Ms. Albers said as an exception because we only had floor exercises. But next time I have to sit on the bench."

Paul was relieved. And then Jonte said something that surprised him. "I thought about always putting my sneakers in my gym bag immediately after football. Then they can't be left lying in the hallway." He picked up the shoes from the hallway, put them in the bag and hung the bag on the hook.

In the weeks that followed, Jonte sometimes forgot something. Once the lunch box, once a worksheet. At one point he actually had to sit on the bench and watch the others play dodgeball, which he called “the most boring thing in the world.” But it became rarer. And after two months, Paul noticed that the secretariat hadn't called in a long time.

Paul also forgot his key less often. One morning in December he stood in the hallway looking for it until Jonte, already in his jacket and hat, pointed to the hook. "Dad. Have you looked at your list?"

## For your everyday life

Children forget things, that's part of their development. Many parents find themselves in a quandary: Carrying on feels caring, but it can prevent children from taking responsibility themselves. At the same time, forgetful children in particular need support to organize themselves. What helped Jonte's family:

- **Ask why.** Hectic mornings, lack of overview or simply too many thoughts: If you know the reason, you can help more specifically.
- **Create a visible system.** A packing list with pictures on the front door, fixed hooks for backpacks and sports bags and a fixed place for everything make it easier for children to keep an overview.
- **Pack in the evening instead of in the morning.** There is more rest in the evening and one task is left out in the morning.
- **Announce new rules in advance.** Children should know that parents will not bring back what they have forgotten before it happens for the first time.
- **Allow natural consequences.** Sitting on the bench or going without a lunch box is unpleasant, but not dangerous. Children often learn more from this than from any admonition.
- **Be a role model yourself.** When parents use their own lists and checkboxes, organization becomes something that affects the entire family, not just the child.
- **Take a closer look if difficulties persist.** If a child remains extremely forgetful for a long time despite support, it may be useful to talk to the teacher or pediatrician.

### When carrying after is still the right thing to do

Natural consequences are a good teacher, but not in every situation. Of course, she always brings medication such as an asthma spray, an emergency kit for allergies or insulin, as well as forgotten glasses or a hearing aid, without which a child can hardly follow lessons. Even if a child is standing in front of a locked door or is out and about without a jacket in the cold, safety comes before education. The rule “We are no longer of any use” applies to things whose absence is unpleasant, not to things whose absence is harmful. Tell your child this difference so that they don't hesitate to call in an emergency.

### Forgetful or more?

In most cases, it's completely normal for a nine-year-old to forget his sneakers but know sixty dinosaurs. Children remember what interests them and they only learn organization little by little. It is different when inattention, forgetfulness or restlessness over a long period of time, in several areas of life and despite good support, is significantly greater than that of peers and noticeably harms the child. Then it makes sense to have a conversation with the teacher and the pediatrician. A diagnosis such as ADHD can only be made by professionals after careful examination.

### To talk to your children

- What do you forget most often and why do you think that is?
- What could help you think about everything in the morning?
- How does it feel when you forget something and no one brings it?
- Is there something that we parents often forget? How could we help each other?
