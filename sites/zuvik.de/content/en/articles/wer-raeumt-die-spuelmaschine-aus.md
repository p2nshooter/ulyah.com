The dishwasher beeped three times. The program was ready. Anke heard it from the bathroom where she was changing the towels, and she knew that no one else in the apartment had heard it. Or that everyone had heard it and decided it was none of their business.

In the living room of the apartment in Erfurt, her husband Stefan was sitting on the sofa and reading the news on his cell phone. Lukas, fourteen, was lying in his room and playing an online game with headphones. Greta, eleven, was painting at the dining room table, surrounded by pens, paper and the remains of her afternoon snack.

Anke went into the kitchen. She unloaded the dishwasher, then reloaded the dirty dishes that were piling up on the counter. She brushed the crumbs of Greta's snack off the table. She wrote “Milk, toothpaste, gift for grandma’s birthday” on the shopping list. She remembered that Lukas needed a signed note for the hiking day tomorrow, looked for it in his backpack and signed it. It was half past eight in the evening. She had been up since six in the morning, including eight hours at her job as a health insurance clerk.

## “Just say if you need help”

Later that evening, when the children were in bed, Anke said to Stefan: "I can't do it anymore. I'm doing everything here alone."

Stefan looked up from his phone, genuinely surprised. "I'll help. I vacuumed on Saturday. And I always take out the trash."

“You’re helping,” said Anke. "That's exactly the problem. You're helping me. Like it's my household and you're doing me a favor."

Stefan frowned. "Then just say if you need help. I'll be happy to do that."

Anke felt anger rising within her and, at the same time, great tiredness. She didn't know how to explain it. That it wasn't just about vacuuming and trash, but about thinking about everything. About the note for the hiking day, grandma's birthday, the dentist appointments, the shoes that are too small, the empty tube of toothpaste. About the constant thinking that no one saw because it happened in her head.

She said nothing more that evening. But the next day she started writing a list.

## The list

For a week, Anke wrote down everything she did around the house. Not just the obvious like cooking, washing, cleaning, but everything that went with it. Write a shopping list. Keep an eye on supplies. Make appointments with your pediatrician. Get birthday presents for children's birthday parties. Read and sign letters from parents. Sort the laundry by color, hang it up, remove it, fold it and put it in the cupboards. Washing Lukas' sports clothes before he needed them on Tuesday. Make sure Greta ate enough fruit. Clear hair from the shower drain. Water the plants. Sort the tax documents.

Next to each point she wrote approximately how long it lasted. At the end of the week she added up. It was almost thirty hours. Stefan's share, which she also noted, was about eight hours. The children totaled just under two.

On Friday evening, after dinner, she placed the list on the dining room table. Three pages, closely described. “I want to show you something,” she said.

## Silence at the dinner table

Stefan read the list first. He read it slowly, page by page, and the further he got, the quieter he became. Lukas was reading over his shoulder and at one point said: "You wash my sports clothes every Monday? I thought they would just... get clean."

Greta got stuck on the line “Make sure Greta eats enough fruit” and looked at her mother with a mixture of emotion and indignation.

“I didn’t know that,” Stefan finally said. "Well, I kind of knew. But not that it was that much."

“I know,” said Anke. "That's the problem with this work. You only see it when it's not being done."

Lukas pushed the list away from him as if it had attacked him personally. "And now? Should we do everything now?"

“No,” said Anke. "We're supposed to share it. All four of us."

## The new distribution

It took two family evenings until they found a new distribution. They went through the list together, point by point, and everyone was allowed to choose tasks. What no one wanted was distributed in turn.

Stefan took care of all the laundry, from dirty laundry to putting it away, as well as communication with the school. He got access to the parents' app, which previously only ran on Anke's cell phone, and was suddenly the one who knew when hiking day was. Anke kept the appointments, the supplies and the shopping. Greta took over the dishwasher, the loading and unloading, and the table setting. Lukas took over the trash, the vacuuming on Saturdays and, after a long negotiation, one meal a week. “Lukas cooks on Thursdays,” Greta wrote in large letters on the new weekly plan that went to the fridge.

The tasks were not formulated as help for Anke, but as what they were: work for the family. “We all live here,” Stefan said when Lukas protested. "We all make messes. We all clean up."

## The first Thursday

Luke's first meal was a disaster. He decided to make spaghetti with tomato sauce because, as he said, “everyone can do it.” He forgot to put salt in the pasta water. The sauce burned on the bottom of the pot because he was chatting on his cell phone. The spaghetti was too soft, the sauce tasted burnt and watery at the same time, and after the first bite, Greta announced, “This is the worst thing I’ve ever eaten.”

Luke was offended. He got up and wanted to go to his room. Stefan stopped him. “Do you know what I cooked the first time I lived alone?” he asked. "Rice. I cooked it for three hours because I thought it wouldn't get soft. I ended up having to throw the pot away."

Luke grinned reluctantly. Anke said: "Next Thursday I'll show you how to make the sauce. Once. Then you can do it yourself."

## After half a year

The next Thursday, Lukas cooked under Anke's guidance, and the day after that, alone. His spaghetti got better, then came rice pans, oven-roasted vegetables and finally a curry, which Greta reluctantly said she found “pretty good”. He started looking for recipes online and writing shopping lists for his Thursday.

Things didn't always go smoothly. Greta forgot the dishwasher some days, and Stefan had to learn that wool sweaters don't belong in the dryer after Lukas' favorite sweater fit Greta. Sometimes Anke would fall back into old patterns and get things done before someone else got around to it. Then Stefan kindly reminded her that it was no longer her job.

But after six months, something fundamental had changed. Some evenings Anke had time to read a book. She was less irritable. And one evening, when the dishwasher beeped three times, Greta got up from the sofa without anyone saying anything and went into the kitchen.

## For your everyday life

In many families, housework is distributed unequally, and it is often not just the visible work, but also the constant thinking and organizing that sticks with one person. Experts speak of “mental load”. Children and partners often don't even notice this invisible work. What helped Anke's family:

- **Make the invisible work visible.** Writing down all tasks for a week, including planning and remembering, shows everyone how much actually needs to be done.
- **Don't help, just take over.** Taking on a task completely, including thinking about it, really relieves the burden. “Let me know if you want me to help” leaves the responsibility on the other person.
- **Distribute together.** If all family members have a say in which tasks they will take on, the willingness to complete them increases.
- **Participate children in an age-appropriate manner.** Even primary school children can set the table and empty the dishwasher. Young people can shop, cook and do their own laundry.
- **Allow for errors.** The first home-cooked meal is rarely perfect. Anyone who criticizes mistakes instead of offering help takes away motivation.
- **Do not take over again.** Assigned tasks should remain submitted, even if they are completed differently than you would do it yourself.

### Which tasks at which age?

Children can often do more than parents give them credit for. The following overview is a rough guide:

| Dude | Possible tasks |
|---|---|
| 2 to 3 years | Put away toys, put dirty laundry in the basket |
| 4 to 6 years | Setting the table, sorting socks, watering plants |
| 7 to 9 years | Empty the dishwasher, make the bed, make lunch |
| 10 to 12 years | vacuuming, taking out the trash, cooking simple meals with help |
| from 13 years | shop, cook for yourself, do your own laundry |

In the beginning everything takes longer and a lot of things don't turn out the way adults would do them. That's part of learning.

### Money for housework?

Many families wonder whether children should be paid for household chores. Experts usually advise against combining everyday tasks with pocket money. Anyone who works in the household does so because they are part of it, not as a service. Otherwise the question quickly arises as to why you should take out the trash for free. For special tasks, such as washing the car or helping a neighbor move, a little extra income can be a nice experience.

### To talk to your children

- Which household tasks do you know and which ones have you perhaps never noticed?
- Which task would you like to take on and which would you not like to do?
- What is the difference between “helping” and “taking responsibility”?
- What would you like to cook all by yourself for the family?
