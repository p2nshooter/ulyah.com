On December 2nd, Ole and Paula Berger's children's room already had three bouncy balls, an eraser in the shape of a unicorn, a key ring with a flashing light and two packages of glitter tattoos. The Advent calendar that Grandma Gisela had sent from Bremen like every year was a large box with twenty-four doors, each with a little something behind it. In addition, each child had a chocolate calendar from the supermarket. And, like every year, Mama Christine had hung a homemade calendar made of twenty-four cloth bags on a string over the loft bed.

On the morning of December 3rd, the siblings argued about who got to open which bag first. Paula, seven years old, had already lost her key ring. Ole, nine, said of the gift in the box: “I already had that last year.” Then he threw it into the box under the bed where the bouncy balls from the previous Advent lay.

## Three calendars and no spark

Christine stood in the doorway and watched her children. She sat down on four evenings in November to fill the bags. She had ordered small things from drugstores and on the Internet, written notes and knotted ribbons. And now the children tore open the bags, looked inside briefly and put the contents aside to open the next door in the other calendar.

In the evening she sat with her husband Tobias on the sofa in their apartment in Hanover-Linden. “I feel like we’re doing something wrong here,” she said. "They're no longer happy. They're just expecting."

Tobias nodded. He had taken out the trash the day before and saw how much packaging was left from the first Advent alone. "When I was little, we had a calendar with pictures behind the doors. Just pictures. And I was happy like crazy every morning."

## What the children actually want

Christine and Tobias decided not to just make a decision themselves, but to ask the children. At dinner on Friday, Tobias asked: “What was the best thing about this Advent so far?”

Paula thought. "When we baked cookies on Sunday. And I got to make the sprinkles." Ole said: "When Dad read the book to us, the one with the gnome. And we all lay on the big bed." None of the children named any of the bouncy balls, the eraser, or the glitter tattoos.

“And if you could make a wish for the next few days?” asked Christine. “Not something to unpack, but something we do together.”

The children looked at each other. Then it bubbled out of them. Ice skating. Watching a Christmas movie with popcorn on the living room floor. Go to the Christmas market and eat chestnuts. Looking at the lights in the neighborhood, in the dark, with flashlights. Sleep one night in the living room, in a tent made of blankets. Ole wanted to build a bird seed wreath with Dad. Paula wanted Mom to show her how to fold paper stars.

Tobias wrote everything down. In the end there were twenty-one ideas on the list.

## A calendar made of pieces of paper

This weekend they rearranged the calendar. The cloth bags remained stuck, but their contents changed. Christine wrote each idea on a small piece of paper, rolled it up and tied it with a red thread. Some of the notes contained little things: “Today there are pancakes for dinner and you can choose the topping.” Some bigger ones: “Saturday: We’re going ice skating at the Maschsee.” And some very simple ones: “Today Mom will read one more chapter.”

So that the family could manage the calendar, Christine arranged the pieces of paper according to the weekly plan. On the days when she worked late at the hospital or Tobias stayed late at the office, there were little things that took ten minutes. The big trips were on the weekends. “A calendar that stresses us out doesn’t help anyone,” she said.

They discussed with the children what should happen to the other calendars. They were allowed to keep the chocolate calendar, but Paula and Ole wanted to suggest that they share it: one calendar was enough, the other could go to the blackboard. And they called Grandma Gisela together to thank her for the box and carefully ask if they wanted to do something different next year.

## A call to Bremen

Christine was afraid of this call. Grandma Gisela was Tobias' mother, and the Advent calendar was her great pride. She grabbed it every October, she went to six stores to do it, and every other day in December she asked what the children had found behind the doors.

But it was Ole who took over the conversation. "Grandma," he said into the phone, "we're making a calendar this year with things we do together. And I was thinking: next year, can't you write down twenty-four stories? From when you were little? And we read one every day?"

There was silence on the other end of the line. Then Christine heard her mother-in-law clear her throat. “That’s a nice idea, my boy,” said Grandma Gisela, and her voice sounded a bit thick. "But I have to start early. I have so much to tell." As it turned out later, she sat down that same evening and wrote the first story, about the winter of 1962, when the Weser in Bremen was frozen over.

## Advent that felt different

The next three weeks weren't perfect. One evening the note said “We are making stars,” but Paula was tired and cried because the first star turned out crooked. On another day, skating was canceled because Ole had a fever; Instead, the three of them watched a film on the sofa, and Ole fell asleep after twenty minutes. They exchanged a piece of paper because Tobias had to work longer at short notice.

But something had changed. The children no longer ran to the calendar in the morning to unpack something and immediately forget it again. They wondered what could be on the note today. They talked about it all day. At school, Ole talked about the night in the blanket tent, and the next day at parent-teacher consultation his teacher asked how one makes such a calendar.

On the evening of December 23rd, when all the papers had been read and almost all the ideas had been experienced, they sat together on the carpet and looked at the photos that Tobias had taken over the three weeks. Paula with red cheeks on the ice. Ole with the bird seed wreath that already had a blue tit hanging on it. The whole family with flashlights in front of the house in the neighborhood, which was decorated with hundreds of fairy lights.

“That was the most beautiful Advent,” said Paula. Then she paused. “Although I didn’t get that many things.”

## For your everyday life

Advent calendars are intended to arouse anticipation for Christmas. But if there are several presents waiting every day, the anticipation can quickly become a habit - and the little things end up in a box unnoticed. For many children, spending time together is what they remember later. What helped the Berger family:

- **The children ask.** What has been nice so far? What do they want? Children often have very specific ideas about what they want to experience with their parents, and they are rarely expensive things.
- **Mix small and large.** Not every day needs a trip. Reading one more chapter, pancakes for dinner, or a flashlight walk around the neighborhood are just as valuable.
- **Adapt the calendar to everyday family life.** Schedule larger activities on days off and only plan small things on stressful days. A calendar that overwhelms parents quickly loses its magic.
- **Stay flexible.** If a child gets sick or an appointment comes up, a note can be exchanged or postponed. More important than the plan is the time together.
- **Speak openly with grandparents.** Instead of rejecting gifts, you can develop new ideas together that suit the family - for example stories, letters or shared experiences.
- **Capture memories.** A few photos or a small Advent diary will help you remember the beautiful moments later.

### Ideas for a calendar full of time

If you would like to fill up your own time calendar, you will find suggestions here that cost little and can be easily incorporated into full weeks:

- Walk around the neighborhood in the evening with flashlights and look for the most beautiful lights
- Bake cookies and take a plate of them to the neighbors
- Send a letter or a painted picture to your grandparents
- Drink cocoa in the living room by candlelight and tell stories
- Fold paper stars and stick them on the window
- Build a bird seed wreath and watch who comes to visit
- Sort out toys together that can make other children happy
- take a winter walk with a thermos and gingerbread
- Build a den out of blankets and read aloud in it

### Less trash in Advent

A time calendar also saves a lot of packaging. Cloth bags, paper bags or small boxes can be reused every year and the pieces of paper fit into any sleeve. If you still want to give away small things, you can use things that will be used up, such as crayons, play dough or a tangerine, instead of toys that end up in the box after a day.

### To talk to your children

- What was the best thing for you in the last Advent – something you received or something we did together?
- What three things would you like to write in a calendar full of time together?
- Why are you sometimes more excited about something when you don't know what it is?
- Who in our family would you like to ask for a story from the past?
