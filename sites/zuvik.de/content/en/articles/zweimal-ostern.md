“Does the Easter Bunny even know where I am?” asked Lilly.

She was eight years old and sitting in the back seat of her mother's car on the highway between Halle and Leipzig. It was Maundy Thursday. Her parents had been living apart since the summer, and this was the first Easter that Lilly would be traveling back and forth between two homes. The parents had agreed: Holy Saturday and Easter Sunday until noon with dad in Leipzig, Easter Sunday from the afternoon and Easter Monday with mom in Halle.

Her mother Anja looked at her in the rearview mirror. "The Easter Bunny finds you everywhere, little mouse. He's done that many times." Lilly nodded, but she didn't look convinced. She held on to her stuffed bunny, whose ears were already worn out.

## With dad

Papa Sven now lived in a two-room apartment in the south of Leipzig, on the fourth floor without an elevator. Lilly had a small room there with a bed, a desk and a poster of horses that Dad had hung up especially for her. It still smelled a bit like fresh paint.

On Saturday they dyed eggs. Dad hadn't bought any dye tablets, but rather onion peels, beetroot and turmeric, just like his own grandma had done in the Ore Mountains. The kitchen looked like a battlefield afterwards, Lilly's fingers were yellow and red, and the eggs were colors Lilly had never seen before: a deep reddish brown, a pink like raspberry yogurt, a bright yellow. They put small leaves from the backyard on the eggs, wrapped them in old pantyhose and cooked them with them. When they peeled off the tights, white leaf prints could be seen on the eggs.

“Like magic,” Lilly said.

Early on Easter Sunday they looked in Clara Zetkin Park for Easter eggs that Dad had hidden before breakfast. Lilly found all of them except one, which a squirrel had probably already taken. They both laughed at that. It was a beautiful morning. And just because he was so beautiful, Lilly got a strange feeling in her stomach.

## The silence on the way back

Mom picked her up at two o'clock. Dad and mom talked to each other briefly and politely at the front door, like two people who don't know each other that well. Lilly hugged Dad for a long time. Then she got into the car.

“So?” Mom asked as they drove off. “How was it with Dad?”

Lilly looked out the window. She thought of the onion skin eggs, the park, the squirrel. She thought of Mom's face when Dad carried his boxes out of the apartment in the summer. And she said, “It’s okay.”

Anja noticed that Lilly was holding something back, but she didn't ask any further questions. They were both silent for the rest of the journey.

## Easter with mom

In Halle, Mom had beautifully set the table with a bouquet of forsythia branches with colorful wooden eggs hanging on them. Grandma Brigitte, mom's mother, was there and had baked an Easter braid. Mom had hidden an Easter basket in the garden of the apartment building with a chocolate bunny and a new book about horses.

Lilly looked for the nest and was happy about it. But she was cautiously pleased, as if she couldn't show too much of it. When Grandma Brigitte asked if she had dyed her dad's eggs, Lilly said "Just a few" and quickly changed the subject.

That evening, when Mom was putting her to bed, Lilly suddenly started crying. Anja sat on the edge of the bed and stroked her back. It took a while before Lilly could speak. “I dyed eggs with onions at Dad’s,” she finally sobbed. "And they turned out great. And I left them with Dad because I thought you'd be sad if I brought them with me."

## The permission

Anja sat very still for a while. She thought about the many times she had complained about Sven to her sister on the phone over the last few months, without paying attention to whether Lilly was listening in the next room. Of her pinched face at the front door. About the question in the car that wasn't actually a question at all.

She pulled Lilly into her arms. "Lilly," she said, "listen to me carefully. You can be happy with Dad. You can have a good time with him and tell me about it. That doesn't make me sad. It really doesn't. Dad and I are no longer together, but you are still our child. Of both of us."

“Even if it was nicer with dad than with you?” Lilly asked quietly.

Anja had to swallow. "It wasn't more beautiful or less beautiful. It was different. And both belong to you."

Lilly looked at her for a long time. Then she told everything. About the tights and the leaf prints, about the squirrel, about the poster with the horses. Anja listened and asked, and when Lilly talked about the pink egg that looked like raspberry yogurt, she laughed.

## A conversation between adults

On Easter Monday, after Lilly fell asleep, Anja called Sven. It was the first time in months that they didn't talk about appointments, maintenance or school things, but about Lilly herself. Anja talked about the conversation that evening. Sven was silent for a moment. Then he said, "She hardly said anything about you to me either. I thought it was because she missed me. But maybe it's the same thing."

They agreed on a few things, very specific. That they wouldn't talk badly about each other in front of Lilly. That Lilly was allowed to carry things back and forth between apartments without having to ask. That they would not only be polite at the front door, but also a little friendly, even if it was difficult. And that every now and then they would tell Lilly together that they both loved her.

The next weekend, when Lilly was back at Dad's, she carefully packed three of the onion-shell eggs into an egg carton. Dad helped her. “For mom,” said Lilly. "And for Grandma Brigitte. And one for me, so I have one in both apartments."

## A year later

The next spring, a year after the first Easter in two apartments, Lilly dyed eggs with onion skins again at Dad's. This time she took half of it with her to Halle without thinking. Mom put them on the table next to the forsythia bouquet, and Grandma Brigitte wanted to know how the leaves worked. Lilly explained it to her in detail.

This time, mom and dad talked for a few minutes longer at the front door in Leipzig. About Lilly's report card, about the new bicycle helmet, about the weather. It wasn't much of a conversation. But Lilly had seen it from the car, and on the drive to Halle she was humming to herself the whole time.

## For your everyday life

Celebrations are often particularly difficult for children of separated parents. They experience traditions in two households, they commute, and many sense, without anyone saying anything, a conflict of loyalty: If I like it with Dad, will I betray Mom? What helped Lilly's parents:

- **Explicitly allow to love both parents.** Children often need a clear sentence: “You can have a good time with the other parent, and you can tell me about it.” This takes a huge burden off them.
- **Pay attention to nuances.** If a child notices noticeably little, is “cautiously” happy or is quiet after being handed over, there may be a conflict of loyalty behind it.
- **Don't talk badly about the other parent.** Not even on the phone if you think the child isn't listening. Children notice a surprising amount.
- **Let items move.** Crafts, gifts or favorite things should be allowed to go back and forth between both apartments without permission.
- **Make handovers friendly.** A few kind words at the door show the child that the parents can get along with each other despite the separation.
- **Talk about the child, not just organization.** Regular communication about how the child is doing helps both parents interpret signals better. If conflicts persist, family counseling centers can provide support.

### Planning holidays after the breakup

Celebrations are easier to celebrate in separate families if the agreements are made early and are not renegotiated every year. Many parents take turns, for example Easter in even years with mom and in odd years with dad, or split the holidays, like Lilly's parents. Write down the agreement and tell your child in good time where they will be and when. Short-term changes often unsettle children more than the separation itself. The older children get, the more they are allowed to have a say without having to choose between their parents.

### Dye eggs like Lilly's dad did

Eggs colored with natural dyes are a fun project for children. For a warm reddish brown you boil a handful of onion peels in water, for pink you use beetroot, for yellow you use turmeric, and red cabbage surprisingly turns white eggs blue. Add a dash of vinegar to the broth to help the color last better. To make patterns, place small leaves or grass on the wet eggs, wrap them tightly in a piece of old tights, tie them up and cook them in the dye stock for about ten minutes. After unwrapping, light-colored leaf imprints remain. Rubbed with a little cooking oil, the eggs shine at the end.

### To talk to your children

- Why didn't Lilly tell her mom how nice it was with dad?
- Is there something you don't tell us because you're afraid it'll make someone sad?
- What is a must for you at Easter?
- Which tradition from our family would you like to pass on to your own children?
- Is there anything you would like to take with you from one apartment to another?
- What could we do to make the handover at the front door feel easier for you?
