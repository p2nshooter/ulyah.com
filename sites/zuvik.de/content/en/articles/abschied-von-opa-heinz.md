The call came on a Tuesday evening in November. Jakob, six years old, was already in bed when the phone rang. He heard his mother Birgit speak quietly, then no longer at all. Then he heard her crying.

The next morning Birgit sat at the breakfast table with teary eyes. Jacob's father Andreas had taken some time off. They looked at each other, and then Birgit said: "Jakob, I have to tell you something sad. Grandpa Heinz died last night."

## What “died” means

Jacob looked at her. He knew that Grandpa Heinz had been in the hospital for a few weeks. He had visited him once with lots of tubes and a beeping machine. Grandpa had smiled and said he would be home soon and then they would continue building the model train in the basement together.

“Is he not coming home now?” asked Jakob.

“No,” said Birgit. The night before she had thought long and hard about which words she would use. A friend who worked as a teacher had advised her to be honest and clear, not to use euphemisms such as “asleep” or “gone away,” which children could misunderstand. "Grandpa Heinz is dead. His body has stopped functioning. His heart is no longer beating, he is no longer breathing, he is no longer in pain. And he can't come back."

Jacob was silent. Then he asked: “And the railway?”

Despite everything, Birgit had to smile. "The railway is still in the basement. Maybe we can finish building it together, someday."

Jacob nodded and ate his bread. Then he asked if he could play outside after breakfast.

## Grief in puddles

Birgit was confused. Her own heart was broken and her son wanted to play outside? Did he not understand? Or did he not care?

Her friend explained it to her later on the phone. "Children grieve differently than adults. It's sometimes said that they jump in and out of puddles of grief. One moment they're sad, the next they're playing. That doesn't mean they don't care. It means they can only endure it in small portions."

In fact, over the next few days, the questions came at unexpected moments. While brushing your teeth: “Is Grandpa in heaven now?” When shopping: “Will he get cold in the coffin?” While driving: “Are you dying too, Mom?”

Birgit and Andreas answered every question as honestly as they could. When asked about heaven, Birgit said: "Some people believe that the soul goes to heaven after death. I hope that is the case. What do you think?" Jacob thought about it and said he thought Grandpa was with the stars now because he liked stars so much. When asked about dying, Andreas said: "All people die at some point. But most only when they are very, very old. I plan to be there for you for a long time."

## The question of the funeral

The most difficult question was the funeral. Should Jacob come with you? Birgit's sister thought a child didn't belong at a funeral, it was too sad. Andreas was unsure. Birgit herself didn't know.

Finally they asked Jacob. They explained to him exactly what happens at a funeral: that many people will come dressed in black, that many will cry, that Grandpa will be in a coffin that will be lowered into the ground, that there will be music and that someone will talk about Grandpa's life. “Would you like to come with us?” asked Birgit. "You don't have to. Either way is fine."

Jacob thought for a long time. “Can I give Grandpa something?”

## A letter for Grandpa

The day before the funeral, Jacob painted a picture. It showed a train traveling over a bridge to the stars. In the first carriage sat a man wearing a cap. Below, Jakob dictated a sentence to his mother, which she wrote down: "Dear Grandpa, I'm finishing building the railway. Your Jakob."

At the funeral, Jacob held his father's hand. He saw the many people, heard the music, saw his mother crying. He didn't cry, but he was very quiet. When the coffin was lowered into the ground, he was allowed to throw his picture behind it. It fluttered briefly and landed on the coffin lid, with the train pointing upwards.

On the way back to the car, Jakob said: “Now Grandpa has the picture.” And then, after a while: “Mom, you cried a lot.” – “Yes,” said Birgit. "Because I'm sad. Grandpa Heinz was my dad." Jacob squeezed her hand tighter. "I'm sad too. Only inside."

## The railway

In the spring, when the initial sadness had subsided a little, Birgit, Andreas and Jakob went into Grandpa's basement. The model train was still there, half finished, with mountains of papier-mâché and a train station without a roof. On the workbench were tools and a list in Grandpa's handwriting: "Roof station. Paint tunnel. Plant trees."

Jacob read the list with difficulty. Then he said, “That’s what we’re doing now.” And so they spent many Saturdays in the basement, finishing what Grandpa had started. Sometimes they were sad, sometimes they laughed at Grandpa's crooked trees. It was a farewell that lasted weeks and yet was full of life.

## A year later

On the first anniversary of his death, the three drove to the cemetery. Jacob was now seven and carried a small locomotive in his jacket pocket that he wanted to place on the grave. Andreas had a candle with him, Birgit a bouquet from the garden. They stood in silence for a while, then Jacob asked, “Do you think Grandpa would be proud of the railroad?” Birgit nodded. "Very. He would have said the trees are straighter than his."

At home, Jakob took out the box that they had put together in the spring: Grandpa's old hat, the list from the workbench, a few photos and the program of the funeral service. He took out the list, ran his finger over the handwriting, and put it back again. He wasn't sad about it. It was more like he was saying hello.

## For your everyday life

When a grandparent dies, many children experience the death of someone close to them for the first time. They need honest information, space for their feelings, and permission to grieve in their own way. What helped Jacob's family:

- **Use clear words.** “Dead” and “died” are more understandable to children than euphemisms such as “asleep” or “lost,” which can trigger fears or give false hope.
- **Explains what death means.** Young children often do not understand that death is final. Simple explanations help: The heart no longer beats, the body no longer functions, the person cannot come back.
- **Accepts “Puddle Mourning.”** Children often switch quickly between sadness and play. This is normal and not a sign of indifference.
- **Answers questions honestly.** Children often ask questions unexpectedly and repeatedly. It's okay to say, "I'm not sure" or "What do you think?"
- **Let children have a say in farewell rituals.** If children are well prepared, attending a funeral can help with the farewell process. A picture, a letter or a flower gives them a task.
- **Show your own sadness.** Children learn that crying is okay when they see adults feeling sad too.
- **Get support if necessary.** If a child appears to be very stressed for a long time, no longer sleeps, withdraws or develops severe fears, a pediatrician, family counseling centers or child bereavement support services can help.

### What children understand at what age

How children understand death changes with age. The following levels are rough guidelines, every child is different:

- **Until about three years**, children do not yet understand death, but they feel the absence of a familiar person and the sadness of adults. Above all, you need closeness and reliable processes.
- **At kindergarten age** many children think death is temporary and keep asking when the deceased will come back. Some people believe they are to blame, for example because they were once angry with grandma or grandpa. Therefore, expressly say that no one causes death through thoughts or arguments.
- **At primary school age** children increasingly understand that death is final and affects everyone. They are often interested in very practical questions, such as what happens to the body in the grave. Factual, calm answers help more than evasion.
- **Older children and teenagers** understand death similarly to adults. They sometimes withdraw or prefer to talk to friends rather than their parents. Offers to talk without pushing.

### Keeping memories alive

Farewell doesn't end with the funeral. A memory box, a shared photo album, the deceased's favorite food on their birthday or a candle on their anniversary give a place to mourn. Many children also like to help design the grave, for example with flowers they have sown themselves or a painted stone.

### When you yourself are grieving

Parents often grieve at the same time as their children and find themselves with less energy than usual. It's okay to get help from friends, neighbors or family with cooking, driving or caring. Be careful not to let your children be your source of comfort and find people you can talk to.

### To talk to your children

Ask carefully: What do you remember most fondly about Grandpa or Grandma? Is there anything else you would like to say to him or her? Sometimes a picture, a letter or a shared project helps to keep memories alive.

Picture books about farewell and death can also help. Ask the library or bookstore for recommendations for your child's age. A book read together is often a good opportunity to talk about your own feelings without anyone feeling questioned.
