Stefan Wagner left the house at 5:10 a.m. He worked as a nurse in the intensive care unit of a hospital in Bremen, and in the weeks with an early shift, his shift started at six. He got dressed in the dark, drank coffee standing up, and then crept past the children's rooms where Noah and Frieda were sleeping. Sometimes he would stop at the door and listen to them breathing for a while.

When he came home in the afternoon he was tired. Not the kind of tired that you fix with a coffee, but the kind that comes from twelve hours of responsibility for seriously ill people. He played with the children as much as he could, had dinner with them and was often asleep by nine.

## The sentence that hurt

On a Wednesday in November, Frieda, six years old, painted a picture of her family in kindergarten. When she proudly showed it off in the evening, Stefan took a closer look: There was Mom, Noah, Frieda, the neighbor's cat, who often came to visit. And he, at the very edge, smaller than the others, with a jacket on and a bag in his hand.

“Why did you paint me there?” he asked cautiously. Frieda shrugged her shoulders. “You always go away.”

Stefan smiled and praised the picture. But once the children were in bed, he sat at the kitchen table for a long time. His wife Anne sat down next to him. “She doesn’t mean any harm,” she said. “I know,” Stefan replied. "That's the bad thing. She simply describes what she sees."

## A note in the lunch box

The next morning, at 4:55 a.m., Stefan stood in the kitchen and saw the two lunch boxes that Anne had prepared the night before. He took a piece of paper from the pad on the refrigerator and wrote on it in thick pen: "Good morning, Frieda! Today I'm in the hospital helping a man who is very sick get better. I think of you when I take my break. Dad." He also painted a small sun. He wrote a joke for Noah, who was nine and could already read.

He put the pieces of paper under the sandwiches and left.

When he came home that afternoon, Frieda ran to meet him and held up the piece of paper, which was now quite crumpled and smelled of cucumber. “Did you help the man?” she asked. “Is he healthy?” Stefan explained to her as best he could that some people take a long time to get well. Frieda nodded seriously. Then she asked, “Will you write to me again tomorrow?”

## The sandwich secret

That one piece of paper became a ritual. Every morning shift week, Noah and Frieda found a message in their lunch boxes. Sometimes it was a puzzle, sometimes a drawing, sometimes a question: “What was the funniest thing at break today?” In the evening the children told the answer.

Noah eventually started writing back. In the evening he put a note next to Stefan's coffee mug: "Dad, how many stairs do you walk a day?" Stefan actually counted the next day and wrote back that evening: "About 400 steps. More than the church tower!" It became an exchange of letters between two people who never saw each other in the morning.

Anne watched this with emotion and a bit of envy. “You have a secret,” she said. “The sandwich secret,” Frieda said solemnly, and that’s what it was called from then on.

## The late shift weeks

In the weeks with the late shift everything turned around. Stefan was there in the morning and gone in the afternoon. Then he took over breakfast, took Frieda to kindergarten and took her on a detour past the bakery, where there was a croissant to share. It was now Anne who was alone with the children in the evenings.

The family put a large weekly calendar on the kitchen wall. Every day had a color: yellow for daddy morning, blue for daddy evening, green for the days off. So the children knew on Sunday when they would see their father, and the absence was no longer a surprise. Frieda counted down the green days like other children counted down the days until Christmas.

On the green days there was one fixed rule: no cell phone at the table, no work conversations, an outing together, even if it was just going to the park. Stefan noticed that he had often filled these days with errands. Now he was protecting her.

## A difficult day in the hospital

Not every note was easy to write. One morning in February, Stefan knew that a patient he had been caring for for weeks was unlikely to survive the night. He stood in the kitchen with the pen and didn't know what to write. Finally he just wrote: "Today is a difficult day on my ward. I'm looking forward to seeing you this afternoon. Dad."

When he got home he was quiet. Noah, who had read the note, sat down next to him on the sofa without saying anything and leaned on his shoulder. After a while he asked, “Did your patient die?” Stefan considered whether he should be evasive and decided on the truth, as child-friendly as possible. "Yes. He was very old and very sick, and we made sure he wasn't in any pain."

Noah nodded. “Then you helped him anyway.” Stefan had to swallow. Anne, who was standing in the doorway, later told him that at that moment she understood how much the notes had made a difference. The children now knew what their father did when he was not there. They were able to share in his life, even the hard parts.

## A new picture

A few months later, Frieda brought another family picture from kindergarten. This time Stefan was in the middle. He still wore a jacket and a bag, but in his other hand he held a lunch box with a note sticking out of it. A large sun shone above everyone, exactly like the one he had drawn on the first piece of paper.

“That’s you when you go to work,” Frieda explained. “But you still write to me.”

Stefan hung the picture in his locker at the hospital. It still hangs there today.

## For your everyday life

Many parents work shifts, travel a lot for work or have working hours that do not fit in with their children's days. Closeness is not created through quantity, but through reliability and small signs. Ideas from the Wagner family’s everyday life:

- **Leave a trace.** A note in the lunch box, a voice message for breakfast, a drawing on the bathroom mirror: small messages show children that you are thinking about them, even when you are not there.
- **Explains where you are.** Children can understand absence better if they know what you are doing. Tell children about your work, why it is important and what you do there.
- **Makes the plan visible.** A colored weekly calendar on the wall shows when which parent is there. Predictability takes away a lot of uncertainty for children.
- **Protects time together.** On days off, it's worth consciously limiting what you do and planning time for the family, without cell phones and without work conversations.
- **Create your own rituals depending on the shift.** Whoever is there in the morning can do breakfast, whoever is there in the evening can do the reading. Every ritual counts.
- **Take care of yourself.** Shift work is tiring. Enough sleep and small breaks for yourself are not selfishness, but the basis for being there for your children.

### When a parent needs to sleep during the day

After night shifts, many shift workers have to sleep during the day while the children are at home. This works better with clear agreements: a sign on the bedroom door that children can design themselves, quiet games during this time and a fixed moment when the sleeping parent gets up and is there for the children. Explain to your children why sleep is necessary during the day: Anyone who cares for other people at night needs their sleep when others are awake. Blackout curtains and earplugs help sleepers avoid having to constantly warn children not to whisper.

### Ideas for small messages

A piece of paper doesn't have to be long. Children enjoy a riddle, a joke, a small drawing or a story that continues a little every day. For older children, a shared notebook is suitable that goes back and forth between the children's room and the kitchen and in which both can write. If you like to do crafts, you can draw a map of your route to work and mark a small dot every day. What is important is not the effort, but that the messages come reliably.

### When children worry

Children of parents who work in hospitals, fire departments, police departments, or other demanding jobs sometimes worry if something will happen to their parents at work. Take these concerns seriously and explain what protects you: colleagues, protective clothing, clear procedures. Also talk about the good moments of your work so that the picture doesn't just consist of difficult days.

### To talk to your children

Ask your children: When do you miss me the most? What would you like to know about my work? And if your children can already write: Would you like to write me a note too?

Keep the papers and answers in a box. Over the years, this will become a small archive of their childhood for your children, and for you a reminder that closeness was possible even in full weeks.
