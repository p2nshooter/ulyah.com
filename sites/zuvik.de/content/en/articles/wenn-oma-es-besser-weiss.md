“The child is dressed much too thinly.”

Grandma Renate stood in the doorway of the apartment in Mainz, her coat still on, and looked at her two-year-old granddaughter Lina, who was padding across the carpet barefoot and in a T-shirt. It was twelve degrees outside, twenty-two inside. Lina was rosy and happy.

Julia, Lina's mother, took a deep breath. "Mom, the heater is on. She's not cold." - "Children don't notice that. They catch a cold before they realize they're cold." Grandma Renate walked past Julia into the children's room and got a cardigan. Lina protested as the jacket was put on her and took it off again after two minutes.

This is how every visit from Grandma Renate began.

## The list of advice

Grandma Renate loved her granddaughter dearly. She came every Wednesday to look after Lina while Julia and her husband Kai worked. She brought home-baked cakes, knitted socks and had a home remedy for every ailment. But she also had an opinion about everything.

Lina didn’t sleep enough (“You put her to bed way too late”). Lina ate too little meat (“How is the child supposed to grow?”). Lina was allowed to decide too much for herself (“Children need clear instructions”). Lina was carried too often (“You’re spoiling her”). Lina wasn’t carried enough (“The poor child is crying”). Sometimes the advice contradicted each other within a week.

Julia listened to this, nodded, bit her tongue. In the evenings, when Kai came home, she let off steam. “She treats me like I’m a child who has no idea.” Kai, who liked his mother-in-law, tried to mediate. “She means well.” – “I know she means well! That’s what makes it so difficult!”

## The Wednesday when there was a bang

One Wednesday Julia came home earlier than expected. She could already hear Lina crying in the stairwell. In the apartment she found her daughter sitting in her high chair, a plate of cold mashed potatoes in front of her, and Grandma Renate next to her, who said in a stern voice: “First we eat, then you can go down.”

Julia and Kai had consciously decided against being forced to eat. They had explained this to their mother several times. And now Lina was sitting here, crying, in front of a plate that she didn't want.

“Mom, please take her out,” Julia said, louder than she wanted. Grandma Renate turned around in surprise. "She has to learn that you eat up. You let her get away with everything."

And then Julia said things she had wanted to say for a long time. That she couldn't stand the constant criticism anymore. That she and Kai were Lina's parents and made their own decisions. That every Wednesday she feels like her testimony is being handed out. Grandma Renate turned pale, took her coat and left without saying a word.

## A week of silence

They didn't hear from Grandma Renate for a week. She didn't call, didn't come on Wednesday. Julia had to arrange other care at short notice and felt miserable. She had been right in what she had said. But not with how she said it.

Kai suggested that they should talk to each other, but not alone. "Let's invite them to dinner. The three of us. And first we'll think about what we really want to say."

They sat down together and wrote a list. Not of accusations, but of things that were really important to them. There were surprisingly few: no pressure to eat. No sweets as a reward. Lina is comforted when she cries. And everything else, they realized as they wrote, was actually negotiable. Whether Lina wore a cardigan or not, whether she went to bed at half past seven or eight, it wasn't as important as it felt at the moment.

## The conversation

Grandma Renate came to dinner, stiff and cautious. Lina ran towards her and hugged her legs, and that solved something. After dinner, when Lina was sleeping, Julia said: "Mom, I'm sorry for the way I spoke to you last week. That wasn't fair."

Grandma Renate was silent for a moment. Then she said something Julia hadn't expected. "I'm sorry too. I felt like I wasn't needed anymore. Like I didn't know anything anymore. I raised three kids and now everyone does everything differently."

Julia suddenly understood that behind all the advice there was no criticism, but rather a fear: the fear of becoming superfluous. “You are needed,” she said. "Very much. Lina loves you. I need you. Without you we wouldn't be able to make it through Wednesday. But I need you as a grandma, not as an examiner."

They showed her the list of three items. Grandma Renate read it and nodded. “That’s all?” - "That's all. For everything else, you can do as you see fit. If Lina wears a cardigan with you, then she'll wear one."

## Another Wednesday

Grandma Renate came back the next Wednesday. She brought cake and, to Julia's surprise, a question instead of advice: "How do you actually do your afternoon nap? I want to do it the way Lina is used to."

The advice didn't stop completely. Grandma Renate was what she was. But they came less often and Julia could take them more easily. Sometimes she even asked on her own initiative: “Mom, how did you do that when I didn’t want to sleep?” And Grandma Renate talked, her eyes shining, and some of her ideas were actually good.

## What grandma Renate said

A few weeks later, on a Wednesday afternoon, Julia and her mother were sitting on the balcony while Lina took a nap. Grandma Renate stirred her coffee for a long time, then she said: "You know, when you were a baby, your other grandma came every Sunday. And every Sunday she explained to me what I was doing wrong. You would cry too much because I picked you up too often. I would have weaned you way too early. I cried every Sunday after her visit."

Julia stared at her. “And then you do the same thing?” Grandma Renate laughed, a little embarrassed. "You don't notice it yourself. You just want to help. And you're afraid that the child will get something that you could have prevented."

From that day on, the two had a code word. Whenever Grandma Renate started to give unsolicited advice, Julia said with a smile: “Sunday, Mom.” Most of the time they both laughed and the advice went unsaid.

## For your everyday life

Conflicts between parents and grandparents over parenting issues are very common. Behind well-intentioned advice there is often love, personal experiences and sometimes the fear of no longer being needed. What helped Lina's family:

- **Clarify what is really important to you.** A short list of the few points that are non-negotiable creates clarity for everyone. Grandparents are allowed to do things differently when it comes to many other things.
- **Speak calmly, not in an argument.** A prepared conversation in a relaxed atmosphere is more effective than an outburst in a tense situation.
- **Ask about the feelings behind the advice.** Often the fear of no longer being needed or valued is behind it.
- **Recognizes the help of grandparents.** Grandparents who provide care do a lot. Thanks and recognition ease many conflicts.
- **Ask for advice if you really want it.** If you ask about experiences from time to time, you give grandparents their space without giving up the decisions.
- **Stick together as parents.** When both parents share the same line, it's easier to remain friendly and clear.

### Why advice has changed

Many conflicts arise because knowledge about children has changed significantly in one generation. In the past, babies were often put to sleep on their stomachs; Today the supine position is recommended because it reduces the risk of sudden infant death syndrome. Children should finish their plates earlier; Today, experts advise letting children decide how much of what they eat so that they stay full. When your grandparents explain reasons like this instead of just saying “that’s how we do it today,” they feel taken seriously and not devalued.

### A card for care

If grandparents provide regular care, it helps to have a short written overview, for example on the fridge: bedtimes, allergies and medications, emergency numbers, the two or three rules that are really important to you, and what the child currently likes to eat or play. This saves discussions in the doorway and gives grandparents security. A quick handover in the evening, during which you also ask how the day was for them, shows that you value their time.

### When it keeps banging

Some conflicts cannot be resolved in a conversation, especially if old injuries from your own childhood resonate. Then a neutral person can help. Family and parenting advice centers offer discussions for several generations, often free of charge. It's also okay to organize care differently for a while if the relationship would otherwise suffer too much.

### To talk to your children

Children are very aware of tensions between parents and grandparents. If you have an argument, explain to them that sometimes adults have different opinions and still love each other. And shows them how to reconcile.

By the way, children cope well with the fact that things at grandma and grandpa's house are different than at home. The only important thing is that the few really important rules apply everywhere and that no one speaks badly about others in front of the children.
