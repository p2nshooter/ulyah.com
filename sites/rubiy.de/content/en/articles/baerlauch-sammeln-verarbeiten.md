In March and April, when the first green carpets emerge from the ground in damp deciduous forests and floodplains, there is a subtle scent of garlic in the air. Wild garlic is one of the first fresh herbs of the year and has had an amazing career in the last few decades: from an almost forgotten wild herb to a spring star at markets and restaurants.

Collecting wild garlic is a nice spring ritual. But it requires care, because plants that look similar to it and are highly poisonous grow in the same locations. Poisonings occur every year because collectors take lily of the valley or autumn crocus leaves. This can certainly be avoided if you look carefully.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Wild garlic is in season from around March to early May. The leaves are most tender before flowering.
> - Each wild garlic leaf has its own thin stem, a matt underside and smells distinctly of garlic when crushed.
> - Poisonous doppelgängers include lily of the valley, autumn crocus and arum. Collect leaf by leaf and examine each one individually.
> - The smell test is unreliable if your fingers already smell like garlic.
> - You can store wild garlic pesto in the refrigerator and use it within about a week, or you can freeze it.

## How you can recognize wild garlic

Wild garlic (*Allium ursinum*) is related to chives, onions and garlic. It prefers to grow in shady, moist, nutrient-rich deciduous forests, often under beech trees or in river floodplains, where it forms dense stands.

Key features:

- **Leaf stem:** Each leaf grows individually from the ground and has its own thin stalk.
- **Underside of the leaf:** It is matt, the top has a brighter shine.
- **Shape:** lanceolate, i.e. elongated and pointed, about 10 to 25 centimeters long.
- **Smell:** If you crush a leaf, it smells intensely of garlic.
- **Flowering:** White, star-shaped flowers appear in a spherical umbel from April or May.

### Why it is called wild garlic

The scientific nickname ursinum comes from the Latin ursus, meaning bear. A common explanation is that bears eat the fresh leaves after hibernation to regain their strength. Whether this is true zoologically is not proven, but the story fits the character of the plant: wild garlic is one of the first strong greens after winter. Regionally it is also called forest garlic, witch's onion or wild garlic.

## The poisonous doppelgangers

| plant | What she looks like | Difference from wild garlic | Toxicity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lily of the valley | usually two leaves that grow together from a stem and embrace each other | no single leaf stalk, shiny underside of leaf, no smell of garlic, flower as white bells | highly toxic, affects the heart |
| Autumn crocus | Leaves grow directly from the ground in spring without a stem, in clumps that enclose each other | stemless, thicker, fleshier, no smell of garlic | very poisonous, just a few leaves can be fatal |
| Arum | arrow- or spear-shaped leaves with net-like veins | Leaf shape significantly different, veins branched instead of parallel | toxic, irritates mouth and stomach |

Wild garlic has parallel leaf veins, like all allium plants. The lily of the valley also has parallel veins, but its leaves grow in pairs on a common stem. The autumn crocus produces strong, tulip-like leaf clusters in spring, often with a seed capsule in the middle.

### Use the smell test correctly

The garlic smell is a good feature, but only for the first leaf. After a few minutes of collecting, your fingers smell so strongly of wild garlic that even a lily of the valley leaf seems to smell of garlic. So never rely on your nose alone. Also check the stem and underside of the leaves. Pick each leaf individually instead of tearing them off in bunches, and sort them again leaf by leaf when you get home.

If you are unsure, buy wild garlic at the market or grow it in the garden. If you suspect poisoning, such as nausea, vomiting, cardiac arrhythmias or diarrhea after eating wild herbs, call your state's poison control center or 112 immediately.

## What is allowed when collecting

According to the Federal Nature Conservation Act, you are allowed to collect wild plants that are not specifically protected in small quantities for your own use. One speaks of the hand bouquet rule. Collecting is generally prohibited in nature reserves and national parks. Also pay attention to these points:

- Instead of pulling out the plant, cut off the leaves with a knife or scissors. The onion remains in the ground.
- Only take one or two leaves from each plant so that it can continue to grow.
- Do not collect on roadsides where there are many dogs, or next to busy roads or fertilized fields.
- Stay on the paths where the vegetation is very dense and do not trample the plants.

### What about the fox tapeworm?

Many wild herb collectors are concerned about the fox tapeworm. Transmission via wild herbs is considered very rare, but it cannot be ruled out. Thorough washing reduces the risk. The eggs are safely killed by heating them above 60 degrees. However, freezing them in a normal household freezer does not kill them. If you want to be absolutely sure, use wild garlic from the garden or cooked.

## Prepare wild garlic

Wash the leaves thoroughly in a bowl of cold water and then rinse them under running water. Shake them dry or dry them in the salad spinner. For pesto, the leaves must be as dry as possible, otherwise it becomes watery and doesn't last as well. Fresh wild garlic, wrapped in a damp cloth, will keep in the vegetable compartment for two to three days.

## Wild garlic pesto

For about two small glasses (together 300 ml):

- 100 g wild garlic leaves
- 50 g pine nuts or sunflower seeds
- 50 g Parmesan or another mature hard cheese
- 120 to 150 ml mild olive oil or rapeseed oil
- ½ tsp salt
- 1 tsp lemon juice

1. Roast the seeds in a pan without fat over medium heat until golden brown. Let them cool down.
2. Roughly chop the wild garlic and grate the cheese finely.
3. Put the wild garlic, seeds, salt and half of the oil in a tall mug and briefly puree everything with the hand blender. Don't mix for too long, otherwise the pesto will become warm and bitter.
4. Stir in cheese, lemon juice and remaining oil.
5. Pour the pesto into clean jars, spread it evenly and cover it with a thin layer of oil.

### Store pesto properly

Fresh herbs in oil are considered a risk for botulism because no oxygen can reach the herbs under the oil layer. Therefore, always store pesto in the refrigerator and use it within about a week. Always remove pesto with a clean spoon and cover the rest with oil again. For longer supplies, freeze it in portions in ice cube trays, leaving out the cheese and adding it fresh after defrosting. It lasts for several months when frozen.

## Wild garlic butter

For about 250g:

- 250 g soft butter
- 40 g wild garlic, finely chopped
- ½ tsp salt, a few squeezes of lemon juice

Mix the butter with wild garlic, salt and lemon. Form it into a roll with baking paper, cool it and cut slices when you need them. It will last about a week in the fridge or several months if frozen. It tastes good on fresh bread, grilled vegetables, potatoes and asparagus.

## Wild garlic salt

Puree 50 g of dry wild garlic leaves with 250 g of coarse sea salt, spread the green mixture on a baking tray lined with baking paper and dry it in the oven at 50 degrees with the door slightly open for two to three hours. Crumble the salt and pour it into a tightly fitting jar. It lasts for several months and flavors eggs, potatoes and salad dressings.

## Wild garlic spaetzle

Wild garlic turns spaetzle bright green and gives them a delicate garlic aroma. For four people, finely puree 60 g of wild garlic with four eggs and around 100 ml of water. Put 400 g of flour, preferably spaetzle flour or type 405 wheat flour, and a teaspoon of salt in a bowl, pour in the green egg mixture and beat the dough vigorously with a wooden spoon until it bubbles and pulls tough from the spoon. Let it rest for ten minutes. Scrape the dough from the board or press it through a spaetzle press into plenty of boiling salted water. As soon as the spaetzle rise to the surface, lift them out with a slotted spoon. Tossed in butter and sprinkled with grated mountain cheese, they are a complete spring meal. A few strips of fresh wild garlic on top enhance the aroma.

## More ideas

- **Wild garlic soup:** Cook potatoes and onions in broth until soft, finally add a large handful of wild garlic and puree. Don't cook any more, otherwise the wild garlic will lose its color.
- **Wild garlic quark:** Quark with a little milk, salt, pepper and finely chopped wild garlic with jacket potatoes.
- **Scrambled eggs and omelette:** Fold in strips of wild garlic at the end.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I still eat wild garlic after it has bloomed?

Yes, but the leaves are firmer and sharper. The flowers and buds are also edible and taste mildly of garlic. Buds can be marinated in salt or vinegar like capers.

### Can I dry wild garlic?

Dried wild garlic loses most of its aroma. Pesto, butter, salt or frozen, finely chopped leaves are better.

### Can wild garlic be grown in the garden?

Yes, in a shady, damp place under bushes or trees. It spreads over time. You plant the plants or bulbs in autumn; seeds need a cold period and germinate slowly.

### How much wild garlic can I collect?

The hand bouquet rule does not specify a fixed number of grams. This means an amount that is enough for one or two meals in your own home, about a handful to a small bag. Larger quantities for sale or for large-scale supplies require approval.

### My wild garlic pesto is too spicy for me. What to do?

Next time, replace a third of the wild garlic with flat-leaf parsley or young spinach and use a few more nuts. You can soften a finished pesto that is too strong by adding cream cheese, ricotta or a little more oil and cheese. Wild garlic also loses some of its spiciness when cooked.
