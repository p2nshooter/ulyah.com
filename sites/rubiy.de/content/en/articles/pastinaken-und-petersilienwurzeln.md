They were almost forgotten for a long time, but today they can be found again at every weekly market: parsnips and parsley roots. Both look like pale, creamy-white carrots, and both are a win-win in the kitchen during the cold season. Before the potato became native to Central Europe in the 18th century, the parsnip was an important staple food. Today we appreciate them for their subtle sweetness and their nutty, spicy taste. The parsley root is stronger and spicier. Anyone who can tell the two apart can use them specifically: the parsnip for sweetness and creaminess, the parsley root for flavor in soups and broths.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Parsnips and parsley roots are in season from October to March. After the first frosts, parsnips taste sweeter.
> - The parsnip is larger and has a wide, sunken leaf base. The parsley root is slimmer and the base of the leaves curves outwards.
> - Taste: Parsnip sweet and nutty, parsley root spicy and parsley-like.
> - You don't have to peel young, thin roots. Thorough brushing is enough.
> - Both will be golden brown and caramelized in the oven at 200 degrees in 25 to 35 minutes.

## Parsnip or parsley root?

At first glance, the two can hardly be distinguished. These features help:

| feature | Parsnip | parsley root |
|---|---|---|
| size | often thick and long, 15 to 30 cm | slimmer and shorter, usually 10 to 20 cm |
| leaf base | wide, sunken inwards, with a border | narrow, slightly curved outwards |
| color | cream-colored to slightly yellowish | whitish to light beige |
| smell | sweet, slightly reminiscent of carrot and celery | clearly like parsley |
| taste | sweet, nutty, mildly spicy | strong, spicy, bitter |
| Best for | Oven vegetables, puree, cakes, baby food | Soups, broths, stews, braised dishes |

If you're unsure, cut a piece and smell it. The parsley scent is clear.

## Shopping and storage

Good roots are firm, smooth and without soft spots or cracks. Medium-sized parsnips are usually more tender than very thick specimens, whose core can be woody. Small brown spots on the peel are normal and will disappear when peeled.

Without greenery and unwashed, both roots will last in the vegetable compartment, wrapped in a damp cloth or a perforated bag, for about two to three weeks. They stay fresh all winter long in boxes filled with moist sand in the cool cellar. Avoid storing next to apples and pears. The ripening gas ethylene makes root vegetables bitter. You can find out more about this in [Store fruit and vegetables properly](/kueche/obst-und-gemuese-lagern/).

## Prepare

Cut off the leaf base and root tip. Just brush young, thin roots thoroughly under running water, as there is a lot of aroma under the peel. Peel older, thick parsnips with a peeler. If the core is very thick, fibrous and darker than the flesh, cut the parsnip lengthways and remove it.

Parsnips discolor slightly when exposed to air after being cut. If you're not cooking them right away, soak them in water with a squeeze of lemon juice.

A note for everyone who gardens themselves: The leaves of the parsnip contain substances that can cause redness and blisters on the skin when exposed to sunlight. Wear gloves and long sleeves when harvesting. The roots themselves are completely unproblematic in the kitchen.

## Roasted parsnips with honey and thyme

The parsnip develops its sweetness best in the oven.

### Ingredients for four people as a side dish

- 800 g parsnips or a mixture of parsnips, parsley roots and carrots
- 3 tablespoons rapeseed oil or olive oil
- 1 tbsp honey or maple syrup
- a few sprigs of thyme or 1 teaspoon dried thyme
- Salt, pepper
- optional: 2 tablespoons coarsely chopped walnuts or hazelnuts

### Preparation

1. Preheat the oven to 200 degrees top and bottom heat.
2. Cut the roots lengthwise into sticks about one centimeter thick. Even pieces cook evenly.
3. Mix them with oil, salt, pepper and thyme and spread them on a baking tray lined with baking paper.
4. Roast them for 20 minutes, turning them once and drizzling with honey.
5. Roast for another 10 to 15 minutes, until the edges are dark golden. Add the nuts in the last five minutes.

Roasted parsnips go well with roasts, poultry, fried fish and lentil dishes. Cold with lamb's lettuce and a mustard dressing, they become a winter salad.

## Creamy parsnip puree

Parsnip puree is a wonderful alternative to mashed potatoes, or a complement: half potato, half parsnip is the perfect mix for many.

1. Peel 500 g parsnips and 300 g floury potatoes and cut both into equal pieces.
2. Cook everything in salted water for 15 to 20 minutes until soft and let it evaporate well.
3. Mash the vegetables with a masher or press them through a potato press.
4. Stir in 50 g butter and 100 to 150 ml warm milk until the consistency is creamy.
5. Season with salt, nutmeg and a pinch of freshly grated lemon peel.

Because of the fibers, parsnip puree is not quite as smooth as mashed potatoes. For a particularly fine result, strain it through a sieve. Our article on [types of potatoes and types of cooking](/kueche/kartoffelsorten-kochtypen/) explains why floury potatoes are important here.

## Parsley root in soup and broth

The parsley root is an integral part of the soup greens in many regions, especially in northern Germany and Austria, where it is called “parsley root”. A piece of parsley root gives a broth a flavorful depth that celery alone doesn't achieve. We describe how to cook a strong broth from root vegetables and leftovers in [Cook your own vegetable broth](/kueche/gemuesebruehe-selbst-kochen/).

**Parsley root soup:** Sauté an onion and 500 g of diced parsley roots in butter, pour in a liter of broth and let everything simmer for 20 minutes. Puree the soup, refine it with cream and a squeeze of lemon juice and sprinkle finely chopped flat-leaf parsley over it. A few drops of pumpkin seed oil make them particularly beautiful.

## Parsnip soup with apple and ginger

For four portions, finely dice an onion and a thumb-sized piece of ginger and sauté both in 2 tablespoons of butter until translucent. Add 600 g of parsnips and a sour apple, both peeled and diced, and sauté them briefly. Pour in a liter of vegetable stock and let everything simmer for about 20 minutes until the parsnips are soft. Puree the soup finely, stir in 100 ml cream or coconut milk and season with salt, pepper and a squeeze of lemon juice. Roasted pumpkin seeds or a few croutons add some bite. The apple brings freshness, the ginger a slight spiciness that balances the sweetness of the parsnip.

## More ideas

- **In stew:** Parsnips and parsley roots go well in any winter stew, for example with [turnips](/kueche/steckrueben-eintopf/), lentils or pearl barley.
- **As chips:** Bake thinly sliced ​​parsnips with oil and salt at 150 degrees for 15 to 20 minutes until crispy. They burn quickly, so keep an eye on them.
- **Raw:** Young parsnips can be finely grated and used like carrots in raw salads.
- **In the cake:** Grated parsnips work in a sponge cake in a similar way to carrots in carrot cake.

## Freeze parsnips

If the harvest is large or the market has a good supply, parsnips can be frozen. Peel them, cut them into slices or sticks and blanch them in boiling water for two to three minutes. Then rinse in ice water, drain well, pat dry and pour into freezer bags in portions. At minus 18 degrees they last for around ten to twelve months.

You can add frozen parsnips directly to soup or stew. Fresh roots are better for crispy oven vegetables because thawed vegetables release more water. Ready-made puree can also be frozen in portions. Our article [Freezing and blanching vegetables] (/kueche/gemuese-einfrieren-und-blanchieren/) shows how blanching works in detail.

## Grow yourself: parsnips and root parsley in the garden

Both roots are grateful crops for gardens and deep raised beds. Parsnips are sown directly into the bed from March to May, in rows about 30 cm apart. They germinate slowly, often only after three to four weeks, and their seeds quickly lose their ability to germinate. Therefore, use fresh seeds every year. A few radish seeds in the same row mark the seed until the parsnips emerge.

You can also sow parsley roots directly from March. Both need deep, loose soil without stones and without fresh manure, otherwise the roots will branch out. Harvesting begins in October. Parsnips are so frost hardy that they can stay in the bed through the winter and be dug up as needed as long as the ground is not frozen. A layer of mulch made from leaves or straw makes harvesting easier on cold days.

## What's inside?

Parsnips contain significantly more carbohydrates than carrots. This is where their sweetness and satiating effect come from. They also provide fiber, potassium and folate. Parsley roots are less sweet and, like the parsley herb, contain essential oils that provide the spicy scent. Both roots are considered to be easily digestible and are therefore a good choice for sensitive stomachs in winter.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I eat parsnips raw?

Yes, young parsnips taste sweet and fresh when grated raw. Older specimens are often fibrous when raw.

### Why does my parsnip taste bitter?

It was usually stored with fruit or is old. Even very thick roots with a woody core can be bitter. Cut out the core and combine them with something sweet.

### Are parsnips suitable for baby food?

Yes. Parsnips are mild, slightly sweet and well tolerated. This is why they are often used as one of the first vegetables for babies. For babies, cook them without salt. Honey is taboo in the first year of life anyway because it can contain spores that are dangerous to infants.

### Can I use parsley root leaves?

Yes. Parsley root is a form of garden parsley that is grown primarily for its roots. Its leaves can be chopped like flat-leaf parsley and sprinkled over soups or potatoes. The leaves of the parsnip, on the other hand, do not belong in the kitchen.
