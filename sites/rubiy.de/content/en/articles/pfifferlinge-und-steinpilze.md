For many, mushrooms from the forest are the epitome of autumn. The first chanterelles appear in the markets as early as July, golden yellow and smelling of apricot. Porcini mushrooms, chestnut boletes and redcaps follow in August and September. A pan with fried wild mushrooms, a little butter, onions and parsley is one of the simplest and at the same time finest dishes of the season.

Store-bought mushrooms are safe. Those who collect themselves bear a great responsibility, because among the thousands of species of mushrooms in German forests there are deadly poisonous ones. This article focuses on the preparation of chanterelles and porcini mushrooms and gives a few basic rules for foragers. It does not replace a mushroom course or mushroom advice.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Only collect mushrooms that you know for sure. If you have the slightest doubt, have your finds checked by a mushroom advice center.
> - Clean chanterelles as dry as possible with a brush and knife. Only wash very dirty mushrooms briefly and dry them immediately.
> - Wild mushrooms are well cooked and not eaten raw.
> - You can reheat mushroom dishes if they are cooled quickly, stored in the refrigerator and heated well within a day.
> - Fresh mushrooms will last one to two days in the refrigerator in a paper bag or open container.

## Get to know chanterelles and porcini mushrooms

| | Chanterelle | Porcini mushroom |
|---|---|---|
| season | around July to October | around August to October |
| Appearance | Yolk yellow, funnel-shaped, with running ridges instead of lamellae | brown cap, whitish to yellowish sponge under the cap, thick, bulbous stem with light net |
| taste | peppery, slightly fruity | nutty, strong |
| Consistency | firm, somewhat chewy | Firm, very tender in young mushrooms |
| Preparation | fry, braise, cream sauce | frying, risotto, sauces, drying |

### Confusions

When it comes to chanterelles, the best-known mix-up is the false chanterelle. It is orange, but has true, thin, forked lamellae that are easily stripped off, while the true chanterelle has thicker, vein-like ridges that are firmly attached to the flesh. The false chanterelle is not considered highly poisonous, but can cause stomach problems. More dangerous, but rare in Germany, is the olive funnel, which grows in clumps on wood.

When it comes to porcini mushrooms, the gall bolete in particular spoils entire meals. It looks very similar to a porcini mushroom, but tastes so bitter that just one mushroom can make an entire pan inedible. It can be recognized by the dark web on the stem and tubes that turn pink with age.

These notes are not an aid to identification. In Germany there are mushroom experts who often offer free advice during the season. Use them, especially as a beginner.

## Rules for collecting

- **Only be sure to take what you know.** Even a single poisonous mushroom in the basket can be dangerous.
- **Take the mushrooms completely:** Carefully twist them out of the ground or cut them just above the ground if you know them for sure. Characteristics at the end of the stem are often crucial for identification.
- **Airy basket instead of plastic bag:** In bags, mushrooms get warm, sweat and spoil quickly.
- **Only young, firm specimens:** Old, spongy or maggot-infested mushrooms remain standing.
- **Note quantities:** Chanterelles, porcini mushrooms and some other edible mushrooms are protected under the Federal Species Protection Ordinance. You may collect them in small quantities for your own use. Collecting is usually completely forbidden in nature reserves.

If you suspect mushroom poisoning, for example if you have nausea, vomiting or diarrhea hours after a mushroom meal, contact the poison control center immediately or dial 112. Save leftovers from the meal and cleaning waste; they will help with identification.

## Clean and store

Mushrooms soak up water and no longer become crispy when fried, but instead cook in their own juice. Therefore, clean them as dry as possible:

1. Cut off the stem end that has soil and needles attached.
2. Brush away soil, sand, and needles with a mushroom brush or soft brush.
3. Cut out bruises and chewing marks.
4. Chanterelles often have sand between the ledges. If they are very dirty, wash them briefly in a bowl of cold water, toss them, remove them immediately and let them drain on a kitchen towel.
5. Cut the porcini mushrooms lengthwise and check whether they are maggot-like. For older porcini mushrooms, you can remove the soft sponge from under the cap.

Fresh mushrooms are delicate. Store them in a paper bag or open in a bowl with a kitchen towel over them in the vegetable compartment and use them within a day or two.

## Fry chanterelles properly

To give chanterelles roasted flavors, you need a large pan and high heat. Fry them without fat or with very little oil over high heat. First they release water. Let it evaporate until the mushrooms begin to sizzle in the dry pan. Only then do you add butter, onions or shallots and continue frying until the mushrooms are golden brown. In total this takes about ten minutes. Add salt at the very end, otherwise the mushrooms will absorb water.

Cut the porcini mushrooms into five to seven millimeter thick slices and fry them in butter or oil on both sides until golden brown. You need about six to eight minutes.

## Creamed chanterelles with bread dumplings

For 4 people:

**Bread dumplings**

- 300 g stale rolls (about 6 pieces), cut into small cubes
- 250 ml milk
- 1 onion
- 1 tbsp butter
- 3 eggs (size M)
- 1 bunch of flat-leaf parsley
- 1 teaspoon salt, some freshly grated nutmeg
- If necessary, 1 to 2 tablespoons of breadcrumbs

**Cream chanterelles**

- 500 g chanterelles
- 2 shallots or 1 small onion
- 2 tbsp butter
- 1 tbsp oil
- 200 ml cream
- 100 ml vegetable broth
- 1 splash of lemon juice
- Salt, pepper
- ½ bunch of parsley

### Preparation

1. Place the bread cubes in a bowl. Heat the milk until it is lukewarm, pour it over it and let it steep for 15 minutes.
2. Dice the onion finely and fry it in the butter until translucent. Chop the parsley.
3. Add the onion, parsley, eggs, salt and nutmeg to the rolls and knead everything well with your hands. The dough should be malleable and not stick to your hands. If it is too wet, add breadcrumbs.
4. With wet hands, form eight dumplings. Bring plenty of salted water to the boil in a large pot, reduce the heat and let the dumplings simmer in the simmering, not bubbling water for 20 minutes. Make a test dumpling beforehand: If it falls apart, add some more breadcrumbs to the dough.
5. In the meantime, clean the chanterelles. Halve or quarter large specimens. Dice the shallots.
6. Heat the oil in a large pan and fry the chanterelles over high heat until the liquid released has evaporated. Add butter and shallots and continue frying for three to four minutes.
7. Deglaze with broth, pour in the cream and let the sauce simmer over medium heat for three to five minutes until it is slightly creamy.
8. Season with salt, pepper and lemon juice and fold in the chopped parsley.
9. Lift the dumplings out of the water with a slotted spoon and serve them with the creamed chanterelles.

## Tagliatelle with porcini mushrooms

If there are only a few porcini mushrooms in the basket, they are enough for a delicious pasta dish. Fry 250 g sliced ​​porcini mushrooms in two tablespoons of butter until golden brown and add a finely chopped garlic clove and some thyme. In the meantime, cook 400 g of tagliatelle in salted water until al dente. Save a cup of pasta water before draining. Mix the pasta with the mushrooms, another piece of butter and enough pasta water to create a slightly creamy sauce. Serve with salt, pepper, chopped parsley and freshly grated hard cheese. The starchy pasta water binds the butter into a sauce, without any cream.

## Cultivated mushrooms as an alternative

Not everyone has access to wild mushrooms, and the season is short. Cultivated mushrooms are available all year round, they are safe and can be prepared using the same techniques. Brown mushrooms have more flavor than white ones, king oyster mushrooms are firm and reminiscent of porcini mushrooms when fried, oyster mushrooms become crispy in the pan, and shiitakes bring a strong, spicy note. A mixture of cultivated mushrooms is also suitable for the cream sauce for bread dumplings. Like chanterelles, fry them in small batches over high heat so that they don't cook in their own juice.

## Reheat and preserve

The old rule that mushroom dishes should not be reheated dates back to the days without refrigerators. Mushrooms contain a lot of protein and water and spoil quickly in the heat. Today the following applies: Cool leftovers quickly, put them in the refrigerator and heat them vigorously to at least 70 degrees within a day. Then warming up is harmless.

Porcini mushrooms are easy to dry. Cut them into slices and dry them in the dehydrator or oven at 40 to 50 degrees until they break. We explain how this works in the article about [drying fruit and herbs](/kueche/obst-und-kraeuter-trocknen/). Chanterelles become tough when dried. It's better to fry them briefly and then freeze them. When frozen raw, they often become bitter.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I eat porcini mushrooms raw?

We generally advise against using wild mushrooms. Raw mushrooms are more difficult to digest, and wild mushrooms can carry germs or parasite eggs that can only be safely killed by heating.

### Are forest mushrooms radioactively contaminated?

In some regions of southern Germany, some species of wild mushrooms are still contaminated with radioactive cesium since the Chernobyl accident. How high the contamination is depends largely on the type of fungus and the location. The Federal Office for Radiation Protection considers the consumption of normal quantities to be harmless and advises not to eat wild mushrooms from heavily contaminated areas in large quantities.

### Why do my chanterelles taste bitter?

This could be due to mushrooms being too old, freezing them raw, or over-frying onions. If a dish with porcini mushrooms is very bitter, it is usually caused by gall bolete.

### How many mushrooms do I need per person?

As a main dish, such as dumplings or noodles, you should expect 125 to 150 grams of cleaned mushrooms per person, and as a side dish with around 100 grams. When cleaning, ten to twenty percent is lost, depending on how dirty it is.
