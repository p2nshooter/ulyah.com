Sauerkraut is one of the oldest long-life foods in Central Europe. Long before refrigerators and cans existed, basements had large earthenware pots full of herbs that provided vitamins throughout the winter. Sailors took it with them on long voyages because it helped against scurvy. Today, fermentation is experiencing a renaissance, and homemade sauerkraut is the best way to get started: all you need is cabbage, salt and a few weeks of patience.

The best time for this is in autumn, when the white cabbage is harvested. The heads are fresh, firm and juicy, and the cabbage is ready in time for the winter season.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - All you need is white cabbage and salt: 15 to 20 grams of salt per kilo of cleaned cabbage, i.e. 1.5 to 2 percent.
> - The cabbage is finely sliced ​​and kneaded with the salt until a lot of juice comes out. This juice is the brine.
> - The cabbage must remain under the brine throughout the fermentation process. Oxygen is the enemy.
> - The herb ferments at 18 to 22 degrees for the first five to seven days, then it ripens at a cooler temperature. After three to six weeks it is ready.
> - A white, flat film is usually harmless yeast. Furry, colored mold means: The herb is being thrown away.

## Herb with history

The example of James Cook shows how valuable sauerkraut was when traveling. In the 18th century, the British sailor had barrels of sauerkraut brought on board because fresh vegetables were missing on months-long voyages and the herb provides vitamin C. In Germany, cabbage cultivation remains strongly regionally rooted to this day. Dithmarschen on the North Sea coast is considered one of the largest contiguous cabbage growing areas in Europe, and the pointed Filderkraut grows on the Fildern near Stuttgart, which is registered as a protected geographical indication and is traditionally made into sauerkraut.

## What happens during fermentation

Lactic acid bacteria live on every cabbage leaf. Mixing the cabbage with salt and sealing it airtight under its own brine creates an environment in which they multiply better than most unwanted germs. The salt slows down putrefactive bacteria and draws water out of the cells. The lactic acid bacteria ferment the sugar in the cabbage to produce lactic acid. The pH value drops, the herb becomes acidic, and in this acidic environment spoilage pathogens hardly stand a chance.

Unlike preserving, fermenting does not involve heating. The shelf life is due solely to salt, acid and the exclusion of oxygen. That's why it's so important that the cabbage always remains completely covered by liquid.

## Equipment

- **Fermentation vessel:** A classic fermentation pot made of stoneware with a water channel and weighting stones is ideal for larger quantities. To start with, large screw-top or swing-top jars with a capacity of one to two liters are sufficient.
- **Cabbage slicer or vegetable slicer:** For fine, even stripes. A sharp knife also works, but takes longer.
- **Large bowl** for kneading.
- **Masher:** A cabbage masher, a potato masher or your fist.
- **Weights:** Glass or ceramic fermentation weights, a small jar that fits into the opening, or a freezer bag filled with water.

All equipment and vessels should be clean. They don't have to be sterile; rinsing them thoroughly with hot water is enough.

## Basic recipe

For about three one liter glasses:

- 2 kg white cabbage, cleaned and weighed (approximately a large head of 2.5 kg)
- 30 to 40 g of salt without additives
- 1 tsp caraway
- 10 juniper berries
- 2 bay leaves

Many people use salt without iodine and anti-caking agents because these additives can affect fermentation. In practice, iodized table salt usually works too.

### Step by step

1. **Prepare the cabbage:** Remove the outer, wilted leaves. Put two or three nice, large leaves aside; they will serve as a cover later. Quarter the cabbage and cut out the stalk.
2. **Slicing:** Plane the cabbage into fine strips two to three millimeters wide.
3. **Salt and knead:** Weigh the shredded cabbage and calculate the salt: 15 to 20 grams per kilo. Put the cabbage and salt in a large bowl and knead everything vigorously with your hands for about ten minutes. The cabbage becomes soft and translucent and plenty of juice comes out. When you squeeze a handful of cabbage and liquid comes out, it's ready. If necessary, let it rest for 20 minutes and then knead again.
4. **Seasoning:** Mix in caraway, juniper and bay leaf.
5. **Layering:** Pour the cabbage into the jars in portions and press each layer with the masher or your fist until juice rises to the top and there are no more air bubbles between the strips. Leave at least three centimeters of space at the top.
6. **Cover:** Place one of the reserved cabbage leaves on top of the cabbage and weigh it down. The brine must cover the herb at least one to two centimeters high. If the juice is not enough, add some brine: 20 grams of salt in a liter of boiled, cooled water.
7. **Closing:** Screw the lid on loosely or use swing-top jars with a rubber ring through which gas can escape. Place the glasses on a plate or in a bowl because brine often overflows during fermentation.

## Fermentation and temperature

Fermentation takes place in two phases:

| phase | Duration | Temperature | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| primary fermentation | 5-7 days | 18-22°C | Bubbles form, the brine rises, the herb becomes sour |
| Secondary fermentation and maturation | 2-5 weeks | 12-16°C | the aroma rounds out, the herb becomes milder and crumbly |

In the first few days, gas bubbles form and the brine can bubble and overflow. During this time, open screw-top jars briefly every day to release the pressure and press the herb back under the brine. In fermentation pots, the water channel does this automatically. Make sure that there is always water in the channel.

After the main fermentation, place the jars cooler, for example in a cellar or a cool room. After a total of three weeks you can try it for the first time. The herb should smell and taste pleasantly sour. The longer it ages, the more sour and milder the cabbage flavor it becomes. If it's to your liking, put the jars tightly closed in the fridge. It stays there for several months.

## Kahm yeast or mold?

A coating sometimes forms on the surface of fermented foods. It is important to classify it correctly:

- **Kahm yeast** is a white to cream-colored, flat, often wrinkled or wrinkled film on the brine. It is caused by contact with oxygen and is considered harmless to health, but can distort the taste. Skim it off, wipe the edge of the glass clean and make sure that the herb is well under the brine again.
- **Mold** is furry, fluffy and usually colored: green, blue, black or pink. It grows in round patches. There is no compromise here. The entire glass is thrown away because mold can grow invisibly into the herb and form toxins.

Even if the herb smells foul, is slimy, or turns pink, brown, or gray, it belongs in the trash. Good sauerkraut smells sour and fresh, sometimes a little sulfurous in the first few days, but never putrid.

## Use sauerkraut

Sauerkraut tastes fresh and crunchy raw and contains live lactic acid bacteria. As a salad, mix it with grated apple, carrot, a little oil and chives.

When cooked it becomes mild and crumbly. For four people, sauté an onion in two tablespoons of lard or butter, add 750 grams of sauerkraut, a grated apple, a bay leaf, a few juniper berries and 200 ml of stock or white wine and let everything simmer covered for 30 to 45 minutes. Bind the liquid with a grated raw potato. This goes well with mashed potatoes and bratwurst, Kassler or Schupfnudeln.

## Krautschupfnudeln: a Swabian classic

For four people, fry 100 g of diced bacon in a large pan and add a finely diced onion. Take both out and fry 600 g Schupfnudeln from the refrigerator shelf or homemade in two tablespoons of clarified butter until golden brown. Add 400 g of well-drained, cooked sauerkraut, bacon and onion and fry everything for a few more minutes, turning, until the cabbage has a light roasted aroma. Season with pepper, a little caraway and a little salt. The dish works vegetarian with roasted onions and a piece of butter. A green salad goes well with it.

## Choucroute from Alsace

Just across the border, sauerkraut is a feast. For the choucroute garnie, the herb is slowly braised with onions, juniper and dry white wine, traditionally Riesling. Various sausages, smoked bacon and smoked pork are cooked on it, along with boiled potatoes and mustard. The dish shows how well the herb's acidity balances hearty ingredients.

## Don't throw away the brine

The juice left over when drained is too good to throw down the drain. When drunk cold, sauerkraut juice tastes refreshingly sour and can add a subtle acidity to dressings, potato salad and beetroot salad. A shot of it in the next fermentation glass also brings along the appropriate lactic acid bacteria and can accelerate fermentation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why is my herb too soft?

Common causes are too little salt, temperatures that are too high during fermentation or cabbage that is too old and has little water. Stick to the amount of salt and store the herb in a cooler place after the first week.

### Can I also ferment other vegetables like this?

Yes. Red cabbage, pointed cabbage, carrots, beets and radishes can also be fermented with salt. Vegetables that release little of their own juice, such as beans, cucumbers or carrot sticks, are placed in a brine with two to three percent salt.

### Is homemade sauerkraut suitable for everyone?

For most people, yes. Those who suffer from histamine intolerance often have difficulty tolerating fermented foods. If you have to eat a low-salt diet, you should keep an eye on the amount.

### Do I have to wash purchased sauerkraut?

Only if it's too sour for you. When rinsing, aroma, some of the lactic acid bacteria and water-soluble vitamins are also lost. It is better to braise very sour cabbage with a little apple, onion and broth; this reduces the acidity all by itself.
