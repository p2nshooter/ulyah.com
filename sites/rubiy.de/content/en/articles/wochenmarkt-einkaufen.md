The weekly market is the most beautiful seasonal calendar there is. What's in the boxes shows you pretty much what's currently growing. In May there are piles of asparagus and rhubarb, in July currants and cherries shine, in October there are piles of pumpkins, cabbages and apples. If you go to the market regularly, you get to know the seasons with all your senses and discover seasonal cuisine almost automatically.

Nevertheless, not everything sold at the market is automatically regional, fresh or cheaper than in the store. With a little knowledge, you can shop more specifically and go home with goods that fit your weekly plan.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Both producers who grow their own and traders who buy in sell on the market. Ask where the goods come from.
> - The term “regional” is not protected by law. Concrete information about origin, the regional window or the conversation at the stand are more reliable.
> - You can recognize freshness by firm, plump fruits, fresh greens, juicy interfaces and the scent.
> - The selection is largest early on the market day, and some prices are lower shortly before the market closes.
> - Plan roughly what you will cook in the next few days and buy delicate items for the first few days first.

## Producer or dealer?

At a typical German weekly market, there are businesses that sell their own fruit and vegetables and traders who buy goods from the wholesale market. Both have their place: the dealer offers lemons, bananas and peppers in winter, the producer the carrots that he harvested himself the day before yesterday.

How you can recognize producers:

- The offering is smaller and clearly follows the season. There are no pumpkins in June and no outdoor tomatoes in January.
- The produce is more uneven, with soil on the carrots, apples of different sizes or heads of lettuce of different sizes.
- The name of a farm or a nursery with a location is written on signs or on the wagon.
- The sellers can tell you when it was harvested, what variety it is and how it is grown.

Traders have to indicate the country of origin for many types of fruit and vegetables. However, a sign with “Germany” says nothing about whether the goods come from the farm around the corner or from a growing area 600 kilometers away. If regionality is important to you, ask directly: “Is this yours?” Most market people are happy to answer questions honestly.

## Seals and terms

| term | What it means |
|---|---|
| Organic, eco, EU organic logo | Protected by law. The goods come from controlled organic cultivation in accordance with EU law. The company must provide a control number. |
| Growing associations (e.g. Bioland, Demeter, Naturland) | Organic goods with stricter guidelines than the EU standard. |
| Regional, from the region, from here | Not legally defined. Can be a radius of 30 or 300 kilometers. |
| Regional window | Voluntary labeling with specific information about the region of origin and the proportion of regional ingredients. |
| From controlled cultivation, close to nature | Advertising terms without legal meaning. |
| Country of origin | Mandatory with fresh fruit and vegetables. Name the country, not the region. |

Many small businesses work organically without being certified because monitoring costs money and bureaucracy. However, they are then not allowed to describe their goods as “organic”. Here, too, the conversation helps.

## Recognize freshness

Each vegetable shows how fresh it is in its own way. Some general signs:

- **Leafy green:** When it comes to carrots, radishes, kohlrabi or beetroot, the green indicates the freshness. You want it to be crisp, not limp or yellow. However, you will soon remove it at home because it removes moisture from the vegetables.
- **Cutting:** For lettuce, asparagus, leeks and broccoli, the cut surface should be light and moist. Brown, dried spots indicate longer storage.
- **Firmness:** Cucumbers, zucchini, peppers and eggplants should be plump and firm, the skin shiny.
- **Fragrance:** Ripe tomatoes, strawberries, melons and apricots are fragrant. If they don't smell like anything, they usually don't taste like much either.
- **Weight:** Citrus fruits, cabbages and celery are intended to be heavy in the hand. Lightweight specimens are often dried out or hollow inside.

Some market stalls allow you to try fruit. Ask beforehand instead of just grabbing it. And please don't squeeze every tomato and peach to find the ripest one. Bruises spoil the goods for everyone who comes after you.

### Cosmetic flaws are not a defect

At the market you often find vegetables that would be rejected in the supermarket: crooked cucumbers, two-legged carrots, apples with scabs. Such blemishes do not affect the taste. Many producers sell this product cheaper as a second choice. It is ideal for cooking, baking and preserving.

## Plan the purchase

If you go to the market without a plan, you can easily come home with too much. The abundance is tempting, and on Thursday half the chard wilts in the fridge. A simple plan helps:

1. Think beforehand about how many days a week you cook at home.
2. Write down two or three dishes that you're sure you'll want to cook, leaving room for impulse purchases.
3. Divide the vegetables according to their shelf life: lettuce, spinach, berries, mushrooms and asparagus for the first two days; broccoli, cauliflower, fennel and zucchini for midweek; Carrots, cabbage, beetroot, potatoes, pumpkin and apples for the weekend and beyond.
4. Buy delicate items last so they don't get crushed at the bottom of the basket.

## Good questions at the stand

Talking to the market people is the real treasure of the weekly market. These questions will get you further:

- **“What’s particularly good right now?”** Nobody knows the goods as well as the people who grow them or buy them every day.
- **“Which variety is that?”** Especially with apples, potatoes and tomatoes, the variety determines their use.
- **“How long will this last?”** This will tell you whether something should be eaten today or wait until the weekend.
- **“Do you have a second choice?”** It is ideal and cheaper for compotes, soups and jams.
- **“Can I pre-order for next week?”** It’s worth it for larger quantities for cooking or for parties.

## A sample basket for a week in October

This is what shopping for two people can look like that lasts through the week without leftovers:

| Purchasing | This becomes |
|---|---|
| a small Hokkaido pumpkin, onions, ginger | Pumpkin soup on Saturday, the rest roasted for a salad |
| 1 kg of predominantly waxy potatoes | Jacket potatoes on Sunday, fried potatoes on Monday |
| a head of red cabbage, sour apples | Apple red cabbage for two days |
| Lamb's lettuce, walnuts | Salad on Sunday while it's fresh |
| Mushrooms from the mushroom stand | Mushroom pan with bread dumplings on Tuesday |
| Carrots, leeks, celery | Midweek vegetable stew, sections for broth |

The delicate ingredients such as lamb's lettuce and mushrooms come to the table first; pumpkin, cabbage, roots and apples wait patiently. How pumpkin soup works can be found in the article about [pumpkin soup and pumpkin varieties](/kueche/kuerbissuppe-und-kuerbissorten/).

## To the market with children

For children, the weekly market is a living classroom. Let them choose a vegetable they don't know yet and prepare it together. Give them a small amount to pay for themselves and ask why there are no strawberries from the farm in winter. In this way, they learn almost incidentally where food comes from, what a season means and that a crooked carrot tastes just as good as a straight one.

## The right equipment

- **Basket or sturdy bag:** Preferably with a solid base so that eggs and berries can lie on top.
- **Cloth bags and tins:** For loose herbs, mushrooms, olives, cheese or meat. Many stands are happy to fill containers you bring with you upon request.
- **Cooler bag:** In the summer, when you buy fish, meat or cheese and then have errands to run.
- **Small change:** Many stalls now accept card payments, but not all.

## When to shop?

The selection is greatest early in the morning. If you want certain specialties, such as the first chanterelles, special types of tomatoes or fresh eggs from the small farm, you should be there at the opening. At lunchtime, shortly before dismantling, some dealers sell leftover goods cheaper so that they don't have to be packed up again. This is worth it for robust vegetables and fruit that you want to cook straight away.

Another advantage of the weekly market: you can buy exactly the amount you need. Half a head of celery, three carrots, two apples, a handful of parsley. This saves money and food.

## What the market tells you about the season

Next time, pay attention to how the offer changes from week to week. When strawberries are scarce and expensive, their season begins or ends. When suddenly every stand offers zucchini for little money, it's peak season and it's time to stock up. When cabbage, leeks and root vegetables dominate in autumn, the storage season begins. It is precisely these rhythms that make seasonal cooking so varied.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the weekly market more expensive than the supermarket?

That depends on the product. Seasonal goods in high season are often cheaper or the same price on the market. Out of season, for special varieties and organic goods, you pay more. You buy exactly the amount you need and throw away less.

### Can I trade?

Haggling is unusual in German markets. However, if you buy larger quantities for cooking, you can ask for a box price. Many stalls offer preserved goods, such as tomatoes, plums or cucumbers, at a cheaper price if you order them in advance.

### How do I know if eggs from the market are fresh?

Eggs that are sold on the market usually have a producer code that indicates the type of farming, country and farm. Very small businesses are sometimes exempt from this and must then provide their name and address. The best way to recognize freshness at home is in a glass of water: a fresh egg sinks and stays flat, an older one sits upright.
