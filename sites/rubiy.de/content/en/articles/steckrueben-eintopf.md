The turnip doesn't have it easy. For many older people it brings back memories of hunger and hardship, for younger people none at all because they have never eaten one. It is an excellent winter vegetable: mild, slightly sweet, somewhat reminiscent of kohlrabi and carrots, inexpensive and can be stored for months. In northern Germany, where it is also called wruke, turnip or butter turnip, it has never completely lost its place in the kitchen. A steaming turnip stew with potatoes and smoked pork is one of the dishes that people look forward to when it gets wet and gray outside.

Turnips are available from October to March. They taste best when freshly harvested, but they last a long time when stored in a cool place, so they bridge the low-vegetable months until spring.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Turnips are in season from October to March. Yellow-fleshed varieties taste sweeter and milder than white ones.

> - Whether served as mashed potatoes, baked fries or raw, turnips show how versatile they are.

## Why the turnip has a bad reputation

During the First World War, in the winter of 1916/17, the potato harvest in Germany suffered a severe collapse due to moisture and disease. At the same time, the naval blockade exacerbated the supply situation. The turnip, until then mainly fodder for livestock, became a staple food. They were available as soup, porridge, bread, jam and coffee substitutes, often without fat or other ingredients. This winter went down in history as the turnip winter. Even after the Second World War, turnips were once again an emergency food in many families.

It is understandable that it is associated with this, but it is a shame. With butter, broth, herbs and the right accompaniments, the turnip tastes completely different to the bland porridge of the emergency years. Today it is being rediscovered in many restaurants, as puree, chips or braised vegetables.

## What is a turnip?

The turnip is a cross between cabbage and turnips and is closely related to rapeseed. It forms a large, round tuber that is purple or greenish above and yellowish to cream below. For cooking, it is best to use yellow-fleshed varieties, which are sweeter, more tender and more aromatic. White-fleshed varieties are primarily grown as fodder beets.

Turnips usually weigh between 500 grams and two kilos. Medium-sized specimens around one kilo are ideal: they are still tender and not woody. When purchasing, look for a firm, smooth shell with no soft spots or cracks. A turnip should be heavy in the hand for its size. Light tubers are often spongy or hollow inside.

## Store

A whole, uninjured turnip will last for several weeks to months in a cool, dark cellar at 1 to 5 degrees. In the vegetable compartment of the refrigerator, wrapped in a damp cloth or in a perforated bag, it lasts about two to three weeks. Wrap cut beets in foil and use them within three to four days. Diced and blanched briefly, about three minutes, turnips freeze well.

## Peel and cut the turnips

The peel of the turnip is thick and sometimes covered with a thin layer of wax that prevents it from drying out. A peeler won't get you very far.

1. Place a damp cloth under the cutting board to prevent it from slipping.
2. Cut a slice off the top and bottom so that the turnip is stable.
3. Using a large, sharp knife, cut the rind into strips from top to bottom, similar to a melon. Cut generously until only the yellow flesh is visible.
4. Halve the beet, place the halves on the cut surface and cut them into slices, then into sticks or cubes.

## North German turnip stew

For 4 to 6 people:

- Weighed 1 kg of turnip, peeled
- 500 g floury potatoes
- 3 carrots (about 300 g)
- 2 onions
- 2 tablespoons clarified butter or rapeseed oil
- 1.5 l meat or vegetable broth
- 600 g Kassler in one piece or 4 Mettenden
- 1 bay leaf
- 2 tsp dried marjoram
- Salt, pepper, a pinch of sugar
- 1 bunch of flat-leaf parsley

### Preparation

1. Cut the turnip, potatoes and carrots into cubes about two centimeters in size. Dice the onions.
2. Heat the fat in a large pot and sauté the onions until translucent. Add the turnip cubes and sauté them for five minutes, stirring, until they slightly color in some places. This increases the sweetness.
3. Pour in the broth, place the smoked pork in one piece and the bay leaf and bring everything to the boil. Cover and simmer the stew over a low heat for 20 minutes.
4. Add the potatoes and carrots and cook for another 20 to 25 minutes until the vegetables are soft. If you use Mettenden, place them in the pot for the last 15 minutes to prevent them from bursting.
5. Remove the smoked pork, cut it into bite-sized pieces and return it to the pot. Remove the bay leaf.
6. Mash some of the vegetables directly in the pot with a potato masher. The floury potatoes bind the stew and make it creamy.
7. Season to taste with marjoram, salt, pepper and a pinch of sugar. Rub the marjoram between your palms before adding it to release more flavor.
8. Sprinkle with chopped parsley and serve.

Like most stews, this one tastes even better the second day. It will keep for three days in the fridge and freezes well. For a vegetarian version, leave out the meat, use a strong vegetable broth and add a teaspoon of smoked paprika powder and finally fried smoked tofu cubes.

## Turnip mash

A puree made from turnips and potatoes is a wonderful accompaniment to bratwurst, meatballs, braised meat or fried fish.

For 4 people:

- 600 g turnip, diced
- 400 g floury potatoes, diced
- 1 carrot, diced
- 50g butter
- 100 ml warm milk
- Salt, pepper, nutmeg

Boil turnips, potatoes and carrots in salted water until soft, about 20 minutes. The turnips take a little longer than the potatoes, so add the turnip cubes to the water five minutes before the others. Pour off the water and let the vegetables steam briefly. Mash it coarsely with butter and milk and season with salt, pepper and nutmeg. A dollop of mustard or roasted onions on top add extra flavor.

## Turnip fries from the oven

Cut a peeled turnip into finger-thick sticks. Mix them with two tablespoons of oil, a teaspoon of salt, half a teaspoon of paprika and a tablespoon of cornstarch, which creates a crispier surface. Spread the sticks a little apart on a baking tray lined with baking paper and bake them at 220 degrees top and bottom heat for 30 to 35 minutes, turning halfway through. They aren't quite as crispy as potato fries, but they taste spicy-sweet and go well with herb quark.

## Turnip curry with coconut milk

The mild sweetness of the turnip goes surprisingly well with curry spices. For four people, sauté an onion, two cloves of garlic and a piece of grated ginger in two tablespoons of oil, stir in two teaspoons of curry powder or a tablespoon of yellow curry paste and add 800 g of diced turnips and a diced sweet potato or carrot. Deglaze with 400 ml coconut milk and 300 ml vegetable stock and let everything simmer, covered, for 20 to 25 minutes until the vegetables are soft. Finally, stir in a handful of spinach or peas and season with salt, lime juice and fresh coriander or parsley. This goes well with rice or flatbread.

## The turnip in other countries

What was long considered emergency food in Germany is a valued holiday vegetable elsewhere. In Scotland, the national dish haggis includes “neeps and tatties”, mashed turnips and potatoes. In Finland, at Christmas there is a turnip casserole on the table, the lanttulaatikko, sweetly spiced and baked in the oven. In Sweden, the puree made from turnips and potatoes is called rotmos and is served with cured meat. In Great Britain the turnip is known as swede because it came there from Sweden in the 18th century.

## What's in the turnip

The turnip is low in calories, provides fiber, potassium and a good portion of vitamin C, which is particularly valuable in winter. It owes its yellow flesh to carotenoids. Because it can be stored for a long time, it was an important source of vitamins for the dark months in times without a refrigerator.

## Raw turnip

You can also eat young, tender turnips raw. Finely grated, with a grated apple, lemon juice, a little oil, salt and roasted walnuts, it makes a crunchy winter salad that is a little reminiscent of kohlrabi.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does my rutabaga taste bitter or spicy?

Rutabaga is a cruciferous vegetable and, like cabbage and radish, contains mustard oil compounds. Older beets or beets that have been stored too warm are spicier. Vigorous sautéing, broth instead of water and a pinch of sugar balance this out.

### Can I eat the leaves?

Turnips are almost always sold without leaves. Young leaves from your own garden can be prepared like chard or stem jam.

### What is the difference between turnip and turnip?

Like the Teltower turnip, the May turnip belongs to the edible turnip family, a different subspecies. It is significantly smaller, usually has a white peel, often purple on top, white flesh and tastes slightly spicy, a little like radish. It is harvested in spring and autumn and has a limited shelf life. The turnip is larger, yellow-fleshed, milder and a typical storage vegetable for the winter.

### How much rutabaga do I need per person?

As a side dish or mashed dish, you should expect around 200 to 250 grams of peeled turnip per person. When the thick shell is peeled, approximately 15 to 20 percent of the weight is lost. A medium-sized beet weighing one kilo is enough for three to four portions.
