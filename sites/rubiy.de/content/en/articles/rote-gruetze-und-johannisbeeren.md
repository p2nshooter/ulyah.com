Currants are one of the shrubs that are found in almost every old cottage garden. They get their name from St. John's Day on June 24th, around which the first berries ripen. Depending on the variety, the season lasts until August. Red currants are the most common, but there are white currants, which taste milder and sweeter, and black currants, which have their own unique, tart-spicy aroma and are known by the French name cassis.

Their close relatives, gooseberries, ripen around the same time. Both berries are sour, aromatic and rich in pectin, which makes them ideal ingredients for jelly, compote, cakes and even for Rote Grütze, probably the most famous summer dessert in northern Germany.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Currants are harvested from June to August, gooseberries usually from June to July.
> - Strip the currants from the vines with a fork. Wash them beforehand while they are still attached to the vines.
> - Unwashed currants last about a week in the refrigerator, and frozen for several months.
> - Red fruit jelly is made from mixed red berries and cherries cooked with juice and thickened with starch.
> - Traditionally it includes vanilla sauce or liquid cream.

## Currants: red, white and black

| variety | taste | Usage |
|---|---|---|
| Red currant | fresh-sour, fruity | Jelly, groats, cake, juice, compote |
| White currant | milder, sweeter, less acid | snacking raw, fruit salad, desserts |
| Blackcurrant | tart, spicy, intense | Jelly, liqueur, juice, jam |

Blackcurrants are known for their high vitamin C content. It is significantly higher than that of lemons. Raw, they taste too bitter for many people; when cooked, they develop a full, almost wine-like aroma.

### Harvest and strip

Currants are ripe when they are full color and fall easily from the vine. With red currants, the berries at the top of the panicle ripen first, the lower ones a little later. If you want to use them for jelly, you should harvest them a little earlier because then the pectin content is higher. If you let them hang longer to snack on, they become sweeter.

Harvest the whole stalks and wash them in a bowl of cold water while the berries are still on. This way they don't burst or get soaked. Drain them in a colander. Then hold each panicle by the stem end and pull it through the tines of a fork. The berries fall into the bowl, the stems remain behind.

## Gooseberries

Gooseberries come in green, yellow and red varieties. Harvested unripe, when they are still hard and green, they are particularly suitable for compotes, cakes and jams because they then contain a lot of acid and pectin. When ripe they are softer, sweeter and can be eaten raw.

Before using, pluck off the stem and flower base, preferably with small scissors. To make compote, cook 500 grams of gooseberries with 100 ml of water and 80 to 120 grams of sugar for about five minutes until the first berries burst. A piece of vanilla pod or some grated lemon peel rounds off the aroma. The compote tastes good with semolina pudding, pancakes and quark dishes.

## Store and freeze

Currants on the panicle will keep unwashed in a shallow dish in the refrigerator for about a week. Gooseberries a little longer. To freeze, spread the washed, picked and well-dried berries on a tray, freeze them for two hours and then put them in bags. This means they don't stick together and can be removed in portions. Frozen berries are ideal for red fruit jelly, cakes and smoothies.

## Red jelly

Rote Grütze comes from northern Germany and Denmark. Originally the fruit juice was made with groats, i.e. coarsely ground grain, or with sago, hence the name. Today we usually use cornstarch. A mixture of different red fruits is important so that acidity, sweetness and aroma are balanced.

For 6 servings:

- 300 g red currants
- 300g raspberries
- 400 g sour cherries, pitted (fresh or from a jar)
- 500 ml cherry juice or red currant juice
- 80 to 120 g of sugar, depending on the acidity of the fruit
- 1 vanilla pod or 1 packet of vanilla sugar
- 1 strip of untreated lemon peel
- 50 g cornstarch

### Preparation

1. Strip the currants from the vines. Pick the raspberries. If you are using fresh cherries, pit the cherries and collect the juice. Drain cherries from the jar and use the juice as part of the liquid.
2. Take about 100 ml of the juice and stir the cornstarch in it until smooth.
3. Bring the remaining juice to the boil with the sugar, the slit vanilla pod and the lemon zest.
4. Add the currants and cherries and simmer for two to three minutes until the currants begin to burst.
5. Stir in the mixed starch and let everything boil vigorously while stirring until the groats thicken and become clear.
6. Remove the pot from the heat, remove the vanilla pod and lemon peel and carefully fold in the raspberries. They only cook briefly in the hot mixture and thus retain their shape.
7. Pour the groats into a bowl or portion glasses and let them cool. Then let it solidify in the fridge for at least three hours.

Red jelly will keep covered in the refrigerator for three to four days. Taste the mixture before thickening and adjust the amount of sugar. Currants and sour cherries can be very sour, while raspberries and sweet cherries bring more sweetness.

### A look at Denmark

In Denmark the dessert is called Rødgrød med fløde, red jelly with cream. The name is notoriously difficult to pronounce for non-Danes and is considered an affectionate tongue twister that guests often ask guests to repeat. The Danish version is usually served with liquid cream, often with strawberries and rhubarb in the fruit mixture. North German and Danish groats are closely related, and the recipes become blurred at the border in northern Schleswig-Holstein.

## Homemade vanilla sauce

For 6 servings:

- 500 ml milk
- 1 vanilla bean
- 3 egg yolks
- 50g sugar
- 1 level tablespoon of cornstarch (10 g)

1. Slit the vanilla pod lengthwise, scrape out the pulp and boil the milk, pulp and pod. Remove the pot from the heat and let the milk steep for ten minutes. Remove the pod.
2. Mix egg yolks, sugar and cornstarch in a bowl until smooth.
3. Pour the hot milk into the egg yolks in a thin stream while stirring.
4. Return everything to the pot and heat the sauce over medium heat, stirring constantly with a wooden spoon or whisk, until it thickens slightly. You don't want it to boil anymore, otherwise the egg yolk will curdle. The sauce is ready when it forms a thin film on the back of a spoon through which you can trace a clear line with your finger.
5. Pour the sauce through a sieve and let it cool. To prevent a skin from forming, place cling film directly on the surface.

The cornstarch stabilizes the sauce and makes it less prone to curdling. If you like it classic, leave it out and heat it up particularly carefully. Instead of vanilla sauce, you can also use liquid, unbeaten cream, vanilla ice cream or a dollop of Greek yogurt.

## Currant juice from the steam juicer

Anyone who has a bush in their garden often harvests more than is necessary for groats and cakes. A steam juicer makes juice with little work. The berries don't even have to be stripped from the panicles: washed, they go into the fruit basket with their stems, where the hot juice collects underneath. After about 45 to 60 minutes, most of it has come out. Pour the juice directly into clean, hot-rinsed bottles and close them immediately. Unsweetened, it is suitable as a basis for jelly, grits and spritzers. If you want to drink it straight, sweeten it with a little sugar before bottling or add water later.

## Currants and gooseberries in the garden

Berry bushes are easy to care for and produce for many years. Correct pruning is important, and it differs: red and white currants mainly grow on two- to three-year-old shoots. Blackcurrants grow best on annual wood. In all cases, after harvesting, the oldest, dark shoots should be cut out close to the ground so that the bush remains light and young shoots grow back.

The most common plague on gooseberries is American gooseberry powdery mildew, a white, later brown coating on shoots and fruits. Mildew-resistant varieties and an airy cut prevent this. For the balcony, there are currants and gooseberries as standard stems that thrive in a large container and bring the harvest to a comfortable height.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I cook red jelly with frozen fruit?

Yes very good. Add the frozen fruit directly to the boiling juice. Because they release juice as they thaw, you may need a little less liquid or a little more starch.

### Why isn't my red jelly setting?

Most of the time the starch was not boiled long enough. It has to boil once so that it binds. However, stirring for too long after cooking can weaken the bond again.

### Are currant seeds a problem?

No. They are small and edible. For particularly fine grits or jelly, you can strain the currants through a sieve beforehand.

### Can I cook the groats without sugar?

Yes, but the acidity of the currants and sour cherries is then very clear. Replace some of the sour fruits with raspberries, strawberries or sweet cherries and sweeten to taste with a little honey or apple syrup. Without any sweetness, the grits still taste surprisingly balanced with sweet vanilla sauce.
