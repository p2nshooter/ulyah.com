When the first orange, green and beige pumpkins are lined up at the farm shops in September, the actual cooking season begins for many. Pumpkin is versatile, inexpensive and can be stored for months. It goes well in soups and curries, on trays of baked vegetables, in risotto, cakes and even bread. Nevertheless, many people are faced with the big, hard thing and don't really know how to start.

This article will help you distinguish between the most important varieties, safely disassemble a pumpkin and use it to cook a soup that will convince even pumpkin skeptics.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - You can eat the Hokkaido peel, but you usually peel butternut and nutmeg squash.
> - Ornamental pumpkins are not intended for eating. If a pumpkin tastes bitter, throw it away because bitter substances can be poisonous.
> - Whole, uninjured pumpkins will last for several months in a cool, dry place at 10 to 15 degrees.
> - Cut pumpkin will last about three to five days in the refrigerator, covered with foil.
> - To make the soup, fry the onion and ginger, add the pumpkin and broth and puree after about 20 minutes.

## The most important pumpkins

| variety | Appearance | Taste and consistency | shell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hokkaido | round, orange, 1–2 kg | nutty, reminiscent of chestnuts, rather dry | edible |
| Butternut | pear-shaped, beige | sweet, buttery, little fiber | peel |
| Nutmeg pumpkin | large, ribbed, greenish to ocher colored | aromatic, juicy, slightly spicy | peel |
| Spaghetti squash | oval, light yellow | mild; When cooked, the pulp breaks down into threads | not edible |
| Patisson (UFO pumpkin) | flat, wavy edge | mild, similar to zucchini | edible young |
| Yellow hundredweight | huge, pale orange | watery, mild | peel |

For beginners, Hokkaido is the easiest choice. It is small enough for a household, the flesh is firm and bright orange, and the skin softens when cooked. Butternut is particularly suitable for oven dishes and purees because its flesh becomes very creamy. The nutmeg pumpkin is usually sold in pieces and is ideal for soup and sweet and sour pickled pumpkin.

### An ancient cultivated plant

Pumpkins come from America and are among the oldest cultivated plants. They were cultivated in Mexico around ten thousand years ago, long before corn and beans. They only came to Europe after Columbus' voyages. Today, hundreds of varieties grow in German gardens and fields, and many farm shops display entire walls of pumpkins in all colors and shapes in autumn.

## Be careful with bitter gourds

Like zucchini and cucumbers, pumpkins belong to the cucurbit family. Wild forms and ornamental pumpkins contain cucurbitacins, bitter substances that, even in small quantities, can cause vomiting, diarrhea and circulatory problems. These substances have been bred away from pumpkins. Problems arise especially when pumpkins cross with ornamental pumpkins in the garden and someone grows new plants from their own seeds.

The rule is simple: taste a small piece raw before cooking. If it tastes bitter, spit it out and throw away the entire pumpkin. Boiling does not destroy the bitter substances. Ornamental pumpkins with warts or bizarre shapes belong on the table as decoration, not in the pot.

## Storing pumpkin

A whole pumpkin with the stem and rind intact makes an excellent winter stock. Store it in a dry, airy place at around 10 to 15 degrees, such as a basement, pantry or cool hallway. Place it on a piece of cardboard or a cloth to prevent pressure marks from forming. The refrigerator is too cold and too moist for whole pumpkins.

Stored this way, Hokkaido and Butternut last three to six months. Check regularly whether soft spots are forming. Wrap cut pumpkins in foil or beeswax cloth and store them in the refrigerator, where they will last for three to five days. Pumpkin also freezes well when diced raw or as a cooked soup.

## Cut pumpkin safely

Pumpkins are hard and round, so the knife slips easily. You can do it safely with these steps:

1. Place a damp cloth under the cutting board to prevent it from slipping.
2. Take a large, sharp chef's knife. Dull knives are more dangerous because you need more pressure.
3. First, cut a thin slice off the side so that the pumpkin has a flat, stable surface to rest on.
4. Halve the pumpkin from top to bottom. Place the knife next to the handle and press the back of the knife with the flat of your hand. If it is very hard, it helps to warm it in the oven at 180 degrees for 5 to 10 minutes beforehand.
5. Scrape out seeds and fibers with a tablespoon.
6. Cut the halves into wedges. For butternuts, remove the shell with a sturdy vegetable peeler; for hard varieties, cut them off from the cracks.

## Pumpkin soup with ginger

For 4 people:

- 1 Hokkaido pumpkin (about 1.2 kg)
- 1 large onion
- 1 piece of ginger, about the size of a thumb (20 g)
- 1 clove of garlic
- 2 tablespoons rapeseed oil or butter
- 1 l vegetable broth
- 200 ml coconut milk or 150 ml cream
- Salt, pepper, a pinch of nutmeg
- 1 to 2 tablespoons lemon or lime juice
- to serve: roasted pumpkin seeds, a few drops of pumpkin seed oil

1. Wash the Hokkaido thoroughly, halve it, remove the seeds and cut it with the peel into cubes about two centimeters in size. You will get about a kilo of pulp.
2. Dice the onion, peel the ginger with a teaspoon and grate it finely, chop the garlic.
3. Heat the oil in a large pot and sauté the onion for five minutes. Add ginger and garlic and let them sauté for a minute.
4. Add the pumpkin cubes and roast them while stirring for three to four minutes. This brings additional roasted aromas.
5. Pour in the broth, bring everything to the boil and let the soup simmer, covered, for 20 to 25 minutes until the pumpkin is completely soft.
6. Add coconut milk or cream and puree the soup very finely with the hand blender. If it is too thick, thin it with a little broth or water.
7. Season to taste with salt, pepper, nutmeg and lemon juice. The acidity is important, it brings out the sweetness of the pumpkin.

Serve the soup with roasted pumpkin seeds and a few drops of pumpkin seed oil. For a hearty version, fry bacon cubes or smoked tofu until crispy and sprinkle them on top. Instead of ginger, you can also use a teaspoon of curry powder, smoked paprika powder or an apple that you cook with the pumpkin.

## Baked pumpkin as a side dish

Cut the butternut or Hokkaido into finger-thick wedges, mix with three tablespoons of olive oil, salt, pepper and a few sprigs of thyme or rosemary and spread on a baking tray lined with baking paper. Bake at 200 degrees top and bottom heat for 25 to 30 minutes, turning halfway through. The pumpkin is ready when it is soft and has brown edges. Feta, walnuts, a yoghurt dip or lamb's lettuce go well with this.

## Pumpkin risotto with sage

For four people, sauté a finely diced onion in two tablespoons of butter until translucent, add 400 g pumpkin cubes, preferably Hokkaido or butternut, and 300 g risotto rice and stir until the rice grains are translucent. Deglaze with a dash of white wine or broth and gradually add about a liter of hot vegetable broth, always just enough to just cover the rice, and stir regularly. After 18 to 20 minutes, the rice will be creamy but still have a slight bite to it, and some of the pumpkin cubes will have broken down, turning the risotto orange. Finally, stir in 50 g of grated hard cheese and a piece of cold butter. Fry some sage leaves in butter until crispy and place them on top.

## Sweet and sour pumpkin

Sweet and sour pickled pumpkin is an old specialty that goes well with cold roasts, cheese and game. To do this, cut the firm pulp, traditionally from nutmeg pumpkin, into cubes and let it steep in mild vinegar overnight. The next day, boil vinegar, sugar, water, cloves, cinnamon sticks and a little ginger into a stock and cook the cubes until they are translucent but still firm to the bite. Filled hot into clean jars and sealed, the pumpkin will keep for many months if stored in a cool, dark place.

## Styrian pumpkin seed oil

The dark green oil from Styria is pressed from the peeled oil pumpkin seeds, which are previously roasted. This is where its nutty taste and color come from. “Styrian pumpkin seed oil” is registered as a protected geographical indication in the EU. It is not suitable for frying because it loses its aroma when heated. It belongs on salads, in potato salad, on soups and, for those curious, in a few drops over vanilla ice cream.

## Roast pumpkin seeds

You can roast and nibble the seeds from pumpkins. However, they have a hard shell. The green seeds in the bag come from Styrian oil pumpkin, which forms shellless kernels.

1. Remove the seeds from the fibers and wash them in a bowl of water. The fibers sink, the cores float on top.
2. Pat them dry and mix them with a little oil and salt.
3. Roast them on a tray at 160 degrees for 15 to 20 minutes, until lightly browned and crackling.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I eat Halloween pumpkins?

The large orange carving pumpkins are mostly edible pumpkins, but taste watery and bland. Once you've carved a pumpkin and left it outside with a candle for a few days, you shouldn't eat it again. For the kitchen, it's better to use Hokkaido, butternut or nutmeg pumpkin.

### How do I recognize a ripe pumpkin?

The stem is woody and dry, the shell is hard and can hardly be scratched with a fingernail. When tapped, a ripe pumpkin sounds hollow.

### Why is my soup getting stringy?

Some varieties such as the yellow centner or some of the nutmeg pumpkins have more fibers. In this case, puree the soup for longer or strain it through a sieve. Hokkaido and butternut make the smoothest soups.

### Is butternut shell poisonous?

No, but it doesn't become as soft as Hokkaido when cooked and remains tough. Baked in wedges in the oven, you can eat them with young fruits; it is better to peel them for soup.
