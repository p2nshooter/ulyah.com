The same vegetable can taste bland or great depending on how it's cooked. A carrot that has been boiled in a lot of water for 20 minutes has little taste. The same carrot, roasted in the oven with a little oil, becomes sweet and caramelly. If you understand the most important cooking methods, you can prepare seasonal vegetables well without a recipe and avoid mistakes that cost you color, bite and vitamins. This overview explains what happens with each method and what it is suitable for.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Cooking **moist** (boiling, blanching, steaming, stewing) preserves shape and color, but does not produce any roasted aromas.
> - Cooking **dry** (frying, roasting, grilling) browns the surface from around 140 degrees and creates intense flavors.
> - **Braising** combines both: first fry, then slowly cook in a little liquid until soft.
> - Water-soluble vitamins go into the cooking water. Little water, short cooking times and use the cooking water for sauces.
> - Pieces of equal size cook evenly. This is the most important rule for any method.

## Damp and dry heat

There are basically two types of heat. In **humid heat**, water or steam transfers heat. The temperature is a maximum of 100 degrees. Food becomes soft but does not brown. With **dry heat**, fat, hot air or a hot surface transfers heat. The surface reaches well over 100 degrees, dries and browns. The so-called Maillard reaction between sugar and protein building blocks creates hundreds of aromatic substances that taste like roasting, nuts and caramel.

| Method | medium | Temperature | Typical of |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cooking | lots of sparkling water | 100°C | Potatoes, pasta, legumes, green beans |
| Boiling, poaching | Water just below boiling | 75 to 95°C | Broth, dumplings, eggs, fish |
| Blanch | sparkling water, very briefly, then ice water | 100°C | Vegetables before freezing, chard, spinach |
| Steaming | Water vapor above the water | about 100 °C | Broccoli, cauliflower, carrots, fish |
| Steaming | little liquid and fat, with lid | below 100 °C | Zucchini, peas, kohlrabi, mushrooms |
| Braising | Fry first, then add a little liquid, cover | 90 to 100 °C in the liquid | Red cabbage, fennel, meat, root vegetables |
| Roast | little fat in the pan | 140 to 200°C | Fried potatoes, mushrooms, zucchini slices |
| Roasting in the oven | hot air, some oil | 180 to 230°C | Root vegetables, pumpkin, Brussels sprouts, cauliflower |
| Deep frying | hot fat, food floats | 160 to 180°C | Fries, pancakes, fried vegetables |
| Grilling | Radiant heat | very high | Peppers, zucchini, corn on the cob |

## Cooking and blanching

When **cooking**, the food is placed in plenty of bubbling water. This makes sense for foods that have to absorb a lot of water or whose ingredients only become harmless after cooking for a long time, such as [green beans](/kueche/gruene-bohnen-richtig-garen/). For most other vegetables, cooking them in lots of water is the method with the least taste because flavors, minerals and water-soluble vitamins such as vitamin C and folic acid are transferred into the water.

**Blanching** means putting vegetables in boiling salt water for just one to three minutes and then immediately rinsing them in ice water. This deactivates enzymes that would destroy color and flavor when frozen, and makes green vegetables vibrant. Blanching is primarily a preparation, for example for the freezer, as described in [Freezing vegetables and fruit](/kueche/gemuese-einfrieren-und-blanchieren/).

**Tip:** Always cook green vegetables in plenty of boiling water without a lid. A little water cools down a lot when you add it, and with the lid on, acids collect and turn the green olive gray.

## Boiling and poaching

When it boils, the water moves only slightly and small bubbles rise. This is the right temperature for broths that need to remain clear, for dumplings that otherwise fall apart, and for delicate foods like fish. Our article [Cooking your own broth] (/kueche/gemuesebruehe-selbst-kochen/) shows how important this is for a clear broth.

## Steaming

When steaming, the vegetables are placed in a sieve over boiling water and cooked in the steam. It does not touch the water and therefore loses significantly fewer vitamins and flavor than when boiling. Steamed vegetables retain their shape and color and taste more intensely like themselves.

- Suitable for broccoli, cauliflower, Romanesco, carrots, potatoes, asparagus and fish.
- The cooking times are slightly longer than when boiling. Broccoli florets take about six to eight minutes, potato pieces 20 to 25 minutes.
- The lid must close tightly and the water must not reach the insert.

## Steaming

When steaming, vegetables are cooked in a little liquid, often just their own juice, with a little butter or oil and the lid closed over medium heat. It is suitable for vegetables with a lot of water such as zucchini, mushrooms, spinach, peas, chard and kohlrabi. The resulting juice is eaten, so hardly any nutrients are lost. A classic is steamed vegetables, which are thickened with a little cream or butter at the end.

**Glazing** is a form of steaming: Cook carrots or turnips with a little water, butter, a pinch of sugar and salt without a lid until the liquid has boiled away and the vegetables are shiny.

## Braising

Braising combines roasted flavors with a long, gentle cooking time. First, the food is fried vigorously, then it is deglazed with a little liquid and cooked, covered, at a low temperature on the stove or in the oven until it is completely soft. Vegetables such as [apple red cabbage](/kueche/apfelrotkohl/), fennel, chicory, leeks and root vegetables benefit enormously. Braised cucumbers also owe their name to this method.

Patience is more important than heat when braising. The liquid should only simmer gently. Overcooking makes meat tough and vegetables fall apart.

## Roast

Vegetables brown in a little fat in the pan. To achieve this, you need three things:

1. **A hot skillet.** Add the vegetables when the oil is hot.
2. **Dry food.** Wet vegetables are steamed instead of frying. After washing, pat it dry well.
3. **Space.** If there is too much in the pan, the temperature drops, water comes out and nothing browns. It's better to fry in two batches.

Mushrooms are the best example: in a full pan they swim in their own juice, in small portions they turn golden brown. More about this in our article about [chanterelles and porcini mushrooms](/kueche/pfifferlinge-und-steinpilze/).

## Roasting in the oven

For root vegetables, squash, cabbages and onions, oven roasting is often the most flavorful method. At 200 to 220 degrees, water evaporates from the surface, the natural sugars in the vegetables caramelize, and the edges become crispy.

- Cut everything into equal pieces.
- Mix the vegetables with a little oil and salt so that each piece is slightly shiny.
- Spread it in a spaced apart layer on the tray. If placed too tightly, it will become soft instead of brown.
- Turn it halfway through the cooking time.
- Place vegetables that have different cooking times on the tray one after the other: potatoes and beetroot first, zucchini and peppers later.

Our articles about [Brussels sprouts](/kueche/rosenkohl-zubereiten/) and [parsnips](/kueche/pastinaken-und-petersilienwurzeln/) show what this looks like with specific vegetables.

## Under pressure: the pressure cooker

In the closed pressure cooker, the pressure increases and water no longer boils at 100 degrees, but at around 116 to 120 degrees. This shortens cooking times significantly, often by more than half. This is particularly worthwhile for foods that otherwise take a long time: beetroot, whole potatoes, legumes, broths and stews. Because less water is needed and the cooking time is short, fewer vitamins are lost. Stick to the manufacturer's minimum and maximum capacity and only open the pot when the pressure has completely reduced.

## Microwave and low temperature

The **microwave** heats the water in the food directly. It is a gentle method for small portions of vegetables: Place the vegetables with two to three tablespoons of water in a covered, microwave-safe bowl and cook them at full power for a few minutes, stirring once. Because there is little water involved and the cooking time is short, vitamins are retained just as well as when steaming. However, the microwave heats unevenly. Equally sized pieces and letting them rest briefly after cooking balance this out.

When cooking in a water bath, also known as sous-vide, the food is vacuum-packed in water at a precisely set temperature. Meat and fish are cooked perfectly. Higher temperatures are necessary for vegetables, usually around 85 degrees, because only then do the cell walls become soft. Carrots or asparagus are cooked evenly and retain their full flavor because nothing passes into the water.

## When are vegetables cooked?

The cooking times in recipes are guidelines as size, freshness and variety change them. So rely on the cooking test:

- **Knife sample:** A pointed knife slides into potatoes and root vegetables without resistance. With broccoli or beans you will still feel a slight resistance when they are al dente.
- **Color:** Green vegetables are at their peak when they are bright green. If it turns olive green, it is overcooked.
- **Try:** Take out a piece, let it cool briefly and bite into it. This is the most reliable sample.

Remember that vegetables will continue to cook after draining. If you want it al dente, remove it from the heat a minute earlier or quench it briefly.

## Protect vitamins

- Wash and cut vegetables just before cooking.
- Cook, steam or steam with a little water instead of boiling.
- Keep cooking times short. Vegetables can still have some bite as long as the method is safe.
- Use cooking water from vegetables for sauces and soups. Exception: the water from potatoes and green beans.
- Avoid keeping warm for long periods of time. It's better to cool it down and warm it up briefly later.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which method is healthiest?

Steaming and stewing preserve most of the water-soluble vitamins. Some ingredients, such as the carotene in carrots or the lycopene in tomatoes, become more available when cooked with a little fat. Variety makes more sense than a single method.

### What is the difference between steaming and braising?

There is no roasting phase when steaming. When braising, the food is first seared vigorously and then cooked in liquid until soft.

### Is oven roasting unhealthy?

No. Just make sure to roast starchy vegetables like potatoes until golden instead of dark brown, as very browning produces more acrylamide.
