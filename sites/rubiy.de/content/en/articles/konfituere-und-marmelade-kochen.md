Homemade jam is one of the most beautiful ways to bring summer into a glass. In June the kitchen smells of strawberries, in July of raspberries and currants, in August of apricots and mirabelle plums, in autumn of plums and quinces. Opening a jar of it in February is a small celebration.

Cooking jam is not difficult. It almost always works with preserving sugar. Nevertheless, there are a few points where there are problems: the jam doesn't set, it molds after a few weeks, or it just tastes like sugar. In this article you will find the basics that prevent this.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Jam sugar 1:1 means one kilo of fruit to one kilo of sugar, 2:1 means two parts of fruit to one part of sugar, 3:1 means three to one.
> - The less sugar, the fruitier the jam, but the shorter its shelf life is. Jars with little sugar should be placed in the refrigerator after opening.
> - The cooking time only begins when the mixture is boiling, usually three to four minutes.
> - Use the jelly test on a cold plate to check whether the jam sets.
> - If there is mold, throw away the entire glass. Skimming is not enough.

## Jam, jam, jelly: the terms

In everyday life almost everyone says jam. Legally, things are regulated differently in the EU: In stores, a fruit spread can only be called jam if it is made from citrus fruits. Everything else is called jam. This doesn't matter for your own kitchen, but the terms help you understand recipes.

| Designation | What it is |
|---|---|
| jam | made from whole fruits or pieces of fruit, cooked with sugar |
| Jam (in the legal sense) | made from citrus fruits, such as oranges or lemons |
| Jelly | from the clear juice of the fruit, without pulp |
| fruit spread | usually with less sugar, not precisely defined by law |
| Mus | long-cooked fruit puree, often without gelling agents, for example plum puree |

## Choose the right jam sugar

In order for jam to set, it needs pectin, sugar and acid in the right proportions. Pectin is a natural fiber found in the cell walls of fruits. Apples, quinces, currants and gooseberries contain a lot of it, while strawberries, cherries and pears contain little. Jam sugar contains added pectin and often citric acid. That's why jam sets reliably, regardless of the type of fruit.

| variety | Fruit to sugar ratio | taste | Shelf life (unopened, cool and dark) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jam sugar 1:1 | 1 kg fruit, 1 kg sugar | very sweet, classic | about a year or more |
| Jam sugar 2:1 | 1 kg fruit, 500 g sugar | fruitier | several months |
| Jam sugar 3:1 | 1.5 kg fruit, 500 g sugar | very fruity, less sweet | several months, use quickly when opened |

When it comes to the amount of fruit, stick exactly to the ratio that is written on the package. Weigh the fruit after cleaning, i.e. without stems, seeds and stones. If you use more fruit, the jam won't set. If you use less, it will be too thick and sweet.

Sugar is not just a sweetener in jam, but also the actual preservative. It binds water, making it harder for bacteria and mold to grow. If you reduce the sugar significantly, the jam will lose this protection.

## Basic recipe: raspberry jam

For about five glasses of 250 milliliters each:

- 1 kg raspberries, fresh or frozen
- 500 g preserving sugar 2:1
- 1 tbsp lemon juice
- If desired, the pulp of half a vanilla pod

### Preparation

1. Rinse the jars and lids in hot water and boil them in water for ten minutes or place the jars in the oven at 120 degrees for 15 minutes. Place the lids briefly in boiling water.
2. Place a small plate in the freezer. You need it for the gelling test.
3. Pick the raspberries. Only wash them when absolutely necessary and then very carefully because they quickly become muddy. You can thaw frozen raspberries.

### Cooking

1. Place raspberries, preserving sugar and lemon juice in a large pot. The pot should not be more than a third full because the mixture foams up a lot when cooking.
2. Mash the fruit with a potato masher. If you don't like seeds, strain the raspberries through a sieve first. Then about 700 grams of fruit pulp remain and you use 350 grams of preserving sugar.
3. If there is time, let the mixture sit for an hour. The sugar then dissolves better.
4. Bring everything to a boil over high heat, stirring constantly. When the mixture bubbles so much that it no longer calms down even when stirring, the cooking time begins. Cook, stirring, for four minutes, or as long as directed on the package.
5. Do the gelling test: put a teaspoon of jam on the cold plate and wait a minute. If it becomes firm and wrinkles when you push it with your finger, it is ready. If it remains liquid, cook for another minute and check again.
6. Skim off the foam if you wish. It is harmless, but looks cloudy in the glass. A small piece of butter added while cooking will reduce foaming.

### Bottling

1. Using a funnel, pour the boiling hot jam into the jars to about half a centimeter below the rim.
2. Wipe the edge with a clean, damp cloth.
3. Seal the jars tightly immediately. Many people then place them on the lid for five minutes. This isn't necessary if the glasses are clean and filled to the brim, but it won't do any harm either.
4. Allow the jars to cool upright and away from drafts. As it cools, the lid pulls inward, often with an audible cracking sound.

## Variations according to season

- **Strawberries:** go well with rhubarb, vanilla, elderflower or a touch of black pepper. Strawberries contain little pectin and acid, so a tablespoon of lemon juice is a must.
- **Currants:** ideal for jelly. You get the juice by boiling the berries with a little water and letting them drip through a cloth overnight.
- **Apricots and mirabelle plums:** de-stone, cut into small pieces, add vanilla or rosemary as desired.
- **Plums:** taste great with cinnamon, cloves or a little red wine. To make puree without preserving sugar, cook pitted plums with a little sugar in an open roasting pan at 180 degrees in the oven for several hours until they are dark and thick.
- **Quinces:** provide the classic jelly of fall. More about this in the article about [quince jelly and quince bread](/kueche/quittengelee-und-quittenbrot/).

## The real jam: made from bitter oranges

If you want to cook jam in the legal sense, wait for winter. In January and February there are bitter oranges, often sold as Seville oranges. Raw they are inedibly sour and bitter; when boiled they produce the classic British orange marmalade with fine strips of peel.

For about six glasses, wash 1 kg of bitter oranges and a lemon in hot water, halve them and squeeze out the juice. You tie the seeds and separating membranes in a cheesecloth because they contain a lot of pectin. Cut the peels into fine strips and let them simmer quietly with the juice, the gauze bag and two liters of water for about two hours until they are completely soft and the liquid has reduced by about half. Squeeze out the bag, add 1.5 kg of sugar, dissolve it completely while stirring over a low heat and then cook the jam until it gels. This can take 15 to 25 minutes. A candy thermometer helps: the gelling point is reached at around 104 to 105 degrees. Let the jam stand for 10 minutes so that the shells are evenly distributed, then bottle it.

## Right tool

- **Pot:** wide and heavy, made of stainless steel or enamelled. In a wide pot, water evaporates more quickly and the cooking time is short. Uncoated aluminum pots are not suitable because of the fruit acid.
- **Long handled wooden spoon:** Boiling jam will splatter and be hotter than boiling water.
- **Filling funnel and ladle:** This keeps the edges of the glass clean and the lids close tightly.
- **Labels:** Write the variety, date and sugar ratio on each jar. This way you will know later which glasses should be used first.

## Give jam as a gift

Homemade jam is a popular souvenir. Small glasses with 125 to 200 milliliters are ideal for this because they are used up quickly. A piece of fabric over the lid, a ribbon and a handwritten label turn the stash into a gift. For low-sugar versions, please note that the jar should be placed in the refrigerator after opening.

## Storage and mold

Store the jars in a cool, dry and dark place. Light bleaches the color, heat changes the taste. An opened jar belongs in the refrigerator. Always remove the jam with a clean spoon or knife, not the butter knife with bread crumbs stuck to it.

If mold appears on the surface, throw away the entire glass. The fungal network can spread far below the visible area in the soft, moist mass, and some molds produce toxins that can neither be seen nor tasted. This is especially true for jam with little sugar. If it smells sour, alcoholic or fermented or bubbles appear, the same applies.

## Frequently asked questions

### My jam didn't set. What now?

Put them back in the pot, add some lemon juice and a small amount of preserving sugar or a packet of pectin according to the instructions on the package and bring them to the boil again until bubbly. Then do the gelling test. Jam often only sets properly after a day or two. So wait and see first.

### Can I use normal sugar instead of preserving sugar?

Yes, with fruits rich in pectin such as currants, quinces or tart apples. However, you usually need more sugar and a longer cooking time, and the jam tastes less fresh. For most other fruits, preserving sugar is the safer choice.

### Can I cook the jam with honey or sweetener?

Honey changes taste and consistency and its aroma is affected when cooked. For low-sugar versions, there are special gelling agents that work with little or no sugar. However, such spreads only have a short shelf life and should be kept as cool as possible even when unopened.
