Hardly any other vegetable has as many opponents as Brussels sprouts. Anyone who had to eat gray, mushy florets with the smell of sulfur as a child often avoids them for the rest of their lives. The problem almost always lies in the preparation. Properly cooked, Brussels sprouts are bright green, nutty and slightly sweet, and when roasted in the oven they have caramelized edges that convince even skeptics. From October to February it is one of the few fresh vegetables left in the field in Germany, and frost makes it even better.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Brussels sprouts are in season from October to February. Buy firm, closed, bright green florets.
> - Remove the outer loose leaves and just cut off the stem. A cross cut in the stalk is not necessary.
> - Don't cook for too long: six to eight minutes in salted water are enough; large florets should be halved beforehand.
> - Roast in half in the oven at 220 degrees for 20 to 25 minutes until the cut surfaces are brown.
> - Fat, a little sweetness and acid balance bitter substances: butter, bacon, honey, mustard, lemon, nuts.

## Why Brussels sprouts can be bitter

Brussels sprouts belong to the cabbage family and, like all types of cabbage, contain mustard oil glycosides. These plant substances protect the plant from predators and are responsible for the slightly bitter, spicy taste. If Brussels sprouts cook for too long, they break down into sulfur-containing compounds. That's the typical smell of cabbage that wafts through the apartment, and then the vegetable also tastes dull and bitter.

Modern varieties are significantly milder than varieties grown several decades ago. The cold also helps: When there is frost, the plant stores more sugar to protect its cells. Brussels sprouts that are harvested after the first night of frost therefore often taste sweeter.

## Origin and name

Brussels sprouts owe their French and English names to the area around Brussels, where they were grown on a larger scale centuries ago: choux de Bruxelles, Brussels sprouts. The German name describes the appearance of the small heads, which sit on the stem like rosebuds. In Austria it is called cabbage sprouts.

## Recognize fresh Brussels sprouts

- The florets are firm, compact and closed. They don't give in when pressed.
- The color is strong green, without yellow leaves or black spots.
- The interface on the stalk is light and fresh, not brown or dried out.
- Small to medium-sized florets of two to three centimeters are more delicate than very large ones.

At weekly markets, Brussels sprouts are sometimes sold on the stalk, a long, thick stick on which the florets sit in a spiral. It stays fresh longer on the stick and looks decorative.

## Store

Store Brussels sprouts unwashed in a bag in the vegetable compartment. It lasts for about four to seven days. If you buy it on a stick, keep it cool and use it within one to two weeks. The longer it sits, the stronger its cabbage flavor becomes. Don't store it next to apples or tomatoes. Their ripening gas, ethylene, causes the leaves to turn yellow.

## Cleaning

1. Remove the outer, loose or discolored leaves. Usually there are only one or two per floret.
2. Cut off the stem just so that the leaves still hold together.
3. Wash the florets in cold water.
4. Halve larger florets lengthwise through the stem. This way all pieces cook at the same speed.

The classic cross cut into the stalk is supposed to ensure that the stalk cooks just as quickly as the leaves. It doesn't do much for normal-sized florets, but it allows water to penetrate and the florets tend to become mushy. Halving is the better solution.

## Cook Brussels sprouts

Bring plenty of water to the boil, salt it generously and add the florets. Cook them uncovered to allow volatile sulfur compounds to escape. Whole, medium-sized florets take six to eight minutes, halved ones take four to five minutes. Do the cooking test with a sharp knife: it should slide into the stalk with slight resistance.

Drain the Brussels sprouts and toss them in a pan with butter, salt, pepper and freshly grated nutmeg. A few roasted almond flakes or hazelnuts make a fine accompaniment to roast, duck or game. If you want to use the Brussels sprouts later, for example for a casserole, rinse them in ice water after cooking. This way it stays green and doesn't overcook.

## Roasting in the oven: the best method for skeptics

Roasted Brussels sprouts completely change their character. The cut surfaces caramelize, the outer leaves become crispy like chips, and the bitterness recedes.

1. Preheat the oven to 220 degrees top and bottom heat or 200 degrees circulating air.
2. Halve 600 grams of cleaned florets and mix them with 2 to 3 tablespoons of oil, salt and pepper.
3. Spread them on a baking tray lined with baking paper and place them cut side down. The florets need space. If they are too close, they will steam instead of roasting.
4. Roast them for 20 to 25 minutes, until the undersides are deep golden brown and the outer leaves have dark edges. Don't turn.

**Mustard-Honey Glaze:** Mix 1 tablespoon of honey, 1 tablespoon of grainy mustard and 1 tablespoon of lemon juice and mix in the hot Brussels sprouts immediately after roasting. The combination of sweetness, spiciness and acidity is the perfect counterbalance to the bitter substances.

**With Parmesan:** Sprinkle grated hard cheese over the florets during the last five minutes.

## In the pan with bacon and onions

This variant is a classic of German winter cuisine.

1. Boil 500 g of halved Brussels sprouts in salted water for four minutes and drain well.
2. Slowly fry 100g diced bacon in a large pan until crispy. Add a finely diced onion and let it become translucent.
3. Place the Brussels sprout halves cut-side down in the pan and cook over medium heat for five minutes, without moving, until browned.
4. Toss everything together, season with pepper and a little salt because the bacon is salty, and finally add a splash of apple cider vinegar.

Without meat, the recipe works with roasted walnuts, a knob of butter and a little maple syrup.

## Brussels sprouts raw and as a salad

You can also eat Brussels sprouts raw, thinly sliced or cut into fine strips. Mix the strips with a dressing made from lemon juice, oil, mustard and a little honey, add apple cubes, pomegranate seeds or roasted nuts and let the salad sit for ten minutes. It tastes fresh and crunchy, completely different to cooked Brussels sprouts. ## Oven casserole with potatoes and cheese

For four people, cook 800 g of waxy potatoes as jacket potatoes, peel them and cut them into slices. Pre-cook 500 g of halved Brussels sprouts for five minutes and rinse them. Layer both in a greased baking dish. Whisk 200 ml cream, 100 ml milk and two eggs with salt, pepper and nutmeg and pour the mixture over. Sprinkle 100 g of grated mountain cheese on top, as well as fried bacon cubes if you like, and bake the casserole at 190 degrees top and bottom heat for about 30 minutes until the surface is golden brown and the icing is set. A green salad with a rich dressing makes it a complete dinner.

## Freeze

Brussels sprouts freeze well. Blanch the cleaned florets for three minutes, rinse them in ice water, dry them well and freeze them on a tray before putting them in bags. This way they don't stick together. Frozen Brussels sprouts last for about ten months and can be cooked or roasted directly from frozen. General rules for blanching can be found in [Freezing vegetables and fruit](/kueche/gemuese-einfrieren-und-blanchieren/).

## What's in Brussels sprouts

Brussels sprouts are one of the vegetables with the most vitamin C. A portion of around 100 grams already covers a large part of an adult's daily requirement, even if some is lost during cooking. It also provides folate, vitamin K, fiber and, for a vegetable, a noticeable amount of protein. If you want to preserve the vitamins, steam the florets or cook them briefly until they are al dente instead of boiling them in water for a long time.

A note for everyone who takes anticoagulant medications of the vitamin K antagonist type: Vitamin K influences their effect. Brussels sprouts are not forbidden, but you should avoid large fluctuations in consumption and discuss the topic with your doctor.

## Brussels sprouts in your own garden

Brussels sprouts require patience. The young plants come into the bed in May or June and stay there until winter. They need a lot of space, about 50 to 60 centimeters in all directions, nutrient-rich soil and even moisture. Harvest from bottom to top as soon as the florets are firm. Many gardeners cut the top of the plant at the end of September. She then puts her energy into the existing florets, which ripen more evenly. Winter varieties can tolerate temperatures well below zero and produce fresh florets until February.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does my kitchen smell like sulfur when I cook?

The Brussels sprouts cook too long. Halve the florets, cook them uncovered and remove them as soon as they are al dente. Roasting produces almost no cabbage smell.

### Do Brussels sprouts bloat?

Like all cabbage, it can cause bloating in some people. Caraway seeds, fennel seeds or getting used to cabbage in smaller portions help many people.

### Can I prepare Brussels sprouts yet?

Cleaned and halved, it will keep well in the fridge for one to two days. Pre-cooked and quenched, it is ready for quick frying.

### What do I do with florets that have yellow leaves?

Pluck off the yellow outer leaves. If the inside is firm and green, you can use the Brussels sprouts as usual. Yellow leaves only show that it has been stored for a long time and the cabbage taste is a little stronger. In this case, roast it rather than boil it.
