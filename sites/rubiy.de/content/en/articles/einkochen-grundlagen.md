Preserving is one of the oldest methods of preserving the harvest and is a fixture in late summer in many families. When cherries, plums, pears and beans ripen at the same time, there are large pots on the stove with glasses clattering in the hot water and the kitchen smells of cinnamon and cloves. In winter there is a shelf full of jars that just need to be opened.

There are a few rules you should know so that the cooked food doesn't spoil. Most are simple. One of them is serious: Vegetables without acid can produce a life-threatening bacterial toxin. We explain in detail how you can avoid this below.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - When preserving, filled, sealed jars are heated in a water bath. As it cools, a vacuum is created that seals the jars tightly.
> - Depending on the variety, fruit is boiled at 75 to 90 degrees for around 20 to 30 minutes.
> - Low-acid vegetables such as beans, peas, carrots or asparagus are tricky: the spores of the botulism pathogen survive at 100 degrees. Double boiling, acidifying or freezing is safer here.
> - Do not try jars with domed or open lids, gas bubbles, cloudy contents or a strange smell, but discard them.
> - Store cooked food in a cool, dry and dark place and use it within a year if possible.

## How preserving works

When preserving, food is put into jars, sealed and then heated in a water bath or in the oven. The heat kills most microorganisms and deactivates enzymes that would otherwise alter the food. At the same time, the air in the jar expands and partially escapes from under the lid. As it cools, the contents contract and a negative pressure is created that pulls the lid tightly onto the jar. As long as this vacuum holds, no new germs can enter.

This is what makes preserving different from preserving jam. When it comes to jam, it is primarily the high sugar content that ensures its shelf life, and hot filling is sufficient. When cooking fruit in light syrup or vegetables in salt water, however, heating in a sealed jar is crucial.

### Why they say “wake up”.

The word einwecken goes back to the Weck company, which popularized jars with glass lids, rubber rings and clips in Germany around 1900. The idea of ​​heating food in a sealed jar and preserving it using the resulting vacuum caught on so quickly that the company name became a verb. Today, to wake up is a natural part of the dictionary.

## What you need

- **Canning pot or large pot:** A canning machine with a thermostat and timer is convenient and keeps the temperature exactly. A large, tall saucepan with a lid also works if the jars fit in it and you have a thermometer.
- **Jars:** Round-rimmed jars with glass lids, rubber ring and clips, swing-top jars with rubber ring or screw-top jars with twist-off lids. Rubber rings and twist-off lids should be undamaged. Sort out lids with scratches in the coating or dented edges.
- **Insert or grid:** Jars must not be placed directly on the bottom of the pot, otherwise they may crack. Place a grid, cloth or special insert inside.
- **Thermometer, funnel, glass lifter, clean cloths.**

### Prepare glasses

Rinse jars, lids and rubber rings in hot water with detergent and rinse clean. Then boil the jars and lids in bubbling water for about ten minutes or place the jars in the oven at 120 degrees for 10 to 15 minutes. Rubber rings are only briefly soaked in hot water, not boiled, so that they remain elastic. Drain the jars upside down on a clean cloth and avoid touching the inside.

## Step by step: Preserve fruit

Sour cherries or plums in light syrup serve as examples. For around six glasses of 500 milliliters each, you need around 2 kilograms of fruit as well as a syrup made from 1 liter of water and 200 to 300 grams of sugar.

1. Wash the fruit, removing stems and, if desired, stones. For plums, halve the fruit.
2. Boil the water and sugar until the sugar has dissolved.
3. Layer the fruit tightly in the jars, about two to three centimeters below the rim.
4. Pour the hot syrup over it, covering the fruit but leaving two centimeters of space around the edge.
5. Wipe the edges of the jar with a clean, damp cloth. Residues of sugar or fruit juice prevent the lid from closing tightly.
6. Close the jars: put on the rubber ring and lid and secure with two clips, or tighten the twist-off lid tightly.
7. Place the jars in the pot and add water at approximately the same temperature as the contents until the jars are three-quarters submerged in water.
8. Heat the water to the specified temperature. The preserving time only begins when this has been reached.
9. After the time has elapsed, remove the glasses with a glass lifter and let them cool slowly on a cloth, away from drafts.
10. Only remove the clamps after they have completely cooled down, i.e. after about 24 hours. Then check that each lid is secure.

## temperatures and times

The values in the table are usual guideline values for glasses up to around one liter. The time is longer for larger glasses. If in doubt, follow the instructions for your canner.

| Food | Temperature | time |
|---|---|---|
| Berries (currants, gooseberries, blueberries) | 75-80°C | 25 minutes |
| Cherries, plums, mirabelle plums | 80-85°C | 30 minutes |
| Apples, pears, quinces in pieces | 90°C | 30 minutes |
| Applesauce, rhubarb compote | 90°C | 30 minutes |
| Tomatoes, acidified | 90-100°C | 30-40 minutes |
| Low-acid vegetables (beans, peas, carrots) | 98-100°C | 90-120 minutes, repeat after 24 to 48 hours |

## Be careful with low-acid vegetables: botulism

Spores of the bacterium *Clostridium botulinum* occur in soil and on unwashed vegetables. These spores are very resilient. They survive boiling at 100 degrees. In an airtight jar, without oxygen and without sufficient acid, they can germinate and form a neurotoxin, botulinum toxin. Poisoning is rare but life-threatening. What's tricky is that contaminated glasses don't always look or smell noticeable.

Beans, peas, carrots, asparagus, corn, pumpkin without vinegar, mushrooms and meat are particularly low in acid. Fruit and acidified foods are significantly safer because the spores do not germinate in an acidic environment.

If you still want to cook low-acid vegetables, follow these rules:

- Wash and clean vegetables very thoroughly so that as little soil as possible gets into the contents of the jar.
- Cook twice: first for 90 to 120 minutes at 98 to 100 degrees, then after 24 to 48 hours at room temperature a second time for the same amount of time. Germinated spores are killed in the second round.
- Or pickle the vegetables sourly, i.e. with a vinegar broth, like with [spiced gherkins](/kueche/gewuerzgurken-einlegen/), mixed pickles or sweet and sour pumpkin.
- The easiest and safest alternative is freezing.
- Boil boiled low-acid vegetables for at least ten minutes before eating. The poison is sensitive to heat and is destroyed in the process. However, this does not kill the spores, so this step is only an additional safeguard, not a replacement for the other rules.

We do not recommend boiling meat and fish in a water bath at home. The only thing that would be really safe here would be a pressure canner that reaches temperatures of around 121 degrees. Such devices are common in the USA, but rare in Germany.

## Cooking in the oven

You can also use the oven for fruit and compote. Place the filled, sealed jars in the drip pan, pour about two centimeters of hot water and slide them onto the bottom shelf. Heat the oven to 175 to 180 degrees top and bottom heat. As soon as small bubbles appear in the contents of the jars, turn off the oven and leave the jars in the closed oven for another 30 minutes. This method is not suitable for low-acid vegetables because the temperature in the jar cannot be precisely controlled.

## Store and control

Store boiled jars in a cool, dry and dark place, for example in the cellar or pantry, without staples. Label each jar with contents and date. Fruit usually lasts for a year, often longer, but loses color and flavor over time.

Check your supplies regularly. Discard a jar without tasting if:

- the lid has come loose or a twist-off lid is curved upwards and cracks when pressed,
- gas bubbles rise or the contents foam,
- the contents are cloudy, slimy or discolored,
- When you open it, it hisses, bubbles or smells unusually sour, putrid or cheesy.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why didn't my glasses seal?

Common causes include a dirty glass rim, an old or deformed rubber ring, glasses that are too full or a damaged lid. You can put jars that don't seal in the fridge and eat the contents over the next few days, or you can boil them again with a new lid.

### Do I have to turn glasses upside down to cool?

Not when cooking. Flipping is a method for hot bottled jam. Cooked jars remain upright.

### Can I use old twist-off jars from purchased products?

Yes, if the glass and lid are undamaged and the lid still closes properly. Many lids have a sealant that weakens after several passes. New lids are worthwhile for food that has to be stored for a long time.

### Can I preserve fruit without sugar?

Yes. When preserving, the heat in the sealed jar and the natural acidity of the fruit ensure its shelf life, not the sugar. The syrup mainly helps to preserve the color, aroma and bite. You can therefore also boil fruit in water or unsweetened juice. It then tastes fresher, but sometimes becomes a little softer and paler.
