Quinces are the diva among autumn fruits. Raw, they are rock hard, furry and so bitter that no one willingly bites into them. When cooked, they transform: the flesh turns rosy to amber, and a scent of honey, rose and citrus wafts through the apartment. Because quinces also contain a lot of pectin, they gel almost on their own. A basket of quinces makes two classics at once: clear quince jelly from the juice and quince bread from the leftover pulp. Both are a nice tradition for stockpiling and as a gift during Advent.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Quinces are in season from late September to November. They are ripe when the skin turns from green to yellow and they have a strong scent.
> - Rub the fluff thoroughly before processing; it tastes bitter.
> - Remove the seeds and do not crush them. The peel and pulp provide enough pectin.
> - To make jelly, boil the pieces until soft and let the juice drip through a cloth overnight.
> - The fruit pulp is boiled down with sugar to form a thick paste, dried and cut into diamonds to make quince bread.

## Apple quince or pear quince?

There are two main forms you come across in stores and in the garden:

| shape | Appearance | pulp | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apple quince | round, smaller, bright yellow | very hard, dry, intensely aromatic | Jelly, quince bread, liqueur |
| Pear quince | elongated, pear-shaped, larger | a little softer and juicier, milder | Compote, cake, puree, jelly |

Both are suitable for jelly and quince bread. Many people mix them: apple quinces add aroma, pear quinces add more juice. The quince has only a distant connection with the Asian ornamental quince that grows in many front gardens. Their small fruits are also edible and very acidic, but provide little yield.

## Where the quince comes from

The quince comes from the Caucasus and what is now Iran and was already cultivated around the Mediterranean in ancient times. The Greeks considered it the fruit of love and marriage, and some interpretations also see quinces in the golden apples of Greek legends. To this day it is contained in a word that we use every day: the Portuguese *marmelo* means quince, and *marmelada* was originally a quince puree. This became our jam.

## Detecting and storing ripeness

Ripe quinces are yellow, perhaps with a slightly greenish tinge, and have a distinct scent. Green quinces ripen within one to two weeks at room temperature. Store quinces separately from other fruit because their intense scent penetrates apples and pears. Undamaged fruits will last for several weeks in a cool cellar. Generously cut away any brown spots on the inside that occur during long storage.

## Prepare quinces

Preparation is the most strenuous part, so it's worth processing a larger quantity straight away.

1. **Remove the fuzz:** Using a dry, coarse kitchen towel, rub each quince vigorously until the peel is smooth. The gray fuzz tastes bitter and clouds the juice.
2. **Wash** and cut out the flower base and stem.
3. **Cutting:** A large, sharp knife and a sturdy board are required. Quarter the quince and cut out the core with the seeds. You don't have to peel because the peel is full of flavor and pectin.
4. **Do not use seeds:** Like apple seeds, quince seeds contain amygdalin, which can produce hydrogen cyanide when chewed or crushed. Whole kernels are not a problem in small quantities, but you still remove them for juice and pulp so that nothing is accidentally crushed.
5. **Cut into pieces:** Cut the quarters into rough pieces of about two to three centimeters.

## Cook the quinces until soft and extract the juice

Put the pieces of about 2 kilograms of quince in a large pot and pour enough water to just cover them, about 2 liters. Bring everything to the boil and then simmer gently, covered, for 30 to 45 minutes, until the pieces are completely soft and can be crushed with a spoon.

Line a colander with a clean, damp tea towel or cheesecloth and place it over a large bowl. Pour in the quince pieces with the liquid and let the juice drain for several hours or overnight. Don't squeeze the cloth if you want a clear jelly. Pressed juice becomes cloudy, but tastes just as good.

You usually get 1 to 1.5 liters of juice from 2 kilograms of quinces. You save the pulp in the cloth for the quince bread.

## Cook quince jelly

For about six glasses of 250 milliliters each:

- 1 liter of quince juice
- 500 g preserving sugar 2:1 or 1 kg preserving sugar 1:1
- Juice of half a lemon
- optional: a vanilla pod, a piece of cinnamon stick or a star anise

Bring the juice, lemon juice and preserving sugar to the boil while stirring and let the mixture boil for four minutes. You take out the spices before bottling. Do the jelly test: Place a teaspoon of jelly on a cold plate. If it sets after a minute and forms wrinkles when you push it with your finger, the jelly is ready. Skim off the foam and fill the jelly to the brim into hot-rinsed glasses. Seal it immediately.

Because quinces contain so much of their own pectin, quince jelly can also be made with normal table sugar. Then you calculate around 750 to 800 grams of sugar per liter of juice and cook for longer, often 10 to 15 minutes, until the gelling test is successful. ## Quince bread made from fruit pulp

Quince bread is a firm, cuttable fruit paste that is available in many countries. In Spain it is called *dulce de membrillo* and is eaten with cheese. In Germany, quince bread is traditionally included on the Christmas plate.

### Ingredients

- the pulp from the cloth, about 1 to 1.2 kg
- Sugar: 80 to 100 percent of the pulp weight, for example 900 g per 1 kg of pulp
- Juice of one lemon
- Sugar for rolling

### That's how it works

1. **Strain:** Strain the pulp through a lotte or coarse sieve to leave any peel residue behind. You will end up with a smooth puree.
2. **Weigh** and measure out the appropriate amount of sugar.
3. **Preserve:** Bring the puree, sugar and lemon juice to the boil in a wide, heavy-based saucepan. Cook over medium-low heat, stirring constantly. This takes 30 to 60 minutes. The mixture will splatter, so use a long wooden spoon and protect your hand.
4. **Done when:** the mixture becomes darker, separates from the bottom of the pot and when you pull it through with the spoon, a trace remains visible that only slowly closes.
5. **Spreading:** Line a baking tray with baking paper and smooth out the hot mixture to a thickness of about 1 to 1.5 centimeters.
6. **Drying:** Leave to dry in the oven at 50 degrees with a wooden spoon in the door for four to six hours, or in a warm, dry place for two to four days, covered with a cloth. Turn once in between as soon as the surface is no longer sticky.
7. **Cutting:** Cut into diamonds or squares with an oiled knife and roll all over in sugar.

Store quince bread in a tin can, layer by layer between baking paper, cool and dry. This way it lasts for several months. Without a sugar crust, it can also be kept in one piece and served in slices with cheese, just like in Spain.

## Quince compote and oven quinces

Not every quince has to turn into jelly. To make a **compote**, peel two large quinces, remove the core and cut them into narrow wedges. Bring 400 ml of water to the boil with 80 g of sugar, the juice of half a lemon and a slit vanilla pod. Add the wedges and simmer, covered, gently for 20 to 30 minutes, until soft but not falling apart. The compote tastes great warm with semolina porridge or pancakes and cold with yoghurt.

To make **oven quinces**, halve the fruit, remove the core and place them cut side up in a baking dish. Place a teaspoon of honey, a piece of butter and some cinnamon in each cavity. Pour a finger's breadth of water or apple juice into the mold, cover it and bake the quinces at 180 degrees for about an hour until they are soft. In the last 15 minutes, uncovered, they get a slightly caramelized surface. Vanilla ice cream, sour cream or a dollop of quark go well with this.

## A quince tree in the garden

Quince trees remain small, rarely grow higher than four to six meters and are usually self-fertile, so a single tree is sufficient. They only bloom in May with large, delicate pink flowers and therefore often escape the late frosts. It is harvested in October, before severe frosts arrive, because frozen quinces turn brown and can no longer be stored. A warm, protected location promotes the aroma.

## Frequently asked questions

### My quince jelly didn't set. What now?

Bring it to the boil again, add a little preserving sugar or the juice of a lemon, depending on the amount, and simmer for three to four minutes. Then do the gelling test again.

### Why did my jelly turn reddish?

This is normal and a nice sign. When cooked for a long time, red color pigments are formed from substances in the quince. The longer and slower you cook, the deeper the color will be.

### Can I eat quinces raw?

Some varieties are soft enough to eat raw in thin slices. The usual varieties in Germany are raw but hard and bitter. When cooked, they develop their aroma much better.

### Can I freeze the fruit pulp?

Yes. If you don't have time for quince bread after making the jelly, strain the pulp, fill it in portions into cans and freeze it. Once thawed, you can process it like fresh marrow, into quince bread or with a little sugar and cinnamon to make a quick quince puree.

### How long does quince jelly last?

If stored unopened and stored in a cool, dark place, it will last about a year. Once opened, the jar belongs in the refrigerator and should be used within a few weeks. If mold appears, throw away the entire glass, not just the affected area.
