When it gets dark early outside and the smell of roasted chestnuts wafts over the Christmas market, the coziest time of the year begins for many people. You don't have to buy chestnuts in a bag from the stand: they are just as good in the oven, and as a glazed accompaniment to red cabbage, goose or game, they are one of the most beautiful autumn ingredients of all. The only thing that's nerve-wracking the first time is peeling. With the right cut and a little steam, it's surprisingly easy.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Chestnuts are in season from October to December. Good fruits are heavy, shiny and plump.
> - Cut the curved side crosswise or crosswise, otherwise they will burst in the oven.
> - Roast in the oven at 200 degrees top and bottom heat for 20 to 25 minutes, with a bowl of water in the oven.
> - While they are still hot, wrap them in a damp cloth, let them rest briefly and then peel while they are warm.
> - Horse chestnuts from parks are not edible. You can recognize chestnuts by the tip and the prickly shell with many fine spines.

## Chestnuts or chestnuts?

Botanically, both come from the sweet chestnut, a tree that grows in southern Europe and in mild areas of Germany, such as the Palatinate, the Upper Rhine and the Taunus. **Chestnuts** or chestnuts are the name given to cultivated varieties with large, heart-shaped fruits, of which usually only one sits in the spiky shell. Their seed coats are easier to peel off and they taste sweeter. **Chestnuts** in the narrower sense are the smaller fruits of wild-growing trees. They sit in pairs or threes in the shell and are often divided inside by a skin. You can prepare both in the same way, chestnuts are just more convenient.

Most chestnuts in the trade come from Italy, France, Spain, Portugal or Turkey. In the Palatinate, the local chestnuts are called “Keschde”, and there is their own chestnut week in October.

### Caution: Horse chestnuts are poisonous

Horse chestnuts from parks and avenues are not relatives of the sweet chestnut and should not be eaten. They contain saponins, which irritate the stomach and intestines. The difference is easy to see:

| feature | Chestnut / chestnut | Horse Chestnut |
|---|---|---|
| cover | densely covered with many fine, long spines, like a hedgehog ball | green, leathery, with a few short, thick spines |
| fruit | flat to heart-shaped, with a light, hairy tip | round, smooth, without tip, with a large light spot |
| size | often smaller than a horse chestnut | big and shiny |

If you collect yourself, only choose fruits that you can identify with certainty.

### Collect yourself: what is allowed

In autumn there are often chestnuts on the paths in the Palatinate and Baden forests. According to the Federal Nature Conservation Act, you are allowed to collect wild fruits in small quantities for your own needs as long as the area is not under special protection. This does not apply to trees in gardens, orchards or on private property. Wear sturdy gloves because the spiky shells will pierce through thin fabric. Open the covers with the soles of your shoes by gently rolling them back and forth.

## Shopping and storage

Good chestnuts are heavy for their size, shiny and have a plump, taut skin. When you press on one, it shouldn't give way. Dull, light fruits with holes are dried out or infected with chestnut moth larvae.

A simple test before roasting: Place the chestnuts in a bowl of water. Fruits that float to the top are often hollow, dried out, or infected. Sort them out.

Fresh chestnuts are live fruits and lose water quickly. They will last for about one to two weeks in a paper or cloth bag in the vegetable compartment of the refrigerator. At room temperature in a plastic bag they quickly mold. You can freeze larger quantities after roasting and peeling.

## Cutting in: the most important step

The shell is tight. When heated, steam forms inside and an uncut chestnut can burst with a loud bang. Place the chestnut flat side on a board and use a small, sharp knife to cut the curved side. Two variants have proven successful:

- **Cross cut:** a cross about two centimeters across the curved side.
- **Cross-section:** a long cut across the arch, almost from side to side. Many people find that this makes chestnuts even easier to peel.

Cut through the hard shell and a little into the seed coat, but not deep into the pulp. There are special chestnut knives with a short, curved blade that are safer to hold. When cutting, keep your fingers away from the cutting direction; the round bowl will slip away easily.

## Roast chestnuts in the oven

1. Preheat the oven to 200 degrees top and bottom heat.
2. Soak the cut chestnuts in water for ten minutes. This way they stay juicier on the inside.
3. Place them on a baking sheet, cut side up.
4. Place an ovenproof bowl of water on the bottom of the oven. The steam prevents the chestnuts from drying out.
5. Roast for 20 to 25 minutes, until the cuts have opened wide and the flesh is golden.
6. Wrap them hot in a damp kitchen towel and let them rest for five minutes.
7. Peel them while they are still warm: break the shell and peel off the light brown, furry seed skin. Cold chestnuts are much more difficult to peel.

The seed skin tastes bitter and should be removed as completely as possible. If it gets stuck in the furrows, it helps to briefly put the chestnut in the oven again.

## Roast in the pan

Without an oven, you can do this in a heavy pan with a lid, preferably made of cast iron. Add the cut chestnuts without fat, cover them and roast them over medium heat for about 20 minutes. Shake the pan every few minutes to ensure nothing burns. There are perforated chestnut pans for the wood stove or fireplace, in which the fruit roasts directly over the embers.

## Cooking instead of roasting

You can also cook chestnuts for purees, fillings and soups. Cut them, put them in boiling water and cook for 20 to 30 minutes. Take them out in portions and peel them, leaving the rest in the hot water. Cooked chestnuts are softer and taste milder than roasted ones.

If you don't have time to peel, you can use pre-cooked, vacuum-packed chestnuts. They're more expensive, but a good shortcut for red cabbage and stuffing.

## Glazed chestnuts with red cabbage

Glazed chestnuts are the classic accompaniment to [apple red cabbage](/kueche/apfelrotkohl/), roast goose and game. For four people:

- 400 g peeled chestnuts, roasted, boiled or pre-cooked
- 2 tbsp sugar
- 20g butter
- 100 ml vegetable or chicken stock
- Salt, optionally a sprig of thyme

Allow the sugar to caramelize in a pan without stirring. Add the butter and toss the chestnuts in it until they are shiny all over. Deglaze with the broth and let everything simmer over medium heat until the liquid is syrupy and coats the chestnuts. Season with a pinch of salt. A dash of apple juice instead of some of the broth goes well with red cabbage.

## Chestnut soup in 30 minutes

Sauté a finely diced onion and a stick of celery in butter, add 300 grams of peeled chestnuts and a small diced apple and pour in 800 milliliters of stock. Let everything simmer for 20 minutes, puree the soup finely and refine it with a dash of cream, salt, pepper and a pinch of nutmeg. Toasted bread cubes or a few crumbled chestnuts on top make them festive.

## Chestnut filling for goose and duck

In many families, St. Martin's goose and Christmas roast include a filling with chestnuts and apples. Soak two rolls from the day before in a little lukewarm milk and squeeze them out. Fry a finely diced onion in butter until translucent. Mix the roll and onion with 300 g of coarsely chopped, peeled chestnuts, two tart apples in cubes, an egg, a tablespoon of dried marjoram, chopped parsley, salt and pepper. Fill the mixture loosely into the bird as it will expand as it cooks. If it doesn't fit, you can bake it in a small tin, basting it with a little of the pan juices.

## The chestnut as a staple food

In the mountainous regions of southern Europe, in Ticino, Corsica, the Cévennes and the Apennines, chestnuts were a staple food for centuries. They were called the bread of the poor. The fruits were dried in kilns over a low fire, peeled and ground into flour. This resulted in porridge, flat cakes and cakes that are still around today: In Tuscany they bake the flat castagnaccio made from chestnut flour, water, olive oil, rosemary and pine nuts; in Corsica they make polenta from chestnut flour.

This role is easily explained from a nutritional perspective. Unlike walnuts or hazelnuts, chestnuts contain little fat but a lot of starch. In the kitchen, they behave more like a potato than a nut: they are filling, become soft when cooked and can be made into puree and flour. They also provide fiber, potassium and B vitamins.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you eat chestnuts raw?

Raw, they are hard, taste bitter and are difficult to digest. Their starch only becomes soft and sweet when heated. Therefore, always eat chestnuts roasted or boiled.

### How long do roasted chestnuts last?

Peeled and covered in the fridge for about three days. They last for several months frozen and are then suitable for soup, puree and fillings.

### Are chestnuts gluten free?

Yes, chestnuts do not contain gluten. Chestnut flour is therefore often used for gluten-free pastries. For ready-made products, you should still check the list of ingredients.

### Why are my chestnuts so difficult to peel?

They are usually already cooled down, roasted too short or not cut deep enough. Next time, cut safely through the seed skin, let the hot chestnuts rest in the damp cloth and peel them while they are warm. Cooked chestnuts are often easier to peel than roasted ones.

### What is chestnut cream?

Sweetened chestnut cream, known in France as crème de marrons, is made from pureed chestnuts, sugar and vanilla and sold in jars. It tastes good with yoghurt and in crepes and is the basis of the dessert Mont Blanc, where it is piped into a small mountain with cream.
