Cooking seasonally means, above all, cutting vegetables. A basket from the weekly market means onions, carrots, leeks, cabbage, pumpkin and herbs that need to be cleaned, peeled and chopped. With a blunt knife and an awkward posture, this becomes torture and the risk of cutting yourself increases. With a sharp knife and a few safe techniques, however, it is quick, relaxed and almost meditative.

You don't need an expensive knife collection or cooking school. Two or three good knives, a sturdy board and a little practice are enough.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - For most work, a chef's knife with an 18 to 23 centimeter blade, a small paring knife and a serrated bread knife are sufficient.
> - A sharp knife is safer than a dull one because you need less pressure and it won't slip.
> - When using the claw grip, you keep the fingertips of your leading hand tucked in. The blade slides along the knuckles.
> - A damp cloth under the board prevents it from slipping.
> - Good knives don't belong in the dishwasher. A sharpening steel keeps the cutting edge in shape and a whetstone sharpens it again.

## Which knives you need

| Knife | Blade length | For what |
|---|---|---|
| Chef's knife | 18-23cm | almost everything: vegetables, herbs, meat, pumpkin |
| Paring knife (office or vegetable knife) | 7-10cm | Peeling, cleaning, small tasks in your hand |
| Serrated bread knife | 20-26cm | Bread, tomatoes, cake |
| Santoku (alternative to chef's knife) | 16-18cm | Vegetables, herbs, especially for sliding cuts |
| peeler | – | Potatoes, carrots, asparagus and pumpkin |

The chef's knife is the most important tool. Take it in your hand before you buy it: It should be well balanced and comfortable to hold. A heavy knife cuts hard vegetables such as pumpkin or turnip more easily, while a lighter knife is more maneuverable for delicate work.

### Stainless, carbon or damask?

Most kitchen knives are made of stainless steel. It is easy to care for and forgives if the knife is left wet. Carbon steel knives can be sharpened even sharper, but if not dried immediately they will rust and develop a dark patina over time. Damascus knives consist of many layers of different steels, the pattern of which, above all, looks beautiful. The pattern says little about the cutting edge. What is more important is the hardness, stated in HRC: European knives are usually between 56 and 58, Japanese knives are often 60 and above. Harder blades stay sharp longer, but are more sensitive to impact and twisting and do not belong on bones or frozen foods.

## The cutting board

A good board is large enough to prevent vegetables from falling, at least 30 by 40 centimeters. Wooden boards protect the cutting edge and are comfortable to work with. After use, they must be cleaned with hot water and detergent and dried well. Plastic boards are lighter and dishwasher safe, but over time they develop deep grooves that harbor germs. Replace them if they are severely ridged. Glass, stone and metal boards quickly dull any knife.

Place a damp kitchen towel or non-slip mat under the board. A slipping board is one of the most common causes of cuts.

## The right posture

### The cutting hand

Don't grab the chef's knife at the back of the handle, but rather further forward: your thumb and index finger grasp the blade on the side directly in front of the handle, the remaining three fingers surround the handle. This seems unusual at first, but it gives you significantly more control.

### The claw grip

The other hand holds the vegetables. Curl your fingers so that the fingertips point inward and the front knuckles are the outermost point, like a claw. The thumb stays behind the fingers. The flat side of the blade rests against the knuckles and is guided by them. This means the blade cannot reach the fingertips. With each cut you push the claw back a little.

### movement

The knife cuts best in a forward and downward motion, not by simply pushing. When doing a rocker cut, the tip stays on the board, and you only raise and lower the back of the blade while pushing it slightly forward. When making a push cut, the entire knife is guided forwards and downwards through the vegetables. Let the knife work, not your strength.

## Basic techniques

### Dice onion

1. Cut the onion lengthwise from the tip to the root. The root stays attached, it holds the layers together.
2. Cut off the tip and peel off the shell.
3. Place one half cut side down on the board with the root facing away from you.
4. Cut them lengthwise into fine strips, just before the root, but not through.
5. Now cut crosswise into thin slices. Even dice fall. You dispose of the root.

Horizontal cuts parallel to the board, as often shown, are not necessary for small and medium-sized onions and only increase the risk of injury. A really sharp knife, which crushes fewer cells, and a cooled onion help against watery eyes.

### Carrots in sticks and cubes

Cut the peeled carrot into pieces about five centimeters long. Cut a thin slice from each piece so that it lies flat and stable. Cut it into slices of equal thickness, stack them and cut them into sticks. Fine pencils of around two millimeters are called juliennes, coarser ones of six to eight millimeters are called bâtonnets. If you cut the pencils crosswise, cubes are created. Very fine cubes of two to three millimeters are called brunoise. Pieces of equal size cook evenly, which is the real purpose of these techniques.

### Chop herbs

Herbs must be dry or they will become mushy when chopped. Pluck off the leaves, collect them in a pile and first cut them roughly. Then place the fingers of the guide hand flat on the back of the knife and chop back and forth with a rocking cut until the desired fineness is achieved. Don't chop for too long, otherwise the herbs will lose their juice and aroma. You cut the chives into rolls with a very sharp knife, not with scissors that crush them. Basil is best cut into strips: place the leaves on top of each other, roll them up and cut them crosswise into fine strips.

### Leeks, peppers and garlic

- **Leek:** Cut off the root and the dark green, hard leaf ends. Halve the stalk lengthwise, fan out the layers under running water and rinse out the soil that tends to hide between the leaves. Then lay the halves flat and cut them into half rings.
- **Peppers:** Cut a cap off the top and bottom, stand the pod up and cut the wall open on one side. Open it up into a long strip and use the flat knife to remove the core. The surface can now be easily cut into strips and cubes. The lids, when removed from the stem, also form cubes.
- **Garlic:** Place the clove on the board and press it briefly with the flat blade, then the peel will come off. Cut them into thin slices and chop them. To make a paste, sprinkle a pinch of coarse salt on top and grate the minced garlic on the board with the flat blade.

### Round and hard vegetables

For pumpkin, celery, turnips and cabbage: First create a flat surface. Cut off a slice and place the vegetables on this surface. Only then do you halve and chop it further. For very hard vegetables, press the back of the knife with the flat of your hand, never the blade itself.

## Sharpen and maintain

A knife becomes slightly duller with every cut. The fine cutting edge bends to the side before it is worn out.

- **Sharpening steel:** He straightens the bent edge again. Hold the knife at a shallow angle of about 15 to 20 degrees to the steel and pull it alternately left and right from heel to tip, five to ten times per side. You can do this before every major operation. A ceramic rod has a similar effect, but removes some material.
- **Whetstone:** If honing no longer helps, the cutting edge must be re-ground. Water stones with a coarse grit of around 1000 for grinding and a fine grit of between 3000 and 6000 for polishing are well suited for household use. The stone is previously soaked or moistened in water. The angle is particularly important here and should remain the same across the entire blade. If you are unsure, have your knives sharpened by a specialist shop.
- **Pull-through sharpeners:** They are comfortable, but often remove a lot of material and create a coarse edge. Fine for simple knives, less suitable for high-quality ones.

After using knives, wash them by hand with warm water and detergent, dry them immediately and store them in a knife block, on a magnetic strip or with a blade guard. The blades collide in the drawer and quickly become dull and jagged. In the dishwasher, the cutting edge and handle suffer from heat, aggressive cleaners and hitting each other.

## Safety rules

- Never catch a falling knife. Step back and let it fall.
- Do not put knives in the dishwashing water where they cannot be seen.
- Keep the tip down when wearing.
- After each cut, bring the fingers of the guide hand back into the claw position.
- For small cuts, clean the wound under clear water, cover it with a plaster and wear a finger cot or glove while continuing to work.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I know if my knife is sharp?

Cut a ripe tomato or a piece of paper. A sharp knife slides through the tomato skin without denting it and cuts paper smoothly without tearing.

### Is a santoku better than a chef's knife?

Not better, but different. The Santoku has a flatter cutting edge and is particularly suitable for sliding cuts on vegetables. The European chef's knife is more curved and intended for cradle cutting. What matters is what you feel more comfortable with.

### Do I need a special paring knife for tomatoes?

A sharp chef's knife is enough. If your knives aren't quite sharp, a small serrated knife works well for tomatoes and other smooth-skinned vegetables.
