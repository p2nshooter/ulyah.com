Drying is the oldest way of stockpiling and perhaps the simplest. It doesn't need sugar, vinegar or jars with rubber rings, just warmth, air and patience. Dried apple rings make a snack that lasts for weeks, dried plums make winter cakes moist, dried porcini mushrooms add depth to any sauce, and a bunch of summer peppermint becomes tea in January. Because the water is removed from the food, bacteria and mold can no longer grow. In order for this to work reliably, they have to be really dry, and that is the point at which many beginners stop too early.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Fruit dries at 50 to 60 degrees, herbs at a maximum of 40 degrees, mushrooms at 40 to 50 degrees.
> - Always leave the door in the oven slightly open to allow moisture to escape, for example with a wooden spoon.
> - Fruit is ready when it is leathery and flexible and no more moisture comes out when cut through. Mushrooms and herbs must rustle and break.
> - After drying, allow to cool completely and store in an airtight, dark and dry place.
> - Check the glass in the first few days: If it fogs up on the inside, the contents need to dry out.

## Three methods in comparison

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages | Suitable for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air drying | costs nothing, gentle | takes days, needs dry, warm air | Herbs, thinly sliced mushrooms, chilies |
| Oven | Available everywhere, easy to control | high power consumption over many hours | small amounts of fruit and mushrooms |
| Dehydrator | uniform temperature, many floors, more economical | Purchase, space requirements | larger quantities, regular drying |

Air drying is often the best choice for herbs. An automatic dehydrator is worthwhile for fruit if you want to dry apples or plums every year. The oven is enough to start with.

## Dry apple rings

Tart, aromatic apples such as Boskoop, Elstar, Jonagold or Cox Orange make the best apple rings. Which variety is suitable for what can also be found in our article about [apple varieties](/kueche/apfelsorten-und-gedeckter-apfelkuchen/).

1. Wash apples and remove the core with an apple corer. Peeling is not necessary, but peeled rings are more tender.
2. Cut into rings of 3 to 5 millimeters. Uniform thickness is important, otherwise some rings will be finished and others will still be wet. A vegetable slicer helps.
3. To keep the rings light, place them briefly in water with lemon juice, about the juice of one lemon per liter. Drain and pat dry.
4. Place side by side on baking racks lined with baking paper or on the inserts of the dehydrator. The rings must not overlap.
5. Dry at 50 to 60 degrees, in the oven with a fan and a wooden spoon in the door. Turn over halfway through.
6. Depending on the thickness and variety, apple rings take 5 to 8 hours.

They are ready when they feel leathery and flexible, no longer stick and there are no wet spots when they are torn through. For crispy apple chips, cut thinner and dry longer until the rings break.

## Dry plums and pears

Plums dry more slowly because the skin retains water. Halve the fruit, remove the stone and place it cut side up on the grid. At 55 to 60 degrees they need 12 to 24 hours, often overnight or half a day in the dehydrator. They are ready when they no longer feel moist when squeezed and no juice comes out. Dried plums remain soft and slightly sticky.

You cut pears like apples into wedges or rings. They are sweeter and brown more quickly, which is why the lemon bath is particularly worthwhile. In many regions, whole small pears used to be dried as “Hutzeln” and used in winter for Hutzelbrot, a fruit bread from southern Germany.

## Dry mushrooms

Porcini mushrooms, chestnut mushrooms and shiitake can be dried very well, as can chanterelles, although they often become tough. Only use perfect, solid specimens. Do not wash the mushrooms, but clean them with a brush or a knife. Cut them into slices 3 to 5 millimeters thick.

Air dry mushroom slices on newspaper or strung on a thread in a warm, airy place in two to four days. In the oven or dehydrator they need about 6 to 10 hours at 40 to 50 degrees. They are ready when they rustle and break like thin pieces of cardboard.

Soak dried mushrooms in warm water for 20 to 30 minutes before using. The soaking water contains a lot of flavor. Pour it through a fine sieve or coffee filter to remove sand and use it for sauce or risotto. You can read how to clean and fry fresh mushrooms in our article about [chanterelles and porcini mushrooms](/kueche/pfifferlinge-und-steinpilze/). Only collect wild mushrooms if you can identify them with certainty and, if in doubt, have them checked at a mushroom advisory center.

## Dry herbs

It is best to harvest herbs for tea and spices on a dry morning when the dew has dried up and the sun is not yet too hot. Shortly before flowering, the aromatic content of many herbs is highest.

**Good for drying:** Peppermint, lemon balm, sage, thyme, oregano, rosemary, savory, marjoram, bay leaf.

**It's better to freeze or use fresh:** Chives, parsley, dill, basil and chervil lose a lot of flavor when dried.

This is how it works:

1. Do not wash herbs if they are clean. Rinse contaminated herbs briefly and shake dry very well.
2. Tie into small, loose bouquets and hang with the leaves facing down in a shady, airy and warm place. Direct sun bleaches the leaves and destroys flavors.
3. After a week or two, the leaves rustle and break when rubbed.
4. Choose a maximum of 35 to 40 degrees in the oven or dehydrator. At higher temperatures the essential oils evaporate.
5. Strip the leaves from the stems and keep them whole if possible. Grind only before use.

## Tomatoes, chilies and vegetable chips

Vegetables can also be dried. Tomatoes are the most popular: Halve small bottle or cherry tomatoes, remove the seeds from larger fruits, lightly salt the cut surfaces and place them on the grid with the cut surface facing up. At 50 to 60 degrees they need 8 to 12 hours, large halves even longer. They are ready when they are leathery and no more juice comes out when pressed.

The safest way to store dried tomatoes is to keep them dry in a jar and soak them in warm water if necessary. If you want to put them in oil, you should know: There is no air under oil, and bacteria such as Clostridium botulinum can multiply in it if there is still residual moisture in the vegetables. Homemade tomatoes, herbs or garlic in oil therefore belong in the refrigerator and are used quickly, within a few days.

Use a needle and thread to thread the chilies through the stems to form a chain and hang them in an airy, dry place. After three to four weeks they are dry and break. To make vegetable chips made from beetroot, parsnips or zucchini, slice into wafer-thin slices, season them lightly and dry them at 50 to 60 degrees until they are crispy.

## Store correctly

Allow anything dried to cool completely before packaging. Warm goods form condensation in the glass. Fill fruit, mushrooms and herbs into clean, dry screw-top jars or cans and keep them dark and dry.

Check the jars daily for the first week. If the inside fogs up or the pieces feel soft again, the residual moisture was too high. Then dry again. Dispose of moldy pieces or glasses with a musty smell completely.

Well-dried fruit lasts six to twelve months, mushrooms and herbs about a year. Afterwards they are not spoiled, but they lose a lot of their aroma.

## Two ideas for the storage container

**Homemade tea from the garden:** Mix two parts dried peppermint with one part lemon balm and add a few thyme leaves to taste. Pour boiling water over a heaped teaspoon per cup and let the tea steep for five to ten minutes. In a dark glass labeled with the date, the mixture also makes a nice souvenir.

**Herb Salt:** Grind dried herbs such as rosemary, thyme, oregano, and savory and mix with coarse sea salt, about one part herbs to three parts salt. The salt is freshly ground at the table in a spice mill and is used to season potatoes, baked vegetables and quark.

## Save electricity when drying

An oven that runs for eight hours with the door open uses a lot of energy. Therefore, only use it when it is fully loaded, distribute the goods on several grids and work with circulating air at a low temperature. An automatic dehydrator is designed for long running times and requires significantly less electricity. On dry, warm late summer days, herbs and thin mushroom slices dry in the air, even without electricity. Then put a fly screen or gauze over it to keep insects away, and bring the goods into the house overnight, otherwise they will absorb moisture from the air again.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why are my apple rings turning brown?

This is due to enzymes that react with oxygen. A quick bath in lemon water slows down tanning. It cannot be prevented completely without additives, and darker rings taste just as good.

### Can I dry on the heater?

For herbs and mushrooms, a grille over the heater works if air can circulate. Fruit dries too slowly there and can become moldy before it is dry.

### How much is left after drying?

One kilogram of apples produces around 100 to 150 grams of apple rings, and one kilogram of fresh mushrooms produces around 100 grams of dried mushrooms. So expect large quantities of fresh goods.

### Do I have to sulphurize fruit before drying?

No. Store-bought dried apricots are often sulphured to keep them light and soft, and then have the words “sulfured” on the package. You don't need this at home. A bath in lemon water is enough, and unsulphured fruit simply becomes a little darker.

### How do I know that herbs have been dried too hot?

They are brown instead of green and barely smell or only smell like hay. The essential oils then evaporate. Next time, set the temperature lower or air dry.
