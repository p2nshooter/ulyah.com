When almost nothing grows outside in winter, there is a leafy vegetable that is unaffected by frost: lamb's lettuce. Its small, dark green rosettes survive temperatures well below zero and are harvested outdoors from October to March. This makes it the most important fresh salad of the cold season.

Lamb's lettuce has many names. In southern Germany it is called field lettuce, in Switzerland Nüsslisalat, in parts of Hesse and the Palatinate Rapunzel, in the Rhineland mouse-ear lettuce and elsewhere sun swirl or Rapunzel lettuce. The taste is unmistakable: delicate, slightly nutty and a little sweet. In Baden and Württemberg it is traditionally dressed with a lukewarm potato dressing that coats it in a creamy manner. Once you've tried this dressing, you'll hardly want to eat lamb's lettuce any other way.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Lamb's lettuce is in season outdoors from October to March and is frost hardy.
> - It is harvested in small rosettes. When cleaning, just cut off the root end so that the rosettes hold together.
> - There is often sand between the leaves. Therefore, wash it in a bowl of water in several passes.
> - If left unwashed, it will last one to two days in the fridge, but wrapped in a damp cloth it will last a little longer.
> - The classic Swabian dressing is made from a cooked floury potato, broth, vinegar, mustard and oil.

## Lamb's lettuce, valerian and rapunzel

Botanically, lamb's lettuce belongs to the valerian family; its scientific name is Valerianella locusta. However, it only shares the relationship with valerian, not the calming effect. The name Rapunzel is exciting: Opinions differ as to whether the Brothers Grimm fairy tale is named after the lamb's lettuce or after the Rapunzel bellflower, whose roots were also eaten in the past. In many regions, lamb’s lettuce is still “Rapunzel” to this day.

## Shopping and storage

Fresh lamb's lettuce has firm, strong green leaves that are not wilted, yellow or mushy. Pay particular attention to the bottom of the package or bag: rotten or wet leaves will quickly spoil everything else. Larger rosettes from the field usually taste stronger than the very small, delicate rosettes from the greenhouse.

Lamb's lettuce is sensitive and should be eaten as quickly as possible. Store it unwashed in a can or bag with a slightly damp kitchen towel in the vegetable compartment. It lasts one to two days, sometimes three. Do not store it next to apples or tomatoes, whose ripening gas will cause them to wilt more quickly. If it has become a little limp, refresh it again in a bowl of ice-cold water for ten minutes.

## Clean lamb's lettuce

Cleaning lamb's lettuce is a bit tedious, but it's worth it. Nothing is more unpleasant than sand between your teeth.

1. Use a small knife to just cut off the root end of each rosette. If you cut off too much, the rosette will fall apart into individual leaves. It's not a bad thing, but it looks less pretty and the leaves wilt more quickly.
2. Remove yellow, wilted, or damaged leaves.
3. Place the lettuce in a large bowl of cold water and move it gently with your hands. Let it sit for a minute so the sand and soil sink to the bottom.
4. Instead of draining the water, lift the lettuce out with your hands. Otherwise the sand will end up back on the leaves.
5. Repeat the process with fresh water until no more sand settles at the bottom of the bowl. Three passes are often necessary for outdoor lettuce.
6. Carefully spin the salad dry in the salad spinner. Wet leaves dilute the dressing.

## Swabian potato dressing

The dressing is mild, creamy and slightly warm. The potato binds oil and vinegar into a creamy sauce that clings to the tender leaves without overwhelming them.

For 4 people as a starter or side dish:

- 150 g lamb's lettuce
- 1 floury potato (about 150 g)
- 150 ml hot vegetable or meat broth
- 1 small onion or 1 shallot
- 3 tablespoons white wine vinegar or mild apple cider vinegar
- 1 tsp medium hot mustard
- 1 pinch of sugar
- 4 tablespoons neutral oil, for example rapeseed oil
- Salt, freshly ground pepper
- 1 tbsp walnut oil if desired

### Preparation

1. Boil the potatoes with the skin on in salted water until soft, about 20 to 25 minutes. Drain them, peel them while they are still hot and press them through a potato press or mash them very finely with a fork.
2. Dice the onion very finely.
3. Mix the warm potato with the hot broth until a smooth, creamy mixture forms. Add onion, vinegar, mustard, sugar, salt and pepper.
4. Gradually beat in the oil with a whisk. The dressing should be thick, like liquid cream. If it is too thick, add a little more broth. If you want it even finer, puree it briefly with the hand blender.
5. Taste vigorously. The dressing may taste a little too sour and salty because the salad will absorb a lot of it.
6. Mix the dressing and salad just before serving, while the dressing is still lukewarm. Lamb's lettuce collapses quickly.

The dressing will keep in the fridge for two days. Before serving, warm it up briefly and dilute it with a little broth.

## Typical mistakes when turning on

- **Dressed too early:** Lamb's lettuce collapses after just a few minutes in the dressing. Mix at the table first.
- **Wet leaves:** Water dilutes the dressing and allows it to drip off. Spin the salad thoroughly.
- **Too much dressing:** The tender leaves tolerate less sauce than robust lettuce. Better pour more.
- **Dressing straight from the fridge:** The potato dressing becomes solid and lumpy when cold. Warm it up briefly and stir it with a little broth until smooth.
- **Too strong olive oil:** A very peppery oil will mask the nutty taste. Rapeseed oil, sunflower oil or a dash of walnut oil are better.

## Lamb's lettuce with bacon and croutons

A heartier version works well as an appetizer before a winter meal.

For 4 people:

- 150 g lamb's lettuce
- 100 g streaky bacon in cubes
- 2 slices of white bread or farmer's bread from the day before
- 1 shallot
- 3 tbsp red wine vinegar
- 1 teaspoon mustard, 1 teaspoon honey
- 4 tbsp oil
- Salt, pepper

1. Fry the bacon cubes in a pan without fat until crispy. Take them out and leave the fat in the pan.
2. Cut the bread into cubes and toast it in the bacon fat until golden brown. If you like it lighter, use a little butter.
3. Mix the vinegar, mustard, honey, finely diced shallot, salt and pepper and beat in the oil.
4. Mix the salad and dressing, divide it between plates and sprinkle bacon and croutons on top.

For a vegetarian version, replace the bacon with toasted walnuts or pumpkin seeds and add a few strips of pear or apple and some crumbled goat cheese.

- **With orange and fennel:** Orange fillets and very thinly sliced ​​fennel bring freshness.
- **With egg:** A waxy-soft boiled egg and chives make the salad a small meal.
- **With mushrooms:** Fried mushrooms or king oyster mushrooms, added to the salad while still warm.
- **As pesto:** Lamb's lettuce can be made into a mild pesto with nuts, hard cheese and oil.

## Lamb's lettuce soup with potatoes

If there is more lamb's lettuce left over from the weekly market than fits in the salad bowl, it becomes a bright green soup. Sauté an onion in butter until translucent, add two floury potatoes in cubes and pour in 700 ml of vegetable stock. After about 20 minutes the potatoes will be soft. Remove the pot from the heat, add 100 g of cleaned lamb's lettuce and immediately puree the soup until fine. The lettuce should not cook, otherwise it will lose its color. Season with cream, salt, pepper, nutmeg and a squeeze of lemon juice and serve the soup with a few fresh leaves and toasted bread cubes.

## What's in lamb's lettuce

For a leafy salad, lamb's lettuce is surprisingly nutrient-rich. It provides provitamin A in the form of beta-carotene, vitamin C, folate and, compared to lettuce or iceberg lettuce, significantly more iron. The body absorbs vegetable iron better when vitamin C is involved at the same time. A squeeze of lemon in the dressing or a few orange fillets in the salad are not only a good idea in terms of taste. Beta-carotene is fat-soluble, so a good oil should always be included in the dressing.

## Lamb's lettuce in the garden

Lamb's lettuce is one of the easiest vegetables to grow in the winter garden. It is sown directly into the bed from August to early October and germinates well in cool temperatures. Depending on when you sow, you can harvest from October to spring. A fleece protects in very severe frost, but it is usually not necessary. It also grows well in a box on the balcony.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does lamb's lettuce contain a lot of nitrates?

Like all leaf lettuces that grow in low light in winter, lamb's lettuce can contain comparatively high levels of nitrates. This is not a problem for adults if consumed normally. Outdoor lettuce usually contains less than greenhouse lettuce. It is best to prepare the salad fresh and not leave it dressed for long.

### Can I steam lamb's lettuce?

Yes, briefly steamed like young spinach, it tastes mild and goes well with potatoes or pasta. It collapses a lot, so expect large quantities.

### Why is my lamb's lettuce bitter?

Lamb's lettuce is usually not bitter. A bitter or musty taste indicates old goods or spoiled leaves in the package. Sort out thoroughly and wash the lettuce carefully.

### Can lamb's lettuce be frozen?

Not as a salad because the tender leaves become mushy when thawed. For soup or pesto, you can wash it and freeze it, drained well, and use it frozen.
