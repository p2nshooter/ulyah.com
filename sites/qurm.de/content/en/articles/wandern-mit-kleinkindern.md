Hiking with a small child is completely different than hiking with adults. The goal is not the summit, but the path to get there, in the literal sense: the stream into which you can throw stones, the fallen tree over which you have to balance, the snail on the side of the path. If you accept this, you can spend wonderful days outside with two to five year olds. On the other hand, those who want to “complete” a route usually experience tears, defiance and a frustrating journey back with a child on their shoulders.

This guide shows you how to choose tours for small children, what is realistic and how to keep motivation high while on the road.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Plan based on time, not miles. Two to three hours outside is a good time for small children.
> - Variety beats views: streams, rocks, animals along the way and playgrounds are more important than a panoramic view.
> - Short stages with goals that the child understands keep motivation high.
> - Sufficient snacks, water and a change of clothing are mandatory.
> - For tired legs, a stretcher or an all-terrain vehicle is required, depending on the route.

## What is realistic?

Small children have their own pace, and that depends heavily on their form on the day, the weather and the tour. As a rough guide, but it varies greatly from child to child:

| Dude | Own walking distance (rough) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 2 years | a few hundred meters to about 1-2 km | Plan to carry a lot, short sections |
| 3 years | about 2-4 km | with lots of breaks, preferably on varied paths |
| 4 years | about 3-5 km | Motivation through stories and goals |
| 5 years | about 4-7 km | first small peaks possible, with few meters in altitude |

These values are not goals, but upper limits on good days. Time is much more important than the route. Expect to play at a fraction of adult speed, often just one to two kilometers per hour, plus long breaks in play. High altitudes are particularly stressful for small children. For the first tours, an altitude of 100 to 200 meters is sufficient.

## Find the right tour

The best toddler tour is varied, short and has a way out. Pay attention to these points when choosing:

- **Water:** A stream, a pond or a waterfall is the highlight for children. Plan enough time to throw stones and build dams.
- **Narrow paths:** Wide forest highways are boring. A path with roots, stones and curves feels like an adventure to a child.
- **Interim destinations:** A lookout tower, a hut with a playground, a large rock. Any tangible goal motivates more than “one more hour.”
- **Circular routes or shortcuts:** You can shorten the tour at any time.
- **Shade:** In summer, forest paths and valleys are more pleasant than open slopes.
- **Safety:** Avoid unsecured cliff edges, rocky plateaus without railings and paths directly on steep embankments.

Many regions now have special themed and adventure trails with stations, puzzles or barefoot sections. They are often ideal for the first hikes because they offer exactly the short sections and intermediate destinations that small children need.

## Motivation on the go

Children don't hike for the exercise, but for the experiences. A few ideas that have proven successful in many families:

1. **Let the child lead.** With the task of finding the path marker, running along becomes a mission. Even three-year-olds can spot a colored symbol on the tree.
2. **Tell stories.** The path leads to the giants' castle, the stream is a river full of treasures. A story that develops along the tour often extends over several kilometers.
3. **Collect.** A small bag for stones, cones or leaves makes the journey exciting. Be careful not to pick or take anything in protected areas.
4. **Hiking enthusiasts.** Things almost always go better with another child in the group. Children pull each other along.
5. **Small rituals.** The summit chocolate bar, the bell on the backpack, the fixed break at the big stone: rituals provide structure.
6. **No pressure.** If the child doesn't want to anymore, it's not a question of parenting. A break, a snack or a pat on the shoulders often solves more than any argument.

## Breaks and provisions

Small children have small stomachs and high energy needs. Plan a short snack and drink break every 30 to 45 minutes, even if the child doesn't ask for it. The following have proven themselves well:

- Pieces of bread, pretzels, rice cakes
- Fruit that doesn't crush, such as apples or grapes in a can
- Vegetable sticks, cheese cubes
- Nuts only from an age when there is no longer a risk of choking
- For motivation: a small reward at the finish

Water or unsweetened tea are the best drinks. Give each child who can already drink their own small bottle their own. Children often don't drink enough because they forget that they are thirsty while playing.

## Equipment for toddlers

Young children don't need expensive special equipment, but a few things make a difference:

- **Sturdy shoes with non-slip soles**, ideally already worn in. Rubber boots are unfavorable on longer journeys because your feet sweat in them and have little support.
- **Change of clothing**, especially socks and pants. Anyone who has a stream nearby will also use it.
- **Rain protection:** Mud pants and rain jacket are ideal for small children because they can walk through puddles without worry.
- **Sun protection:** Hat with brim or neck protection, sunscreen.
- **Your own little backpack** that only contains a stuffed animal and a snack. Children love carrying their own luggage as long as it is light.
- **A carrier for when you're tired.** For two-year-olds, a back carrier is almost indispensable; for older ones, a baby carrier or the shoulders is often enough.

Strollers are only suitable for wide, well-developed paths. Off-road vehicles with large wheels can also manage gravel paths, but there are stairs, root paths and narrow places.

## Weather and season

Children cool down more quickly and overheat more quickly than adults. In summer, make sure you have shade and enough fluids and avoid the hottest hours. In spring and fall, you dress them in the onion style so you can put on or take off layers depending on your movement. Children who are being carried freeze significantly more quickly than adults who are moving.

If there is a risk of thunderstorms, choose short tours with shelter options or postpone the hike. You can't get off quickly with small children.

## Ticks and other little things

After every hike through meadows and forests, check the child thoroughly for ticks in the evening, especially on the head, neck, behind the ears, in the back of the knees and in the diaper area. Long, light-colored clothing helps detect ticks earlier. A small first aid kit with plasters, disinfection and a tick card is always part of a tour with children.

## What has proven particularly useful as a goal

Many families have similar experiences about which landscapes are well received by small children. Stream valleys with flat banks are at the top of the list, closely followed by rocky landscapes with small caves and crevices, such as those found in the Palatinate Forest, Franconian Switzerland or the Elbe Sandstone Mountains. Short distances to a viewing tower also work well because climbing the stairs is an experience in itself. Small children are usually less enthusiastic about long, uniform paths through dense spruce forests or across open fields without variety.

A restaurant or a hut with a playground at the destination is the perfect reward for many families. Plan on taking a long break there. For the child, this is often the best part of the day, and for you it is the opportunity to replenish your energy reserves for the journey back.

## Games on the go

Little games turn a tough route into an adventure. For example, the following have proven successful:

- **I see something you don't see** with colors, shapes or animals from the surrounding area.
- **Nature Bingo:** Before the tour, draw a small card with things you want to find, such as a cone, a feather, a round leaf, a mushroom and a snail. Anyone who has discovered everything can choose the next break.
- **Stick Race:** At a bridge, everyone simultaneously throws a stick into the water on one side and sees whose stick comes out the other side first.
- **Looking for clues:** Animal tracks in the mud, feeding marks on cones, holes in the ground. A small magnifying glass turns every beetle into an event.
- **Counting and Estimating:** How many steps to the next tree? How many signposts to the hut?

## Nap on tour

Many toddlers still need a nap. Plan the tour so that it suits your child: either you start after sleep, or the child sleeps in the carrier on the way. Many children fall asleep surprisingly well while rocking while walking. Then make sure that the child's head is supported and put an extra layer on them because they cool down more quickly when they sleep. The sleep phase is well suited for the part of the route where there is little to discover.

## Frequently asked questions

### When can my child go hiking on their own?

That is very different. Some two-year-olds run enthusiastically for several kilometers, others want to be carried after ten minutes. What matters is not age, but rather that the child has fun. Start with short distances and increase slowly.

### What to do if the child doesn't want to go anymore?

First break, snack and drink. Hunger or tiredness are often behind it. If that doesn't help, wear it for a while or shorten the tour. Coercion usually leads to the child not even wanting to participate the next time.

### Do hikes with strollers make sense?

On wide, level paths, yes. However, a stretcher is the better choice for typical hiking trails because you will quickly reach your limits with bikes.

### How do I deal with minor falls and injuries?

Small children often stumble, and the shock is almost always greater than the damage. First comfort, then look at the place. Rinse small abrasions with clean water and put a plaster on them; A colorful consolation patch often works wonders. After a fall on the head, watch the child closely. If he vomits, becomes unusually sleepy, or behaves differently than usual, you need medical attention.
