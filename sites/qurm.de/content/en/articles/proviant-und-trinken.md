If you don't drink or eat enough while hiking, you often don't notice it until late: your legs get heavy, your mood drops, your head hurts, and suddenly the last climb feels twice as long. With the right provisions, however, you will stay productive all day, make better decisions and have more fun on the tour. It's not about expensive sports nutrition. A few basic rules about water, salt and energy, as well as food that can survive a day in a backpack, are completely sufficient.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Drink small amounts regularly, not just when you are thirsty. A rough guide is 0.3 to 0.5 liters per hour, more in hot weather and strenuous exertion.
> - You also lose salt in the heat and on long tours. Salty snacks or an electrolyte drink compensate for this.
> - Eat a small snack every hour or two, rather than one big meal at the top.
> - Whole grain bread, hard cheese, nuts, dried fruit, muesli bars and fruit that is not crushed are well suited.
> - Water from streams is not automatically clean. Filter or boil if no drinking water source is available.

## How much water do you need?

The amount of water you need depends on temperature, exertion, body weight and tendency to sweat. This table gives you an idea of the amount you should take with you in addition to breakfast and dinner:

| tour | Amount of water to take with you |
|---|---|
| 3 hours, cool, flat | 0.75 to 1 liter |
| 5 to 6 hours, mild, low mountain ranges | 1.5 to 2 liters |
| 6 to 8 hours, warm, lots of altitude | 2.5 to 3 liters or refill option |
| Midsummer, steep southern slopes | 3 liters and more, plan refill points |

If there are huts, restaurants or wells with drinking water along the way, you can carry less and refill. But check to see if they are open and plan a reserve.

**Drinking tips:**

- Drink a large glass of water before you start.
- Take a few sips every 20 to 30 minutes. Thirst is a late signal, especially in the elderly and children.
- A look at the urine helps: light yellow is good, dark yellow means you should drink more.
- More is not always better: Anyone who drinks large amounts of pure water over many hours and sweats a lot without absorbing salt can develop a dangerous salt deficiency. Therefore, combine water with salty snacks or electrolytes.

## Salt and electrolytes

When you sweat, you lose water and minerals, especially sodium. On short, cool tours this doesn't matter. On long, hot tours, missing minerals can contribute to fatigue, headaches and cramps.

Simple solutions:

- **Salty snacks:** Pretzel sticks, salted nuts, cheese, bread with butter and salt.
- **Schorle:** Fruit juice with mineral water in a ratio of one to two or one to three provides water, sugar and minerals.
- **Electrolyte powder or tablets:** practical for very hot days and multi-day tours.
- **Broth:** A cup of broth at the hut is an old, effective mountaineering trick.

What is also important in extreme heat is stated in [Heat when hiking](/ratgeber/hitze-beim-wandern/).

## Water from streams, wells and springs

Clear mountain water looks tempting, but can contain germs, especially below pastures, alpine pastures and huts. Even a spring is not automatically drinking water.

- **You can use fountains with a “drinking water” sign** without hesitation.
- **Fountains with a “No Drinking Water” sign** are only there for cooling and washing.
- **Streams and springs without labels:** filter with a water filter, boil or treat with sterilization tablets if you have no other choice.
- High above all the pastures, the risk is lower, but never completely ruled out.

## Energy: what you should eat

Depending on your weight, luggage and terrain, you burn significantly more energy when hiking than in everyday life. The body first resorts to quickly available carbohydrates. If the memory is empty, there is a risk of **hunger attack**: sudden weakness, tremors, dizziness, problems concentrating and a bad mood. Then the only thing that helps is quickly available sugar and a break, for example glucose, a piece of chocolate, a bar or juice.

It is better not to let it arise at all. Eat a snack every one to two hours and plan a real break with a larger snack.

## The best snacks for on the go

| Food | Advantages | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Whole grain bread or rolls | saturates for a long time, can be combined | in a box so it doesn't get crushed |
| Hard cheese such as mountain cheese or Gouda | High in protein, salty, lasts a day without refrigeration | in wax paper or can |
| Nuts and trail mix | lots of energy, light, salty or sweet | small portions are enough |
| dried fruit | fast and medium-term energy | Apricots, dates, apple rings |
| Muesli or oat bars | handy, reliable | Pay attention to the list of ingredients, some are very sweet |
| Apples, bananas | fresh, watery | Put bananas in a box; they quickly become mushy |
| Vegetable sticks | refreshing | Carrots and cucumbers keep well |
| Hard-boiled eggs | rich in protein | not cooled for more than a day in summer |
| Chocolate | fast energy | melts in the heat, store in the middle of the backpack |

## What doesn't belong in your backpack in the heat

Food that spoils quickly is a risk in your backpack in summer temperatures. Avoid salads with mayonnaise, raw eggs, cream cheese, minced meat products and cream desserts. Sausage and soft cheese will last longer with a cold pack in an insulated lunch box, but should be eaten by midday. Only pack cooked pasta or rice from the day before in a well-chilled place and eat it early.

## Provisions for children

Children need short breaks with food and drink more often than adults, and provisions are often the best motivation for them. Pack small portions that they can carry themselves and agree on “snack destinations”: at the viewpoint, at the bridge, at the hut. More about this in [Hiking with small children](/ratgeber/wandern-mit-kleinkindern/) and [Hiking with school children](/ratgeber/wandern-mit-schulkindern/).

## Drinking bottle or bladder?

**Bottles** are easy to clean, you can see how much is left in them, and you can fill them quickly at fountains. Insulated bottles keep drinks cool in summer and warm in winter. **Drinking bladders** with tubes allow you to drink while walking, so many people drink more regularly. But they are harder to clean and you can't see the fill level. A combination of bladder and small reserve bottle is practical for long tours.

## Take the rubbish back with you

Packaging, banana peels, apple casings and tissues belong back in the valley. Organic residues rot much more slowly in the mountains than in the garden and attract animals. A small resealable bag for garbage belongs in every backpack.

## Provisions for multi-day tours

On a hut tour, you eat at the hut in the evening and morning and only carry snacks for the day. Things are different if you travel with a tent and look after yourself. Then every gram counts, and you look for foods with a lot of energy per weight. Fat provides twice as much energy per gram as carbohydrates or protein, which is why nuts, nut butter, hard cheese and a little olive oil are classic trekking ingredients. As a rough rule of thumb, many trekkers plan on eating 700 to 900 grams of dry food per person per day.

The following have proven themselves:

- **Breakfast:** Oatmeal or muesli with milk powder, dried fruit and nuts, with instant coffee or tea.
- **On the go:** Bread, crispbread or tortillas with hard cheese, salami or nut butter, plus bars and trail mix.
- **Evening:** Couscous, bulgur, instant mashed potatoes or pasta with a short cooking time, supplemented with vegetable broth, dried vegetables and oil. Freeze-dried ready-made meals are more expensive, but are easy and quick to prepare.

Portion everything into labeled bags at home, one bag per meal. This way you know exactly what's left when you're on the move and you don't have to carry large packs with you.

## Make your own energy balls

Instead of buying bars, you can easily make energy balls yourself. A simple basic recipe: Process 200 grams of pitted dates, 100 grams of nuts, 50 grams of oat flakes, a tablespoon of cocoa and a pinch of salt in a blender to form a sticky mass. Form balls the size of a walnut and roll them in coconut flakes as desired. They will last for about a week in the fridge and easily for a day of hiking in a backpack. Dried apricots or figs can also be used instead of dates, and if you can't tolerate nuts, use sunflower seeds.

## Drink in winter

When it's cold, you feel less thirsty, but you still lose water, especially through the air you breathe and when you sweat on the climb. An insulated bottle with warm, slightly sweetened tea is worth its weight in gold in winter because cold drinks cool down the body and no one likes drinking ice-cold water. You can put regular water bottles in a sock or an insulated cover. Because water begins to freeze from above, it helps to carry the bottle upside down to prevent the cap from freezing. Drinking hoses freeze quickly in frost and are less suitable in winter.

## Special dietary needs

Hiking vegetarian and vegan is no problem: nuts, legumes, whole grain bread, nut butter and dried fruit provide energy and protein. If you eat gluten-free, pack rice cakes, corn cakes or gluten-free bread and ask about huts when making your reservation. It is best for people with diabetes to discuss longer tours with their doctor beforehand, take measurements more frequently along the way and always have quick-acting sugar on hand. If you have severe allergies, your personal emergency kit should be in every backpack and those accompanying you should know where it is.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a beer at the hut a problem?

Not in moderation, but alcohol impairs your sure-footedness, reactions and judgment, which is exactly what you need on the descent. Drink plenty of water and save the beer for the valley.

### What should I have for breakfast before a long tour?

Something with carbohydrates and some protein that you tolerate well: oatmeal, bread with cheese or egg, fruit. Don’t try new foods on tour day.

### Do I need special sports bars?

No. Normal food is completely sufficient for hikes. Sports products are practical, but not a requirement.

### How much energy do I use when hiking?

This depends heavily on body weight, luggage, speed and incline. Roughly you can expect 250 to 500 kilocalories per hour, or more on a steep climb with a heavy backpack. On a long day trip, it's easy to get the energy needed for an additional meal. That's why regular snacks are more important than a big lunch break.
