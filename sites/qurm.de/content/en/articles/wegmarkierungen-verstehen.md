A white St. Andrew's cross on a tree, a red diamond on a post, a yellow signpost with walking times, a logo with a stylized castle: Anyone hiking in Germany will encounter a confusing variety of signs. There is a simple reason for this. Most trails were created and marked by regional hiking clubs, often over a hundred years ago, and each club has developed its own system. There are also logos for long-distance hiking trails, award-winning trails and themed trails. Anyone who understands the principle can still find their way around quickly. This guide explains the most important signs and gives rules for when you're on the go.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - There is no nationwide uniform marking system. Each hiking area has its own signs, which are explained in maps and tour descriptions.
> - Long-distance hiking trails usually have their own logo, often in addition to the regional symbols.
> - In the Alps, mountain trails are usually marked red-white-red. The difficulty is shown as a colored dot on the signposts.
> - “Premium Trail” and “Quality Trail Hiking Germany” are seals of quality for particularly well-marked and varied trails.
> - If you don't see a mark for more than about ten minutes, go back to the last mark.

## Who marks the paths?

In Germany, it is primarily volunteer trail keepers from hiking and mountain clubs who take care of the markings, for example from the Black Forest Club, the Palatinate Forest Club, the Harz Club or the Eifel Club. There are also nature parks, municipalities, tourism associations and, in the Alps, the sections of the Alpine clubs. That's why the characters look slightly different in each region.

## Typical markings in the low mountain ranges

**Diamonds in the Black Forest:** The Black Forest Association uses diamonds in different colors for different types of paths. The red diamond indicates long-distance hiking trails such as the [Westweg](/ratgeber/westweg-schwarzwald/), blue diamonds stand for regional connecting paths and yellow diamonds for local hiking trails. There are also signposts at intersections that indicate destinations and distances.

**Colored bars, crosses and dots:** Many clubs mark paths with simple shapes in different colors, such as a blue bar, a red cross or a yellow dot. In the [Palatinate Forest](/ratgeber/pfaelzerwald-wandern/) or in the Harz, several such paths often intersect. Before you start, remember which sign you are following.

**Circular routes:** Local circular routes often have numbers or letters, for example “A3” or a symbol with a number, and are often explained on boards at the parking lot or town entrance.

**Long-distance routes with logo:** The [Rennsteig](/ratgeber/rennsteig-wandern/) with its white “R”, the [Rheinsteig](/ratgeber/rheinsteig-wandern/), the [Eifelsteig](/ratgeber/eifelsteig-wandern/) or the [Harzer Hexenstieg](/ratgeber/harzer-hexenstieg/) have their own waymarks that apply throughout. They are often displayed in combination with regional markings.

**European long-distance hiking trails:** Several European long-distance hiking trails labeled E1 to E12 also lead through Germany. They usually follow existing regional routes and are not always marked with their own sign.

## Markings in the Alps

In the Bavarian and Austrian Alps, trails are usually marked with red-white-red paint markings on rocks, trees and posts. There are yellow signposts at intersections with destinations and walking times in hours. A colored dot or frame shows the difficulty: blue, red or black. [Mountain trails blue, red, black](/ratgeber/bergwege-schwierigkeit/) explains what these colors mean.

**Stone men**, i.e. piles of stones, also mark the path on rubble and rock. Do not build stone men yourself, as they can lead other hikers astray.

## What a signpost reveals

Signposts according to the standard in Germany show more than just one direction:

| Information | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Near target | the next prominent point, such as a hut or intersection |
| Long-distance goal | a more distant place on the same path |
| Walking time or kilometers | Walking time without breaks for an averagely fit person, in the mountains usually in hours |
| Path number or sign | the path you follow |
| Location information | Name and altitude of the location, important for emergency calls |

The location information with altitude is particularly valuable in an emergency. This allows the rescue services to find you more quickly, as described in [Emergency on the Mountain](/ratgeber/notfall-am-berg/). The walking times on signposts are based on similar rules of thumb as in [Calculate walking time](/ratgeber/gehzeit-berechnen/).

## Predicate routes: premium route and quality route

Two quality seals help you find particularly beautiful and well-marked paths:

- **Quality Trail Hiking Germany:** awarded by the German Hiking Association. Among other things, the condition of the path, markings, variety and infrastructure are assessed.
- **Premium trail:** awarded by the German Hiking Institute. The paths are checked according to a points system and regularly recertified.

Both seals mean: little asphalt, many natural paths, complete markings in both directions and interesting destinations along the way. They are therefore a good choice, especially for beginners and for the [first long-distance hike](/ratgeber/erste-fernwanderung-planen/).

## Rules for on the go

1. **Clarify before starting:** Which sign applies to my path? A photo of the information board at the parking lot helps.
2. **Look consciously:** Stop briefly at intersections and look for the sign. There are usually markings shortly after a junction so that you can confirm the direction.
3. **Looking Back:** Turn around every now and then. Many paths are marked in both directions, and signs in the opposite direction show you that you are still in the right direction.
4. **Turn around after ten minutes of no sign:** Go back to the last safe marker and search from there.
5. **Don't follow blindly:** Markings may be missing, removed from woodwork, or altered by unknown persons. A map or app with an offline map is therefore included, as described in [Hiking apps and GPX tracks](/ratgeber/wander-apps-und-gpx/).
6. **With a map and compass** you can orientate yourself even without a battery. The Basics Explained [Map and Compass](/ratgeber/karte-und-kompass/).

## Report damage

If you discover a missing marker, a fallen signpost or a closed path, report it to the responsible hiking club, the nature park or the tourist information center. Many clubs have contact forms or email addresses for this purpose. The volunteer waykeepers rely on such information. But never paint or stick signs on trees or stones yourself.

## Discover signposts with children

For children, the search for the next sign quickly becomes a game. Before you start, decide which sign applies and let the children be the first to look at each intersection. Whoever discovers the symbol first gets to move forward. Older children can read the map and compare whether the signpost and the map match. In this way, they learn orientation at the same time, and the kilometers go by faster. More ideas can be found in [Hiking with school children](/ratgeber/wandern-mit-schulkindern/).

## Markings in winter

In winter, many markings are hidden under snow and color marks on rocks or the ground cannot be seen. At the same time, winter hiking trails often lead on different, cleared routes than in summer and are marked with their own poles or signs. In winter, only follow designated winter trails and do not rely on summer markings. In fog and snow, a map or GPS is more helpful than any color marking.

## A look into history

Marked hiking trails in Germany are closely linked to the founding of hiking and mountain clubs in the 19th century. The Black Forest Club was founded in 1864, and many other clubs followed in the decades that followed. They built paths, set up shelters and observation towers and began marking their paths with colored signs. In 1883, several clubs came together to form an umbrella organization, the forerunner of today's German Hiking Association. The Westweg in the Black Forest, which was opened around 1900, is one of the oldest long-distance hiking trails in Germany. Because each club found its own solutions in its region, the variety of signs we see today emerged.

## Markings in neighboring countries

Anyone who crosses the border encounters other systems. In **Austria** paths are marked red-white-red like in the Bavarian Alps. In **Switzerland** hiking trails are marked yellow, mountain hiking trails white-red-white and challenging alpine hiking trails white-blue-white. In **France** and **Belgium** the large long-distance routes, the Grande Randonnée routes, have a white-red double marking, regional routes are often marked yellow-red and short routes are marked yellow. In **Czech Republic** there is a nationwide uniform system: a colored stripe in red, blue, green or yellow between two white stripes, with red usually indicating the most important long-distance routes. Anyone who knows the basic idea will quickly find their way around. A look at the legend on the hiking map shows the details.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why are there so many different characters?

Because the paths were created by different associations over generations. There is no nationwide standardization, but within an area the signs are usually consistent.

### Do signs apply in both directions?

In most ways, yes. Some themed and circular trails are only marked in one direction. This is stated on the overview board or in the description.

### What do I do if I get lost?

Stay calm, stop, determine your location on the map or in the app and go back along the familiar route. Never descend without a path in the mountains to take a shortcut.

### Can I add chalk or tape to marks?

No. Even well-intentioned signs, cairns or ribbons on branches can mislead other hikers and contradict the clubs' marking system. Instead, report any gaps to the responsible hiking club or the tourist information office.

### Why are some markings faded or barely visible?

Sun, rain and frost take a toll on the paint, trees grow and overgrow signs, and marked trunks disappear during woodwork. The volunteer waykeepers regularly check their sections and renew the signs, but they cannot be everywhere at the same time. That's why a map is so important as a supplement.

### What do arrows, angles and crossed symbols mean?

Many systems indicate a change in direction with an arrow or angled sign. If there is such a sign at an intersection, the path turns there. On the French and Belgian Grande Randonnée trails, a cross made up of the two trail colors means you are going in the wrong direction. Such warning signs are rarer in Germany. The following applies here: If the sign is missing after a junction, you are probably in the wrong place.
