The Elbe Sandstone Mountains southeast of Dresden are one of the most unusual hiking areas in Germany. Over millions of years, water has shaped the sandstone into mesas, rocky pinnacles, gorges and reefs. Pines and beeches grow between the rocks, the narrow grounds are cool and mossy, and from the viewing points you can see far across the Elbe valley. Part of this landscape has been protected as the Saxon Switzerland National Park since 1990.

The region is ideal for day tours: many starting points are on the S-Bahn line in the Elbe Valley, the paths are well marked, and after just a few kilometers you will find yourself at a viewing point that looks like something out of a painting. However, many others know this too. This guide introduces the most famous tours and explains how to avoid the biggest crowds.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - You can easily reach most starting points with the S-Bahn from Dresden through the Elbe Valley and with the Elbe ferries.
> - It is very crowded at the Bastei on weekends and during the holidays. It's worth starting early.
> - Many paths lead over steps, ladders and iron staircases. Sure-footedness is important; fear of heights can become a problem.
> - In the core zone of the national park you are only allowed to use marked paths.
> - After the major forest fire in 2022, some areas were closed for a long time. Before the tour, check the current information from the national park administration.

## Arrival and orientation

The Elbe Valley is the region's lifeline. The S-Bahn runs from Dresden via Pirna and the spa town of Rathen to Bad Schandau and on towards Schöna on the Czech border. Ferries connect the train stations on the left bank of the Elbe with the towns on the right. For many tours you don't have to travel by car, which is a real advantage given that parking spaces are scarce and quickly fill up on weekends.

The paths are marked with colored lines and dots, as well as special signs for long-distance routes such as the Malerweg. A good hiking map at a scale of 1:25,000 or an offline map is still useful because many paths branch off and you often don't have cell phone reception on the grounds.

## Bastion and Sweden holes

The Bastei is the most famous destination in Saxon Switzerland. The rocks rise almost 200 meters above the Elbe, and the stone Bastei Bridge from the 19th century connects several rock towers. The view of the Elbe loop, the Lilienstein and the table mountains is magnificent.

A nice round begins in the health resort of Rathen. You climb the steps up to the bastion, go over the bridge and past the Neurathen rock castle, for which there is a small entrance fee. The path then leads through the Schwedenhöhlen down into the Amselgrund: a narrow, wildly romantic gorge with many stairs and bridges. Below you reach the Amselsee and return to Rathen.

Allow two to three hours of walking time for this round. The Sweden holes are slippery when wet and can be closed after storms. And you should visit the Bastei either very early in the morning or late in the afternoon when the bus groups have left.

## Lilienstein

The Lilienstein is the most striking table mountain in Saxon Switzerland and the only one on the right bank of the Elbe. From the spa town of Rathen you take the ferry and then follow the path through forest and meadows to the foot of the rock. The climb is via stairs, steps and short iron ladders. At the top you will find a plateau with several viewpoints, an obelisk and a restaurant.

The Lilienstein is a good choice if you are looking for a compact tour with a great panorama. Allow around three to four hours for the return trip from Rathen, depending on the option you choose.

## Pfaffenstein and Barbarine

On the left side of the Elbe lies the Pfaffenstein, a table mountain with its own small labyrinth of rock alleys. It is best known for the Barbarine, a slender, free-standing rock pinnacle on the edge of the plateau, which is one of the region's landmarks. The climb is possible on several paths, one of which leads through the so-called eye of the needle, a narrow crevice with ladders through which you have to squeeze yourself with a larger backpack.

The Pfaffenstein is easy to do with children of primary school age and up and is significantly less crowded than the Bastei. The starting point is usually the town of Pfaffendorf.

## Schrammsteine

The Schrammsteine near Bad Schandau are a long, jagged chain of rocks with towers, ridges and gorges. The best-known tour leads from Postelwitz or Bad Schandau to the Schrammstein view, which is reached via iron stairs, ladders and steps. From there we continue along the ridge path, with spectacular views of the rock world and the Elbe valley.

This path is not for people with a strong fear of heights. Some stairs are steep and exposed, secured by railings and handholds. It becomes dangerous when it is wet or icy. However, with surefootedness and good footwear, the tour is very doable for experienced hikers. Plan half a day to a full day.

## Cowshed and ladder to heaven

In the back part of Saxon Switzerland, in the Kirnitzschtal, is the Kuhstall: a large rock gate on the Neuer Wildenstein that you can walk through. According to tradition, the name comes from the fact that farmers are said to have hidden their livestock here during times of war. Next to the gate, the narrow ladder to heaven, a steep wooden staircase through a crevice in the rock, leads up to the summit.

The starting point is usually the Lichtenhain waterfall, which you can reach with the historic Kirnitzschtalbahn from Bad Schandau. The tour can easily be combined with the surrounding viewpoints to form a longer tour.

## Rules in the national park

A large part of the rock world is in the national park. In the **core zone** you are only allowed to use marked paths and designated mountain paths. Making fires is prohibited, as is leaving the paths to take shortcuts. Dogs must be kept on a leash in the national park.

A special feature is spending the night in so-called **Boofen**, i.e. under rocky overhangs. This tradition is closely linked to climbing in Saxon Switzerland and is only permitted in certain designated places and under certain conditions. Before spending such an overnight stay, be sure to find out about the current rules from the national park administration. Open fire is taboo.

## After the 2022 forest fire

In the summer of 2022 there were fires on both sides of the border in Bohemian and Saxon Switzerland. On the German side, areas in the rear of Saxon Switzerland were particularly affected. As a result, numerous paths were closed, partly due to damage and partly because trees were at risk of falling. Some closures have now been lifted and other routes have been relocated. Because the situation is changing, you should check the National Park Administration website before each tour.

## Tips against the rush

Saxon Switzerland is very well attended on nice weekends. With a few simple rules you can still find quiet routes: Start early, ideally with one of the first S-Bahn trains. Visit the well-known viewpoints in the early morning or late afternoon. Avoid the table mountains on the left side of the Elbe, which are often much quieter than the Bastei. And on holidays, it's better to plan a tour away from the big names, for example through one of the many quiet grounds.

## The quiet table mountains to the left of the Elbe

If you want to avoid the bastion, you will find a series of table mountains on the left side of the Elbe, which rise individually from the landscape and can easily be combined to form circular tours. The **Papststein** near Gohrisch has a wide summit plateau with a mountain restaurant and one of the most beautiful panoramic views of the rock world. The neighboring **Gohrisch** beckons with its winding rocky streets. In the very south, near the Czech border, are the **Kaiserkrone** with its jagged peak rocks and the small **Zirkelstein**, which is considered the smallest table mountain in Saxon Switzerland. These destinations are shorter and less spectacular than the Schrammsteine, but are quiet, scenic and well suited for families.

## Taking photos in the Elbe sandstone

Saxon Switzerland is a dream destination for photographers. It is particularly atmospheric in autumn, when fog gathers in the Elbe valley in the morning and the rock towers protrude like islands. If you want to experience the sunrise at a viewing point, start in the dark with a headlamp and ideally already know the route from the day before. For the perfect picture, stay behind railings and on the paths, as many rock edges break off vertically and the sandstone can crumble. Drones are not allowed to fly over the national park without permission, and you should only set up your tripod at narrow viewing points if others are still passing by.

## Frequently asked questions

### When is the best time for Saxon Switzerland?

Spring and autumn are particularly beautiful and less hot. In summer it's worth starting early and planning for shady areas at lunchtime. In winter, steps and staircases can become icy and therefore dangerous.

### Is Saxon Switzerland suitable for children?

Yes, many tours are an adventure for children. Ladders, rocky alleys and caves inspire almost every child. If you have small children, pay attention to unsecured rock edges at viewing points and hold their hands there.

### Do I need climbing experience?

No. The hiking trails described are secured with ladders, steps and railings. Climbing on the rock itself, on the other hand, is strictly regulated and only permitted with experience and according to the Saxon climbing rules.

### Are there tours without lots of stairs and ladders?

Yes. The **Polenztal** between Hohnstein and Bad Schandau is a meadow valley with largely flat paths along the river, famous in spring for the flowering of Märzenbecher. Paths along the Elbe and through the wider grounds also require just a few steps. Ask the tourist information about routes that suit your needs.
