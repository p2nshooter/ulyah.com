A sprained ankle on the descent, a fall on wet roots, a circulatory collapse in the midday heat: emergencies while hiking usually come without warning, and the people accompanying you are often alone in the situation at that moment. If you then know what to do and in what order, you will remain calmer and help more effectively.

This guide describes the process from the first minute until the rescue arrives. It does not replace a first aid course. You should refresh such a course, ideally a special outdoor course, every few years if you hike regularly.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - First your own safety, then the injured: keep an eye on falling rocks, falling edges and the weather.
> - Emergency call 112: Where, what, how many, what injuries, then wait for questions.
> - Many cell phones automatically transmit the location to the control center when making an emergency call. Nevertheless, describe the place yourself as precisely as possible.
> - Protect injured people from the cold: emergency blanket, clothing, insulation from the floor.
> - Instruct the helicopter: both arms up in a Y shape means “Yes, we need help”.

## Keep calm and protect yourself

The first impulse is to immediately rush to the injured person. Stop for a moment and look around. Is there a danger from falling rocks, a fall edge, a raging stream or an approaching thunderstorm? Then you secure yourself first and help afterwards. A second injured person only makes the situation worse.

If possible, move the injured person out of the immediate danger zone, but only if this can be done without additional risk. If you suspect a spinal injury, you should only move it if it cannot stay in place.

## Assess the condition

Speak to the person. She answers, asking about pain and how the accident happened. If she doesn't answer, check her breathing: tilt her head back slightly, lift her chin, see, hear and feel whether she is breathing normally.

- **Unconscious, but breathing normally:** Rest on your side, call emergency service, check your breathing regularly, protect yourself from the cold.
- **Not breathing normally:** Call emergency services and begin cardiopulmonary resuscitation immediately. If two people help, one speaks on the phone while the other presses.
- **Response with Injury:** Emergency call if the person cannot move on their own or the injury is serious. Stop bleeding, immobilize fractures, keep warm.

## Make the emergency call

You can reach **112** throughout Germany and the EU, free of charge and without an area code. In Germany you need an inserted SIM card, but credit is not necessary. The mountain rescue service in the mountains is also alerted via the control center.

If you have no reception in your own network, your cell phone will try to dial into another available network when making an emergency call. If that doesn't work, climb up a bit, go to a clearing or a place with a clear view of the valley and try again. In some cases, an SMS still gets through if a call doesn't work. The federal states' emergency call app makes it possible to call for help via the app; it is primarily intended for people who cannot make phone calls.

You answer the control center’s questions on the phone. This information is particularly important:

1. **Where:** Name of trail, last signpost, near hut, peak or location, elevation if known. If you have a map app, read the coordinates. Many smartphones also automatically transmit their location to the control center when making an emergency call. But don't rely on that alone.
2. **What:** What happened? Fall, circulatory problem, heart problems, lost?
3. **How ​​many:** How many people are affected?
4. **What injuries:** Consciousness, breathing, bleeding, pain, misalignment.
5. **Wait:** Don't hang up until the control center ends the call. Then keep your cell phone accessible and save battery.

## First aid until rescue arrives

A mountain rescue can take time, depending on the location, weather and availability of a helicopter. During this time, your job is to keep the injured person stable and warm.

**Heat preservation** is often the most important thing. Even on a warm summer day, a person who is injured and lying on the ground cools down quickly, especially on a mountain, in windy conditions or after sweating. Place a sleeping pad, backpack or clothing under the person, cover them with whatever you have and use the emergency blanket. The most important thing is that the blanket completely covers the body and protects it from the wind.

**Stop bleeding** by applying pressure to the wound, ideally with a compress and a pressure bandage. **Suspected fractures** of arms or legs: position in the way that is most comfortable for the person and immobilize, for example with clothing, a sleeping mat or sticks. Don't try to correct misalignments.

**Care** should not be underestimated. Stay with the person, talk to them, explain what you are doing and that help is on the way. Do not give her anything to eat or drink if surgery is likely or if she is not fully awake.

## When you're alone

If there is no one nearby who can help, call 911 as early as possible. Without reception, you can give the **Alpine emergency signal**: a signal six times per minute, i.e. a call, a whistle or a light signal about every ten seconds, then a one-minute break and all over again. The answer is three times per minute. A whistle is much better for this than the voice.

If you have to leave the injured person to get help, first treat them as best you can, keep them warm, write down or remember the exact location, and leave their cell phone, water and a lamp if possible.

## Instruct helicopter

If a rescue helicopter comes, it helps if you make it aware of you and give it the proper instructions:

| characters | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Both arms diagonally upwards (Y-shape) | “Yes, we need help” |
| One arm slanted upwards, one slanted downwards (N-shape) | “No, we don’t need help” |

Stand with your back to the wind so the crew can see the direction of the wind. Secure loose items such as jackets, backpack covers, caps or emergency blankets beforehand, as the downdraft from the rotor is enormous. Never approach the helicopter on your own initiative, but wait for signs from the crew. Then only approach from the front and within the pilot's field of vision.

## Cost of rescue

In Germany, medically necessary rescue is usually covered by health insurance. It becomes more difficult when no one is injured, for example when rescuing exhausted or lost hikers. Such operations are often not covered by health insurance and can be expensive. Many hikers therefore take out insurance for rescue costs or are covered by membership in an Alpine club. Especially before touring abroad, check whether you are adequately insured. However, the question of costs should never cause you to hesitate to call for help in a real emergency.

## Prevention is the best solution

- Refresh your first aid knowledge regularly
- Always have a complete first aid kit with you, including a rescue blanket and whistle
- Keep your cell phone charged and take a power bank with you
- Tell someone where you are going and when you will be back
- Choose tours that suit your experience and turn back in time

## Who comes when you dial 112?

The control center will decide what help is needed based on your information. In Germany, volunteer mountain rescue teams carry out rescue work in the mountains and in impassable terrain, usually under the auspices of the German Red Cross. They come on foot, in off-road vehicles or together with a rescue helicopter, which, if necessary, uses a winch to lower the emergency services. In low mountain ranges, the rescue service often responds first, supported by the mountain rescue service or fire brigade if the area is difficult to access.

When traveling abroad, the following applies: 112 works throughout the EU and also in Switzerland. In Austria there is also the Alpine emergency number 140, which leads directly to the mountain rescue service, and in Switzerland you can alert the Rega air rescue service via 1414. Save these numbers in your cell phone before a trip abroad.

## Recognize and treat hypothermia

Hypothermia is one of the most common consequences of an accident on the mountain because injured people lie still for long periods of time. The first signs are severe shivering, cold skin and restlessness. As the hypothermia deepens, the person becomes confused, sleepy, and clumsy, and the shaking eventually stops. This is not a good sign, but rather an alarm signal.

How you can help:

- Move the person as little and as gently as possible and keep them flat.
- Protect them from below with a sleeping mat or backpack and from above with clothing, a rescue blanket and a bivouac bag, if available.
- Do not rub the arms and legs warmly, or let the person walk around, lest cold blood from the limbs suddenly rush to the core of the body.
- Warm, sweetened drinks only help if the person is fully awake and can swallow safely.
- Expressly report to the control center that the person is hypothermic.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I call 911 if I have a sprained ankle?

If the person can get down safely with support, possibly with tape and sticks, this is often a better solution. If the descent is long, steep or it's about to get dark, it's better to call 112. The control center then decides what help is needed.

### What if I don't know the exact location?

Describe as best you can what you see: the last signpost, prominent peaks, a lake, a hut. Stay available. The rescue workers can often pinpoint your location using your cell phone.

### Do I have to stay with the injured person until they are rescued?

If possible, yes. If you need to leave to get help or reception, ensure you are warm and safe beforehand and return as soon as you can.

### What belongs in an emergency card in your backpack?

Write down your name, a contact person for emergencies, allergies, previous illnesses and medications you take regularly on a card. Put it in the lid compartment of the backpack or in the first aid kit. Most smartphones also have an emergency pass that helpers can access even when the screen is locked. It's worth filling it out before your next tour.

### Does the gold or silver side of the emergency blanket have to be on the outside?

There is a lot of debate about this, but in practice the difference is small. It is crucial that the blanket encloses the body as completely as possible, keeps out wind and moisture and that the person underneath lies on an insulating layer. Wrap the blanket tightly around your body and head, leaving your face exposed, and secure the edges to prevent it from flapping away in the wind.
