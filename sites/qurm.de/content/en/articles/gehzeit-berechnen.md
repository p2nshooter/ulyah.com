“How long will this take us?” is the most important question before every hike. The answer depends on when you start, whether you reach the last cable car, whether the hut still serves dinner and whether you get down from the summit before the afternoon thunderstorm. If you only look at the kilometers, you're almost always making a miscalculation, because altitude difference takes significantly more time than flat routes.

Fortunately, there are tried and tested rules of thumb. They do not replace self-paced experience, but they provide a very useful foundation. This guide explains the common formula according to DIN 33466, an alternative with mileage and the surcharges that you should take into account in practice.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Guideline values according to DIN 33466: 4 km per hour on the flat, 300 meters per hour on the ascent, 500 meters per hour on the descent.
> - Calculate the time for distance and altitude separately. The larger number plus half the smaller number equals the walking time.
> - Breaks are not included. Allow about a ten minute break for every hour of walking.
> - Terrain, luggage, children, heat and fitness can significantly extend the time.
> - After each tour, compare planning and reality, then you will get to know your own pace.

## The formula according to DIN 33466

The DIN 33466 standard actually regulates hiking trail signs. But it also contains the guideline values ​​that are used to calculate walking times on many signs in Germany. These values are based on an averagely trained person with light luggage:

| movement | Guideline |
|---|---|
| Route on the plain | 4 kilometers per hour |
| Ascension | 300 meters altitude per hour |
| Descent | 500 meters of altitude per hour |

The trick lies in how you combine the values. You first calculate two separate times:

1. **Time for the route:** Kilometers divided by 4.
2. **Time for altitude:** Ascent meters divided by 300 plus descent meters divided by 500.

Then you take **the larger of the two times and add half of the smaller ones.** The result is the pure walking time without breaks.

## A calculation example

Let's take a circular hike in the low mountain range: 14 kilometers, 600 meters in altitude on the ascent and the same amount on the descent.

- Distance: 14 km ÷ 4 = **3.5 hours**
- Altitude: 600 ÷ 300 = 2 hours ascent, 600 ÷ 500 = 1.2 hours descent, total **3.2 hours**
- The larger time is 3.5 hours, half of the smaller time is 1.6 hours
- Walking time: 3.5 + 1.6 = **5.1 hours**, a good five hours

For comparison: If you only look at the kilometers, you would estimate 3.5 hours and miss the estimate by more than an hour and a half. It is precisely this mistake that causes groups to get off in the dark or miss the last bus.

A second example of a mountain tour: 9 kilometers, 1,100 meters up and down.

- Distance: 9 ÷ 4 = 2.25 hours
- Altitude: 1,100 ÷ 300 = 3.67 hours plus 1,100 ÷ 500 = 2.2 hours, totaling 5.87 hours
- Walking time: 5.87 + 1.13 = **about 7 hours**

The height clearly dominates here. The route is short, but the tour fills a whole day.

## Alternative: performance kilometers

The principle of performance kilometers is widespread in Switzerland and among some tour planners. Altitude meters are converted into additional kilometers. A common variant: Every kilometer of distance counts as one performance kilometer, and for every 100 meters in altitude an additional performance kilometer is added. Steep descents are taken into account with a smaller surcharge. You then divide by an assumed speed, often around four kilometers per hour.

For the first example, this results in 14 + 6 = 20 kilometers of performance, or at four per hour, five hours. The result is close to the DIN formula. Which method you use is secondary. It is important that you take the altitude into account.

## Breaks and supplements

The formulas provide pure walking time. In practice, there are many additional things that you should plan for:

- **Breaks:** Allow roughly ten minutes per hour of walking. For a five-hour tour that's around 50 minutes, with a lunch break it's more like an hour or more.
- **Difficult terrain:** Root paths, scree, boulders, rocky steps or wet sections slow down the pace significantly. On black-marked mountain trails or in snowfields, the guidelines may be too optimistic.
- **Luggage:** With a backpack for a multi-day tour you are slower than with day luggage, especially uphill.
- **Heat:** On hot days the pace slows down and you need more breaks to drink.
- **Children:** Small children walk much slower and need many more breaks. It's better to plan half the route and twice the time with children.
- **Group:** The group is always as fast as the slowest person. The larger the group, the more time is spent collecting, taking photos and taking toilet breaks.
- **Photography and stopping:** Don't underestimate how long stopping at the hut takes when it's full.

## From plan to schedule

With the total time you can set up a schedule. Work backwards from the desired end: When do you have to be back at the latest? When does it get dark? When does the last bus leave? On thunderstorm days, the end should be well before early afternoon.

A simple structure for a day tour could look like this:

| Period | Scheduled time | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Start at the parking lot | 7:30 | Checked weather the evening before and in the morning |
| Hut (intermediate destination) | 9:45 | short break, fill up with water |
| Summit | 11:15 | Turnaround time: 11:45 at the latest |
| Return to the hut | 12:45 | Lunch break |
| Parking lot | 14:45 | Buffer until the risk of thunderstorms in the afternoon |

The **reversal time** is the most important element. It determines when you should turn back at the latest, even if you have not yet reached your goal. Anyone who decides this in advance does not have to discuss it on the mountain.

## Don't forget daylight

Especially in spring and autumn, many people underestimate how early it gets dark. At the end of October, depending on the region, the sun in Germany sets between around 4:30 p.m. and 5:00 p.m. In the deep forest or on eastern slopes it becomes dim even earlier. Therefore, compare the calculated total time including breaks with the time until sunset and keep at least an hour in reserve. A headlamp still belongs in every backpack, because even well-planned tours sometimes take longer.

## Get to know your own pace

Formulas are average values. Some people go uphill much faster, others are slower on the descent because they are cautious or have knee problems. After a few tours, note how long it took you to complete certain sections, or use the recording on a hiking app. After a few weeks, you will know whether you need to correct the guideline values ​​by a factor, such as “ten percent faster uphill, twenty percent slower downhill.”

Please note that apps measure differently. Elevation meters from GPS data are often inaccurate, especially in forests and narrow valleys. A barometric altimeter in a watch or cell phone is usually more reliable, but occasionally needs to be calibrated at a known point.

## Read the signpost correctly

Many signposts in Germany show walking times instead of kilometers, especially in the Alps. This information is usually based on similar guidelines and applies to a person of average fitness, without breaks. If you notice that you regularly exceed the specified times, this is an indication that the planned tour may be too long. In the mountains this is a reason to turn back in time.

## Walking time with children and groups

The usual formulas apply to adults of average fitness. With children you need significantly more time, not only because of the shorter legs, but also because of the many breaks, discoveries and games along the way. Plan generously for family hikes and allow double the time rather than tight estimates.

In groups, the slowest member sets the pace. If you are planning a tour for a group, you should adjust the walking time to the pace of the weakest people and take additional breaks into account. A group that stays together is safer and has more fun than one that spreads out across the mountain.

## Use digital tour planners correctly

Many map apps and tour portals automatically calculate walking times based on distance and altitude. This information is a good guide, but is based on average values. After a few tours, compare the calculated times with your actual times, then you will know how to adjust the information for yourself.

Download maps before the tour for offline use, as reception is often poor in the mountains. A paper map as an addition never hurts. The guide [Hiking Apps and GPX](/ratgeber/wander-apps-und-gpx/) explains how to combine maps and apps.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why is the descent faster than the ascent but feels more strenuous?

Downhill, the muscles slow down, which puts particular strain on the thighs and knees. You may be faster, but the strain is high. On steep, slippery sections the descent can be even slower than the guideline, and this is where most falls happen.

### Does the formula also apply to children?

Only very limited. Children move at a completely different pace and need lots of breaks. With small children, plan based on time rather than distance, for example two hours total time including play, and choose the route accordingly.

### How many hours of walking makes sense for beginners?

For the first tours, three to four hours of pure walking time is a good framework. With breaks you'll be on the road for five hours. Build up slowly instead of starting straight away with an eight-hour tour.

### How does snow change walking time?

Snow slows down walking significantly, especially if it is soft or you have to track. Plan significantly more time in winter and when there is old snow in spring and take the short days into account. For winter hikes, it's best to choose shorter tours on cleared or groomed paths.

### How accurate are the times on signposts?

They apply to average hikers without breaks and are usually rather limited. They are good for comparing paths, but not a personal schedule. Always include breaks and a reserve.
