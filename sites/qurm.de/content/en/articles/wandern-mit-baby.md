Going hiking with a baby initially sounds like a contradiction to many new parents. Babies are often wonderfully relaxed hiking companions: they sleep in the carrier, are soothed by the rocking and look into the treetops with big eyes. With the right equipment and realistic expectations, you can go out into nature with your child in the first few months.

This guide will help you choose the carrier, plan your tour and protect yourself from the weather and sun. It's best to discuss with your pediatrician's office whether your baby's health is ready for longer trips, especially for premature babies or children with previous illnesses.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - In the first few months, slings and ergonomic comfort carriers that hold the baby close to the body are suitable.
> - A back carrier with a frame, often called a carrier, is only an option if the child can sit safely and stably on its own.
> - Babies cool down and overheat more quickly than adults. They hardly move in the carrier and therefore need different clothing than you.
> - Direct sun is taboo for babies. Shade, long clothing and a sun hat are the best protection.
> - Plan short tours with a flat profile, rest areas and a shortcut.

## Which carrier for which age?

The right carrier depends primarily on how well your child can hold their head and torso.

| Age (rough) | Wear | Advantages | Boundaries |
|---|---|---|---|
| Newborns to a few months old | Sling, ring sling | very close to the body, individually adjustable | Tying takes practice and is less comfortable on long tours |
| First months to toddlerhood | ergonomic comfort carrier (belly or back) | Quickly put on, well padded, hip belt | not for very heavy children on long journeys |
| From safe, free sitting to toddlerhood | Back carrier with frame (Kraxe) | carries weight via hip belt, storage space, sun canopy | heavy, bulky, only when you can sit firmly |

**Slings and comfort carriers** keep the baby in an anatomically favorable position: the legs are spread and squatted, the so-called squat-spread position, the back is slightly rounded, the head is supported. This is ideal for the first few months. When hiking, you usually carry the baby on your front in front of your stomach; later, with many models, you can also carry it on your back.

**Back carriers with a frame** are particularly practical for hikes because they transfer the weight to the pelvis via a padded hip belt and offer space for luggage. Many models have a sunroof. However, they are only suitable when your child can sit stably on their own, which is usually not the case before the second half of their life. Get advice before buying and try out several models with your child.

## The right tour

Short, less demanding tours are suitable for the first hikes with a baby:

- **Short routes:** One to two hours of walking is enough to start with. With breaks for breastfeeding, feeding and changing, you'll be out and about for longer anyway.
- **Low Profile:** Steep climbs and descents are strenuous with additional weight on your back or stomach and increase the risk of falling.
- **Safe paths:** Avoid exposed paths, scree, climbing and slippery paths. A fall with a child in a carrier can have serious consequences.
- **Shade:** Forest paths are ideal in summer.
- **Break places:** Benches, shelters or a stop along the way make changing diapers and breastfeeding easier.
- **Shortcuts:** Choose circular routes or routes with stops so you can replan at any time.

Altitudes above around 2,000 meters and quick cable car climbs to high altitudes are usually not recommended for babies. Talk to your pediatrician's office beforehand if you are planning a vacation in the mountains.

## Clothes for the baby

The most important principle: **Your baby hardly moves in the carrier.** While you get warm as you climb, the child may freeze, especially on the legs, feet and hands that protrude from the carrier. At the same time, heat accumulates through physical contact and the baby can overheat on sunny days.

- **In summer:** light, long clothing made of breathable material, sun hat with neck protection, socks. It's warm under the stretcher; It's better to wear one less layer.
- **In the transition period:** Onion principle with wool bodysuit, fleece suit and hat. A babywearing jacket or babywearing cover that fits over the baby and carrier will keep out wind and rain.
- **In winter:** warm layers, thick socks and booties, hat and gloves. Cold feet are a common problem because the legs hang down.

Check your baby's temperature regularly, preferably in the neck. A cool neck indicates that he is cold; a sweaty neck shows that it is too warm. Hands and feet are often cooler and less suitable for control.

## Sun protection

Baby skin is very sensitive. You should avoid direct sun as much as possible in the first year of life. The best protection is shade, clothing and hat. Plan trips through the forest, go early in the morning or late in the afternoon and use the stretcher's sun canopy. It's best to get advice about sunscreen for babies from your pediatrician's office.

Also think about the eyes: a hat with a brim helps, and on bright days baby sunglasses can be useful if your child accepts them.

## What belongs in the backpack

| Category | Content |
|---|---|
| Wrap | Diapers, wet wipes, changing mat, bags for used diapers |
| Food and drink | Milk or bottle, porridge or snacks depending on age, water for you |
| Clothing | Change bodysuit, spare clothes, hat, socks, warm layer |
| Protection | Sun hat, rain or wind cover for the carrier, possibly mosquito repellent after consultation with the pediatrician's office |
| First aid | Plasters, disinfection, tick card, clinical thermometer, medication according to medical advice |
| Comfort | Favorite toy, pacifier, burp cloth |

Of course, you can take used diapers with you. A tightly closing bag prevents smells in the backpack.

## On the road: rhythm instead of route

Babies set the rhythm. Schedule breaks when your child is hungry, needs to be changed, or just wants to lie on a blanket and kick for a while. Sitting in the same position in the carrier for long periods of time is stressful for babies; Breaks are just as good for him as they are for you. Many families find that babies sleep best in a carrier while their parents are moving. Use these phases for the route and the awake phases for breaks.

## Your own safety

With your baby in the carrier, you change your center of gravity, and your view of your feet is restricted when you're carrying your baby on your stomach. Therefore, walk deliberately slowly, especially downhill, on roots, stones and wet ground. Hiking poles give you additional stability. And if possible, don't go alone so that someone can help in an emergency.

## Your own regression

After giving birth, your body needs time until the pelvic floor, abdominal muscles and connective tissue are resilient again. A baby in a carrier and a backpack mean additional weight that takes a toll on these areas. Therefore, start with short, flat walks and increase slowly. Talk to your midwife or gynecologist's office about when longer tours are right for you, and pay attention to warning signs such as a feeling of pressure in the pelvis, pain or unwanted urine loss. A postnatal course helps you find the right posture when carrying something. And if parents take turns carrying the child, the load is spread over several shoulders.

## Breastfeeding and bottles on the go

Breastfeeding is the easiest solution when you're on the move because you don't have to prepare anything. Find a sheltered place in the shade or behind a hut where there is no wind, take a light seat cushion with you and, if you feel more comfortable, a thin cloth as a privacy screen. If your baby is bottle-fed, take boiled hot water in an insulated bottle and the milk powder already measured in a portion container. This means you can mix the milk fresh when you need it and don't have to keep ready-made milk warm for hours. You transport porridge in a well-insulated food container. Plan a quiet break for each meal, as babies often eat more slowly on the go because there is so much to see.

## Ticks and mosquitoes

Babies get bitten by ticks just like adults. Long, light-colored clothing makes it easier to spot the animals. After each tour, check your child carefully, especially the hairline, the areas behind the ears, armpits, back of the knees, diaper area and all folds of skin. Remove a tick quickly with a tick card or tweezers. Many insect repellents are not approved for use on infants. Therefore, ask the pediatrician's office which medication is suitable from which age and in summer it is better to wear clothing and a mosquito net over the carrier. ## Wear alternately

If two adults are traveling, take turns carrying it before your shoulders hurt. Before the tour, adjust the carrier so that it fits both people, or remember the settings, because every change takes time along the way. Anyone who is not carrying the baby takes the backpack with diapers, provisions and a change of clothes. This way the load stays spread out and no one is exhausted at the end of the day.

## Frequently asked questions

### When can I go hiking with my baby?

Short walks in nature are possible in the first few weeks with a suitable carrier. You should slowly increase longer hikes and discuss them with your pediatrician if you are unsure.

### How long can my baby sit in the carrier?

There is no fixed limit. What is important are regular breaks during which the baby can move and an ergonomically correctly adjusted carrier. Get advice when adjusting, for example from a babywearing advisor.

### Stroller or carrier?

Both are possible on wide, level paths. For typical hiking trails with roots, steps and narrow sections, the stretcher is the better choice.

### Can I stay in a hut with a baby?

This is quite possible in low mountain ranges and in low-lying huts. When making your reservation, ask for a family room instead of a bunk room so that your baby and other guests can sleep peacefully. You should avoid high-altitude huts with long climbs with a baby, as described above in the section on choosing a tour.
