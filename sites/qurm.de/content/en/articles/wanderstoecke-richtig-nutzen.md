Twenty years ago, hikers with sticks on German low mountain paths were laughed at. Today you can see them everywhere, in the Allgäu as well as in the Palatinate Forest. There are good reasons for this: When used correctly, poles relieve the strain on the knees and hips when descending, provide support on slippery surfaces, help when crossing streams and put the arms to work when going up steep hills. However, if used incorrectly, they are useless or even a hindrance. Many people wear their poles too short, grip the strap incorrectly or use them where they should have their hands free. This guide explains how to find the right poles, adjust them correctly and walk with them.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - On the plane, the length is correct if your elbow is approximately at a right angle when the stick is in place.
> - When going uphill, shorten the poles by 5 to 10 centimeters and lengthen them by 5 to 10 centimeters when going downhill.
> - Grab the loop from below: The hand goes through the loop from below and then grasps the handle and loop together. This is how you transfer force through the wrist.
> - When descending, you place the poles in front of your body and use them to brake. This noticeably relieves the strain on the knees and thighs.
> - Wherever you need your hands, on ladders, ropes, rock steps, the sticks go on the backpack.

## What sticks are good for

**Relief on the descent:** Every step downhill supports the body weight and the thigh muscles work to brake. Use sticks to distribute some of the load between your arms. Many people with sensitive knees can only walk long descents without discomfort using poles.

**Strength in the ascent:** On steep passages you push yourself with your arms. The work is spread over more muscles and you can find a consistent rhythm more easily.

**Balance:** Two additional support points give you security on rubble, wet roots, snow or when crossing a stream. This is particularly valuable with a heavy backpack on a multi-day tour.

**Rhythm:** On long, flat stretches, the sticks help some people maintain a steady pace, similar to Nordic walking.

## When sticks get in the way

- **Secured climbs, ladders, ropes:** You need both hands here. Stow the poles on your backpack beforehand, not in the middle of the passage.
- **Block terrain and rocky steps:** On large blocks like on the Lusen or in rocky gorges you can grip better with your hands.
- **Narrow, busy paths:** In gorges or on narrow paths with oncoming traffic, stick tips are a danger to others.
- **If you want to train your balance:** If you only ever walk with sticks, you rely on them. Take familiar, easy paths without them.

## Which sticks are right for you?

| Construction type | Advantages | Disadvantages | Suitable for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Telescopic pole, 2 or 3 segments | infinitely adjustable, robust, inexpensive | longer in pack size | almost all tours, uphill/downhill adjustment |
| Folding stick (Z-stick) | very small pack size, light | Length only adjustable to a limited extent or not at all | Tours where the poles often hang on the backpack, travel |
| Aluminum | stable, bends rather than breaks | a little heavier | general use, high load |
| Carbon | lightweight, dampens vibrations | can break under lateral load | Weight-conscious, well-groomed handling |

**Closure:** Clamp locks with levers can be operated quickly even when wearing gloves and are easy to adjust. Twist locks are slim, but can jam or slip in cold or dirty conditions.

**Handle:** Cork conforms to the hand and remains grippy when sweating. Foam is light and soft. Rubber handles are sturdy, but get sweaty when it's hot. An extended grip zone under the main handle is practical: when crossing, you can simply grip the upper stick deeper without adjusting the length.

**Tips and Plates:** Carbide tips grip rock and ice. Rubber buffers protect asphalt and stone slabs and dampen the clicking. Small plates prevent sinking into soft ground, large plates are intended for snow.

## Adjust the correct length

Stand upright, let your arms hang loosely and place the stick vertically next to your foot. Grip the handle so that your forearm is horizontal and your elbow forms approximately a right angle. This is the basic length for flat terrain. As a rough guide, it often corresponds to around two thirds of the body size.

| Terrain | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| level | Base length, elbows about 90 degrees |
| Steeply uphill | 5 to 10 cm shorter |
| Steeply downhill | 5 to 10 cm longer |
| Cross passage on the slope | Make the stick shorter or deeper on the mountain side |

With telescopic poles, first set the upper segment to the basic length and only adjust the lower segment in the terrain. Note the maximum marking on the pipe: If a segment is pulled out further, the connection is no longer stable.

## Grip the loop correctly

Most people put their hand into the strap from above and then grab the handle. So the strap hangs uselessly on the wrist. It works correctly like this:

1. Guide your hand through the loop from below so that the loop lies over the back of your hand.
2. Now grab the handle with the two loop strands between your thumb and index finger and under your palm.
3. Adjust the loop so that it fits snugly around the wrist without constricting.

Now you can use the loop to support yourself without having to grip the handle tightly. The hand remains relaxed and the forearms tire less.

**Exception:** In terrain where you could fall, on boulder fields or when crossing streams, some mountain guides pull their hands from the straps. If the stick gets caught between stones, your wrist will be twisted or you won't be able to catch yourself when you fall.

## Walking technique

### On the flat and slightly rising

Place the sticks diagonally to your step: right foot in front, left stick in front, and vice versa. This corresponds to the natural arm swing. The tip of the stick lands about level with the heel of the front foot, not far in front of it.

### Steeply uphill

Shorten the sticks and place them close to the body. The **double pole use** helps on very steep passages: place both poles in front of you at the same time, then take two steps up and push yourself up over the poles. Make sure to stay upright. Anyone who bends over the poles puts strain on their backs and loses their balance.

### Steeply downhill

Extend the sticks and place them in front of the body, about one step ahead. Then you step into the sticks and let them absorb some of your weight. Walk with your knees slightly bent and take short steps. On slippery surfaces, place both poles in front of you at the same time, then your feet follow. This is slower but very stable.

### Cross passage on the slope

If the path runs diagonally along the slope, the uphill slope is too long and the downhill slope is too short. Simply grab the upper stick lower on the pipe or shorten it and lengthen the lower one. This keeps both shoulders at the same height.

## Common mistakes

- **Sticks too short:** Then you bend over and put strain on your back.
- **Sticks far in front of your body when climbing:** You pull yourself instead of pushing yourself off.
- **Points facing up on the backpack:** They endanger others in huts, buses and on narrow paths. Attach them with the tips facing down or put on rubber caps.
- **Hands cramped:** If you use the loop correctly, you can grip loosely.
- **Sticks as a crutch for tours that are too difficult:** They help, but do not replace fitness or sure-footedness.

## Care

After a wet tour, pull telescopic poles apart and let the segments dry. Moisture and dirt in the connections lead to corrosion and allow fasteners to slip. Regularly check the tips for wear and the clamps for tightness. Many manufacturers sell replacement tips, plates and locking parts, so good poles last for many years.

## Sticks in winter

Poles are particularly valuable for winter hikes on snow-covered paths because they stabilize your balance on slippery surfaces. Swap the small summer plates for large snow plates so that the sticks don't sink into the snow. Carbide tips provide good grip on icy paths, but are no substitute for grit on your shoes. In cold weather, clamp fasteners are easier to use with gloves than screw fasteners, which can also freeze in frost. A piece of insulating tape around the handle prevents cold metal parts from cooling down your hands.

## More than just walking aids

Hiking poles can perform multiple tasks while on the move. Some models have a threaded handle onto which a camera can be screwed, allowing the stick to serve as a simple monopod. Many lightweight trekking tents use hiking poles as support instead of their own poles, which saves weight on multi-day tours. In an emergency, you can temporarily splint an injured arm or finger with a stick and tape, and you can use a stick to feel your way in tall grass or on unclear ground.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need two sticks or is one enough?

Two poles provide symmetrical relief and provide more stability. A single stick can be sufficient in light terrain, but puts more strain on one side. For long descents, two are clearly better.

### Are hiking poles useful for children?

Usually not for small children, they need their hands to balance and play. From school age onwards, short children's poles can help on long descents. More about this in [Hiking with school children](/ratgeber/wandern-mit-schulkindern/).

### Can I take hiking poles on the plane?

Usually only in checked baggage because the tips are considered a dangerous item. Check your airline's regulations.

### Do sticks help against sore muscles?

They distribute the load as you descend and can reduce muscle soreness in the thighs. However, you may feel your shoulders and arms after the first few tours.

### Is an expensive model worth it?

For occasional hikes, solid aluminum poles with a clamp lock from the mid-range price range are sufficient. Expensive carbon poles are lighter, which is especially important on long tours and when carried on a backpack. More important than the price are a reliable closure, a comfortable grip and the ability to buy spare parts such as tips and plates.
