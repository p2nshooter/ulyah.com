“Surefootedness and a head for heights required”: This sentence is found in many tour descriptions, and everyone understands it differently. For some, a narrow path with a few stones is a challenge, while others consider an exposed passage with a wire rope to be a walk. There are uniform categories in the Alps so that hikers can assess the difficulty of a trail in advance. If you understand them and assess yourself honestly, you will avoid the most common cause of mountain accidents: the wrong tour for your own abilities.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - **Blue:** easy mountain path, mostly wide, moderately steep, no places where there is a risk of falling.
> - **Red:** moderately difficult mountain path, mostly narrow, often steep, short insured sections possible, sure-footedness required.
> - **Black:** Difficult mountain path, narrow, steep, risk of falling, insured passages require the use of hands, sure-footedness and a head for heights.
> - The SAC hiking scale from Switzerland divides even more precisely, from T1 hiking to T6 difficult alpine hiking.
> - The classification applies to good conditions. Wetness, snow and fog make every route more difficult.

## The color scale of signposts

In the Bavarian Alps and Austria, mountain trails are divided into three levels of difficulty according to a common system. You can find the color on signposts, maps and tour portals.

| Category | Road condition | Requirements |
|---|---|---|
| Blue: easy mountain path | mostly wide, can be steep, no passages that pose a risk of falling | normal fitness, sturdy shoes |
| Red: moderate mountain trail | predominantly narrow, often steep, short passages with a risk of falling or insured are possible | Surefootedness, mountain experience, good fitness |
| Black: difficult mountain trail | narrow, steep, risk of falling, often insured walking passages and easy climbing areas where hands are necessary | Sure-footedness, a head for heights, alpine experience, very good fitness |

There are also **alpine routes** that are not designed as paths and are not regularly maintained. They require orientation skills and alpine experience. **Via Ferrata** has its own scale from A to E and requires via ferrata equipment.

There is no uniform difficulty scale in the low mountain ranges. Tour descriptions with kilometers, altitude meters and information about rocky passages are helpful. There are also steep sections there, for example in the Elbe Sandstone Mountains or in the Bode Valley, as described in [Saxon Switzerland: Day Tours](/ratgeber/saechsische-schweiz-tagestouren/).

## The SAC walking scale

The Swiss Alpine Club has developed a six-stage scale that is also used in many German-language tour portals:

| stage | Designation | Typical |
|---|---|---|
| T1 | Hiking | Well-paved paths, hardly any risk of falling |
| T2 | Mountain hiking | Continuous path, partly steep, risk of falling is not excluded |
| T3 | challenging mountain hiking | exposed areas, boulders, easy places with hands |
| T4 | Alpine hiking | Trails are not always present, hands are needed, steep slopes of grass |
| T5 | challenging alpine hiking | often pathless, climbing areas, simple glaciers |
| T6 | difficult alpine hiking | very exposed, climbing spots, glaciers with equipment |

Roughly, blue paths correspond to levels T1 to T2, red paths T2 to T3 and black paths T3 to T4. The transitions are fluid.

## What sure-footedness means

You are sure-footed if you stand and walk safely on uneven, steep or slippery surfaces without tripping or slipping. These include:

- that you consciously place your foot on stable places, on stones, roots, rubble and grass,
- that you go down steep descents in a controlled manner and without fear,
- that you can keep your balance even with a backpack and when tired,
- that you can assess where a slip would have consequences.

Sure-footedness can be trained: on low mountain paths with roots and stones, on red-marked paths in dry weather, through balance exercises at home. Good shoes with solid soles help, but do not replace them. More about this in [Buy hiking boots](/ratgeber/wanderschuhe-kaufen/).

## What a head for heights means

You will have no fear of heights if you remain calm and concentrated in exposed areas, i.e. where the path is steep or vertical. Fear of heights is not a weakness, but a natural reaction. But it becomes dangerous if it paralyzes you in a narrow area or makes you hectic.

Test yourself on paths with short exposed passages before venturing onto black paths. Turn back when you feel the depth unsettling you. This is a wise decision, not a defeat.

## Assess yourself honestly

Before choosing a tour, answer these questions honestly:

1. What paths have I taken recently without problems, and how did they feel?
2. How many meters of altitude can I climb without being exhausted at the end? How to calculate this can be found in [Calculate walking time](/ratgeber/gehzeit-berechnen/).
3. Have I ever descended a narrow, steep path when I was tired?
4. How do I react to exposed areas?
5. Is my companion on the same level, especially children?

Improve yourself in small steps: first several blue trails, then red trails in good weather, later black trails with experienced support or a guided tour.

## Conditions change everything

The color of a path describes it in dry, snow-free conditions. When it rains, grass, roots and rocks become slippery, snow fields in early summer quickly turn a red path into a dangerous passage, and fog makes orientation difficult. Before the tour, check the weather as described in [Read mountain weather](/ratgeber/bergwetter-lesen/) and ask at the hut or at the tourist information about current path conditions.

## Use insured places correctly

On red and black paths, wire ropes, chains, iron brackets or ladders help you get over difficult places. Here's how to use it safely:

- Stow hiking poles in your backpack beforehand so that both hands are free.
- Grab the rope, but don't rely on it alone. Your feet carry you.
- Walk individually: Only one person per rope section so that no one is dragged.
- Check anchors briefly before holding on to them. You should report loose safety devices to the hut or section.
- For children, a rope can be useful for longer belays. Get advice from experienced mountaineers.

## Common misjudgments

Many accidents on mountain trails don't happen in the most difficult part, but afterward. You should know these errors:

- **Underestimating the descent:** Anyone who was able to cope well on a rocky step during the ascent will find themselves with tired legs in the same place on the descent, only now they have to look down. Plan descents consciously and check whether you would safely go down a difficult section.
- **Grass is more harmless than rock:** Steep, wet grassy slopes are some of the most dangerous surfaces in the mountains. If you slip on grass, you can hardly find your footing.
- **The tour is short, so easy:** A black path remains difficult even if it only lasts two hours.
- **The group can do it:** The difficulty is based on the weakest group member, not the strongest.
- **Others go too:** Lots of people on a path doesn't mean it's right for you.

## Training for red and black paths

Sure-footedness and a head for heights can be developed in a targeted manner. In the weeks before a demanding tour, walk on rooty, stony paths in low mountain ranges, practice descents on steep terrain and progress from blue to red paths. Balance exercises at home, such as standing on one leg or balancing on a narrow edge, strengthen the small muscles in the feet and ankles. If you don't want to walk your first black trails alone, you can find guided tours and courses at Alpine club sections and mountain schools.

## Difficulty information in neighboring countries

Anyone who hikes across the border in the Alps will encounter different systems. In Germany and Austria, mountain trails are classified with the colors blue, red and black. In Switzerland, hiking trails are marked yellow, mountain hiking trails white-red-white and challenging alpine hiking trails white-blue-white. In Italy, the Alpine Association CAI uses abbreviations: T for tourist trails, E for hiking trails, EE for trails for experienced hikers and EEA for trails with protection.

These systems cannot be compared one-to-one. A white-red-white mountain hiking trail in Switzerland can be significantly more demanding than a red trail in the Bavarian foothills of the Alps. Read the directions before every tour abroad and, if in doubt, ask at huts or tourist information centers.

## Evaluate difficulty and length together

The difficulty rating says something about the terrain, not about the length and altitude of a tour. A blue path can be very long and have many meters of elevation, a black path can only be a short, exposed section. Therefore, always plan both: the technical difficulty and the endurance that a tour requires. The guide [Calculate walking time](/ratgeber/gehzeit-berechnen/) explains how you can realistically calculate your walking time.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a black path a via ferrata?

No. Black paths have insured passages for which no via ferrata equipment is normally required. Via ferratas are fully secured climbing routes with their own rating and require a harness, via ferrata set and helmet.

### Can I walk red trails with children?

With sure-footed schoolchildren, yes, if the route is not too long and has no exposed areas. Smaller children stay on blue paths. More in [Hiking with school children](/ratgeber/wandern-mit-schulkindern/).

### Are the classifications the same everywhere?

The colors are awarded in Bavaria and Austria according to common principles, but the assessment of individual paths remains a matter of discretion. Always read the tour description.

### Who determines how difficult a path is?

The classification is carried out by the trail keepers, usually sections of the Alpine clubs, municipalities or tourism associations. They assess the terrain, risk of falling and safety features. Because people judge, there are regional differences. Therefore, treat a rating as guidance, not as a guarantee.

### How do I know if I'm ready for a red path?

If you walk blue trails safely and without fear, even on longer descents on rocky surfaces, a short, well-marked red trail is a good next step. Choose a day with stable weather and plan time for breaks. If possible, walk the first red paths with experienced companions.

This is how your experience grows step by step.

Every well-mastered tour makes the next one easier.
