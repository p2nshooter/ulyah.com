A gorge is a narrow, deeply cut gorge that a mountain stream has dug into the rock over thousands of years. The walls are so close together that you can only see the sky as a narrow strip, the water thunders beneath you through pools and over steps, and everywhere it drips, sprays and rushes. There are a number of such gorges in the Bavarian Alps, which are accessible through paths, galleries, tunnels and bridges. For many families and hikers, they are among the most impressive excursion destinations ever.

This guide introduces the most famous gorges and gives tips on how to best experience them. Most gorges are maintained by communities or associations that charge an entry fee to maintain the paths. Opening times and prices change; check them on the official sites before visiting.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Gorges are cool, wet and loud. A rain jacket and non-slip shoes are also essential on hot days.
> - Most gorges have fixed opening times and seasons, some close in winter, others are open all year round.
> - After heavy rain, thunderstorms or falling rocks, gorges can be closed for a short time.
> - On summer weekends and during the holidays there are sometimes long queues at the entrance. It's worth coming early.
> - The paths are usually not accessible with strollers; a stretcher is the better choice.

## Partnachklamm near Garmisch-Partenkirchen

The Partnachklamm is the most famous gorge in Bavaria. The Partnach has dug its way through the rock for around 700 meters long, and the walls rise to well over 80 meters at the highest points. The path leads along a path through tunnels and galleries directly above the roaring water.

The starting point is usually the Olympic ski stadium in Garmisch-Partenkirchen, from which a wide path leads to the gorge entrance. The Partnachklamm is usually open all year round. In winter, massive icicles and ice curtains form on the walls, which are particularly impressive when illuminated.

The gorge can easily be combined with a circular hike, for example up to the alpine pastures above the gorge or further into the Reintal, through which the longest path to the Zugspitze leads. Because the gorge is so well known, it is very crowded on nice days. Those who come early in the morning will experience a much more relaxed experience.

## Höllentalklamm near Grainau

The Höllentalklamm lies below the Zugspitze and is the access to the Höllental. It is wilder and more pristine than the Partnachklamm. The path was created at the beginning of the 20th century and leads through numerous tunnels and over bridges close to the water.

The Höllentalklamm is only open in the summer season, approximately from late spring to autumn, depending on snow conditions and avalanche remnants. A path with a steady incline leads from the Hammersbach district to the gorge entrance. After the gorge you reach the Höllentalangerhütte, a nice destination for a day tour with noticeable elevation gain. The tour is easy for sure-footed families with school-age children.

The water is often particularly strong at the end of the season and after thunderstorms. It is cool and wet in the gorge, even if it is midsummer outside.

## Breitachklamm near Oberstdorf

The Breitachklamm in the Allgäu is one of the deepest rocky gorges in Central Europe. The Breitach has dug a gorge into the rock here that is up to 150 meters deep. A well-developed path leads around two kilometers through the gorge, past rock faces and waterfalls.

The Breitachklamm is also open for much of the year, with impressive ice formations in winter. It can be reached from Oberstdorf with a longer hike or by bus and can be easily combined with tours to Kleinwalsertal. In spring the gorge may be temporarily closed due to melting snow or ice break-off.

## Gorges in Berchtesgadener Land

There are also gorges worth seeing around Berchtesgaden:

- **Wimbachklamm in Ramsau:** a short but very impressive gorge at the entrance to the Wimbachtal, ideal as a start to a hike to Wimbach Castle and further into the Berchtesgaden National Park.
- **Almbachklamm near Marktschellenberg:** a longer gorge with many bridges, steps and tunnels that leads up to a pilgrimage church. It is varied and has numerous waterfalls and pools.

## More gorges

In the Bavarian Alps you will find many smaller gorges, such as the Starzlachklamm near Sonthofen or the Leutaschklamm on the border between Mittenwald and Tyrol, which is accessible with paths and panoramic bridges. They are often less crowded and are particularly worthwhile for anyone who already knows the large gorges.

## What you should wear

It's almost always significantly cooler in a gorge than outside, and the spray will make you wet. This equipment has proven itself:

- **Non-slip shoes:** The paths are damp, partly mossy or covered with wet wood. Hiking shoes or sturdy sports shoes with good tread are mandatory; sandals and smooth soles are unsuitable.
- **Rain jacket:** Even on sunny days, against spray mist and dripping water from the tunnels.
- **Warm layer:** Especially for children who get cold easily.
- **Waterproof case for cell phone:** The spray also reaches the camera.

Helmets are not required in most gorges. In some gorges, however, signs point out the danger of falling rocks; Then the following applies: move on quickly and don't stop under the signs.

## Safety in the gorge

The paths in the large gorges are well secured, with railings, ropes and paved paths. Nevertheless, a few rules apply:

1. **Stay on the trail.** Do not climb over railings or descend into the stream bed. The current is stronger than it looks and the water is ice cold.
2. **Take children by the hand.** Especially in narrow places and bridges.
3. **Walk slowly.** The paths are wet and oncoming traffic sometimes comes unexpectedly around the corner.
4. **Take closures seriously.** After heavy rain, water can rise quickly and rock falls are more common. A closed gorge is closed for a reason.
5. **Not during thunderstorms.** Thunderstorms in the area can cause sudden rises in water in the gorge.

## Into the gorge with children

Almost all children are fascinated by gorges: tunnels, bridges, waterfalls, the roar of the water. A back carrier is the best choice for small children, as strollers are not allowed on the narrow paths with steps or are impractical. Schoolchildren can usually go themselves. Take into account that it is loud in the gorge and that some children find this frightening. Afterwards, plan a quiet break in the sun, because warmth feels good after the cool gorge.

## How a gorge is created

Most of the gorges in the Bavarian Alps are geologically young. During the Ice Ages, mighty glaciers carved out the large valleys, leaving behind valley steps over which streams tumbled as the ice melted. Since then, the water has worked its way into the rock. It not only dissolves rock, but also carries sand, gravel and stones with it that act like sandpaper. Where the water circulates, stones turn into depressions and drill out round basins, so-called whirlpool pots or pools. Because the stream digs deeper faster than the walls weather, the typical narrow, vertical gorges are created. You can often see where the stream once flowed on the smooth, sanded walls high above the current water level.

## Arrival without a car

The parking spaces at the famous gorges fill up quickly on nice days and are usually subject to charges. You can easily reach many gorges by train and bus. Garmisch-Partenkirchen has a train station with direct trains from Munich, and a footpath or a bus leads from the town to the Olympic Ski Stadium, the starting point for the Partnachklamm. In Grainau, the cog railway to the Zugspitze stops in the Hammersbach district, near the path to the Höllentalklamm. Buses run to Breitachklamm from Oberstdorf, and in Berchtesgadener Land regional bus lines connect the towns with the starting points. In many holiday destinations, bus travel is free or reduced with the guest card. Check the timetables beforehand, as buses in some valleys rarely run in the evening.

## Gorges in winter

Some gorges are also open in winter and then show a completely different side. Mighty icicles, ice curtains and frozen waterfalls grow on the walls from seepage water. But the visit has its own pitfalls: steps and footbridges can be icy, the days are short, and it is even colder in the gorge than outside. Light bridles or spikes for shoes, warm gloves and a hat make the trip safer and more pleasant. Before visiting, check whether the gorge is open, as ice break-off and the risk of avalanches on the slopes above always lead to closures.

## Consideration and nature conservation

Gorges are delicate habitats where mosses, ferns and rare plants thrive in the moist, cool climate. Stay on the path, don't throw stones into the water, as there may be other people below you, and take your rubbish with you. Drones are expressly forbidden in many gorges and are a nuisance in narrow, crowded places anyway. In narrow passages, stop briefly and let oncoming traffic pass instead of pushing past. If you want to take photos, look for a wider spot so that the path remains clear.

## Frequently asked questions

### When is the best time to visit a gorge?

Early in the morning, before the bus groups arrive, or in the late afternoon. After a few days of rain, the streams carry a lot of water, which is particularly impressive as long as the gorge remains open.

### Can my dog ​​go into the gorge?

Dogs on a leash are allowed in many gorges, but not in some. For some dogs, gratings and the noise are unpleasant. Check the rules beforehand.

### How long does a visit to the gorge take?

The gorge itself can often be crossed in half an hour to an hour. With the climb, the way back and breaks, you should plan two to four hours for an excursion, depending on the gorge and the circular route.

### How much does entry into a gorge cost?

Most gorges charge an entry fee of a few euros for adults; children pay less. The money goes towards maintaining the paths, railings and bridges, which is expensive in the wet, rockfall-prone terrain. In some holiday regions there are discounts with the guest card. Because prices and payment options change, check the gorge's official website before visiting and take some cash with you.
