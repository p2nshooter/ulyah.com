The Bavarian Forest is the largest contiguous forest area in Central Europe, together with the Bohemian Forest on the Czech side. Its peaks reach almost 1,500 meters, and yet many tours here feel wilder than in many Alpine valleys. In the Bavarian Forest National Park, the first German national park, founded in 1970, the forest has been allowed to develop freely for decades. Dead spruce trees, young beech trees and mountain ash trees, raised bogs and granite boulders characterize the picture. Anyone who climbs the well-known peaks will experience this development up close and will be rewarded with far-reaching views of the Alps when the air is clear.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Großer Arber, 1,456 meters: highest peak in the Bavarian Forest, with mountain railway and two karst lakes.
> - Großer Rachel, 1,453 meters: wild, steep climb from Rachelsee with Rachel Chapel.
> - Lusen, 1,373 meters: Summit made of a huge sea of ​​granite blocks, ascent via the stone ladder to heaven.
> - Großer Falkenstein, 1,315 meters: jungle-like forests and the Höllbachg Spreng gorge.
> - There are trail regulations in the national park to protect rare animals. Stay on the marked paths.

## Big Arber

The Arber is the “King of the Bavarian Forest”. Its summit region is dotted with rocks, pines and radar domes, and when visibility is good the view extends over the Bohemian Forest and the Danube Valley to the Alps. Because a gondola lift goes just below the summit, it is busy at the top on nice days.

The climb from the **Großer Arbersee** is most beautiful on foot. The dark Karsee lies at the foot of a steep wall carved out by Ice Age glaciers. From the lake you climb through mountain forest up to the summit plateau. A worthwhile option is the descent to the **Little Arbersee** with its floating islands made of peat and plants that move slowly in the wind. You should plan a whole day for the tour with both lakes.

## Big Rachel and Rachelsee

The Rachel is only a few meters lower than the Arber, but appears much wilder. It is located in the middle of the national park, without a cable car. The classic ascent begins at the parking lot at Gfäll and first leads to **Rachelsee**, a dark karst lake in a hollow under the summit wall. On the steep slope above the lake is the small **Rachelkapelle**, from whose rock you have a famous view of the lake.

From the lake it is a steep and rocky climb up to the summit. At the very top there is a rocky plateau with a wide view over the forest seas. In the summit region you can clearly see how the forest renews itself after many spruce trees have died. The tour requires fitness and sure-footedness on scree and roots.

## Lusen about the ladder to heaven

The Lusen is unmistakable: its summit consists of a huge sea of granite blocks over which you climb from block to block to the summit cross. When wet, the blocks are slippery and there are gaps between them. Go slowly and carefully here.

The best-known route to the summit is the **Himmelsleiter**, a path made of countless stone steps that leads from the Lusenschutzhaus to the summit. The refuge just below the summit is managed. At the foot of the Lusen near Neuschönau is the national park center with the treetop path and a large outdoor animal area, ideal for a day with children.

## Großer Falkenstein and Höllbachg Spreng

The Falkenstein is located in the northern part of the national park near Zwiesel and Lindberg. It is known for mixed mountain forests, some of which are reminiscent of jungle, and for the **Höllbachg Spreng**, a steep, wildly romantic gorge with boulders and waterfalls. The path through the gorge is steep, rocky and slippery when wet. At the top, the Falkenstein shelter awaits you with a stop. Many people choose a more comfortable forest path for the descent.

## Wilderness in the national park: what you need to know

Where the bark beetle has caused spruce trees to die, there are often dead trunks still standing. They provide habitat for many species, but can collapse during storms or heavy snow. Avoid the forest in strong winds and pay attention to closures. The national park only removes dead trees along trails if they pose a danger.

In parts of the national park, trail regulations apply all year round or at certain times. It protects animals that are sensitive to disturbance, such as the capercaillie, which is particularly dependent on peace and quiet in winter and spring. Follow the signs, keep dogs on a leash and stay on marked paths. Camping is not permitted in the national park.

## Weather and seasons

The Bavarian Forest has a harsh climate. In winter there is often meter-deep snow on the peaks, and many paths can only be accessed with snowshoes. In spring the snow in the higher altitudes lasts until May. Inversion weather conditions often occur in autumn: there is fog in the valleys, while the peaks are in the sun above a sea of ​​fog. These are the days when you can see the Alps from Arber or Lusen. What you should know about weather conditions in the mountains can be found in [Read Mountain Weather](/ratgeber/bergwetter-lesen/).

## Arrival without a car

The Waldbahn connects several places in the Bavarian Forest, including Zwiesel, Bayerisch Eisenstein and Grafenau. In the national park, the hedgehog buses run to the most important starting points such as Lusen, Rachel and Falkenstein in summer. This also makes it easy to plan route tours. More about this in [Hiking without a car](/ratgeber/wandern-ohne-auto/).

## Shorter distances for rest days and families

Not every day has to be a summit day. There are short, well-developed paths around the national park centers that show the wilderness of the Bavarian Forest without requiring a lot of fitness. At the Lusen National Park Center, the treetop path leads along wooden walkways through the crowns of the trees. There is another outdoor animal area at the Falkenstein National Park Center near Ludwigsthal. Short circular routes through raised bogs like the Filze, with wooden walkways over the wet ground, show a very unique landscape with cotton grass and sundew.

## What you should take with you

Even on warm days it is significantly cooler and often windy on the peaks. A rain jacket and a warm layer always belong in your backpack. For Lusen and Rachel you need sturdy shoes with good tread, because granite slabs and roots are slippery when wet. In many areas there is no consistent mobile phone network. Download the map offline as described in [Hiking apps and GPX tracks](/ratgeber/wander-apps-und-gpx/) and take enough water with you, as managed refuges have rest days.

## The national park and its idea

The Bavarian Forest National Park was founded in 1970 as Germany's first national park. His guiding principle is “let nature be nature”: in large parts, humans no longer intervene. When the bark beetle caused large stands of spruce trees to die in the 1990s, the dead trees remained standing in many places. Today a young, species-rich forest is growing there, which impressively shows how nature renews itself.

On the Czech side is the Šumava National Park. Together, both protected areas form one of the largest contiguous forest landscapes in Central Europe. For hikers this means: wide, quiet forests, lots of wild animals and paths where you often don't meet anyone for a long time.

## Rules in the national park

There are some rules in the national park that protect nature. In certain zones and at certain times there is a travel requirement, so you are not allowed to leave the marked paths. Dogs must be kept on a leash, drones are prohibited, and wild camping or spending the night outdoors is not permitted. Pay attention to the signs at the entrances.

## Hiking in winter

Even in winter, the Bavarian Forest is a beautiful hiking area, with snow-covered forests and often clear distant views. Cleared winter hiking trails and guided snowshoe tours offer safe options. Consider the short days, the cold on the peaks and possible slippery conditions. Some trails are closed in winter to protect wildlife.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which summit is suitable with children?

The Arber with the mountain railway and a short summit circular route, or the Lusen via the Himmelsleiter with sure-footed schoolchildren. The block sea delights children, but requires supervision.

### Can I combine multiple summits in one day?

Yes, Rachel and Lusen can be combined on a long hike, as can Arber and the neighboring peaks on the ridge. Such tours are long and require good fitness.

### Are there bears or wolves in the Bavarian Forest?

Wolves and lynxes live in the region, but are very shy and avoid people. Encounters are rare. There are no bears there.

### Can I take a dog with me in the national park?

Yes, but only on a leash. This protects wild animals such as deer, red deer and ground-nesting birds that live in the quiet forests. Bring enough water for the dog and note that some facilities such as animal enclosures are not accessible to dogs.

### What time of year is best for the peaks?

Late summer and autumn often offer stable weather, clear views and colorful forests. In spring there may still be some snow left on the highest peaks and the paths will be muddy after the snow melts. In midsummer, early starts are worthwhile because thunderstorms can also form in low mountain ranges.

### Are there huts with refreshments in the Bavarian Forest?

Yes, there are shelters and mountain inns on and at several peaks. Their opening times change depending on the season, and some close in winter or on certain weekdays. Check them before the tour and always take some provisions with you.

It's worth making a quick call in advance, especially after holidays or in late autumn.
