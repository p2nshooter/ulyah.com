Cows in an alpine meadow are part of the image that many people have in their minds about hiking in the mountains. Most of the time the animals are peaceful, grazing, dozing and at most just looking curiously after hikers. Nevertheless, incidents occur every year in which hikers are harassed, knocked over or injured by cows. Two things almost always play a role: mother cows with calves and dogs. If you understand the animals' body language and follow a few simple rules, you can almost always get past any herd without any problems. This also applies to flocks of sheep, which are increasingly being guarded by large livestock guard dogs.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Keep your distance, at least 20 to 50 meters if the path allows it. Do not pet, feed or photograph cows up close.
> - Mother cows defend their calves. Never walk between cow and calf.
> - Keep dogs on a short leash. Attacks the herd: Let go of the leash so that the dog and human can escape separately.
> - Warning signs: lowered head, pawing, snorting, loud roaring, the herd is moving together and getting closer. Quietly retreat, don't run.
> - For flocks of sheep with livestock guard dogs: stop, let the dogs sniff, walk in a wide circle and calmly.

## Why cows can be dangerous

Cows are not aggressive animals. They are herd animals that react together to threats. It becomes particularly dangerous in two situations:

**Suckler cows with calves:** In suckler cow farming, the calves stay with their mothers on pasture throughout the summer. The cows have a strong protective instinct. If they see their calf threatened, they defend it, and a cow often weighs more than 600 kilograms.

**Dogs:** To cows, a dog looks like a predator, a wolf or fox, that can be dangerous to their calves. Many serious incidents occur when people walk through a herd with dogs. The cows direct their defense against the dog and also against the person who is holding him on a leash.

There are also situations in which animals are frightened: people who suddenly appear, run, scream, wave sticks or want to touch calves.

## Body language: this is how you recognize warning signs

| behavior | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Grazing, lying, chewing, occasionally looking | relaxed, no problem |
| Raise your head, ears forward, eyes fixed on you | be attentive, keep your distance |
| Cows move together, calves are placed in the middle | Herd feels disturbed |
| Head bowed, pawing with hooves, snorting | Threatening to calmly withdraw immediately |
| Loud roar, quick approach, several animals follow | Readiness to attack |

## Rules for each pasture

1. **Keep your distance:** If the path passes the herd, move on calmly and quickly. If possible, avoid the herd in a wide berth.
2. **Not between cow and calf:** If a calf is on one side of the path and the mother is on the other, wait or walk wide around both.
3. **Stay calm:** Don't run, don't scream, don't make any hasty movements. Speak calmly so that the animals notice you.
4. **Don't touch, don't feed:** Calves look cute, but stroking them provokes the mother.
5. **Close pasture gates:** Every gate you open, you close again. Escaped animals are a danger to themselves and others.
6. **Do not touch electric fences:** Use gates, ladders or the designated passageways.
7. **Please pay attention to warning signs:** Many alpine farms point out suckler cows. Take these tips seriously.

## Crossing the pasture with a dog

- Keep your dog on a short leash and close to you.
- Stay particularly clear of herds with calves.
- If the cows become alert and approach: Walk calmly without running and leave the pasture via the shortest route.
- **If the herd attacks: Let go of the leash.** Your dog is faster than any cow and can get to safety. The cows usually follow the dog and you are out of the danger zone. Whoever holds the dog ends up in the middle.
- Plan tours with your dog so that they do not take you through pastures with mother cows for hours.

More about leash rules and consideration when out and about can be found in [Hiking with a dog](/ratgeber/wandern-mit-hund/).

## With children across the pasture

Children love cows, and cows are curious. This combination needs supervision:

- Explain beforehand that cows are not cuddly animals and that you do not run, scream or touch calves in the pasture.
- Hold small children by the hand or arm as you walk past a herd.
- Older children walk between the adults, not in front.
- Stay calm. Children sense the insecurity of adults.

[Hiking with small children](/ratgeber/wandern-mit-kleinkindern/) and [Hiking with school children](/ratgeber/wandern-mit-schulkindern/) describe how you generally plan tours for children.

## When a cow harasses you

- Stay calm and turn toward the cow without staring directly at it. Don't turn your back on her.
- Slowly walk backwards or sideways out of the pasture towards the fence or gate.
- Try to put an obstacle between you and the animals: a fence, a large rock, a tree.
- As a last defense if a cow actually attacks you, alpine organizations recommend a targeted blow to the animal's nose with a hiking stick. This is an emergency solution, not a tactic.

After an incident with injuries, make an emergency call as described in [Emergency on the Mountain](/ratgeber/notfall-am-berg/) and inform the alpine farm.

## Herds of sheep with livestock guard dogs

Since the wolf returned to Germany, many shepherds have been protecting their flocks with large livestock guard dogs. These dogs live with the sheep and defend them against anything they see as a threat. They are not herding dogs that are controlled by a human, but work independently.

This is how you behave correctly:

- Do not approach the herd, but walk around the outside in a wide circle if possible.
- If the dogs come towards you barking: stand still, remain calm, speak calmly, and do not look directly at the dogs.
- Let the dogs sniff. They want to check whether you are a danger. Afterwards they usually withdraw.
- Then continue walking slowly, without running or swinging sticks.
- With your own dog: keep a short leash and keep a particularly wide distance. If possible, avoid the pasture entirely.
- Cyclists get off and push.

## Inform before the tour

Many alpine regions publish information about which pastures have mother cows in summer and mark such areas with signs. Ask at the tourist information office or at the hut if you are traveling with a dog, and if in doubt, plan a route that avoids large pastures. There are hiking trails without grazing in almost every region.

## Why we see mother cows more often today

In the past, many pastures were primarily occupied by dairy cows, whose calves were separated from their mothers shortly after birth. Today, suckler cow farming is widespread: the calves grow up with their mothers over the summer, often on large, extensively used areas. This form of grazing is valuable for landscape management and biodiversity because the animals keep open meadows that would otherwise become overgrown. They are not only found on alpine pastures, but also in low mountain ranges such as the Eifel, Rhön, Harz or Black Forest, where hiking trails lead through the middle of the pastures. Robust breeds such as Galloway, Angus or Scottish Highland cattle are usually calm, but defend their calves just as determinedly as any other cow.

## Fences, gates and cattle grids

Pastures are usually secured with electric fences. The current flows in short pulses, is painful, but usually not dangerous for healthy adults. Children and people with pacemakers should still not touch the wire. At passageways there are often plastic gate handles that are insulated: you unhook the gate from the handle, go through and hook it back up. Leave gates as you found them, closed gates closed and open gates open, because sometimes the farmer has a reason for it.

Cattle grids, i.e. grids made of metal rods in the ground, keep animals back without the need for a gate. They are dangerous for dogs because their paws can slip between the bars. Carry small dogs across or use the side passage available at many livestock gates.

## What happened after accidents

A fatal incident in the Tyrolean Pinnistal in 2014, in which a hiker with her dog was attacked by mother cows, sparked debate throughout the Alpine region. In Austria, official rules of conduct for dealing with grazing cattle were then published, and the legislature expressly strengthened the personal responsibility of alpine visitors. In Bavaria, Switzerland and South Tyrol, alpine farming, Alpine clubs and tourism organizations have since then provided more information about correct behavior. The message is the same everywhere: the mountain pastures remain open, and hikers are welcome as long as they keep their distance, walk their dogs briefly and take the animals' signals seriously.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are bulls more dangerous than cows?

Bulls can be dangerous, but are rarely free on hiking trails. Statistically, most incidents originate from mother cows, often in conjunction with dogs.

### Am I even allowed to enter a pasture?

On marked hiking trails, yes. You should not enter pastures off the path, also because the grass is fodder for the animals.

### What if the herd is on the way?

Wait a few minutes, the animals often move on. Otherwise, avoid the herd at a large distance and then return to the path without damaging plants and fences.

### Are Scottish Highland cattle with their long horns dangerous?

Highland cattle appear threatening with their powerful horns, but are considered extremely calm and good-natured. They are often used for landscape maintenance and are therefore often found in pastures in hiking areas. Like all mother cows, they defend their calves. The horns make any collision more dangerous, which is why the same rules apply to them as to all cattle: keep a distance, not between cow and calf, and keep the dog on a short leash.

### What can I do if I am very afraid of cows?

Plan tours using maps and information from the tourist information office so that they do not cross large pastures, or choose paths through forests and along fields. If you do have to pass a herd, it helps to be aware of the rules beforehand and to go with someone. Breathe calmly, continue walking steadily and look ahead at the path. Cows are almost always more interested in their grass than in you.
