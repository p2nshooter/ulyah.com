A special phase when hiking with children begins with school age. They are no longer carried and no longer just run from puddle to puddle, they create real routes, first peaks and maybe even a night in the hut. At the same time, they have their own ideas about what a good day is, and “hiking” sounds boring to many people. But with realistic tours, exciting destinations and a little involvement in the planning, it can become a passion that lasts a lifetime. This guide will help you choose the right tour and keep everyone happy along the way.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - As a loose guide, children can cover about as many kilometers as they are years old, on a good path and with breaks. Elevation meters count extra.
> - Choose tours with destinations: summit cross, castle, lake, stream, hut, cave, observation tower.
> - Let the children help you plan and take the lead along the way.
> - Having your own equipment makes you proud: a small backpack, good shoes, your own water bottle.
> - Agree on clear rules: wait at intersections, stay within sight, give your hand at precipices.

## How far and how high?

Children are very different. Some six-year-olds enthusiastically run ten kilometers, some ten-year-olds think five is too much. Practice, motivation and terrain are crucial. This table provides a rough guide for children with some hiking experience:

| Dude | route | Elevation meters in the ascent | Walking time without breaks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 to 7 years | 5 to 7 km | 200 to 400 m | 2 to 3 hours |
| 8 to 9 years | 7 to 10 km | 400 to 600 m | 3 to 4 hours |
| 10 to 12 years | 10 to 14 km | 600 to 900 m | 4 to 5 hours |

Always plan more time for breaks with children than with adults, about a third to half of the walking time. The formula for walking time for adults in [Calculate walking time](/ratgeber/gehzeit-berechnen/) only applies to children to a limited extent. It's better to start with a tour that's too short than one that's too long. A successful tour is the best advertisement for the next one.

## Tours that kids love

Children rarely hike for the view. They want to experience something. Good ingredients for a children's tour are:

- **Water:** Build streams to dam, waterfalls, lakes to throw stones.
- **Rocks and caves:** Climbing rocks on the side of the path, seas of blocks like at [Lusen](/ratgeber/bayerischer-wald-gipfel/) or sandstone cliffs in the [Palatinate Forest](/ratgeber/pfaelzerwald-wandern/).
- **Castles and Ruins:** Knight games and stories make every climb easier.
- **Huts and playgrounds:** A Kaiserschmarrn halfway through is a strong argument.
- **Observation towers and summit crosses:** A real destination seen from below.
- **Experience trails:** Many regions have barefoot trails, forest nature trails or themed trails with stations.
- **Gorges:** Footbridges over roaring water, like in [Glammen in Bavaria](/ratgeber/klammen-in-bayern/), are an adventure for children. However, long, narrow footbridges with oncoming traffic require supervision.

## Motivation without bribery

**Let them plan:** Show your children the map, let them choose the destination and follow the route with their finger. Anyone who has planned will also want to arrive.

**Be guided:** Older children can look for the right sign at intersections and lead the group for a while. How the signs work is explained in [Understanding Waymarks](/ratgeber/wegmarkierungen-verstehen/).

**Give tasks:** Take photos, look for animal tracks, identify plants, collect stamps. In the Harz, for example, many families collect stamps from the Harz hiking pin.

**Geocaching:** Treasure hunting with your smartphone turns any forest path into an expedition.

**Take friends with you:** With a friend at your side, children run twice as far and complain half as much.

**Play on the go:** I see something you don't see; word chains; retelling stories; who will find the next marker first.

**Take breaks seriously:** Children need frequent, short breaks to eat and drink. What is useful as provisions is listed in [Provisions and Drinks When Hiking](/ratgeber/proviant-und-trinken/).

## Equipment for children

- **Shoes:** Children's hiking shoes with profile soles and good grip. Sneakers are slippery on wet roots and steep paths. Children's feet grow quickly, check the size before each season.
- **Backpack:** a small, separate backpack with a hip or chest strap. It should weigh a maximum of around ten percent of your body weight: water bottle, snack, hat, rain jacket.
- **Clothing:** Onion principle like adults. Children cool down quickly during breaks and sweat profusely while running around. It's worth having a spare set of socks and a shirt in your parents' backpack.
- **Sun Protection:** Cap or hat, sunscreen, sunglasses at altitude.
- **Sticks:** helpful for longer descents from around ten years old, see [Use hiking poles correctly](/ratgeber/wanderstoecke-richtig-nutzen/).

## Safety rules

Agree on the rules before the tour and stick to them consistently:

1. Everyone is waiting at every intersection.
2. Whoever runs ahead stays in sight.
3. There are no games on precipices, rocks and streams with strong currents.
4. In exposed places, adults take the children by the hand or walk next to them directly down the valley.
5. Anyone who gets lost stops and calls or whistles. A whistle on each child's backpack is a good idea.

The following applies to the difficulty: With school children you stay on the blue paths at the beginning. Sure-footed children aged around ten and over can walk short red paths if the weather is good. More about this in [Mountain trails blue, red, black](/ratgeber/bergwege-schwierigkeit/).

## The first night in the hut

An overnight stay in a hut is the highlight of the year for many children: sleeping in a mattress, starry sky, breakfast above the clouds. How to plan your first night in a hut with children is described in [The first night in a hut with children](/ratgeber/erste-huettennacht-mit-kindern/).

## Hiking with young people

From around the age of twelve, a lot of things change. Young people want to have a say, don't always want to be with their parents and need challenges. Give them responsibilities: navigation, scheduling, carrying some of the equipment. A via ferrata for beginners with a guide, a multi-day tour with friends or a night at a [trekking site](/ratgeber/trekkingplaetze-deutschland/) can awaken new enthusiasm. And sometimes it's okay if there's a period of no hiking.

## After the tour

What happens after the hike will determine whether the children want to come back. An ice cream in town, a swim in the lake or simply the memory over dinner of the stream, the summit and the cow on the side of the path rounds off the day. Some families keep a hiking book in which the children enter stamps, photos, pressed leaves and a few sentences. After a few years it's a treasure.

## Understanding nature: learning without a sense of school

Schoolchildren want to be able to explain the world. A hike offers countless opportunities to do this without it sounding like a lesson. Which tree has these leaves? Why are there such round stones in this stream? How high is the summit and how many floors would that be? A small identification book for trees or birds, a magnifying glass and a pair of binoculars turn children into researchers. Many national parks, nature parks and biosphere reserves also offer junior ranger programs in which children discover the nature of their region with rangers. Ask the visitor centers about tours and activity days for families.

Card reading is also good for this age. Give your child their own copy of the hiking map and have them show where you are along the way. Anyone who learns to interpret contour lines and place a map at the age of eight or nine will have a head start for life. The basics are in [Map and Compass](/ratgeber/karte-und-kompass/).

## Siblings at different paces

If the children are different ages, the tour is based on the youngest. So that the older ones don't get bored, they are given their own tasks: they navigate, carry the map, pay attention to the markings or take a short detour to a viewpoint with an adult while the others take a break. A little head start helps the younger ones: let them go first after each break. Avoid comparisons like “Your sister was faster at your age,” because every child has their own pace and their own enjoyment of the journey.

## Cell phones and technology on the go

Technology can get children excited about hiking if it leads them towards nature rather than away from it. Apps for identifying plants and bird calls, a star map in the evening at the hut or recording your own tour are fun for children. Agree in advance what the cell phone will be used for and when it will stay in the backpack. For many families, it has proven that photos are allowed, but games and videos wait until the evening.

## Frequently asked questions

### What do I do if my child no longer wants to continue?

First take a break with food and drink. Hunger or thirst is often the reason. If that doesn't help, shorten the tour. A child who has had to struggle will be reluctant to hike next time.

### How much weight can a child carry?

As a guide, no more than ten percent of your body weight. Heavy things like water for everyone and a change of clothes are carried by the adults.

### At what age is a real mountain tour possible?

With sure-footed, well-trained children, easy peaks of 600 to 800 meters are possible from around the age of ten. Experience, motivation and weather are crucial.

### How often should we take breaks?

Plan a short break to drink about every hour and a longer break to eat after two to three hours. Schoolchildren often signal hunger and tiredness late, but then clearly. Regular breaks prevent your mood from suddenly changing.

### How do I prepare my child for their first long tour?

Go on a few shorter tours with similar terrain beforehand so that your child gets used to inclines and carrying their own backpack. You break in new shoes together, and your child packs the backpack himself the evening before, with your checklist. Tell us what awaits you, show photos of the destination and plan together where you will take the big break. This makes the tour a joint project that everyone looks forward to.
