No piece of equipment determines a good day of hiking as much as shoes. A shoe that's too tight will turn a nice descent into torture, a shoe that's too soft will cause you to twist your ankle on scree, and a boot that isn't broken in will give you blisters on the second day of a long-distance hike that will stay with you for the rest of the week. At the same time, the selection in specialist retailers is huge, and the terms on the labels are often of little help.

This guide explains which type of shoes are suitable for which terrain, how to go about trying them on and how to break in new shoes so that they serve you well from the first real day of hiking.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - The shoe must suit the terrain, not your ambition: a light shoe is usually sufficient for low mountain paths, but you need more stability for stony alpine terrain.
> - Try on shoes in the afternoon, with your hiking socks and possibly your insoles.
> - There should be about a thumb's width of space at the front and the heel should not slip.
> - Break in new shoes on short tours before going on a long stretch.
> - A waterproof membrane is practical when it's wet, but often too warm in midsummer.

## What type of shoes do you need?

In stores, hiking boots are often divided into categories from A to D. This classification is not standardized; some manufacturers use it, others not. It is still helpful as a rough guide because it describes how stiff the sole is and what terrain a shoe is intended for.

| Category | Type | sole | Suitable for |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | light hiking shoes, often low shoes | flexible | Walks, wide forest and field paths |
| A/B | lightweight trekking shoe | medium flexible | Low mountain ranges, long-distance paths such as Rennsteig or Westweg |
| b | Trekking boots | more torsionally rigid | Stony paths, hut tours with luggage, easy alpine paths |
| B/C | light mountain boots | stiff | challenging mountain paths, scree, snowfields |
| C and D | Mountain boots, mountaineering boots | very stiff, crampon-proof | Alpine tours and glaciers, not for normal hikes |

For most hikes in Germany, i.e. for low mountain ranges and long-distance hiking trails on forest and woodland paths, a shoe in category A/B or B is the right choice. If you mainly travel in the Alpine region, on red or black marked mountain paths with scree and rocky steps, you are more likely to choose B or B/C. Shoes in categories C and D are intended for mountaineering. On a normal hiking trail they are uncomfortably heavy and do not provide any additional safety.

## Low shoes or boots?

A high shaft protects the ankle from impacts and provides a little more support with a heavy backpack. But it doesn't reliably prevent you from twisting your ankle. This is mainly due to a stable, not too soft sole and a well-fitting heel area. Many experienced hikers therefore wear low shoes on well-developed trails and only use boots when the terrain is rough or they have heavy luggage.

Low shoes are lighter and more airy, which is particularly noticeable in summer and on long stages. A frequently cited empirical value is that every gram on your foot weighs significantly more than a gram in your backpack. For children, beginners with weak ankles or tours with overnight luggage, an ankle-high shoe may still be a better choice.

## With or without a membrane?

Many hiking boots have a waterproof, breathable membrane. It keeps water out from outside as long as it doesn't run into the shaft from above. This is pleasant in wet grass, rain and mud. The disadvantage: The membrane only allows moisture from the inside to escape to a limited extent. On hot summer days, feet sweat more and damp skin is more prone to blisters.

A simple rule of thumb: If you hike a lot in transitional periods, in autumn, in low mountain ranges with wet paths or in the mountains with snowfields, a membrane makes sense. For pure summer tours on dry paths, a shoe without a membrane is often more comfortable. If you get wet in a shoe without a membrane, it usually dries faster.

## This is how you really try things on

The most important part of buying shoes happens in the store. Take your time and plan on an hour rather than ten minutes.

1. **Walk in the afternoon.** Feet swell throughout the day, including when hiking. A shoe that fits perfectly in the morning can be tight in the evening.
2. **Bring your hiking socks.** Thin everyday socks will distort the result. If you wear insoles, take them with you too.
3. **Measure both feet.** Many people have different sized feet. The larger foot determines the size.
4. **Check the length.** With the laces open, push your foot all the way forward. There should be room for about one finger behind the heel. When you're laced up, you should be able to wiggle your toes at the front without hitting them.
5. **Check the heel.** Lace the shoe normally and walk a few steps. The heel should only lift minimally when rolling.
6. **Go downhill.** Good specialty stores have a small ramp. When walking downhill, your toes should not hit the front. This is exactly what causes black toenails after long descents.
7. **Compare several models.** Lasts vary greatly. A model that fits your girlfriend perfectly can be a problem for you.

## The right lacing

Many problems can be solved with lacing. When going downhill, it's worth having tight laces on the instep so that your foot doesn't slip forward. For climbs you can loosen the upper shaft area a little. Many boots have clamping hooks in the middle with which you can adjust different tension in the lower and upper part.

If the shoe presses on the instep, it often helps to leave out an eyelet at this point. If your heel slips, a so-called heel loop can help: You pass the shoelaces on the upper hook crosswise through the loop on the opposite side.

## Break in new shoes

Modern hiking boots are significantly less stiff than the leather boots of earlier decades. You still have to break them in because the material only adapts to your foot after a few hours. And you'll learn where the shoe might pinch before you're ten kilometers from the nearest road.

- First wear the shoes for a few days in everyday life, when shopping or on the way to work.
- Then go on two or three short hikes with ups and downs, preferably with a light backpack.
- Only then do you go on long tours. Before a long-distance hike, the shoes should have covered at least 30 to 50 kilometers.
- If you notice a pressure point, tape it off immediately, not just when there is a fire.

## Care and lifespan

After each tour, knock off any coarse soil and, if necessary, rinse the shoes with clean water and a soft brush. To dry, take out the insoles and stuff the shoes loosely with newspaper. Do not place them near the heater or in the blazing sun, as this can damage leather and adhesives. Leather shoes occasionally need care products, while synthetic shoes need more impregnation.

The lifespan of a hiking shoe depends less on age than on use. A clear warning signal is a worn sole: If the tread on the heel and ball of the foot is slippery, the shoe will dangerously lose grip on wet roots and stones. On high-quality boots, you can often have the sole replaced by a shoemaker.

## Socks: often underestimated

Good hiking socks are almost as important as the shoe itself. They should be made without thick seams on the toes, fit well and wick moisture away from the foot. Socks made from merino wool or blended fabrics have proven to be effective. Cotton socks soak up sweat, stay moist and promote blisters. On tours lasting several days, take at least one pair to change.

## Prevent blisters

Blisters occur when skin rubs repeatedly in the same area, especially when it is warm and moist. The best prevention is a well-fitting, broken-in shoe with matching socks. When you're on the move, it helps to pay attention to the first warning signs: A slight burning sensation or a reddened area is the right moment to stop and cover the area with a blister plaster or tape. If you wait until the bubble has formed, it will be too late. On long tours, change your socks when you get sweaty and let your feet air dry during longer breaks. Short-cut toenails prevent pressure when descending.

If a blister does develop, protect it with a blister plaster, which acts like a second skin, and leave it in place for several days. The skin over the blister is the best protection against infection and should not be peeled off.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I hike with trail running shoes?

On well-developed paths and with light luggage, yes, many long-distance hikers swear by it. The shoes are light, dry quickly and provide good cushioning. However, they often lack stability and protection on scree, with a heavy backpack or on wet, steep mountain paths. If you want to try them out, start with day tours.

### How much space does the foot need at the front?

The guideline is about a thumb's width, i.e. around one centimeter. It is crucial that your toes do not hit when walking downhill. However, a shoe that is too large is also problematic because the foot slips in it and creates friction.

### Should I buy shoes online?

If you already know your model and size, there's nothing wrong with it. When buying hiking boots for the first time, we recommend trying them on in a specialist store, ideally with advice and a test ramp. The differences between the bars are too large to be appreciated from a distance.

### When do I need a crampon-proof shoe?

Only if you are planning high tours on glaciers or in glaciated terrain. For hikes in low German mountain ranges and on normal mountain paths in the Bavarian Alps, such a shoe is unnecessarily heavy and stiff.

### Do I need special insoles?

Many people get by with the insole that is in the shoe. Anyone who has foot problems will benefit from anatomically shaped insoles from specialist retailers or from orthopedic insoles as prescribed by a doctor. Be sure to take them with you to try them on, because a thicker insole changes how much space your foot has in the shoe.
