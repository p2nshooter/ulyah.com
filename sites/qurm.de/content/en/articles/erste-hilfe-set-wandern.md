Most hiking injuries are small: a heel blister, a scraped knee, a splinter, a tick bite. But sometimes it is more serious, such as a twisted ankle far from the nearest path or a fall that leaves someone lying there and cooling down. A well-put together first aid kit only weighs a few hundred grams and makes the difference in both cases. Ready-made kits from stores are a good start, but they often contain things you don't need and are missing what really matters when hiking. Here you will find out what belongs in it, how you can complement it sensibly and how you can use the items in an emergency.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Blister plasters, tape and a small pair of scissors belong in every kit, because blisters are the most common hiking injury.
> - For wounds, you will need disposable gloves, sterile compresses, a gauze bandage, an elastic bandage and plasters of various sizes.
> - A rescue blanket protects you from getting cold and weighs almost nothing. It belongs on every tour.
> - Personal medications and known allergies are more important than any standard kit. If necessary, talk to your doctor or pharmacy.
> - A first aid course is the best supplement. Material only helps if you know how to use it.

## Basic equipment for day trips

| area | Content | For what |
|---|---|---|
| Feet | 2 to 4 blister plasters (hydrocolloid), 1 roll of tape, small scissors | Prevent and treat blisters |
| Wounds | Disposable gloves, 2 to 4 sterile compresses, 1 gauze bandage, plaster strips in different sizes, wound disinfection | Abrasions, cuts, bleeding |
| Injuries | 1 elastic bandage, 1 triangular scarf | Support sprains, immobilize the arm, apply a pressure bandage |
| Warmth | 1 rescue blanket | Protection against cooling of injured people |
| Small items | Tweezers or tick card, safety pins | Fix splinters, ticks, bandages |
| Emergency | Whistle, emergency card with contacts and previous illnesses | Call for help, inform emergency services |

Pack everything in a waterproof, easily recognizable bag, preferably red or orange. Always put them in the same place in your backpack so that you and your companions can find them quickly.

## Supplements for multi-day tours and mountain tours

- **More blister plasters and tape:** On long tours, pressure points often only appear on the third or fourth day.
- **Additional compresses and a second gauze bandage.**
- **Splint Material:** A lightweight, malleable aluminum splint stabilizes an injured ankle or forearm. If necessary, a sleeping mat, sticks and a triangular towel can help.
- **Cooling:** An instant cold pack weighs little and relieves swelling after twisting your ankle.
- **Personal medication** in sufficient quantity plus reserve.
- **Hygiene:** small bottle of hand disinfectant and tissues.
- **Headlamp:** This is actually part of the basic equipment, but is indispensable in an emergency if the rescue lasts until dusk.

## Medication: personal instead of flat rate

Which medication you take with you depends on you and your companions. We cannot give general recommendations on dosage here. Clarify with your doctor or the pharmacy what makes sense for you, especially if you have previous illnesses, allergies or if you regularly take medication.

A few principles apply to everyone:

- Always take prescribed long-term medication with you, even on day trips, and pack a reserve for multi-day trips.
- Anyone who has a severe allergy, such as to insect bites, always carries the prescribed emergency kit with them, and those accompanying them know where it is and how to use it.
- Check the expiration date every year before the season.
- Write down important medications, allergies and previous illnesses on the emergency card. Emergency responders need this information.

## Prevent and treat blisters

Blisters are caused by friction, moisture and heat. The best care is to prevent them from occurring in the first place:

1. **Stop at the first pressure point.** A “hotspot,” a red, burning area, is the precursor to a blister. Now stick tape or a blister plaster on it. A five-minute break saves days of pain.
2. **Keep socks and feet dry.** Change sweaty socks during your lunch break.
3. **If the blister is already there:** Apply a generous amount of hydrocolloid blister plaster over it and leave it on until it comes off on its own. It cushions and creates a moist wound environment in which the skin heals faster.
4. You should only **puncture** a blister if it is very large and bulging and makes it impossible to move on. Then disinfect, open the edge with a sterile needle, leave the skin as protection and cover. If there is redness, warmth, pus or fever in the days afterwards, the wound should be seen by a doctor.

## Treat wounds

For small abrasions, clean the wound with clean drinking water, remove any coarse dirt, disinfect it and cover it with a plaster or compress. If you are caring for someone else, put on disposable gloves beforehand.

Pressure helps with heavier bleeding: put a compress on the wound and press firmly. If the bleeding doesn't stop, apply a pressure bandage: a compress on the wound, an unopened bandage packet or a rolled-up bandage as a pressure pad over it, and wrap everything tightly with the gauze bandage. Elevate the injured area as high as possible and call emergency services as early as possible if bleeding is severe.

## Twisted: the first few minutes

A twisted ankle is the typical hiking injury. The first few minutes determine how severe the swelling becomes:

- **Pause:** Sit down, don't put any more pressure on your foot.
- **Cool:** with an ice pack, cold stream water or a wet cloth, but never apply ice directly to the skin.
- **Compression:** Wrap an elastic bandage moderately tightly around the joint, from the toes towards the lower leg.
- **Elevate:** place your foot on the backpack.

You then decide whether a careful descent is possible. If the foot can hardly be put under pressure, if it hurts a lot or is significantly deformed, don't rely on perseverance, but get help. How to make an emergency call correctly can be found in [Emergency on the Mountain](/ratgeber/notfall-am-berg/).

## Use the emergency blanket correctly

A rescue blanket is a wafer-thin, metallic-coated film. It protects injured people from wind, moisture and heat loss. It is not important which side is on the outside, but rather that the blanket surrounds the body as completely as possible.

1. If possible, place insulation under the person: sleeping pad, backpack, jacket. A lot of heat is lost through the ground.
2. Wrap the blanket around your body, keeping your head free but covering your neck and neck.
3. Put a jacket or sleeping bag over it to prevent the foil from flapping away.
4. Stay with the person and talk to them.

The ceiling is loud and tears easily. Practice unpacking at home so that you don't have to struggle with the packaging in an emergency.

## The alpine distress signal

If there is no mobile phone network, you can use the Alpine emergency signal to draw attention to yourself: give a signal six times a minute, i.e. every ten seconds, by shouting, whistling or flashing the lamp, then pause for a minute and repeat. The answer is: one character three times per minute. A whistle carries further than the voice and protects it.

## Check before every season

Take the set completely apart once a year. Replace used materials, check expiry dates of compresses, disinfectants and medications and replace torn emergency blankets. Sterile packaging that has become wet or damaged is no longer considered sterile.

## Sun and insect protection are included

Many problems when hiking are not caused by accidents, but by the sun and insects. UV radiation is stronger in the mountains than in the valley, and sunburn occurs quickly, even on cloudy days and in spring when snow additionally reflects light. Sunscreen with a high sun protection factor, a lip balm with UV protection and sunglasses belong in every set or right next to it.

An insect repellent that you apply to your skin and clothing before the tour helps against mosquitoes, horseflies and ticks. A tick card or tick tweezers and a cooling gel against insect bites complete the set. You can find out more about ticks in the guide [Ticks, TBE and Lyme disease] (/ratgeber/zecken-fsme-borreliose/).

## Know emergency numbers

In an emergency, every minute counts. The emergency number 112 is valid throughout Europe and connects you with the rescue control center. In Austria there is also the Alpine emergency number 140, in Switzerland you can alert the Rega air rescue service on number 1414. Save these numbers in your phone before a tour abroad.

When calling 911, please state where you are, what happened, how many people are affected and what injuries there are. Stay reachable after the call, as the control center often responds with questions. A photo of the area or the coordinates from a map app will help the rescuers find you.

## Refresh first aid

The best first aid kit is of little use if no one knows how to use it. For many people, a first aid course was years ago. Aid organizations regularly offer courses, and the Alpine clubs have special courses for first aid in the field, in which topics such as hypothermia, rescue blankets and evacuation are also practiced. A refresher course every few years provides security, especially if you are traveling with children or a group.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the first aid kit from the car enough?

It contains a lot of what you need, but is too heavy and bulky. Put together a light hiking kit and leave the car first aid kit in the car where it is required.

### How heavy is a good hiking set?

For day tours around 200 to 300 grams, for multi-day tours 400 to 500 grams. That's less than half a drinking bottle.

### What do I do against ticks?

Tweezers or tick cards are part of the set. [Ticks while Hiking](/ratgeber/zecken-fsme-borreliose/) explains how to remove ticks correctly and what you should pay attention to afterwards.

### Do I need a first aid course?

Yes, absolutely. Many aid organizations offer courses, some specifically for outdoor situations. Anyone who has ever practiced applying a pressure bandage will act more calmly and correctly in an emergency.

### Are medications for children included in the set?

Yes, if there are children. Pack child-friendly fever medicines and painkillers in the appropriate dosage, children's plasters and a mild disinfectant and, if in doubt, discuss your selection with the pediatrician's office or pharmacy.

### What is the best way to store the set?

In a waterproof, high-visibility bag located on the top or in an external compartment of the backpack so you can find it quickly. Check the shelf life of medications and ointments before each season and replace used items immediately after the tour.
