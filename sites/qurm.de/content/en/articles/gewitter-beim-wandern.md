A summer thunderstorm is one of the greatest dangers for hikers, and not just in the Alps. A thunderstorm can also be life-threatening in low mountain ranges, on open plateaus, at observation towers or on rocky plateaus such as in Saxon Switzerland. The good news: Most dangerous situations can be avoided with planning, and if it does happen, there are clear rules of conduct.

This guide explains how thunderstorms form, how you can recognize them, how to plan your tour so that you don't get caught in them, and what to do if there is lightning anyway.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Thermal thunderstorms usually occur in the afternoon. Starting early and returning early is the best protection.
> - Warning signs include rapidly growing cumulus clouds, a dark, flat cloud anvil, increasing wind and distant rumbling thunder.
> - If you hear thunder, you are already within range of lightning.
> - Avoid peaks, ridges, isolated trees, metal cables and open areas.
> - In an emergency: put your feet together, squat down, tuck your head in, do not lie flat.

## How summer thunderstorms form

In Germany there are roughly two types. **Heat thunderstorms** occur on hot, humid days when the sun heats the ground, warm, moist air rises and builds up into powerful cumulus clouds. These thunderstorms typically form during the afternoon and early evening, often earlier over mountains than in the lowlands because the slopes warm up more quickly and the air there rises more easily.

**Frontal thunderstorms** occur when a cold front pushes warm air in front of it and quickly lifts it. They can occur at any time of day, including at night or in the morning, often move in a line and often bring squalls, heavy rain and hail. They are usually well predicted, but often come faster than expected.

## Plan the tour correctly

The best protection against thunderstorms is planning that takes them into account. Check the weather report the evening before and the morning of the tour. Special mountain weather reports are helpful for the Alps, for example from the German Weather Service or the Alpine clubs. Pay attention not only to the symbol, but also to the probability of a thunderstorm and the specified time.

- **Start early:** On days with a chance of thunderstorms, start the tour at sunrise or shortly after. You should have summits and exposed sections behind you by late morning.
- **Know alternative options:** Before the tour, mark huts, descents and sheltered places that you can reach quickly.
- **Choose shorter options:** If the weather is uncertain, a valley hike or a forest path is a better choice than a long ridge path.
- **Determine turnaround time:** Decide in advance by when you have to be at the summit at the latest. When time is up, you turn back, no matter how close the goal is.

## Warning signs on the way

The weather usually predicts a thunderstorm if you look closely. Typical characters are:

| Observation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cumulative clouds grow rapidly in the morning | The air is unstable; Thunderstorms likely in the afternoon |
| Towers of clouds with sharp, cauliflower-like edges | The cloud is still growing; it can develop into a thunderstorm |
| A flat, fibrous umbrella spreads out at the top | The “anvil” of a storm cloud; Thunderstorms are here or imminent |
| Humid, oppressive air, suddenly a cool gust of wind | Gust front of a nearby thunderstorm cell |
| Distant rumbling, lightning | thunderstorm nearby; Lightning strikes are already possible |
| Crackling, buzzing on metal parts, hair standing up | Acute danger; Immediately look for a lower, protected area |

## How far away is the thunderstorm?

Light is there almost immediately, sound takes about three seconds to travel one kilometer. So count the seconds between lightning and thunder and divide them by three to get the approximate distance in kilometers. Ten seconds means about three kilometers.

That sounds reassuring, but it's not. Lightning can strike several kilometers in front of a thunderstorm cell, seemingly out of the blue. The rule of thumb is: **As soon as you hear thunder, you are within range.** Many experts also recommend waiting about half an hour after the last thunder before entering exposed areas again.

## Where you shouldn't be

Lightning prefers to strike elevated points and spread across the ground and conductive structures. Therefore, if there is a risk of thunderstorms, avoid:

- Peaks, ridges, knolls and observation towers
- isolated trees, masts and summit crosses
- Wire ropes, ladders and via ferratas
- open meadows and plateaus where you are the highest point
- Watercourses, wet gullies and cave entrances where the stream can spread
- the edge of the forest directly under tall trees

A closed building with lightning protection or a car with a metal body provides good protection. An open shelter without a lightning rod is better than a ridge, but it is not a safe place. Stay there in the middle and don't touch the walls.

## What to do if there is lightning?

If you can no longer get down or reach a building in time, these rules apply:

1. **Leave exposed areas.** Descend from the ridge or summit, even if it's only a few dozen meters in elevation. Every meter away from the highest point helps.
2. **Stay away from metal.** Put down hiking poles and stay away from ropes and ladders.
3. **Squat down.** Place your feet close together, squat down, hug your knees and tuck your head. In this way, you offer the smallest possible area and reduce the step voltage, i.e. the current that can flow through your body across the floor.
4. **Do not lie down.** Lying flat on the floor increases the contact area and therefore the danger.
5. **Isolate.** If possible, sit on the backpack or a sleeping pad as long as there are no metal parts sticking out.
6. **Spread the group.** Stay a few meters away from each other so that not everyone can be hit at the same time, but stay within sight.

## First aid for lightning accidents

People who have been struck by lightning carry no electrical charge and can be touched immediately. Dial **112** and begin cardiopulmonary resuscitation immediately if you are unconscious and not breathing normally. Lightning accidents often cause cardiac arrhythmias, and early resuscitation can save lives. Even anyone who appears to be uninjured after a lightning accident should be examined by a doctor because consequences may only appear later.

## After the thunderstorm

Thunderstorms often bring heavy rain. Paths can quickly become streams, stones become slippery, small rivulets swell. As you continue, check to see if streams are safe to cross and avoid steep, muddy slopes. In gorges and narrow valleys, the water can still rise even after the actual rain. If a path no longer seems safe, choose the alternative or wait.

## Thunderstorms on multi-day tours

On a long-distance hike or hut tour, you can't always avoid thunderstorms by simply staying at home. This makes it all the more important to keep the daily stages flexible. On days with a thunderstorm warning, start particularly early and plan the exposed sections for the morning. If the weather looks bad, a day of rest in the accommodation or hut is not a waste of time, but a wise decision. Hut owners often know the local weather better than any app. In the evening, ask them how they feel about the next day.

A rain radar on your cell phone helps you track the direction of thunderstorm cells while you are out and about. But don't rely on that alone: ​​reception is often weak in the mountains, and thunderstorms can form directly above you within a short period of time without first appearing on the radar.

## Safe places: huts, buildings and vehicles

Buildings with lightning protection systems and closed vehicles with metal bodies offer the best protection against lightning because the electricity is conducted through the outer shell. A managed hut is therefore a good refuge when a thunderstorm approaches, as is a car in the parking lot. However, open shelters, small wooden huts without lightning protection and tents do not offer reliable protection.

If there is a safe place within reach, get there well before the storm arrives. Stay there until the storm has completely passed.

## Use weather apps and lightning maps

Modern weather apps show rain radar and lightning strikes almost in real time. On a lightning map you can see where lightning is currently occurring and in which direction the thunderstorm cells are moving. The German Weather Service warning app and similar apps can also warn you about severe weather in your region via push notification.

But don't rely solely on your smartphone. Reception is patchy in the mountains, and thunderstorms can form faster than any app shows. So always pay attention to the sky and the warning signs along the way.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does the forest protect me from lightning?

A dense forest with many trees of the same height is much better than an open area or a ridge. But keep your distance from individual, particularly tall trees and trunks. You are in comparatively good hands in the middle of the crop, preferably in a depression.

### Can I use my cell phone?

Yes. A cell phone doesn't attract lightning. You can use it to call the emergency number, check the rain radar or determine your position.

### Are thunderstorms less dangerous in the low mountain ranges?

Not necessarily. On open ridge paths, high moors and viewpoints you are just as exposed as in the Alps. The difference is that you can usually descend or reach a forest faster. Take advantage of this and don't wait too long.

### How long should I wait after the last thunder?

A tried and tested rule of thumb is: at least 30 minutes after the last thunder. Even when the rain subsides and it becomes brighter, lightning can still strike at the edge of a thunderstorm.

### Do hiking poles attract lightning?

Metal objects do not attract lightning to any significant extent. What matters is how exposed you are. However, do not hold sticks up high and, if a thunderstorm is approaching, place them a few meters away so that you can protect yourself by keeping yourself as low as possible.
