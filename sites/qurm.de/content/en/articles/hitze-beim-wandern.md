Hot summers have become more common in Germany. Days with more than 30 degrees, where the air is already cold in the morning, are now part of every summer. For hikers this means: A tour that was pleasant in May can become a serious strain on a south-facing slope in July. The body cools itself primarily through sweating. If it generates more heat than it can get rid of, body temperature rises and performance drops. In the worst case, heat stroke occurs, a life-threatening emergency. With clever planning, you can also go on beautiful tours in midsummer.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Start early in the morning and plan lunchtime in the shade or in the hut.
> - Choose shady forest paths, gorges, paths along streams or tours at higher altitudes. Avoid steep, open south-facing slopes.
> - Drink regularly and supplement with salt, for example with salty snacks or a spritzer.
> - Warning signs of heat exhaustion: weakness, headache, nausea, dizziness, profuse sweating. Pause immediately, cool and drink.
> - Confusion, hot skin, loss of consciousness with a high body temperature indicate heat stroke: call 112, cool down.

## Why heat is so stressful when hiking

When walking uphill, the muscles generate a lot of heat. The body releases them through the skin by dilating blood vessels and sweating. Evaporating sweat cools you down. This works worse at high temperatures, high humidity and no wind. At the same time, the body loses water and salt, blood volume drops, and the heart has to work harder. A heavy backpack, dark clothing and direct sun increase the effect.

Small children, older people, people with cardiovascular diseases or certain medications, overweight people and anyone who is not yet used to the heat are particularly sensitive. Anyone who has previous illnesses should check with a doctor before a summer tour to determine how much exercise makes sense in the heat.

## Choose the right tour

| Good in heat | Better to avoid |
|---|---|
| Forest paths with a dense canopy of leaves | open meadow slopes and vineyards in the midday sun |
| Gorges, gorges, stream valleys | steep southern slopes with many meters of elevation |
| High-altitude trails over 1,500 meters | Tours without water points and refreshments |
| Circular paths with bathing areas | long distances on asphalt or gravel |
| North slopes and waterfront paths | Tours where you are on the climb at midday |

It is cooler at altitude: for every 100 meters of altitude, the temperature drops on average by around 0.6 to 1 degree. The UV radiation is more intense. Gorges stay cool, but are often full during the summer holidays, as described in [gorges in Bavaria](/ratgeber/klammen-in-bayern/). Many forest areas such as the [Palatinate Forest](/ratgeber/pfaelzerwald-wandern/) or the [Bavarian Forest](/ratgeber/bayerischer-wald-gipfel/) offer shady paths.

## The right time

- **Start early:** It is coolest in the first few hours of the morning. If you start at seven o'clock, you will have completed the climb before the midday heat comes.
- **Lunch break in the shade:** Plan a long break at a cabin, in the forest or by a stream between 12 p.m. and 3 p.m.
- **Evening:** Short evening tours are nice, but the days become short more quickly in late summer. Take a headlamp with you.
- **Thunderstorms:** Hot days often end with thermal thunderstorms. How you can recognize them early is written in [Thunderstorm while hiking](/ratgeber/gewitter-beim-wandern/).

## Drink and eat

In hot weather, you can easily lose half a liter of sweat or more per hour. So drink regularly, even before you get thirsty, and take enough with you. Replenish salt with salty snacks, broth, or an electrolyte drink. Eat lightly and in small portions, as a large, fatty meal puts additional strain on the circulatory system. You can find detailed quantities in [Provisions and drinks when hiking](/ratgeber/proviant-und-trinken/).

## Clothing and sun protection

- **Bright, loose, airy clothing** made of functional material or thin merino wool. Cotton cools through moisture, but becomes heavy and chafes.
- **Headwear:** A wide-brimmed hat or cap with a neck guard prevents the sun from shining directly on the head and neck.
- **Sunscreen** with a high sun protection factor, reapply every two hours and after heavy sweating. Don't forget your ears, neck, nose and back of your hands.
- **Sunglasses** with UV protection, especially at height.
- **A wet cloth** around the neck or a hat dipped in stream water cools through evaporation.

## Recognize warning signs

### Heat exhaustion

Heat exhaustion occurs due to loss of water and salt. Typical are:

- severe fatigue, weakness, dizziness
- Headache, nausea, sometimes vomiting
- heavy sweating, pale, clammy skin
- rapid pulse, muscle cramps

**What to do:** Stop immediately and get into the shade. Lie down, elevate your legs. Loosen tight clothing, moisten skin with water and fan. Drink cool, not ice-cold drinks in small sips, ideally with a little salt or sugar. Only move on when things are significantly better and choose the shortest route back. If the symptoms do not improve within a short time, call for help.

### Heat stroke

In heat stroke, the body's temperature regulation fails. The body temperature rises sharply. This is an emergency.

- Confusion, drowsiness, unusual behavior, seizures, or loss of consciousness
- hot, often red skin, sometimes dry, sometimes still moist
- rapid pulse, rapid breathing

**What to do:** Make an emergency call to 112. Put the person in the shade, open clothing and cool as quickly as possible: pour water over them, place wet cloths on the neck, armpits and groin, fan. If unconscious with normal breathing, place in the recovery position and monitor breathing. Do not give anything to drink to an unconscious person. [Emergency on the Mountain](/ratgeber/notfall-am-berg/) describes how to properly make an emergency call on the mountain.

## Children and dogs in the heat

Children overheat more quickly than adults and often notice thirst too late. Plan shorter tours with lots of breaks in the shade, water games on the stream and regular drinking. Babies in carriers absorb the heat of the body they are being carried on and are particularly at risk. More about this in [Hiking with Baby](/ratgeber/wandern-mit-baby/).

Dogs can only give off heat by panting and using their paws. Hot asphalt and rock slabs burn their paws. Plan tours that include streams, take water with you for the dog, and avoid long hikes on very hot days. Tips for this can be found in [Hiking with a dog](/ratgeber/wandern-mit-hund/).

## The night before a summer tour

Good preparation starts the night before. Drink enough throughout the day, avoid a lot of alcohol and get enough sleep. Have clothing, sun protection and filled bottles ready so that you can start early without being rushed. Freeze half of a bottle of water and fill it up in the morning: This way you'll have cool water until lunchtime. Check the heat warnings from the German Weather Service for your region and adjust the tour if necessary.

## Fill up with water along the way

On hot days, there is often not enough water in the backpack for the entire tour. Therefore, plan in advance where you can refill: huts, inns, village wells and cemeteries. Not every well provides drinking water. Look for “No Drinking Water” signs and don’t rely on stream water to be clean, especially below pastures and towns.

A small water filter is a good reserve for long tours. It's better to carry a little more water than too little, and drink regularly in small sips rather than waiting until you're thirsty.

## Heat in the mountains and lowlands

With every meter of altitude it gets a little cooler, on average by around half a degree to just under one degree per hundred meters. A tour at a higher altitude can therefore be more pleasant on hot days than a walk in the valley. At the same time, solar radiation is stronger at higher altitudes, and it still gets very hot on shadeless ridges and south-facing slopes.

In the lowlands and low mountain ranges, dense forests, stream valleys and ravines offer the best protection. Plan such tours consciously on hot days and avoid open fields, vineyards and heathland at lunchtime.

## Salt and electrolytes

When you sweat, the body not only loses water, but also salts. Anyone who hikes for long hours on hot days and drinks large amounts of pure water should also supplement salt, for example with salty snacks such as pretzels, nuts or cheese, or with electrolyte tablets from the pharmacy. This prevents muscle cramps and exhaustion.

## Heat and thunderstorms

Hot summer days are typical thunderstorm days. The warmth causes moist air to rise and storm clouds often build up in the afternoon. So start early, plan the tour so that you are back in the early afternoon and keep an eye on the sky. More about this in the guide [Thunderstorms while hiking](/ratgeber/gewitter-beim-wandern/).

## Frequently asked questions

### At what temperature should I avoid a tour?

There is no fixed limit. The decisive factors are humidity, sun, altitude and your habituation. If there are heat warnings from the German Weather Service, only plan short, shady tours in the early morning or postpone the hike.

### Does it help to cool down in the stream?

Yes, hands, arms, neck and face cool noticeably in cold water. However, jumping into very cold water when you are extremely overheated can put a strain on your circulation. Cool down gradually.

### Does the body get used to heat?

Yes. After about one to two weeks of regular exercise in the warmth, the body sweats earlier and more effectively. Therefore, increase the tours slowly during a hot period.

### Should I rather hike in the forest on hot days?

Yes, forests, stream valleys and ravines are significantly cooler on hot days than open landscapes. A tour through a gorge or along a stream is a good choice. You can find ideas for this in the guide [Klammen in Bayern](/ratgeber/klammen-in-bayern/).

### Do medications affect heat tolerance?

Some medications, such as certain high blood pressure medications or diuretic medications, can alter heat tolerance. Anyone who regularly takes medication and is planning longer tours in the summer should discuss this with their family doctor beforehand.

### What is the best way to recover after a hot tour?

Continue drinking in the hours after your tour, even if you no longer feel thirsty, and eat something salty to replenish lost minerals. A lukewarm shower cools down more pleasantly than ice-cold water, and legs elevated in the shade relieve the strain on the circulation. Watch out for warning signs that may continue later, such as headaches, nausea, or very dark urine. If they do not go away or get worse, seek medical advice.
