Ticks are a companion on almost every hike through forests and meadows in Germany. Most stings have no consequences. However, some ticks transmit pathogens, especially Borrelia, which can cause Lyme borreliosis and, in certain regions, the tick-borne encephalitis virus, or TBE for short. If you know how ticks live and how to protect yourself, you can significantly reduce the risk without spoiling your enjoyment of being outside.

This guide explains where ticks occur, which protective measures really help, how to properly remove a tick and what signs you should look out for in the days and weeks afterwards. It does not replace medical advice, especially not on the issue of vaccination.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Ticks sit in grasses, ferns and bushes, usually close to the ground up to about knee height, and are brushed off. They don't fall from trees.
> - Long, light-colored clothing, tucking pant legs into socks and anti-tick products on the skin reduce the risk.
> - After each tour, search yourself thoroughly, especially the backs of the knees, groin, armpits, hairline and behind the ears.
> - Remove ticks as early as possible, slowly and straight, with tweezers or a tick card. No oil, no glue.
> - There is a vaccination against TBE, but not against Lyme disease in humans. Look for spreading redness after a sting.

## Where and when ticks are active

The most common woodbuck in Germany prefers to live in moist, overgrown areas: on the edges of forests, in clearings, in tall grass, in ferns and in the undergrowth, but also in parks and gardens. Ticks climb onto stalks and leaves and wait there until a person or animal passes by. They usually stay close to the ground. The popular idea that they fall from trees is not true.

Ticks become active even at mild temperatures. In many years the season begins in early spring and extends into late fall; In mild winters they can also be out and about in between. Stings typically occur most between spring and fall.

## TBE and Lyme disease in comparison

| | TBE | Lyme disease |
|---|---|---|
| pathogen | virus | Bacteria (Borrelia) |
| Occurrence in Germany | especially in designated risk areas, predominantly in the south and east | across the country |
| Transmission | possible shortly after the bite | The risk increases with the duration of suction |
| Vaccination | yes, recommended for risk areas | not available to humans |
| Treatment | no causal therapy; Treatment of the complaints | Easily treatable with antibiotics, the earlier the better |

The Robert Koch Institute publishes a current map of TBE risk areas every year. A large part of it is in Bavaria and Baden-Württemberg, there are other areas in Hesse, Thuringia and Saxony, among others, and occasionally in other federal states. The list is regularly expanded. If you often hike in these regions or plan a longer stay, talk to your family doctor about vaccination. The Standing Vaccination Commission recommends it for people who are exposed to ticks in risk areas.

## Prevention: clothing and behavior

The simplest protection is to give ticks as little opportunity as possible to get to your skin:

- **Long pants and closed shoes:** After getting off, ticks usually crawl upwards until they find a suitable spot. Tuck the pant legs into the socks; It doesn't look elegant, but it's effective.
- **Light-colored clothing:** You can see the small, dark ticks much more easily on light-colored fabrics.
- **Stay on the path:** Walking through tall grass, ferns or bushes significantly increases the risk. Wide paths in the middle are safer than narrow, overgrown paths.
- **Choose a break spot:** Sit on a bench, a stone or a seat mat, not directly in the tall grass.

## Anti-tick remedies

Repellents on the skin can keep ticks away for a few hours. Proven active ingredients include icaridin and DEET. The effect is temporary and wears off with sweat, so you have to renew it on long tours. Read the package leaflet, especially for children, because not every medication is approved for every age.

There are also clothing treated with an insecticide or sprays to treat clothing. They can be an additional help, especially for people who travel in the forest for work or very frequently. No remedy offers complete protection; searching for the tour remains important.

## After the tour: search thoroughly

Ticks like to look for warm, thin-skinned areas. Therefore, after every hike in the forest and meadow, search your body systematically, preferably in good light and with the help of a second person for your back:

1. Back of the knees and lower legs
2. Groin and intimate area
3. Belly button and waistband
4. Armpits and armpits
5. Neck, nape, hairline and behind the ears
6. In the case of children, the entire head as well, because ticks are more likely to sit there

Young ticks, so-called nymphs, are tiny, barely larger than a poppy seed. Look for small dark spots that cannot be wiped away. Showering after the tour will help rinse off any ticks that haven't yet become attached, and shake out or wash your clothes.

## How to properly remove a tick

If you find an attached tick, remove it as quickly as possible. The shorter you vacuum, the lower the risk of Borrelia being transmitted.

1. **Tool:** a pair of fine, pointed tweezers, a tick card or tick tweezers. If necessary, you can also use your fingernails.
2. **Grab close to the skin:** Grab the tick as directly as possible by the head, very close to the skin, not by the soaked abdomen.
3. **Pull slowly and straight:** Pull the tick straight out with an even pull. Gently moving back and forth can help. You avoid jerky tearing and strong squeezing.
4. **No oil, no glue, no alcohol beforehand:** Such home remedies can irritate the tick and are not recommended.
5. **Disinfect:** Disinfect the area afterwards.
6. **Note:** Write down the date and body location or take a photo with the date. This will help later if problems arise.

If a small residue remains in the skin, it is usually harmless. It is usually rejected by the body. Observe the area and seek medical attention if it becomes infected.

## What you should pay attention to afterwards

A little redness immediately after the bite is normal and usually disappears after a few days. You should pay attention if, days to weeks later, a **ring-shaped or flat redness that slowly spreads** appears around the injection site or elsewhere. This so-called wandering redness is a typical sign of early Lyme disease and should be treated medically. However, it does not occur in all infections.

Flu-like symptoms such as fever, headache and body aches after a tick bite can indicate TBE or another infection, especially if you were traveling in a risk area. Even then you go to the doctor and mention the tick bite.

## Ticks on dogs

If your dog accompanies you on hikes, he often brings large numbers of ticks with him, which can then be passed on to people. Talk to your veterinary practice about suitable tick treatments and check your dog after every tour, especially on the head, ears, neck, armpits and between the toes.

## More tick species on the rise

In addition to the common wood tick, other tick species are spreading in Germany. The **alluvial forest tick** is larger and has a striking pattern. It occurs in many regions, is active even in winter on mild days and is particularly important for dogs because it can transmit pathogens of a dangerous dog disease. In recent years, ticks of the genus **Hyalomma** have also been found sporadically, which actually come from warmer regions and are introduced by migratory birds. They stand out because of their size and their striped legs. Anyone who finds such a tick can photograph it and report it to research institutes that observe the occurrence.

## Ticks while traveling

TBE is not only an issue in southern Germany. There are also risk areas in Austria, Switzerland, the Czech Republic, the Baltic states, parts of Scandinavia and many countries in Eastern Europe. If you are planning a hiking trip to these regions, find out about the situation in good time and talk to your doctor about whether a vaccination makes sense for you. Multiple vaccinations over a few months are necessary for complete protection, so it's worth thinking about this early on.

## Common misconceptions

- **“Ticks jump on people.”** False. They wait for plants and are stripped off as they pass by.
- **“You have to unscrew a tick.”** Ticks have no threads. Whether you twist slightly or pull straight is less important than a smooth, even pull without squeezing the body.
- **“Ticks only exist in mid-summer.”** They are active in mild temperatures in spring and often until late autumn.
- **“There are no ticks in the city park.”** Parks, gardens and green areas are also habitats for ticks.
- **“With the vaccination I am completely protected.”** The vaccination only protects against TBE, not against Lyme disease. Searching remains important.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to go to the doctor after every tick bite?

No. If you have completely removed the tick and the area remains inconspicuous in the following weeks, you do not need to see a doctor. However, if redness spreads or there are signs of illness, you should not hesitate.

### Should I send a tick in and have it examined?

This is usually not recommended. A detection of pathogens in the tick does not mean that you have become infected, and a negative result does not definitely rule out infection. The complaints are crucial.

### How long does the TBE vaccination protect?

For complete protection, several vaccinations are necessary, followed by boosters at intervals that depend on age and vaccine. Get advice from your doctor's office.

### Can ticks survive in the apartment?

The common woodbuck needs high humidity. It usually does not survive long in dry apartment air and cannot reproduce there. You should still dispose of a tick that you find on the carpet in the evening. The situation is different with the brown dog tick, which is mainly brought in with dogs from southern countries and can permanently settle in apartments.
