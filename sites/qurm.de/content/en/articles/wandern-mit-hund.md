For many dogs, there is nothing better than a long day outside: new smells, streams to jump in, roots and stones under their paws and their human with them all day. Hiking with a dog can be a wonderful shared experience. In order for it to stay that way, it takes a little planning, because a dog has different needs and limits than a human, and along the way you will meet other hikers, grazing animals and wild animals that you have to be considerate of.

This guide explains how to choose a suitable tour, what to take with you, what rules apply and what to pay attention to on the way. If you have any questions about your dog's health, such as whether he is fit enough for longer tours, it is best to ask your veterinary practice.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Adjust length and difficulty to suit your dog: age, breed, weight, health and training play a role.
> - Leash requirements vary by state, municipality and protected area. Stricter rules apply in many places during the breeding and breeding season in spring and early summer.
> - Take enough water and a bowl with you. Dogs cannot sweat and overheat quickly.
> - With cows in the pasture: keep the dog on a short leash, keep your distance and walk past calmly. If cows attack, let go of the leash.
> - Poop bags are part of this, even in the forest.

## Is my dog ​​a hiking dog?

Most healthy adult dogs can learn to walk. How far and how steep depends largely on the individual animal. A few factors to consider:

- **Age:** Growing puppies and young dogs should not go on long tours because their joints are not yet fully developed. When a dog reaches maturity depends on its breed and size. Older dogs also need shorter, more comfortable routes.
- **Physical structure:** Short-nosed breeds develop breathing problems more quickly when exposed to exertion and heat. Very small dogs tire more quickly on long journeys, while very heavy dogs put more strain on their joints, especially downhill.
- **Coat:** Dogs with thick, dark fur overheat more easily in the summer.
- **Training:** A dog that usually only goes for short laps in the park will be overwhelmed by 20 kilometers, even if he is healthy.

Increase the tours slowly, just like you did for yourself. Start with two to three hours and see how your dog reacts in the evening and the next day. If he is very tired, lame or doesn't want to walk anymore, it was too much.

## The right tour

Paths with shade and water are particularly suitable for tours with dogs. A stream or lake on the way is a welcome place for the dog to cool down and drink. However, the following are unsuitable:

- long sections on hot asphalt or light-colored rock in the sun
- Via ferratas, ladders and iron steps
- exposed ridges and paths with a risk of falling
- sharp-edged debris that can injure paws
- Paths that lead through protected areas where dogs are prohibited

Check the tour description for ladders and climbing spots in advance. Some paths in low mountain ranges, such as in rocky areas, have individual steps or ladders that you cannot manage with a dog or only with a stretcher.

## Leash requirement and rules

There is no uniform nationwide rule. Leash requirements are determined by the federal states, municipalities and protected area regulations. However, a few principles apply in many areas:

- **Breeding and setting season:** In many federal states, there is a leash requirement in forests and open countryside or an urgent recommendation to do so in spring and early summer, often around April to mid-July. During this time, wild animals are raising their young and a prowling dog can cause great damage.
- **National parks and nature reserves:** Leashes are almost always required here, and some areas are completely closed to dogs.
- **Towns and Parks:** Local bylaws often require leashes.
- **Grazing:** When grazing with livestock, you generally keep your dog on a leash.

Even where there is no obligation, the following applies: your dog must be available at any time. He is not allowed to chase wild animals, jump on other hikers or run into other people's gardens. If you are not sure about the recall, keep him on the leash. A tow line of a few meters gives him space without you losing control.

## What belongs in the backpack

| For the dog | Why |
|---|---|
| Water and folding bowl | Dogs need significantly more water when out and about than in everyday life |
| Food or snacks | an extra portion of energy for longer tours |
| Leash and harness | A well-fitting harness is more comfortable than a collar for longer tours |
| Poop bags | also in the forest, especially on paths and meadows |
| Tick tweezers | Check for ticks after every tour |
| First aid supplies | Bandage, paw protection or dog shoes, disinfection |
| towel | for wet dogs after the stream or in front of the car |

On long tours, a dog backpack can be useful in which the dog carries some of its own provisions. It should fit well and not be too heavy. Ask at the vet's office or a specialist consultation how much weight is acceptable for your dog.

## Heat and water

Dogs regulate their body temperature primarily by panting. They cannot sweat through their skin like humans can. On warm days, they quickly overheat, especially when exerting themselves. Warning signs include heavy panting, tongue hanging out, restlessness, staggering, vomiting or lethargy. Then you need shade immediately, water to drink and cool down with damp cloths, but not ice-cold water, and if in doubt, a vet's office quickly.

In summer, plan early in the morning or evening, choose forest trails and water tours. Use the back of your hand to check how hot the asphalt or rock is in the sun: if you can't stand it there for a few seconds, it's too hot for your paws.

## Encounters with grazing animals

On alpine pastures and pastures in the low mountain ranges you often come across cows, sometimes mother cows with calves. They see a dog as a possible attacker and can vigorously defend their calves. Follow these rules:

1. Put the dog on a short leash before entering the pasture.
2. Keep as much distance as possible from animals, especially calves.
3. Walk past calmly and quickly without running.
4. Move out of the way if the animals are on the path, or avoid the pasture if there is an alternative.
5. **If cows attack, let go of the leash.** The dog is faster and can escape, and the cows tend to focus on him instead of you.

## Accommodation and arrival

Many guesthouses and hotels accept dogs, sometimes for an additional charge. Ask before booking. This is regulated differently at huts, more about this in our guide to tours with dogs in the mountains. Depending on the transport association, different rules apply on buses and trains, often requiring a muzzle for larger dogs or a ticket. Check this before you arrive, especially if you are planning a long-distance hike with a return journey.

## After the tour

After a long day of hiking, your dog needs rest. Give him time to drink and eat and a quiet place to sleep. Check the paws for small cuts, stones or sores between the pads, and search the fur thoroughly for ticks. The next day you should pay attention to whether he walks stiffly or is lame. Slight muscle fatigue is normal after an unusually long tour; significant lameness or pain should be taken to the vet's office.

## Wild animals: deer, wild boars and wolves

Many dogs have a strong hunting instinct. A deer jumping out of the bushes in front of you can cause even well-trained dogs to rush. This is not only dangerous for the game, but also for the dog, who ends up on roads, cliff edges or in unfamiliar territory. Leash your dog as soon as you see game or are in dense cover.

Wild boars are particularly dangerous in spring when the streams bring their young. If a dog approaches them, they defend themselves violently. Give Rotten a wide berth and keep your dog close to you. Wolves now also live in many regions of Germany. Encounters are rare because wolves generally avoid people. However, they can see a dog as an intruder in their territory. Put him on a leash in wolf territory, stay calm during an encounter, speak loudly and back away slowly without running.

## First aid for the dog

The most common hiking injuries involve the paws. Rinse a cut with clean water, apply a compress, and protect the paw with a bandage, sock, or dog boot. If it bleeds heavily or the wound is gaping, the dog should be taken to the veterinary practice. A wasp or bee sting in the mouth or throat can be dangerous if the mucous membrane swells. Then watch your dog closely and go to the vet immediately if you have breathing problems.

Adders are rare in Germany and live primarily in moors, heaths and in the mountains. If your dog is bitten, keep him calm, carry him if possible and drive straight to the nearest veterinary practice. Before a longer tour, save the number of a practice and an emergency animal service in the region.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much water does my dog need when hiking?

This depends on size, temperature and effort. Offer him water regularly, about every 30 to 60 minutes, and more often in hot weather. It's better to take too much with you than too little, because not every stream has water in summer.

### Does my dog ​​need shoes?

Most dogs don't. However, dog shoes can be useful on sharp-edged rubble, snow with ice residue or for injured paws. Get your dog used to it beforehand.

### What to do if my dog ​​no longer wants to walk?

Take a break, offer water, check paws for injuries. If he still doesn't want to, the tour is over. Trim or wear it if possible.

### Can my dog ​​drink from streams and puddles?

Most dogs drink from clear, flowing streams without any problems. Caution is advised with standing water in summer: blue-green algae can multiply in warm lakes and ponds, and their toxins are life-threatening for dogs. Avoid water with greenish streaks or foam and rather offer your dog water from a bottle. Puddles on paths and parking lots can contain oil or other pollutants. Ask your veterinary practice which vaccinations are useful for a dog that spends a lot of time outdoors.
