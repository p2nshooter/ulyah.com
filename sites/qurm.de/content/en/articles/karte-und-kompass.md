Today, most hikers navigate using their cell phones, and it works surprisingly well. Nevertheless, it is worth mastering a map and compass. Batteries run out, cell phones fall into the stream, and the GPS signal is sometimes inaccurate in deep valleys or thick fog. Above all, a paper map helps you to keep an overview: you can see at a glance where valleys, ridges, huts and descent options are and can better assess your tour in the terrain.

This guide explains the most important basics: how to read a hiking map, how to locate it with a compass and how to determine a direction. You don't need anything more for most hikes in Germany.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Maps with a scale of 1:25,000 or 1:50,000 are suitable for hikes. At 1:25,000, 1 centimeter on the map corresponds to 250 meters.
> - Contour lines show the terrain: if they are close together, it is steep.
> - Uninorth means rotating the map so that its north points in the actual north direction.
> - A plate compass with a resting edge and rotating scale is completely sufficient for hikes.
> - The deviation between magnetic and geographic north is currently small in Germany and is negligible for most hikes.

## The right card

Two standards have been established for hikes:

| scale | corresponds to 1 cm on the map | 1 km corresponds to on the map | Suitable for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1:25.000 | 250 m | 4cm | detailed orientation, mountain tours, day tours |
| 1:50.000 | 500 m | 2cm | Overview, long-distance hikes, bike tours |

The smaller the number after the colon, the more detailed the map is. 1:25,000 is ideal for day trips and especially for hikes in the mountains. It also shows small paths, springs, rocks and huts. For a long-distance hike over several days, a map at a scale of 1:50,000 is often more practical because you need fewer sheets of paper.

Many hiking maps in Germany are published by state surveying offices or publishers together with hiking clubs and contain the marked hiking trails. Pay attention to the year of publication: paths are moved, and an old map sometimes shows paths that no longer exist.

## Read the map

A hiking map is full of information. The most important elements:

- **Legend:** At the edge or on the back it explains all the signs, from paths to forest types to viewpoints and refuges. It's worth taking a look inside before every tour.
- **Paths:** Wide roads, forest paths and narrow paths are shown differently. Marked hiking trails are often highlighted in color, sometimes with the symbol of the marker.
- **Grid:** Many topographic maps have a grid with edges of one kilometer. This allows you to quickly estimate distances and read coordinates.
- **Highlights:** Peaks and prominent points are labeled with their height.

## Understanding contour lines

Contour lines connect points of the same height. They are the key to “seeing” the terrain on the map. The distance between two lines, the so-called equidistance, is shown on the map, often 10 or 20 meters. From this you can read:

- **Narrow lines** mean steep terrain, **wide lines** mean flat terrain.
- **Closed circles** mark crests and peaks, sometimes also valleys.
- **Lines that taper uphill like a V** show a valley or gully. A stream often flows through it.
- **Lines that taper like a V downhill** indicate a ridge or ridge.
- **A saddle** is the lowest point between two peaks, visible on the map as a waist between two circles.

If you look at the contour lines along your route before a tour, you can see where it gets steep, where you should plan breaks and where the path leads over an open ridge that you don't want to be on during a thunderstorm.

## The compass

A so-called plate compass is sufficient for hikes: a transparent base plate with a contact edge and directional arrow, on top of which is a rotating capsule with a needle and degree scale. More expensive models have a mirror for direction finding, which is practical but not necessary.

The red or marked end of the needle points to magnetic north. When reading, keep the compass horizontal and away from metal, cell phones and magnets, such as backpack fasteners, as they can distract the needle.

## Place the map north

Aligning means orienting the map so that it is consistent with the terrain. Then everything that is in the landscape to your right is also to the right of your location on the map.

1. Lay the card horizontally or hold it flat in front of you.
2. Place the compass with the edge parallel to the vertical grid lines or to the edge of the map.
3. Rotate the capsule so that the north mark on the scale points towards map north.
4. Now turn the map and compass together until the needle points to the north mark on the capsule.

The map is now aligned. You can also do it roughly without a compass: Align the map with prominent points on the terrain, such as a river, a straight road or a peak that you can identify.

## Determine a direction of march

Suppose you are standing at an intersection and want to go to a hut that is marked on the map, but the route is not clear.

1. Place the compass's edge on the map so that it connects your location to the destination. The directional arrow on the plate points to the target.
2. Rotate the capsule until its north lines are parallel to the map's north-south grid lines and the north marker faces map north.
3. Take the compass from the map, hold it horizontally in front of you and turn until the needle points to the north mark on the capsule.
4. The directional arrow on the plate now points in your direction of travel. The number on the scale is the march number in degrees.

Find a prominent point in this direction, such as a tree or rock, and walk toward it. Then repeat the bearing. This way you stay on track even if you don't have a path.

## Mismatch: Do I have to take this into account?

Magnetic north and true north are not in the same place. The angle in between is called declination or declination. In Germany it is currently low, it lies within a few degrees east and changes slowly over the years. For most hikes where you orientate yourself on paths and terrain points, you can ignore them. You should take them into account for long bearings over pathless stretches or abroad with greater declination. The current value is written on the edge of many cards.

## Determine your own location

The most common task on the road is not taking direction, but asking the question: Where am I right now? To do this, you combine several clues. First you north the map. Then you compare prominent points in the area with the map: a crossroads, a stream, the edge of the forest, a hut, a peak in the distance. The contour lines help you check whether you are standing on a slope, on a ridge or in a depression. Finally, you think about how long you have been traveling since the last safe point and approximately how far you can have come in that time. Usually there is only one spot left on the map that fits everything.

## Practice before it matters

Orientation is learned through practice, not through reading. Take the map with you on your next hikes, even if you know the route. Determine where you are at intersections and compare terrain and map. Find the peak you see on the map. And try northing if you have time and nothing depends on it.

## If you're lost

Sooner or later it happens to everyone: the path no longer fits the map, a marker is missing, and you don't know exactly where you are. Then a simple sequence that is taught in many orientation courses helps:

1. **Stand still.** If you keep running to “get back on track quickly”, you often end up moving even further away.
2. **Think.** When was the last time you were sure where you were? How long ago was that and what direction have you taken since then?
3. **Orientate.** North the map and compare the terrain with the contour lines and prominent points.
4. **Plan.** Decide consciously what happens next. If in doubt, going back to the last safe point is the best choice.

Never descend cross-country into unknown, steep terrain just because there is a valley below. Streams in the mountains often lead over cliffs and into gorges from which there is no further progress.

Two techniques from orienteering help you avoid getting lost in the first place. A **catchline** is a long, unmissable terrain feature such as a river, road, or edge of a forest that you will always come across if you are going in the right direction. With **conscious aim** you intentionally aim a little to the left or right past a point that lies on such a line, such as a bridge over a stream. When you reach the stream you will then know exactly which direction to turn.

## Coordinates in an emergency

Many topographic maps have a grid that allows you to specify your location as coordinates. Most hiking apps and GPS devices also show your position as coordinates. In an emergency, this can save the rescue team a lot of time: When calling 112, be as specific as possible about where you are, for example the route, the next prominent point and the coordinates from the app. It's worth checking out where your app displays this information before the tour so that you don't have to search for long in a stressful situation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I still need a paper card if I use an app?

For simple tours on well-marked paths, an offline map on your cell phone is often sufficient, ideally with a power bank. In the mountains, on long tours and in remote areas, a paper map is a valuable addition because it does not require electricity and offers a better overview.

### What is an altimeter and do I need one?

An altimeter, often built into outdoor watches, shows the current altitude. Together with the contour lines, you can determine your location on the slope very precisely. This is helpful for mountain tours, but usually not necessary for low mountain paths.

### How do I best learn this?

Many Alpine club sections, hiking clubs and adult education centers offer orientation courses. An afternoon of field exercises will do more than any book.

### What is the difference between grid north, geographic north and magnetic north?

Geographically north is the direction to the North Pole. Magnetic north is the direction your compass needle points. Grid North is the direction of the vertical grid lines on the map, which may deviate slightly from true north due to map projection. When hiking in Germany, the differences between all three directions are so small that you don't have to take them into account when heading north and following paths. They only become important for long bearings through pathless terrain.
