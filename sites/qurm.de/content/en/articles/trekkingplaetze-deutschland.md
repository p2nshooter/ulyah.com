A tent between trees, dinner on the stove, the sound of the forest as you fall asleep and dew on the outer tent in the morning: for many hikers, the night outside is the epitome of freedom. In Germany, this wish quickly comes up against legal limits, as wild camping is prohibited in almost all federal states. The good news: In more and more regions there are trekking sites, small, simple campsites in the middle of nature where you can legally spend a night. Here you can find out how the system works and how to plan your first tour with a tent.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Wild camping is generally not permitted in forests and protected areas in Germany. The rules vary depending on the federal state, but it is forbidden almost everywhere.
> - Trekking sites are simple, official campsites in the forest with space for a few tents, often with a composting toilet.
> - You book it online in advance for one night, usually for a small fee. You often only find out the exact location after booking.
> - There are trekking places in the Palatinate Forest, the Eifel, the Black Forest and other nature parks, among others.
> - Open fires are usually prohibited or only permitted in existing fire pits when the risk of forest fires is low. Take all rubbish back with you.

## Why wild camping is forbidden

In Germany you are allowed to enter the forest to relax; this is regulated by the Federal Forest Act together with the state forest laws. However, the right of entry does not include setting up tents. Most federal states expressly prohibit camping in the forest or only allow it with the consent of the forest owner. It is forbidden in national parks and nature reserves anyway. Anyone who still camps wild risks a fine.

The reasons are understandable: wild animals need rest at night, many people in the same popular places leave trash, feces and fire pits behind, and fires in the forest are a major danger in dry summers.

**Bivouacing**, i.e. a night without a tent in a sleeping bag in the open air, is rated differently depending on the federal state. Legally it is a gray area. It is almost always forbidden in protected areas. You are on the safe side at trekking sites.

## What is a trekking site?

A trekking site is a small, leveled area in the forest that has been designated for overnight stays by a nature park, forestry company or community. Typical are:

- Space for a few tents, often three to five, sometimes with wooden platforms
- a composting toilet or outhouse
- sometimes a fire pit, benches or a table
- no water supply, no electricity, no showers
- There is no access by car, you can only reach the place on foot

The sites are deliberately located off the beaten track and often close to long-distance hiking trails, so that you can incorporate them into multi-day tours.

## Where are there trekking spots?

The network is growing and new regions are being added regularly. Well-known areas with trekking places are:

| region | Character |
|---|---|
| Palatinate Forest | one of the first trekking networks in Germany, places in red sandstone forests, easy to combine with hut hikes |
| Eifel | Places in the North and Vulkan Eifel, some near the Eifelsteig |
| Black Forest | Trekking camps in several nature park regions, remote in the forest |
| More nature parks | Various low mountain regions have set up their own offerings |

You can find information on the websites of the nature parks and tourism regions. Search for the term “trekking site” along with the region. More about the regions themselves can be found in our articles about the [Palatinate Forest](/ratgeber/pfaelzerwald-wandern/), the [Eifelsteig](/ratgeber/eifelsteig-wandern/) and the [Westweg in the Black Forest](/ratgeber/westweg-schwarzwald/).

## Book

1. Find a place that suits your route on the region's website.
2. Book online for a specific date. The fee is usually small and charged per tent or person.
3. After booking you will receive the exact coordinates and directions. Many regions do not publicly disclose the location to prevent misuse.
4. Popular spots fill up quickly on summer weekends. You have better chances during the week and in the off-season.
5. Many trekking sites are only open in the warm season, around April to October.

## Equipment for the first night

- **Tent:** lightweight, freestanding or with few pegs. Some sites have wooden platforms that you attach the tent to with strings.
- **Sleeping mat and sleeping bag** suitable for the season. Even in summer it can cool down below ten degrees at night in the forest.
- **Stove, pot, lighter:** A gas stove is permitted on most sites. But pay attention to forest fire warnings.
- **Water:** There is no water point. Take enough for dinner, breakfast and the next morning, about three to four liters per person.
- **Headlamp, toilet paper, trash bags.**
- **First Aid Kit** as described in [Hiking First Aid Kit](/ratgeber/erste-hilfe-set-wandern/).

With a tent, sleeping bag and stove, the backpack becomes significantly heavier. How to pack and adjust it sensibly is explained in [Hiking backpack: size, adjustment and packing](/ratgeber/rucksack-packen-und-einstellen/).

## Local rules

- **Peace:** Trekking places are not party places. Loud music and group celebrations are taboo.
- **Fire:** Only in existing fire pits and only if there is no fire ban in effect. In dry summers, fires in the forest are often completely forbidden. Check the region's wildfire danger index.
- **Wood:** Only collect dead wood from the ground if fire is allowed. Don't saw off branches.
- **Toilet:** Use the composting toilet. If none is available, bury droppings at least 6 inches deep, away from watercourses.
- **Garbage:** Take everything back with you, including leftover food and toilet paper that doesn't belong in the composting toilet.
- **One night only:** Most of the time you are only allowed to stay one night.
- **Avoid leaving marks:** Leave the place as if you were never there.

## Wildlife

There are no animals in the German forest that are dangerous to people in tents. However, foxes, raccoons and mice are interested in food. Keep food and trash locked inside the tent or in a bag hanging from a tree, not open in front of it. Ticks are the bigger nuisance: check yourself in the evening and morning, as described in [Ticks while hiking](/ratgeber/zecken-fsme-borreliose/).

## A sample two-night tour

A good first tour with a tent combines two trekking sites with a start and finish at train stations. On the first day you leave at midday and hike for three to four hours to the first site so that you have enough time to set up and cook. On the second day there is time for a longer stage with a stop before you reach second place. On the third day you go to the destination station in two to three hours. You fill up water with drinking water in places, huts or wells. Plan the route so that you have a chance to refill before each place in the afternoon.

## The first night: a typical sequence

Plan to arrive at the site late in the afternoon with at least two hours of daylight left. First, find a flat spot and look up: dead branches or crooked trees hanging over the tent are dangerous, especially in windy conditions. Then set up the tent while it's light and arrange the sleeping gear so they can unfold. Then you cook, wash up sparingly with a little water and store food and garbage in a sealed container.

Before you go to sleep, go to the toilet again so that you don't have to go out in the dark at night, and put a headlamp, water and a warm hat close at hand next to your head. In the morning the tent is often wet from dew or condensation. Then pack it up separately from the dry things and set it up to dry at home or at the nearest accommodation so that it doesn't get moldy.

## Sleep well in the tent

Whether you freeze at night depends less on the sleeping bag than many people think, but often on the sleeping pad. The ground takes away your heat. How well a mat insulates is shown by its **R-value**: low values ​​are sufficient for warm summer nights, medium values ​​of around three are useful for spring and autumn, and higher values ​​for cold nights. The standardized temperature information helps with sleeping bags: The **comfort temperature** is the value at which an average person sleeps relaxed, the **limit temperature** is the value at which they don't freeze when curled up. Orientate yourself to the comfort temperature.

Other tricks: Put on dry clothes and socks for the night, wear a hat, eat something warm before going to sleep, and make a hot water bottle out of a well-sealed drinking bottle filled with hot water.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I go to a trekking site with children?

Yes, for many families it is a great adventure. Choose a spot that's not too far from the parking lot or train station and try a night in the garden beforehand. More ideas can be found in [Hiking with school children](/ratgeber/wandern-mit-schulkindern/).

### Can I stay overnight with a hammock?

That depends on the space. Some sites allow hammocks on designated posts or trees, others do not. Read the site rules before booking.

### What if the weather changes?

If there are storm warnings, you should not spend the night under trees as branches and trees can fall. Then cancel or move to permanent accommodation.

### How much does a night at the trekking site cost?

The fees are deliberately low and are usually in the low double-digit euro range per tent per night. This finances care, toilets and administration. The current prices can be found on the booking pages of the respective region.

### Can I stay alone at a trekking site?

Yes, and many people are enjoying this peace and quiet. Tell someone in advance where you are staying and when you will be back, keep your phone charged and write down the coordinates of the place in case of emergency. The first night alone in the forest is unusual: cracking, rustling and calls from owls or deer are completely normal. Earplugs help if the noise prevents you from sleeping.
