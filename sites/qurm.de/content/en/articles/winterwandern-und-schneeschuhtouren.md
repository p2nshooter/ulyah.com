A winter day in the low mountain ranges has its own magic. The forests are quiet, the snow crunches under your shoes, hoarfrost covers the branches, and from an open summit the view often extends much further when the air is clear than in summer. Many paths that are full in summer are almost yours alone in winter. At the same time, hiking in winter is more demanding: the days are short, the ground is slippery or covered in deep snow, cold and wind take a toll on the body, and in steep terrain there is also the risk of avalanches.

This guide explains what types of winter hiking there are, what equipment you need, how to go with snowshoes and what you need to pay attention to when planning, cold weather and avalanches. It is aimed at hikers who have already gained experience in summer and want to rediscover winter.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - For beginners, cleared or groomed winter hiking trails are the best choice. Grödel under your shoes provides stability on hard-packed snow and ice.
> - You only need snowshoes when there is deep, loose snow away from cleared paths.
> - Avalanches can occur on slopes with an inclination of around 30 degrees or more. Away from secured paths, this includes an avalanche report, emergency equipment and training.
> - Plan for short days: Be back at least an hour before sunset and always take a headlamp.
> - Wild animals save energy in winter. Stay on the paths and avoid game rest areas.

## Winter hiking, snowshoeing, ski touring: the differences

The term winter hiking covers a wide variety of activities. They differ primarily in terrain and requirements.

| Art | Terrain | Equipment | For whom |
|---|---|---|---|
| Winter hiking on cleared paths | Prepared, often signposted winter paths | warm hiking boots, Grödel, sticks | almost everyone, including families |
| Hiking on snowy summer paths | well-trodden paths and paths | as above, plus gaiters | Hikers with experience |
| Snowshoeing | deep snow, forests, gentle slopes | Snowshoes, poles with snow plates | Beginners on marked trails, experienced ones also in open terrain |
| Ski tour | Alpine terrain, often at risk of avalanches | Touring skis, skins, avalanche equipment | Experienced with training |

For this guide, the focus is on the first three. Ski tours and snowshoe tours in steep alpine terrain require training and experience that a text cannot replace.

## Where winter hiking is a success

In many German low mountain ranges and in the Bavarian Alps, communities and tourism associations maintain their own winter hiking trails. They are cleared or rolled with machines after snowfalls and are often marked with their own signs. Good regions include the Black Forest, the Bavarian Forest, the Harz, the Thuringian Forest, the Ore Mountains, the Rhön and the Allgäu. In winter, many places publish current information about which paths have been cleared. A look at the tourist information website or a call saves disappointment.

Make sure that winter paths do not always follow the path of summer paths. Some summer paths lead through steep slopes, over bridges without winter services or through avalanche paths and are closed in winter. Therefore, follow the winter signs and not automatically the markings that you know from summer. More information can be found in [Understanding waymarks](/ratgeber/wegmarkierungen-verstehen/).

## The right equipment

In winter, more than in summer, equipment determines whether a trip will be pleasant or dangerous.

**Shoes:** Warm, waterproof hiking boots with a non-slip profile sole are the basis. You should have enough space so that your toes can move freely even with thick socks, because cramped feet freeze more quickly. **Gaiters** prevent snow from falling into the top of your shoes.

**Grödel and Spikes:** Grödel are small chains with spikes that you pull over your shoes with a rubber band. They provide a surprising amount of grip on icy paths and packed snow and weigh little. They are not a replacement for crampons in alpine terrain, but are ideal for winter hiking trails.

**Sticks:** Hiking poles with large snow plates help with balance and provide relief when descending. [Using hiking poles correctly](/ratgeber/wanderstoecke-richtig-nutzen/) explains how to adjust them.

**Clothing:** The [Onion Principle](/ratgeber/zwiebelprinzip-wanderbekleidung/) is more important than ever in winter. A warming base layer made of merino or synthetic fiber, a fleece, a warm jacket for breaks and a windproof outer layer can be combined depending on the effort. There is also a hat, a tube scarf and two pairs of gloves so that you can change wet ones.

**Other:** An insulated bottle with warm tea, high-energy provisions, a headlamp with full batteries, sunglasses to protect against the glare from snow, sunscreen, a first aid kit with an emergency blanket and a charged cell phone that you carry close to your body because the cold quickly drains the battery.

## Snowshoes: selection and technique

Snowshoes distribute your weight over a larger area so that you don't sink up to your knees in the powder snow. Modern models are made of plastic or aluminum, have a binding for normal winter shoes, spikes under the sole for grip on rough terrain and a **climbing aid**: a bracket under the heel that relieves the strain on the calves when climbing.

It's worth renting for the first few tours. Many sports shops in winter sports resorts rent out snowshoes and poles, and in several regions there are guided snowshoe hikes where you can learn the technique under supervision.

This is how you do it correctly:

1. **On the flat:** Walk a little wider than usual so that the snowshoes don't bump into each other. The steps remain natural, the sticks help with the rhythm.
2. **Uphill:** Unfold the climbing aid, take small steps, press the front points firmly into the snow. On steeper sections, you go in flat serpentines instead of straight up.
3. **Downhill:** Fold in the climbing aid, shift your weight slightly backwards, keep your knees slightly bent and touch down with your heel first.
4. **Crossing:** On a slope, press the uphill edge of the snowshoe into the snow and grip the uphill-side pole shorter.
5. **Tracks:** Anyone who leads the way in deep snow is doing hard work. Take turns in the group regularly.

## Avalanches: the most important thing for hikers

Avalanches are not a danger that only affects ski tourers. Snowshoe hikers and winter hikers who leave cleared paths can also end up in avalanche-prone terrain. The most important basic rules:

- **Steepness:** Slab avalanches occur primarily on slopes with an inclination of around 30 degrees or more. Even flat paths can be at risk if they run below steep slopes.
- **Avalanche situation report:** In the Alps, the avalanche warning services publish a daily report during the season with the danger level on a Europe-wide scale from 1 to 5. Many accidents occur at levels 2 and 3, which sound more harmless than they are.
- **Note closures:** Closed paths are closed because an avalanche can occur above them.
- **Equipment and knowledge:** Anyone traveling off secured paths in the mountains needs an avalanche transceiver, a shovel and a probe and must be able to use them. The Alpine clubs and mountain schools offer avalanche courses.

For most winter hikes in the low mountain ranges, the risk of avalanches is low. In the Alps, however, the following applies: Without training, you stay on cleared, designated winter paths and marked snowshoe trails.

## Short days, slower steps

Around the winter solstice, the days in Germany are only bright for around eight hours. In the forest and shady valleys it becomes dusky in the early afternoon. At the same time, you progress more slowly in the snow than in summer. On hard-packed paths the difference is small, but in fresh snow the walking time can be significantly longer, sometimes double.

So plan like this:

- Choose shorter tours than in summer and allow yourself a generous time buffer. The basics of calculating walking time can be found in [Calculate walking time](/ratgeber/gehzeit-berechnen/).
- Set a turnaround time and stick to it, even if the goal seems close.
- Start early so you can take full advantage of the light hours.
- Always take a headlamp with you, even for a short afternoon tour.

## Cold: hypothermia and frostbite

When it's cold, you lose heat primarily through wind, through wet clothing and through contact with cold ground. Hypothermia often starts gradually: you shake, become slower, more clumsy, and start making mistakes. By then it's time for a warm layer, something sweet and a warm drink, and if in doubt, for the way back.

**Frostbite** primarily affects fingers, toes, nose, ears and cheeks. The skin first becomes red, then whitish, cold and numb. Slowly apply body heat to heat-affected areas, for example by putting your hands under your armpits. Do not rub them with snow, as old advice recommends, as this will further damage the tissue. If you have blisters, persistent numbness, or dark discoloration, you need medical attention. [Emergency on the mountain](/ratgeber/notfall-am-berg/) describes what to do in an emergency.

Prevention is easier than treatment: Eat and drink regularly, even when you don't feel hungry or thirsty, change wet gloves and socks, and take breaks in places protected from the wind. You can find information about food in [Provisions and drinking when hiking](/ratgeber/proviant-und-trinken/).

## Respect for wildlife

Winter is a tough time for wildlife. Roe deer, deer, chamois and grouse such as capercaillie slow down their metabolism in order to survive on little food. Every escape through deep snow costs them energy, which they urgently need until spring. In many regions there are designated wildlife rest areas or wildlife protection areas that are not allowed to be entered in winter.

This is how you behave correctly:

- Stay on paths and marked trails, especially with snowshoes.
- Avoid forest edges and feeding areas at dusk when wildlife is active.
- Keep dogs on a leash.
- Be quiet and don't venture into thickets or young forests.

## Cross-country ski trails are taboo

In many winter sports areas, winter hiking trails lead along or cross cross-country ski trails. The trail tracks are made with great effort, and footprints or snowshoe tracks destroy them. Therefore, never walk on the cross-country ski trail, but rather on the winter path next to it, and only cross it in designated places.

## Read the weather in winter

The winter weather can change quickly. Fresh snow, storms, fog and black ice change the conditions within a few hours. Before every tour, check the weather report for the altitude of your tour, not just for the location in the valley. During inversion weather conditions, it is often sunny and mild up in the mountains, while cold fog lies down in the valleys. Conversely, it can be stormy above when there is calm below. How to correctly interpret weather reports for the mountains is explained in [Read Mountain Weather](/ratgeber/bergwetter-lesen/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Will Grödel be enough or do I need snowshoes?

On cleared and hard-packed winter paths, Grödel is completely sufficient. You need snowshoes when the snow is deep and loose and without them you will sink in with every step. Many experienced winter hikers have both with them and decide on the way.

### Can I go snowshoeing with children?

Yes, there are smaller snowshoes for children, and most of them really enjoy them. Choose short laps on flat terrain, plan plenty of breaks and make sure that the children don't get cold. A toboggan ride or a stop at the finish make the day perfect.

### How do I know if a winter path is cleared?

Many towns and tourist regions publish a current clearance report on their website in winter. On site you can recognize cleared paths by the wide, smooth track and the winter path signs. If a path is not cleared, it becomes significantly more strenuous and you need more time.

### Is winter hiking healthy in severe frost?

For healthy people, exercise in cold, clear air is not a problem if they are dressed warmly and do not overexert themselves. Anyone who has heart or respiratory diseases should speak to their doctor beforehand, as cold air can irritate the respiratory tract and put additional strain on the circulatory system. A tube cloth over your mouth and nose warms the air you breathe slightly.
