The Rothaarsteig leads over the ridge of the Rothaar Mountains, one of the most wooded low mountain ranges in Germany. Over around 150 kilometers it connects Brilon in the Hochsauerland with Dillenburg in Hesse and crosses the Sauerland, the Wittgensteiner Land and the Siegerland. Since it opened in 2001, it has been nicknamed the “Path of the Senses”: along the way, viewpoints, forest sofas, towers and works of art invite you to consciously perceive the landscape. The path is well marked, technically easy and easily accessible by train, making it an ideal first long-distance hike.

In recent years the image of the Rothaar Mountains has changed significantly. Storms, drought and bark beetles have destroyed large spruce forests, and in many places you can now hike through open areas with wide views and young forest. This guide describes the route, the highlights and the planning, and explains what the forest change means for hikers.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Length around 150 kilometers from Brilon to Dillenburg, usually in six to eight daily stages.
> - The path runs mainly on the ridge at altitudes between around 500 and a good 800 meters, with moderate inclines.
> - Highlights are the Kahle Asten, the Hochheiden, the sources of the Ruhr, Lenne, Eder and Sieg, the Rhein-Weser Tower and numerous viewpoints.
> - Many stage locations have a train station or a bus connection. Long-distance hikes are possible without a car.
> - After storm and beetle damage, some sections are shadowless. Sun protection and enough water are important in summer.

## Course and character

The Rothaarsteig begins in **Brilon** on the northern edge of the Hochsauerland and soon climbs to the heights. It leads near the Bruchhauser Steine, mighty rocks made of volcanic rock, and reaches the area around Winterberg via **Willingen**. There is the **Langenberg**, the highest mountain in North Rhine-Westphalia, and the **Kahler Asten**, one of the most famous peaks in the Sauerland, on which there is a weather station and a viewing tower.

South of Winterberg the path follows the main ridge of the Rothaar Mountains. The headwaters of several rivers lie here: the Ruhr, Lenne, Eder, Sieg and Lahn arise in this area and flow in very different directions. The path passes several of these springs, which are often lovingly landscaped. The watershed between the catchment areas of the Rhine and Weser runs at the **Rhein-Weser Tower**, which is what the observation tower has in its name.

In the last part, the Rothaarsteig crosses the Siegerland and finally reaches **Dillenburg** in the Lahn-Dill district over the heights on the edge of the Westerwald. The city with its Schlossberg and the Wilhelmsturm is a worthy end point.

## The sections at a glance

The division of stages depends on where you stay overnight. Many accommodations are located in places below the ridge, which you can reach via signposted access routes. A typical structure looks like this:

| Section | Roughly from – to | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Hochsauerland North | Brilon-Willingen | Bruchhauser stones, forests, first views |
| Around Winterberg | Willingen – Winterberg | Langenberg, Hochheiden, Kahler Asten, Ruhrquelle |
| Red hair comb | Winterberg – Rhine-Weser Tower | lonely ridge, springs, vast forest areas |
| Wittgenstein and Siegerland | Rhein-Weser Tower – Lützel | Eder and Sieg springs, watershed, Ginsberger Heide |
| conclusion | Lützel – Dillenburg | Transition to Westerwald and Dilltal, arrival in town |

The daily stages are usually between 15 and 25 kilometers long. Because the path mainly remains on the ridge, the elevation gain is limited. In addition to the accommodation in the valley, there are often access routes with descents and ascents again. Factor them into your planning as described in [Calculate walking time](/ratgeber/gehzeit-berechnen/).

## Hochheiden and springs

A special feature of the Hochsauerland are the **Hochheiden**. On the barren, windy heights around Winterberg and Willingen, heather, blueberries and cranberries grow in open areas that have been created by grazing over centuries. In late summer, when the heather blooms, the tops turn purple. The heaths are protected and cared for so that they do not become overgrown. Stay on the paths because the plants and ground-nesting birds that live here are sensitive.

The springs along the way are small, quiet places where it's worth taking a break. But don't drink the water unfiltered, even if it looks clear. It is not tested as drinking water. You can find information about water along the way in [Provisions and drinking when hiking](/ratgeber/proviant-und-trinken/).

## Forest change: what awaits hikers

In January 2007, Hurricane Kyrill swept over Germany and knocked down millions of trees in the Sauerland. The forests had barely recovered from this when several very dry summers followed from 2018 onwards, during which the bark beetle attacked the weakened spruce trees on a large scale. Today, large parts of the Rothaar Mountains are bare or overgrown with young forest in which birch, rowan, beech and newly planted mixed tree species grow.

For hikers this has two sides. On the one hand, many sections that were once in the dark spruce forest are now open and offer wide views. On the other hand, there is no shade in summer and it gets hot on the bare areas. So take enough water and sunscreen with you. In areas with dead trees, the following also applies: During a storm, hikers have no place in the forest, as dead trees and branches can fall at any time. Please note closures and diversions after storms and woodwork.

## The Kyrill Path: Storm surface you can touch

The Kyrill path near Schanze, a small town on the Rothaarsteig near Schmallenberg, shows how powerful the hurricane was. In an area that was deliberately not cleared up after the storm, footbridges, stairs and narrow paths lead over and between fallen trunks and erected root plates. Information boards explain how nature recovers in such areas without human intervention: first grasses, raspberries and foxgloves grow, then birches and mountain ash, and under their protection beech and other deciduous trees slowly emerge. For children, the short path is an adventure, for adults it is an impressive lesson about the forest of the future. Allow about an hour and go slowly as the walkways can be slippery when wet.

## Arrival and return journey

The Rothaarsteig is easily accessible by train. Brilon, Willingen and Winterberg have train stations, and in the south Bad Berleburg and Erndtebrück, among others, are on a railway line that opens up the Wittgensteiner Land. Dillenburg is on a main route with good connections to Siegen, Gießen and Frankfurt. This allows you to walk the path in sections and get on and off at many points. Buses connect the towns between the train stations, although they are often less frequent on weekends and holidays. How to plan long-distance hikes by bus and train can be found in [Hiking without a car](/ratgeber/wandern-ohne-auto/).

## Overnight stay and luggage

There are hotels, inns, guesthouses and holiday apartments along the way. The number of beds is limited, especially in small towns, and many hosts have adapted to hikers, picking them up along the way upon request and bringing them back up the next morning. Several providers organize package tours with luggage transport. If you carry yourself, you can get by with a backpack of 30 to 35 liters. [Packing a hiking backpack correctly](/ratgeber/rucksack-packen-und-einstellen/) describes what belongs in it.

## The best season

- **Spring:** From the end of April and May the paths are mostly free of snow, the beech trees are sprouting and it is still pleasantly cool.
- **Summer:** Long days, blueberries along the way and heather blossoms in August. It can get hot on the bare areas.
- **Autumn:** Colorful deciduous forests, clear views and less activity. For many the best time.
- **Winter:** There is often snow on the heights, and around Winterberg and Willingen it's all about winter sports. The Rothaarsteig can then only be accessed in sections on cleared winter paths. More about this in [Winter hiking and snowshoe tours](/ratgeber/winterwandern-und-schneeschuhtouren/).

## Other routes in the region

In addition to the Rothaarsteig, the Sauerland has other well-known hiking trails. The **Sauerland-Höhenflug** runs over the heights of the Sauerland to northern Hesse and crosses the Rothaarsteig. Between Bad Berleburg and Schmallenberg, the **Forest Sculpture Trail** leads past large works of art that artists created specifically for the forest. Many places also have shorter circular routes that are labeled as premium or quality trails. They are a good way to get to know the region over a weekend.

## Equipment

- Comfortable, worn-in hiking boots for long stretches on forest roads and paths, as described in [Buy hiking boots](/ratgeber/wanderschuhe-kaufen/).
- Rain jacket and warm layer, because it is often windy and cooler at the heights than in the valley
- Sun hat and sunscreen for the open bare areas
- At least 1.5 to 2 liters of water per stage because refreshment stops on the ridge are rare
- Map or offline map, especially for access to the accommodation
- Blister plasters and a small first aid kit

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the Rothaarsteig suitable for beginners?

Yes. Technically the route is easy and the gradients are moderate. The challenge lies in the length of the stages over several days. Anyone who has already done day tours of 15 to 20 kilometers will get along well with a relaxed division of stages.

### How is the path marked?

The Rothaarsteig has its own signpost, which is attached to trees, posts and signposts. There are also separate signs for access to towns and train stations. Due to woodwork on the bare areas, markings may be missing at times, which is why a map or app makes sense as an addition.

### Can I walk the Rothaarsteig with a dog?

Yes, the trail is dog friendly and many accommodations accept dogs. In nature reserves such as the Hochheiden and during the breeding and breeding season, leashes are required. There is a lack of shade and water on the bare areas in summer, so plan breaks and water for your dog. More about this can be found in [Hiking with a dog](/ratgeber/wandern-mit-hund/).

### Which direction should I go?

Most descriptions follow the direction from Brilon to Dillenburg, from north to south. This is not mandatory because the path is marked in both directions. If you want to experience the most famous highlights around Winterberg right from the start, start in the north.
