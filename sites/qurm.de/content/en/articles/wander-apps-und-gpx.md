For most hikers today, the smartphone is the most important navigation tool. A good hiking app shows you topographical maps, your location with an accuracy of a few meters, elevation profiles and walking times. You can plan tours, load GPX files from trail operators and record your route. This is a huge improvement over earlier times when people puzzled at intersections with folded paper maps.

Nevertheless, people get lost with their cell phones in their hand, for example because the map was not saved offline, the battery was empty or an app suggested a route that no longer exists in the area. This guide explains how to use digital navigation safely.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Download maps offline before the tour. In the forest and in valleys you often have no reception.
> - GPX files from official bodies such as trail operators, nature parks or hiking clubs are usually more reliable than any user tours.
> - A cell phone shows you where you are, but not whether the path is accessible. Signs and on-site closures take precedence.
> - Battery saving: flight mode with active GPS, screen off, power bank in the backpack.
> - A paper map or a second device is the best protection for longer tours.

## What a good hiking app should be able to do

There are a number of popular apps for hiking, both free and paid. Instead of recommending a specific one, it's worth paying attention to these features:

| Function | Why it is important |
|---|---|
| Topographic maps with contour lines | you recognize slopes, valleys and ridges |
| Offline maps | Navigation without cell reception |
| Import GPX files | Use official routes and load your own tours |
| Elevation profile and walking time | Planning the tour and scheduling |
| Record your own path | Find your way back, get to know the pace |
| Display of coordinates | important for emergency calls |
| Waymarks and hiking trails on the map | you see which sign you follow |

Many apps use maps based on OpenStreetMap, an open world map maintained by volunteers, or official topographic maps from state surveys. Both have advantages and disadvantages. OpenStreetMap is often very current and detailed, but may also contain paths that are unofficial, closed or not allowed in protected areas. Official maps are reliable, but are updated less often.

## What is a GPX file?

GPX is a file format used to store waypoints, routes and recorded tracks. A GPX file of a hike contains a sequence of coordinates that describe the route, often with elevation information. You load it into your app and it shows the course as a line on the map.

For long-distance hiking trails such as Rennsteig, Westweg or Rheinsteig, the operators or tourism associations usually provide official GPX files, sometimes for each stage individually. These files are the best basis because they show the current official history. You should take a closer look at tours from communities: who created the tour, when was it last visited, are there any comments about closures or problems?

## Plan a tour digitally

Most apps allow you to plan your own tours. You set the start and destination and the app calculates a route along the network of paths. This is practical, but has its drawbacks:

- **The app does not know the condition of the path.** A narrow path on the map may be overgrown, washed out or closed in the terrain.
- **The app doesn't know your abilities.** Some route planners will take you over steep, exposed climbs if they are shorter.
- **Protected areas are not always taken into account.** In the core zones of national parks, there is often a path requirement. A path on the map is not proof that you are allowed to walk it.

Therefore, always check the planned route: look at the elevation profile, path types and the marked hiking trails. Follow marked trails where possible and compare the walking time with your own estimate.

## Go offline

Cell phone reception is often poor in forests, valleys and mountains. The GPS still works because it receives signals from satellites and does not need a cellular network. What doesn't work without a net is reloading map tiles. Therefore: **Download the map area of ​​your tour in advance**, preferably at home via WiFi. Then check in airplane mode whether the map is actually displayed completely.

## Save battery

A smartphone with the screen on and navigation active drains the battery faster than many people think, especially in cold weather. These measures help:

1. **Airplane mode with GPS:** In airplane mode, the cell phone does not constantly search for a network. GPS remains active on most devices or can be turned on again.
2. **Screen off:** Only turn on the screen briefly when you need to orient yourself. Many apps warn you with a sound when you stray from the path.
3. **Reduce brightness** and close unnecessary apps.
4. **Keep your phone warm:** Battery performance drops significantly in cold weather. Carry your cell phone in an inside pocket close to your body.
5. **Take a power bank with you:** A small power bank with a suitable cable is essential on longer tours.
6. **Reserve for emergencies:** Plan so that you always have enough battery for an emergency call.

## Watches and GPS devices

GPS watches and standalone GPS devices often have significantly longer battery life than smartphones and are more robust. Many watches show a route as a line and warn if you deviate. They are a good addition for long tours and long-distance hikes. Satellite communicators, with which you can send messages or make an emergency call even without a cell phone, are particularly interesting in very remote areas. For most tours in Germany, a cell phone with an offline map is sufficient.

## The limits of technology

Digital navigation encourages you to just look at the screen. This has two disadvantages: you notice the landscape less, and you lose the feel for the terrain. If your cell phone fails, you often no longer know where you are.

Therefore, use the app as a help, not as a replacement for attention. Pay attention to waymarks and signposts, note prominent points and look at the map from time to time, even if you are sure. This way you remain able to act, even if the technology fails.

## Load a GPX file into the app

The path is similar for most apps. Download the file from the official trail or region website. On your smartphone, tap the downloaded file and select “Open with” and your hiking app. Many apps also have a tour planner in the browser: You import the file there on your computer and it also appears on your cell phone after synchronization. Then check whether the route is complete and the start and finish are correct, and save the map area around the route offline.

It's good to know the difference between **Track** and **Route**. A track is a dense sequence of recorded points that accurately traces the path. A route consists of a few waypoints between which the app calculates a route itself. A track is usually more reliable for hikes because the app does not automatically choose a shorter version.

## Record your own tours

Record your hikes every now and then, even if you know the route. You will get to know your actual pace, uphill and downhill, and can plan future tours more realistically. The recording also helps you find your way back if you get lost. When sharing or publishing your tracks, keep your privacy in mind: only start recording when you've left your house, or use privacy zones that many apps offer so that your address isn't visible to everyone.

## Share location

Many smartphones and apps can share your location live with selected people. This is a useful security feature for solo hikers: someone at home can see where you are and sound the alarm if you don't move for a long time or don't arrive. But only share the location with people you trust and turn sharing off again after the tour. You should not post your current location publicly.

## Alerts on your cell phone

In addition to the navigation app, a weather and warning app is worthwhile. The German Weather Service offers official severe weather warnings for your location with the WarnWetter app, and warning apps such as NINA provide information about dangerous situations such as forest fires or storms. Set notifications to alert you to thunderstorms in your area. For people who cannot make phone calls, there is also the federal states' official emergency call app, which can be used to alert the police, fire brigade and rescue services via text.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which app is the best?

That depends on your preferences. More important than the brand are good offline maps, the ability to import GPX files and a display that you can handle well. Try two or three apps on short tours.

### Are the apps’ walking times reliable?

They are a rough guide. Some apps are very optimistic, others cautious. Compare it with your own estimate of kilometers and elevation and with your actual pace.

### Can I walk any path shown in the app?

No. Maps also show trails that are closed, private or prohibited in protected areas. The signage on site and the rules of the respective area are decisive.

### Do I need the paid version of an app?

The free functions of many apps are sufficient for simple tours. Paid versions often offer exactly what counts when you're on the move: offline maps, detailed topographic maps and warnings when you deviate from the route. Try the free version first and then decide which features you are missing.

### How accurate is the GPS in the cell phone?

In the open air, the accuracy is usually a few meters. In narrow valleys, between rock faces and in dense forest, the position can change because signals are reflected or shielded. The altitude from the GPS is less accurate than the position. Devices with barometric altimeters display altitude more precisely, but must be calibrated regularly at known points because air pressure changes with the weather.
