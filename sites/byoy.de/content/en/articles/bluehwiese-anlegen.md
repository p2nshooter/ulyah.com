A flower meadow is one of the most beautiful elements of a natural garden. From May to September, daisies, meadow sage, knapweed, bluebells and horn trefoil bloom, and wild bees, bumblebees, butterflies and beetles hum and buzz between the stalks. At the same time, a meadow is less work than a lawn: instead of mowing it every week, you only mow it once or twice a year. However, a real meadow is not created overnight with a colorful bag of seeds. It needs the right soil, local seeds and a little patience. This guide shows how to do it.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Meadow flowers love poor soil. In nutrient-rich soil, grasses and nettles crowd out the flowers.
> - Use local, regional seeds instead of colorful mixtures with foreign species.
> - Most meadow flowers germinate in the light: just press them down, do not cover them with soil, and keep them moist.
> - In the first year, one or two cupping cuts against fast-growing weeds.
> - Later, mow once or twice a year, leave the cuttings for a few days and then remove them.

## Flower strips or flower meadows?

| | Annual flower strip | Perennial flower meadow |
|---|---|---|
| Plants | Cornflower, corn poppy, marigold, phacelia, buckwheat | Marguerite, meadow sage, knapweed, bellflower, horn trefoil, wild carrot |
| floor | normal garden soil | poor, nutrient-poor soil |
| blossom | lush in the first summer | fully developed from the second year onwards |
| Effort | re-sow every year | Put it on once, then little care |
| Benefits for insects | good, but only one season | very good, also as a place to spend the winter |

An annual flower strip is a quick solution for a vacant bed or the edge of the vegetable garden. A perennial meadow is the more sustainable habitat. You can find ideas for flowers directly in the vegetable patch in [Flowers in the vegetable patch](/gartenwissen/blumen-im-gemuesebeet/).

## Regional seeds: why local origin counts

Many seed bags labeled “bee pasture” or “wildflowers” contain species from other regions or continents, cultivated varieties, and annual ornamentals. They are less valuable to native insects, and some species can spread uncontrollably.

**Regional seeds** come from wild plants that occur naturally in your region and were propagated in the same region. Native wild bees and butterflies are adapted to these plants. For this purpose, Germany is divided into regions of origin. In the open countryside, local seeds are required by law, but in the garden they are the best choice. When buying, look for certified regional seeds with an indication of the region of origin and a high proportion of perennial herbs and flowers.

## Prepare the soil

The most common problem with flower meadows in the garden is soil that is too nutrient-rich. Grasses grow so vigorously on fertilized lawns or garden soil that meadow flowers hardly stand a chance. You have several options:

1. **Remove topsoil:** Cut off the top five to ten centimeters of lawn with roots and use it elsewhere, for example in a raised bed, as described in [Creating a raised bed](/gartenwissen/hochbeet-anlegen/).
2. **Weightening with sand:** Add additional sand or fine gravel to heavy, nutrient-rich soils.
3. **Harvesting for years:** Stop fertilizing an existing lawn and always remove the mowing material. After a few years it becomes thinner and flowers can establish themselves. This is slow, but possible without excavation.
4. **Stripwise:** Instead of the entire area, prepare and sow only strips or islands. From there the flowers spread.

Before sowing, thoroughly remove root weeds such as couch grass, dock and thistle. The surface should be finely crumbled and even. Then leave them for two to three weeks and chop off the germinating weeds before sowing.

## sowing

**Time:** Good dates are spring from April to May or late summer and autumn from the end of August to October. Many meadow flowers are cold germinators that need a winter to germinate. If sown in autumn, they germinate reliably in spring.

1. **Mixing:** Mix the seeds with dry sand or sawdust to spread the tiny seeds evenly. Expect the amount stated on the package to be usually only a few grams per square meter.
2. **Sowing:** Sow half in lengthwise strips and the other half in crosswise strips.
3. **Do not incorporate:** Most meadow flowers germinate in light. Don't cover them with soil.
4. **Press:** Roll the surface or press it with a board so that the seeds have good contact with the ground.
5. **Keep moist:** The surface must not dry out in the first six weeks. In dry weather, water carefully with a fine spray.

## The first year

In the first year, a newly sown meadow often looks disappointing. Fast-growing herbs and grasses germinate first, while the perennial meadow flowers slowly form roots. You can help them with a **cupping cut**: When the area is 20 to 30 centimeters high around six to eight weeks after sowing, you mow down to around 10 centimeters. This is how the small meadow flowers get light. Depending on the growth, a second cupping cut may be necessary. Many meadow flowers only bloom in their second year.

## The right mowing

You mow an established meadow once or twice a year:

- **First Pruning:** Late June to July after most of the early flowers have set seed.
- **Second cut:** in September to prevent the area from becoming matted.

This is how you do it:

1. Mow with a scythe or bar mower, not a lawn mower or mulching mower that shreds insects.
2. Leave the cuttings lying around for two to three days so that seeds fall out and insects can escape.
3. Afterwards, cut off the mown material and put it on the compost heap. If it stays there, it fertilizes the meadow and the grasses take over again.
4. Leave about a tenth of the area for each cut. Insects find refuge and food there, and butterfly caterpillars and pupae can develop further.

A meadow is never fertilized and is only watered shortly after sowing in extremely dry conditions.

## Plants of a typical meadow

- **Marguerite:** large white flowers, very popular with insects
- **Meadow sage:** blue flowers, important for bumblebees and wild bees
- **Meadow knapweed:** violet flowers until autumn
- **Horn trefoil:** yellow flowers, food plant for bluebird caterpillars
- **Wild carrot:** white umbels, food for hoverflies and wild bees
- **Bluebells:** Food for specialized wild bees
- **Small rattle pot:** weakens grasses as semi-parasites and thus helps the flowers

Which wild bees benefit from this and how you can help them can be found in [Promoting wild bees](/gartenwissen/wildbienen-foerdern/).

## Common mistakes in flower meadows

- **Colorful mixes from the supermarket:** They bloom spectacularly in the first year, but often contain foreign annual species and disappear in the second year.
- **Fertilized Soil:** Grasses and nettles grow off the flowers.
- **Leave cuttings lying around:** It fertilizes the area and suffocates small plants.
- **Too frequent mowing:** The flowers do not reach seed maturity.
- **Impatience:** A species-rich meadow develops over three to five years.

## Even small areas count

A flower meadow doesn't have to be large to help insects. Even a strip of a few square meters on the fence, a corner of the lawn or an edge strip along the vegetable garden provides food for wild bees, hoverflies and butterflies. Several small flower islands scattered throughout the garden are often more valuable than a single area because the animals can always find flowers on short routes.

If you only have a small garden, you can simply mow the lawn less often and let it grow in some places. Then daisies, dandelions, clovers, ground worms and speedwell, which were always mowed down beforehand, often bloom in the first summer. This flower lawn variant is not a replacement for a species-rich meadow, but it is an easy start.

## What a meadow does for animals

A flower meadow is much more than food for bees. Many butterflies need certain plants for their caterpillars to feed on, such as grasses, clover or wild carrots. Grasshoppers, beetles and spiders live in the vegetation, and birds find insects there for their young and seeds there in the fall.

An important tip: never mow the entire area at once. Leave a strip or corner each time you mow so animals can get out of the way and insects can survive in hollow stems and seed heads during the winter. These unmown areas change from year to year so that the meadow as a whole stays in shape.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I enter a flower meadow?

A narrow, frequently mown path through the meadow lets you be in the middle of it all without trampling the flowers. There is a patch of grass next to it for lying down and playing.

### What is a flower lawn?

A mix of hardy grasses and low-growing flowers such as daisies, speedwell and thyme, which can be mowed and walked on more frequently. It is a good compromise for family gardens.

### Why does my meadow bloom less in the second year?

Often the soil is too nutrient-rich or the crop has been left lying around. Mow consistently twice and remove the cuttings. Over the years the meadow becomes more species-rich.

### Can I fertilize a flower meadow?

No. Species-rich flower meadows thrive on poor soils. Fertilizer and compost primarily promote strong grasses, which displace the wildflowers after a few years. The cuttings should also be removed after drying so that the soil is not gradually enriched with nutrients.

### Do I need a permit for regional seeds?

Not in your own garden. In the open countryside, however, according to the Federal Nature Conservation Act, wild plants can generally only be planted from local sources. Regional seeds are still the best choice for the home garden because local insects are precisely adapted to these plants.

### Can I create a flower meadow in partial shade?

Yes, with a suitable mix. For semi-shady locations, there are seed mixtures with species that can cope with less sun, such as meadow bluebell, daisy or meadow cranesbill. The meadow usually blooms less luxuriantly there than in full sun, but is still valuable for insects. On the other hand, hardly any meadow thrives under dense trees with deep shade; there, a border of shade-tolerant wild perennials is the better choice.

The same applies here: do not fertilize and clear away the cuttings.
