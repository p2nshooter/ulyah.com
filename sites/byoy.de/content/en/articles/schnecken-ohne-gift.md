Anyone who walks through the garden in the morning in a damp early summer knows the picture: eaten heads of lettuce, bare bean seedlings, traces of slime over the bed. Slugs are the biggest nuisance of all for many vegetable gardeners. You won't get rid of them completely, and that's not necessary. With a combination of habits, barriers and beneficial insects, you can keep the numbers small enough for your vegetables to thrive.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - The main cause is usually the Spanish slug; Tiger snails and snails, on the other hand, are allies.
> - Consistent collection at dusk is most beneficial, especially in spring.
> - Water in the morning instead of in the evening so that the soil surface is dry at night.
> - A well-built snail fence reliably protects particularly vulnerable beds.
> - Beer traps attract snails from the neighborhood and often do more harm than good.

## Know your snails

Not every snail in the garden is a pest. Before you collect, it's worth taking a closer look.

| Art | Identifying features | role in the garden |
|---|---|---|
| Spanish slug | 7 to 12 cm, orange, reddish brown to almost black, without pattern | Main pest, eats almost everything young and soft |
| Netted field snail | 3 to 5 cm, light brown-grey, reticulated markings, milky mucus | Pest, especially on lettuce and seedlings |
| Tiger Snail | 10 to 20 cm, gray with dark spots and stripes | Beneficial insect, eats eggs and young of other snails, carrion and wilt |
| Roman snail | large, cream-brown housing | Protected, eats mainly wilt and snail clutches |
| Ribbon snails | striped, yellow or brown casing | mostly harmless, eat algae and wilt |

You should definitely leave the tiger snail in the garden. It occasionally eats plants, but especially the clutches of other slugs. Roman snails are also not a danger to your vegetables and are protected under the Federal Species Protection Ordinance.

## How slugs live

The Spanish slug is a hermaphrodite and lays eggs in groups of a few dozen in moist ground cavities, under boards or in plant residues in late summer and autumn. An animal can lay several hundred eggs a year. Some of the young snails hatch in the fall and some not until spring. Most adults die in the fall.

This results in two important time windows: In **spring** you collect the young snails, which are still small, before they can reproduce. In **late summer and fall** you look for clutches before the new generation hatches next year. Any animal you remove in April will stop laying eggs in August.

Snails are dependent on moisture. They are active at night and when it rains and hide in cool, damp places during the day. They avoid dry, open areas.

## Collecting: laborious, but most effective

The best time to collect is dusk or early morning after a wet night. You can find most animals after dark with a flashlight. Daily collecting is particularly worthwhile in the first few weeks after the start of the season.

**Hiddens** are a simple help: place boards, damp jute sacks or upturned cabbage leaves between the beds. The snails gather underneath during the day and you can easily pick them up in the morning.

You have to decide for yourself what happens to the animals you collect. Many gardeners kill them quickly with a cut of sharp scissors or with boiling water. Salt, on the other hand, is a painful method and pollutes the soil. Releasing it somewhere else only shifts the problem, and snails sometimes find their way surprisingly far back.

## Watering and soil care

One simple habit makes a big difference: **Water in the morning instead of in the evening.** If you water in the evening, the soil stays moist all night and the snails can migrate undisturbed. Watered in the morning, the surface dries by evening.

Further measures in the bed:

- **Loose soil:** Regular shallow hoeing destroys hiding places and exposes eggs, which then dry out or are eaten by birds.
- **Autumn work:** In September and October, hack through the bed edges and hiding places and specifically look for the pearl-like, translucent clutches.
- **Keep edges short:** Tall grass strips directly next to the vegetable patch are ideal daytime hiding places. A mown strip or a gravel path in between helps.
- **Adjust mulch:** In wet weeks, mulch thinner and only use dried material.

## Barriers and snail fence

A **snail fence** made of galvanized sheet metal or plastic is the most reliable barrier. It is buried about 10 cm deep and protrudes 10 to 15 cm high from the ground. The top edge is angled outwards so that the snail does not come over the edge when crawling up. It is important that no plants hang over the fence and serve as a bridge and that you carefully collect the snails inside the fence. A fence is particularly worthwhile for lettuce, cabbage and growing beds.

Raised beds are not automatically snail-proof, but you can simply attach an angled metal strip to the outer edge.

Other barriers have a weaker effect:

- **Snail collars** made of plastic around individual young plants help in the sensitive initial phase.
- **Sawdust, lime or ash** only work as long as they are dry. They lose their effectiveness after the first rain or dew.
- **Coffee grounds** are supposed to be a deterrent, but in practice the effect is unreliable.

## Beneficial organisms as allies

Many animals eat snails or their eggs: hedgehogs, toads, slowworms, shrews, ground beetles and their larvae, firefly larvae, blackbirds and song thrushes. A garden with piles of leaves, dead wood, piles of stones, a hedge of native bushes and a small pond offers them a habitat.

Runner ducks are a proven help in larger gardens. However, they need to be kept in a species-appropriate manner with a stable, a watering hole and company, at least in pairs, and are not suitable for just fighting snails for a few weeks.

## Plant selection and cultivation

Snails have preferences. Lettuce, bean seedlings, cucumbers and zucchini, young cabbage, strawberries and dahlias are particularly at risk. Less popular are onions, garlic, leeks, older tomato plants, potatoes, chard and most herbs with firm, aromatic leaves. In the experience of many gardeners, red-leaved lettuce varieties are eaten somewhat less.

Plant strong, early seedlings instead of tiny seedlings. The larger and more robust a plant is when planted, the more likely it is to survive damage.

## A snail year at a glance

If you spread the measures out over the year, you will need much less time for snail hunting in the summer.

| period | What to do |
|---|---|
| March to April | Lay out boards, collect young snails every day, set snail fences around breeding beds |
| May to June | Plant strong young plants, water them in the morning and check them with a lamp in the evening |
| July to August | Keep the mulch thin, check hiding places, look for the first clutches |
| September to October | Chop the ground flat, collect the nests under boards and in the mulch, create hedgehog quarters |
| November to February | Leave piles of leaves and brushwood to rest for hedgehogs and toads |

Many gardeners report that after two to three years of consistent work in spring and autumn, the number of slugs has significantly reduced, especially when beneficial insects have become native to the garden.

## Together with the neighbors

Snails don't stick to garden borders. If your garden borders a damp meadow, a stream or an unkempt neighboring garden, new animals are constantly immigrating. A snail fence along the endangered side or a dry, mown strip one meter wide can slow down this influx. In allotment gardens, it is worth organizing measures such as the autumn collection of clutches together.

## Which doesn't help

**Beer traps** catch a lot of snails, but they attract them over long distances, including from neighboring gardens. If the trap is not far enough away from the bed, many animals will eat your vegetables on the way there. Ground beetles also drown in them.

**Snail pellets** with the active ingredient iron III phosphate are permitted in organic farming. If you use it at all, then only sparingly according to the package instructions as a last resort in an emergency. Preparations containing metaldehyde are dangerous for hedgehogs, dogs and other animals and have no place in organic gardens.

## Snails in raised beds and on the balcony

Raised beds and balconies are not automatically snail-free. Snails often get there with purchased young plants, soil or pots, and their eggs survive the transport without any problems. Therefore, check new plants and the edges of pots carefully before they are added to the bed.

On raised beds, a snail edge made of sheet metal that is angled outwards or a copper band at the top edge helps. Keep the walls free of overhanging plants and grass that snails can crawl over like a bridge. On the balcony it is often enough to check the undersides of pots and boxes in the evening.

## Runner ducks: what you should know

Indian Runner Ducks are known to be avid snail hunters, and in larger gardens they can actually significantly reduce the number of slugs. But they are not a quick solution; they are pets with their own needs. They need a safe stable against foxes, water for bathing, company of other animals and daily care, even on vacation.

Young lettuce and vegetable plants are also not always safe from ducks. If you want to keep ducks, you should inform yourself well beforehand and protect the vegetable beds in the first few weeks after planting.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why are there so many snails some years?

A mild winter and a wet spring allow many eggs and young animals to survive. After a dry summer and cold winter, the pressure is usually significantly lower.

### Does copper tape help against snails?

Copper tape on raised beds and pots has some deterrent effect, but this diminishes as the copper oxidizes or becomes dirty. An angled metal fence is more reliable.

### How do I recognize snail eggs?

The eggs are about 3 to 4 mm large, round, glassy-white to yellowish and lie in piles, usually under boards, stones, in mulch or in cavities in the ground.

### Are snails useful?

Roman snails primarily eat wilted parts of plants and are considered enemies of slug eggs. They hardly cause any damage to the garden. They are also under special protection and may neither be collected nor killed. So just let them be.
