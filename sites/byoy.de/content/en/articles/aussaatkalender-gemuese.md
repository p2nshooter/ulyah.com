The right time is half the battle when sowing. Beans sown too early rot in the cold soil, lamb's lettuce sown too late will no longer grow large before winter, and tomatoes that are only grown in April hardly ripen before the first autumn rain. A sowing calendar gives you guidance for the entire gardening year. It is not a rigid set of rules: depending on the region, altitude and weather, the dates are postponed by two to three weeks. If you also pay attention to nature, for example to the blossoms of forsythia, apple and elderberry, you will find the right moment even better.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - **February to March:** Prefer peppers, aubergines, celery and tomatoes indoors, from March onwards the first direct seeds such as peas and spinach.
> - **April:** Sow carrots, radishes, lettuce, beetroot, plant cabbage and lettuce.
> - **Mid-May:** sow beans after the Ice Saints, plant tomatoes, zucchini and cucumbers.
> - **June to August:** Follow-up seeds, autumn and winter vegetables such as kale, endives, lamb's lettuce.
> - **September to October:** Put lamb's lettuce, winter purslane, spinach for the winter, garlic and winter onions.

## How you read the calendar

The information applies to average locations in Germany. In mild wine-growing regions on the Rhine you can often start one to two weeks earlier. In low mountain ranges, in the foothills of the Alps and on the coast with cool springs, it is more likely one to two weeks later. The decisive factor for direct sowing is the soil temperature, as explained in [Direct sowing in the bed](/gartenwissen/direktsaat-im-beet/).

- **Prefer:** Sow in pots or trays indoors, in a greenhouse or in a cold frame.
- **Direct sowing:** Sow directly into the bed.
- **Plants:** Put pre-grown or purchased young plants in the bed.

## February

- **Prior indoors:** Peppers, chili, eggplant and celeriac need a long development time and warmth of 22 to 25 degrees to germinate. The first tomatoes can also follow at the end of February if you have a very bright spot.
- **In the cold frame:** Radishes, lettuce and spinach, if the cold frame is well protected, see [Using the cold frame](/gartenwissen/fruehbeet-nutzen/).
- **Planning:** Create a bed plan and check seeds as described in [Create a bed plan](/gartenwissen/beetplan-erstellen/) and [Store germination sample and seeds](/gartenwissen/keimprobe-und-saatgut-lagern/).

## March

- **Prefer:** Tomatoes from mid-March, kohlrabi, early cabbage, lettuce, leek.
- **Direct sowing** in open, dry soil: peas (shell peas tolerate cold best), spinach, radishes, broad beans, parsnips, early carrots under fleece.
- **Stake:** Onion sets from mid-March, as described in [Stake onions and garlic](/gartenwissen/zwiebeln-und-knoblauch-stecken/).
- **Potatoes:** pre-germinate.

## April

- **Prefer:** Zucchini, pumpkin and cucumbers indoors from mid-April, about three to four weeks before planting out. Basil.
- **Direct sowing:** Carrots, radishes, beetroot, chard, lettuce, rocket, dill, parsley, sugar peas, spinach.
- **Plants:** Hardy kohlrabi, lettuce, early cabbages and onions, with fleece on cold nights.
- **Potatoes:** lay as soon as the soil is around 8 to 10 degrees warm, see [Growing potatoes](/gartenwissen/kartoffeln-anbauen/).

## May

- **Until the Ice Saints:** further seeds of lettuce, radishes and carrots. Beetroot, chard. Harden off young plants.
- **According to the Ice Saints:** Plant bush beans and runner beans, sow zucchini, pumpkin and cucumbers directly or plant them early, sow corn.
- **Plants according to the Ice Saints:** Tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, celery, cucumbers, zucchini, pumpkin, basil. [Hardening off and planting out young plants](/gartenwissen/jungpflanzen-abhaerten-und-auspflanzen/) explains how hardening works.

## June

- **Direct sowing:** French beans as a second set, carrots for storage, beetroot, chard, lettuce and radishes in small sets, kohlrabi.
- **Plants:** Brussels sprouts, kale, winter leeks, fall cabbage.
- **Strawberries:** Prepare cuttings for new beds, see [Planting strawberries](/gartenwissen/erdbeeren-pflanzen/).

## July

- **Direct sowing:** Endive, sugar loaf, Chinese cabbage and pak choi for autumn, last bush beans until the beginning of July, radishes, radishes, autumn carrots, fennel.
- **Plants:** Kale, savoy cabbage and broccoli for fall, endive.
- **After early potatoes and peas:** Sow post-crop or green manure as described in [pre-crop, main crop, post-crop](/gartenwissen/vor-und-nachkultur/).

## Aug

- **Direct sowing:** Lamb's lettuce from mid-August, spinach for autumn and winter, winter purslane, Asian lettuce, radishes, rocket, winter radish until the beginning of August.
- **Green manure:** Phacelia, mustard (not before cabbage) and clover on vacant beds, see [Green manure](/gartenwissen/gruenduengung/).
- **Strawberries:** Plant new plants by the end of August.

## September

- **Direct sowing:** Lamb's lettuce, winter spinach, winter purslane, green manure.
- **Stewing:** Winter onions.
- **In the cold frame:** Winter salads for harvest in late fall and winter.

## October

- **Planting:** Garlic for a strong harvest next summer.
- **Sowing:** Winter green manure from rye and vetch until the beginning of October.
- **Prepare:** Cover beds with compost and leaves, plant fruit trees and berry bushes.

## Nature as a clock: the phenological calendar

Instead of going by date, you can go by the plants in your area. The phenological calendar divides the year into ten seasons, which can be recognized by typical natural events. They reflect the actual weather in your region.

| Natural event | Phenological season | Appropriate gardening |
|---|---|---|
| Hazel is blooming | Early spring | Prefer peppers and celery |
| Forsythia blooms | First spring | Sow peas, spinach, radishes, carrots, plant onions |
| Apple blooms | Full spring | Lay potatoes, plant cabbage and lettuce |
| Elderberry is blooming | Early summer | Sowing beans, heat-loving crops are safe outside |
| Linden blossoms | Midsummer | Plant fall and winter vegetables |

## Plan subsequent seeds in the calendar

Many vegetables only provide good quality for a short time: radishes become furry, lettuce sprouts, bush beans become tough. So that you don't harvest everything at once, plan subsequent seeds in your calendar:

| vegetables | Space between sentences | period |
|---|---|---|
| Radishes | every 2 weeks | March to September, break in midsummer |
| Pick lettuce | every 2 to 3 weeks | March to August |
| Lettuce | every 3 to 4 weeks | March to July |
| French beans | every 3 to 4 weeks | Mid-May to early July |
| carrots | every 4 to 6 weeks | March to June |
| Kohlrabi | every 4 weeks | March to July |

Small quantities in each set are enough: half a row of radishes or six lettuce plants are often enough for two people.

## Climate change in the garden calendar

Many gardeners have observed that spring is beginning earlier in recent decades and summers are becoming hotter and drier. The phenological calendar reflects this: On average, forsythia blooms earlier than before. For the garden, this means: Heat-loving crops can often go out a little earlier, but at the same time late frosts after a mild spring are particularly dangerous because the plants are already well developed. Therefore, always have a fleece ready until mid-May and plan breaks in mid-summer for crops that shoot in the heat, such as lettuce, spinach and radishes.

## Get seeds and young plants in good time

The garden calendar doesn't just start with the first sowing, but with shopping. It's best to order seeds in January or February, as rare varieties and seed-resistant organic varieties are often sold out quickly in spring. Check your supplies beforehand: Older seeds no longer germinate reliably, and a simple germination test will show you in good time whether you need to buy more. The guide [storing germ samples and seeds] (/gartenwissen/keimprobe-und-saatgut-lagern/) explains how this works.

You can find young plants of heat-loving crops such as tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers or pumpkin in organic nurseries and weekly markets from mid-April onwards; the best selection is usually available shortly before the Ice Saints. Seed festivals and swap meets also take place in many cities in the spring, where amateur gardeners exchange seed-resistant varieties. There you can often get regional varieties that are hard to find in stores.

## The calendar for culinary herbs

Herbs also have their dates. Parsley is sown directly from March to July, but germinates slowly and often takes three to four weeks. You sow chives in March or April, but it is easier to divide an existing plant in spring. Dill works best with several small sowings from April to July because it blooms quickly.

Basil needs warmth: grow it on the windowsill from April and only plant it after the Ice Saints. You sow coriander in April and May and again in August and September because it shoots seeds quickly in the summer heat. The easiest way to buy perennial herbs such as thyme, sage and oregano is as young plants and plant them in a sunny, well-drained spot in spring.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does gardening work according to the lunar calendar?

Many gardeners sow according to the lunar calendar and report good experiences. An effect has not yet been scientifically proven. More important for success are soil temperature, moisture and healthy seeds.

### What do I do if I missed an appointment?

Many vegetables can still be sown a few weeks later, with slightly lower yields. Then choose early, fast-growing varieties. When it comes to autumn and winter vegetables, the deadlines are tighter because the days are getting shorter.

### Does the calendar also apply to raised beds?

Yes, raised beds warm up faster in the spring. You can often start there one or two weeks earlier, see [Creating a raised bed](/gartenwissen/hochbeet-anlegen/).

### Can I sow earlier if spring is mild?

The date is less important than the ground temperature. A few warm days in March warm the air quickly but hardly the soil, and in cold, wet soil many seeds rot before they germinate. It is therefore better to sow sensitive crops a week too late rather than too early. If you still want to start early, cover the seed rows with fleece or use a cold frame.
