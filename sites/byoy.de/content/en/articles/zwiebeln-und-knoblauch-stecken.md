Onions and garlic are among the most rewarding crops in the vegetable garden. They require little space, hardly any care, and at the end of the summer there are golden onion braids and white garlic bulbs hanging to dry under the eaves. Home-grown tubers often last until spring and taste more aromatic than commercially grown ones. Anyone who knows a few things about planting time, distance and the typical pests will be rewarded with a reliable harvest.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - You plant onion sets from mid-March to mid-April, winter onions in September and October.
> - Garlic is best planted in October or November. It needs cold to form large, divided tubers.
> - Only stick the bulbs deep enough so that the tip sticks out of the soil. Garlic cloves about five centimeters deep.
> - Alternating rows of carrots and a culture protection net protect against onion and carrot flies.
> - Harvest when two-thirds of the foliage is yellow and falling over. Then let it air dry for one to two weeks.

## Onion sets, seed onions or shallots?

| Art | What you buy | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onion sets | small onions grown the previous year | simple, fast development, less weed competition | smaller selection of varieties, slightly higher risk of bolting |
| Seed onions | seeds | Large selection of varieties, inexpensive, can often be stored for a longer period of time | slower, sow early, more care |
| winter onions | Onion sets for autumn | Harvest in June, the bed is free again early | only has a limited shelf life |
| Shallots | Onions | form several onions from one, fine aroma | more expensive to purchase |

For beginners, onion sets are the easiest way. Heat-treated onion sets bloom less often. When buying, look for firm, dry onions with no mold or shoots and about one to two centimeters in diameter. Larger onion sets are more likely to sprout.

## Location and soil

Onions and garlic like sun and loose, well-drained soil. Waterlogging causes the tubers to rot. They are medium feeders and do well with a light addition of compost from the previous year. Fresh manure or a lot of nitrogen make them soft, fragile and difficult to store. Don't grow onions and garlic in the same place every year: a three to four year break prevents disease. [Planning crop rotation](/gartenwissen/fruchtfolge-planen/) explains how this works.

## Stick onions

1. Loosen the soil flat and make grooves 20 to 25 centimeters apart.
2. Place the onion sets in the groove with the tips facing up, eight to ten centimeters apart.
3. Only press it deep enough into the soil that the tip just barely sticks out. Onions that are planted too deep form long necks and store more poorly.
4. Press the soil lightly.

Blackbirds like to pull out freshly planted onions because they look for worms underneath them. A fleece in the first two weeks prevents this until the bulbs are rooted.

You plant **winter onions** in the same way, only in September or October. They overwinter with small green leaves and continue to grow quickly in spring. The harvest begins in June.

## Stick garlic

Garlic needs a cold period for the bulb to split into cloves. That's why autumn is the best date.

1. Divide a healthy, large tuber into individual cloves just before sticking. Take the outer big toes. Small cloves from the center make small bulbs.
2. Insert the toes, point up, about two inches deep, 5 to 6 inches apart, in rows 10 to 12 inches apart.
3. Cover the bed with a thin layer of leaves.

Garlic planted in spring is possible, but often forms smaller bulbs or just a single, undivided clove. Buy garlic from nurseries or seed distributors. Garlic from the supermarket often comes from other climate zones and is not always suitable for German winters.

## Care

- **Weeding:** Onions and garlic have narrow leaves and hardly cast any shade. Weeds therefore grow quickly and compete strongly. Hoe shallowly between rows regularly without damaging the tubers.
- **Watering:** only during prolonged drought in spring and early summer while the tubers are growing. Stop watering from July when the tubers ripen. Dry onions last longer.
- **Flower stems:** When an onion blooms, you snap off the stem and use this onion first. It stores poorly. With garlic, the flower stalks, the so-called garlic snakes, are broken off in June. When fried in a pan, they taste mildly garlicky.

## Onion fly and other pests

The **onion fly** lays its eggs on young onions. The maggots eat into the onion, which then wilts and rots. Prevention:

- **Mixed culture with carrots:** Alternating rows of onions and carrots are a classic. The scent of one plant is said to confuse the pests of another. More about this in [Mixed culture: good and bad neighbors](/gartenwissen/mischkultur-gute-und-schlechte-nachbarn/).
- **Culture protection net:** A fine-mesh net over the bed from the cutting onwards reliably keeps onion and leek flies away.
- **Crop rotation:** Do not grow on areas where there were onions or leeks the previous year.

The **leek leaf miner fly** primarily attacks leeks, but also onions. A net also helps here during the spring and late summer flight periods.

**Downy mildew** appears as a gray-purple coating on the leaves in damp weather. Wide spacing, permeable soil and crop rotation prevent this. Remove affected leaves. You can find out more about this in [Preventing fungal diseases](/gartenwissen/pilzkrankheiten-vorbeugen/).

## Harvest and dry

Onions are ripe when about two-thirds of the foliage has turned yellow and fallen over, usually in August. Bending over the leaves with a rake is not necessary and can promote rot.

1. Pull the bulbs out of the ground on a dry day or loosen them with a digging fork.
2. Leave them on the bed for a few days in sunny weather. If it rains, take them under a roof.
3. Then dry them for one to two weeks in an airy place protected from rain, for example on a rack or hanging.
4. When the shells rustle and the neck is completely dry, cut off the leaves or make braids from the dry leaves.

You harvest garlic in July when the lower leaves turn yellow but there are still five to six green leaves left. If you wait too long, the tubers will break down into individual cloves. Dry it like onions.

## Store

Store onions and garlic in a cool, dry, dark and airy place, in nets, baskets or in braids. Moisture causes them to germinate and mold. Well-dried storage onions last until spring, and garlic often lasts just as long. Check the supply regularly and use soft or sprouting tubers first.

## Shallots and spring onions

You plant **shallots** like onion sets from March to April, with a little more spacing, about 15 to 20 centimeters in the row. A nest of five to ten new shallots grows from each onion. They ripen in July and store very well. Their aroma is finer and milder than that of onions, ideal for sauces and salad dressings.

You sow **spring onions** directly into the bed from March to August, in dense rows. They are harvested while green and tender, about eight to ten weeks after sowing. With subsequent sowing every three to four weeks, you will have a fresh harvest from early summer to autumn. Winter-hardy varieties, sown in August, produce the first spring onions in early spring.

## Winter onions and winter hedge onions

In addition to the classic spring onion sets, there are winter onion sets that are set in September or October. They root before winter, survive frost well and produce an early harvest in early summer the following year when storage bulbs from the previous year run low. However, they do not last as long as late-harvested summer onions.

The winter hedge onion is a perennial onion that does not form large tubers but is used like chives. Its tubular leaves emerge in early spring as one of the first fresh greens in the garden and can be harvested until autumn. A nest can stay in the same place for many years.

## Growing onions from seeds

Seeding onions require more patience than onion sets, but they have advantages: they sprout less often, there is a larger selection of varieties, and they can often be stored for a particularly long time. It is sown directly into the bed in March or April, in rows about 20 to 25 centimeters apart. Later, separate the plants a few centimeters apart so that large bulbs can form.

Carefully keep the bed free of weeds in the first few weeks, as the thin onion seedlings are unlikely to prevail against competition. You can use the little plants you pull out when you separate them like spring onions in the kitchen.

## Garlic with and without flower stalks

There are two large groups of garlic. Varieties with hard flower stalks form a long stem with bulblets in early summer, are considered particularly hardy and often have a strong aroma. Varieties without solid flower stalks, on the other hand, can usually be stored for longer and are well suited for braiding.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I stick sprouted onions from the kitchen?

Yes, in spring. However, they usually do not form a new large bulb, but rather leaves and flowers. You can harvest the green tubes like spring onions.

### Why did my onions stay small?

Common causes are spacing that is too narrow, too many weeds, dryness in spring or a place that is too shady. Cutting too late also shortens the growing time.

### Can I put onions and garlic next to beans?

Better not. Onion plants and legumes are considered bad neighbors because they can inhibit each other's growth.

### Should I cut off the flower stalks of garlic?

Yes, for varieties that form a flower stalk. Cut it off as soon as it curls up so that the plant puts its energy into the tuber. The young, tender stems are edible and taste mildly of garlic, for example in stir-fries or as pesto.
