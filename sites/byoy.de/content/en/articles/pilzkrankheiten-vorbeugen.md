White coating on zucchini leaves, gray fur on strawberries, black spots on apples: fungal diseases are part of everyday gardening. In the organic garden, however, there is no miracle cure in a bottle, and that's a good thing. Most fungal diseases can be suppressed with simple measures to such an extent that they hardly affect the harvest. The key is understanding: If you know when and how a pathogen attacks, you can deprive it of the conditions it needs.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Most mushrooms need wet leaves. Water in the morning and only on the ground.
> - Airy conditions and sunny places dry plants quickly after rain.
> - Resistant varieties are the most effective prevention, especially for apples, tomatoes and cucumbers.
> - Remove affected plant parts early and do not add them to the compost.
> - Powdery mildew is an exception: it loves dry, warm weather and stressed plants.

## How fungal diseases arise

Fungal spores are practically everywhere. They overwinter on plant residues, in the soil, on bark or in buds and are spread by wind, rain spray or insects. Whether an infection occurs almost always depends on three factors: a susceptible plant, an existing pathogen and the appropriate conditions, usually humidity and temperature. You can influence this third factor the most in the garden.

Many pathogens need a film of water on the leaf that lasts for several hours. If leaves dry quickly after a rain, they hardly stand a chance. This results in the most important rules of prevention.

## The most common fungal diseases at a glance

| disease | Typical hosts | Identification mark | Favored by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Powdery mildew | Zucchini, pumpkin, cucumber, pea, apple, gooseberry | white, wipeable coating on the top of the leaf | dry, warm weather, drought stress, excess nitrogen |
| Downy mildew | Lettuce, onion, cucumber, spinach, vine | yellow spots above, gray-purple lawn below | long leaf wetness, cool nights, dew |
| Gray mold | Strawberry, raspberry, lettuce, bean | gray, dusty fungal turf on fruits and leaves | Wet, dense conditions |
| Late blight and brown blight | Tomato, potato | brown spots on leaves, stems and fruits | Rain, wet leaves, warm, humid days |
| Apple scab | Apple, pear (own pathogen) | olive brown spots on leaves, cracked scabs on fruits | wet spring, fallen leaves |
| Monilia | Cherry, plum, apple | Fruit rot with spore rings, wilted shoot tips after flowering | wet flowering period, fruit mummies |
| Frizz disease | Peach, nectarine | curled, reddish thickened leaves in spring | wet weather during bud break |
| Rust fungi | Pear, leek, bean, currant | orange to brown pustules | Moisture, intermediate hosts |

## The most important preventive measures

### The right location

Plants that are in the sun and blown around by the wind dry out quickly after rain and dew. A shady, windless spot behind a thick hedge, on the other hand, is a paradise for mushrooms. Plant particularly susceptible crops such as tomatoes, strawberries and cucumbers in the sunniest, airiest spots.

### Keep your distance

Plants that are planted too densely create a damp microclimate. Follow the recommended planting distances, even if the young plants still look tiny in May. Thin out dense stands, remove the lower leaves of tomatoes and cut berry bushes so that air and light can reach the interior.

### Water correctly

Water in the morning so the plants dry out before evening falls. Water directly onto the soil at the base of the plant, not over the leaves. Drip hoses or watering cans without a showerhead are better than lawn sprinklers. A layer of mulch also prevents soil with spores from splashing onto the leaves.

### Fertilize balanced

Too much nitrogen causes plants to grow soft and mushy. Your tissue is then more susceptible, especially to powdery mildew and aphids. Fertilize with compost and organic fertilizers as needed, not according to the motto “a lot helps a lot”.

### Adhere to crop rotation

Many soil-borne pathogens, such as clubroot in cruciferous plants or Fusarium wilt, accumulate when the same crop is left in the same place for years. A crop rotation with several years of cultivation breaks for the same plant family prevents this.

### Garden hygiene

- Remove infected leaves and fruits early and dispose of them in household waste or in the organic waste bin.
- Remove fruit mummies from cherry, plum and apple trees during winter pruning. Monilia overwinters in them.
- Rake up fallen leaves under scab-prone apple trees in the fall, or shred them with a lawnmower so that earthworms can quickly ingest them. The scab fungus overwinters on the leaves.
- Clean plant stakes, pruning tools and growing pots regularly.

## Hardy varieties

The choice of variety is often more important than any care measure. Some examples:

- **Apples:** Scab-resistant or less susceptible varieties such as 'Topaz', 'Santana', 'Rebella' or 'Florina' will save you a lot of trouble.
- **Tomatoes:** Varieties with tolerance to late blight and brown rot last significantly longer.
- **Cucumbers and Zucchini:** Many modern varieties are powdery mildew tolerant.
- **Peach:** Varieties such as 'Benedicte' are considered to be less susceptible to curl disease.
- **Lettuce:** There are varieties with resistance to common races of downy mildew.

When buying seeds and young plants, pay attention to information about resistance or tolerances.

## Strengthening plants with broths and manures

In organic gardening, plant extracts are traditionally used to make plants more resilient. Their effects are not always clearly proven in studies, but many gardeners appreciate them.

- **Horsetail broth:** Field horsetail contains a lot of silica. Soak 1 kg of fresh or 150 g of dried herb in 10 liters of water, simmer for half an hour, strain and spray diluted 1 to 5 on the leaves as a preventive measure, preferably on sunny mornings.
- **Milk for powdery mildew:** Diluted milk, about 1 part to 8 to 10 parts water, is often recommended against powdery mildew on zucchini and cucumbers and can slow the infestation.
- **Garlic or onion tea:** Is traditionally used against fungal diseases on strawberries, the effect is rather weak.

Although products containing copper or sulfur are sometimes permitted in organic farming, copper accumulates in the soil and harms earthworms. In the home garden, we recommend avoiding it and focusing on prevention and variety selection.

## Orchard: scab, monilia and curl disease throughout the year

In the orchard, it is worth spreading prevention throughout the year, as many pathogens infect at a very specific time.

**Apple scab** overwinters on the fallen leaves. In spring, from around the time of budding to the beginning of June, the fungus throws spores onto the young leaves every time it rains. In this phase it is decided how severely a tree will be affected. An airy crown structure that dries quickly after rain is therefore the best option. In winter, cut out branches that grow inwards and cross each other so that the crown is light. Infected fruits are unsightly and less storable, but are edible.

**Monilia** shows itself in two ways. In peak drought, the fungus penetrates the flowers during flowering, especially in sour cherries such as 'Morelle' and apricots. The shoot tips suddenly wilt shortly after flowering. Cut it immediately back into the healthy wood, about 15 to 20 cm below the wilted area. In fruit rot, the fungus penetrates ripening fruit through small injuries, such as wasp or hail damage. Constantly collect rotten fruit and remove any fruit mummies that are still hanging on the tree in winter.

**Purple disease** infects peaches in a very short time window: when the buds swell in late winter, often as early as February. If you see the leaves curled in April, it is too late to take action. A rain cover over small peach trees on a house wall, from January until the leaves have fully sprouted, reliably prevents infection because the spores need moisture. Affected leaves fall off and the tree then sprouts again. It is then crucial to provide it with good water and compost so that it recovers.

| month | Measure in the orchard |
|---|---|
| January to February | Remove fruit mummies, thin out the crown, protect the peach from rain |
| March to May | Immediately cut back peak drought on cherries and mulch the tree disc |
| June to August | Collect rotten fruits, summer pruning for airy crowns |
| October to November | Shred or remove fallen leaves from under apple trees |

## If it did happen

If you discover a fungal disease, act quickly but without panic:

1. Determine the disease as accurately as possible so that you know whether it is powdery mildew or downy mildew, because the measures differ.
2. Remove infected plant parts immediately.
3. Check watering habits, plant spacing and fertilization.
4. Make note of the variety, location and weather so you can plan differently next year.

## Correctly identify fungal diseases

Before you act, it's worth taking a closer look, because the diseases require different measures. Powdery mildew forms a white, wipeable coating, especially on the upper side of the leaves, and tends to appear in warm, dry weather. Downy mildew, on the other hand, appears as grayish-purple fuzz on the underside of the leaves and yellowish spots on the top; he loves damp, cool weather.

You can recognize rust fungi by their orange to brown pustules, usually on the underside of the leaves. Gray mold forms a gray, dusty coat on fruits and flowers. Photograph affected leaves from both sides. Nurseries and the state plant protection services help with the identification.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I compost leaves affected by powdery mildew?

You can add powdery mildew in small quantities to a well-working compost. If you have brown rot, monilia or soil-borne diseases such as clubroot, the organic bin is better.

### Why do my plants get powdery mildew despite the sun?

Because powdery mildew loves dry, warm weather. It primarily attacks plants that suffer from a lack of water. Even watering and mulching helps.

### Is mildew dangerous for people?

No. You can safely eat fruit from infected plants as long as they look healthy.

### Does milk help against mildew?

Diluted milk is an old home remedy for powdery mildew, and experience with cucumbers, zucchini and pumpkin suggests a certain preventative effect. It is not a substitute for good location choice and robust varieties, but it can help if used regularly. It does not work against downy mildew.

### Should I remove infected leaves in autumn?

In the case of fruit trees with scab or roses with star sooty mold, it makes sense to remove the affected leaves in autumn because the pathogens overwinter on them. Healthy leaves, on the other hand, can be left as mulch.
