Carrots are one of the most popular vegetables in the home garden, and when freshly pulled from the ground they taste sweeter and more aromatic than almost any carrot from the supermarket. At the same time, they are considered somewhat capricious: the seeds germinate slowly, the seedlings are tiny, and the carrot fly can spoil an entire harvest. Anyone who knows the requirements of carrots will hardly have any problems.

This guide explains step by step how to successfully grow carrots: from the right soil, to sowing and separating, to protecting them from pests, to harvesting and storing them well into the winter.

## What carrots need

Carrots are medium-eaters and belong to the umbelliferous family, like parsnips, parsley, celery, dill and fennel. They love loose, deep soil with little stone in which the beet can grow downwards unhindered. In heavy, compacted or stony soil, carrots become crooked, leggy or branch.

Fresh manure or large amounts of unripe compost immediately before sowing are unfavorable. They promote branchy, hairy beets and attract the carrot fly. A bed that had a well-fertilized heavy feeder, such as cabbage or pumpkin, is ideal the previous year. A little mature compost in autumn or early spring is sufficient.

The location should be sunny to partially shaded. Because carrots are an umbelliferous family, they should not be placed directly after celery, parsnips or parsley. More about this in the guide [Planning crop rotation] (/gartenwissen/fruchtfolge-planen/).

## Prepare the soil

In early spring, loosen the bed deeply with a digging fork without turning the soil and remove large stones and root weeds. Then rake the surface finely to create a crumbly seedbed. Mounds have proven effective on heavy soils: mound the earth into ridges about 15 centimeters high and sow the carrots on top. In the loose, more quickly warmed soil of the dams, the beets become straighter and longer.

If you have very heavy clay soil, you can also grow carrots in raised beds or choose short, round varieties that require less depth.

## The right variety

Carrots come in many shapes and colors, and your choice of variety determines when and how long you can harvest.

- **Early varieties:** They are sown from March or April, are ready to harvest after a good two to three months and are suitable for fresh consumption. They can only be stored for a short time.
- **Medium-early varieties:** Sow in spring to early summer, harvest in summer and early fall.
- **Late varieties and storage carrots:** Sow until around June, harvest in October and November. They are more robust, often larger and last in storage until next spring.
- **Round and short varieties:** good for heavy soils, boxes and containers.
- **Colored varieties:** In addition to the orange ones, there are yellow, white and purple carrots. The purple ones are not a modern invention: carrots were originally purple or yellow, the orange varieties only emerged later through breeding.

Seed-resistant varieties allow you to grow your own seeds, although they only appear in the second year because carrots are biennial. More about this in the guide [Seed Festival or F1](/gartenwissen/samenfest-oder-f1/).

## Sowing without frustration

Carrots germinate slowly, after two to three weeks or longer depending on the soil temperature. The surface must not dry out during this time, otherwise the seedlings will die before they become visible. This is the most common reason for failure.

1. **Choose time:** Early varieties from March or April, as soon as the soil has dried and warmed up slightly. Store carrots until around June.
2. **Draw grooves:** about one to two centimeters deep, with 20 to 30 centimeters between rows.
3. **Sow thinly:** The seeds are fine. Mix them with some dry sand and they will spread more evenly. Alternatively, seed tapes make the work easier.
4. **Marking seeds:** Sow a few radishes in the row. They germinate after a few days, show you where the carrot row runs, and are harvested before the carrots need the space.
5. **Press down and keep moist:** Press the soil lightly and water with a fine spray. A board or fleece on the row keeps the moisture until the first seedlings appear. Check daily and remove the cover immediately if anything appears.

The guide [Direct sowing in the bed](/gartenwissen/direktsaat-im-beet/) describes the technology in more detail.

## Separating: space for beautiful beets

If the carrots have sprouted too tightly, they will remain thin and crooked. Therefore, pull out excess plants as soon as they are a few centimeters high, leaving about three to five centimeters of space between the remaining carrots. You can add the pulled plants to salads.

It is best to separate on a calm evening and then water. The smell of freshly pulled carrots attracts the carrot fly. If you then press the soil down, water it and don't leave the plant residue on the bed, you reduce this risk.

## Watering, hoeing and mulching

After germination, carrots are surprisingly undemanding. Above all, they need consistent moisture. Long dry periods followed by heavy rain cause the beets to burst. It is therefore better to water less frequently and thoroughly in dry times.

Keep the rows carefully free of weeds in the first few weeks, as young carrots are unlikely to assert themselves against competition. Later, a thin layer of mulch made from grass clippings between the rows helps to retain moisture. Lightly mound the carrots when their shoulders protrude from the soil. Otherwise the upper parts will turn green and taste bitter.

## The carrot fly

The carrot fly is the most important pest of carrots. Their larvae eat holes in the beets, which turn brownish, become putrid and are hardly edible. An infestation can often be recognized above ground by reddish, wilting leaves.

Prevention is particularly helpful against carrot flies:

- **Culture protection net:** A fine-mesh net over the carrots, placed directly after sowing and well sealed all around, is the most reliable method. The flies then cannot reach the plants.
- **Airy location:** The carrot fly flies low and avoids windy, open areas.
- **Sowing date:** Very early and late sowing often avoids the peak flight times of flies.
- **Crop rotation:** Grow carrots in a different spot every year, as far away as possible from the previous year's bed.
- **Mixed culture:** The classic combination with onions is intended to confuse the flies with the smell. The effect is not equally strong in every garden, but it doesn't do any harm. More about this in the guide [Mixed Culture](/gartenwissen/mischkultur-gute-und-schlechte-nachbarn/).

## harvest

You can harvest early carrots as soon as they have reached the desired size. Pull them out carefully on dry days; if the soil is heavy, loosen the row with a digging fork beforehand. This prevents the beets from breaking off.

Storage carrots remain in the bed until October or November. They tolerate light frosts well, but they should be harvested before severe frosts. Instead of cutting off the foliage, twist it right after harvesting, leaving a short stump. Otherwise the leaves would deprive the beets of water.

## Store until spring

Only undamaged, healthy carrots are suitable for storage. The classic method is to wrap them in moist sand: Layer the unwashed carrots in a box, alternating with slightly moist sand, and store the box in a cool, frost-free room such as a basement or garage. This means they often stay crisp until spring.

Without a cellar, you can also store carrots in the garden in a pile of soil lined with straw or keep small quantities in the vegetable compartment of the refrigerator, wrapped in a damp cloth or in a perforated bag. Check supplies regularly and remove rotting carrots immediately.

## Carrots in buckets and raised beds

Even if you don't have a vegetable garden, you don't have to go without your own carrots. Short and medium-length varieties thrive in a pot or planting bag with at least 30 centimeters of soil depth. Use loose soil without stones, sow thinly and keep the substrate evenly moist. The soil in the raised bed is loose anyway, and the carrot fly has a harder time there because it flies low.

## Practical example: The carrots that never sprouted

A reader sowed carrots in April two years in a row and only saw individual seedlings. The seeds were fresh and the grooves were neatly drawn. The reason was the surface: the clayey soil crusted over after every rain, and on sunny days the top layer dried out within a few hours.

In the third year he sowed on a slightly raised ridge, covered the grooves with fine, mature compost instead of garden soil and, after watering, placed a board on each row. After almost three weeks, the carrots were tightly lined up. Using a marking seed made from radishes and a culture protection net against the carrot fly, he harvested so many carrots in the fall that he stored them in sandboxes.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why are my carrots leggy or branchy?

The soil is usually too compacted or too stony, or fresh manure or unripe compost was added shortly before sowing. Carrots also branch out when transplanted, which is why they are always sown directly.

### Can I prefer carrots?

No, carrots tolerate transplanting poorly. The taproot is injured and the beets grow stunted. Always sow them directly into the bed or bucket.

### Why did my carrots split?

Usually after a long dry period, which was followed by heavy rain or abundant watering. The beet then suddenly absorbs a lot of water and tears. Even watering prevents this. Carrots that have been left in the ground for too long are also more likely to burst.

### Can I eat the carrot greens?

Yes, young carrot greens are edible and taste spicy, for example in pesto, soups or salads. Only use fresh, clean leaves from carrots that have not been treated with pesticides.

## Conclusion

Carrots need loose, rock-poor soil without fresh fertilizer, careful sowing with consistent moisture until germination and timely separation. A culture protection net reliably protects against the carrot fly, and with storage varieties and a sandbox in the cellar, the harvest lasts until spring. If you follow these basic rules, you will harvest carrots that taste like more.
