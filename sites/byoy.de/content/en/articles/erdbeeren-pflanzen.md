If you want to harvest your own strawberries next June, you need to take action now. This surprises many people, because strawberry plants are available in every garden center in spring. But most varieties produce their flowers for the coming year in late summer and autumn. Plants planted in July or August have enough time to grow vigorously and form many flower buds. Strawberries planted in spring usually only produce a little in the first year.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Best planting time for single-bearing varieties: mid-July to the end of August.
> - Spacing: 25 to 30 cm in row, 50 to 60 cm between rows.
> - The heart of the plant must remain above the ground otherwise it will rot.
> - A strawberry bed lasts well for three to four years, then it is replanted elsewhere.
> - Straw under the fruits keeps them clean and prevents gray mold.

## Single-bearing, multiple-bearing or monthly strawberry?

Before you buy plants or take cuttings, it's worth taking a look at the variety groups:

| group | Harvest time | Properties | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-bearing garden strawberries | June to early July, three to four weeks depending on the variety | highest yield, best fruits, many runners | 'Senga Sengana', 'Korona', 'Polka', 'Malvina' |
| Multi-bearing (remontant) varieties | June to October in several spurts | smaller harvests over a long period of time | 'Ostara', 'Mara des Bois' |
| monthly strawberries | June to October | small, aromatic fruits, usually without runners, can be grown from seeds | 'Rügen', 'Alexandria' |

A special feature is the old variety **'Mieze Schindler'** with small, dark red and exceptionally aromatic fruits. It only produces functionally female flowers and therefore needs another variety nearby to provide pollen, for example 'Senga Sengana' or 'Korona'.

For gardens with little time, a combination of an early, a mid-early and a late single-bearing variety is recommended. The harvest is spread over five to six weeks.

## The right location

Strawberries need sun, at least six hours a day, otherwise the fruit will remain sour and susceptible to gray mold. The soil should be humus, loose and slightly acidic to neutral; a pH value between around 5.5 and 6.5 is ideal. They cannot tolerate waterlogging. On heavy clay soils, it helps to plant the plants on slightly raised ridges.

The history of the bed is important: Do not plant strawberries where there have been strawberries in the last four to five years. Strawberries are incompatible with themselves and root diseases such as verticillium wilt accumulate in the soil. Caution is also advised after potatoes or tomatoes because these can also promote verticillium pathogens. Good pre-crops are peas, bush beans, early potatoes with sufficient spacing or lettuce.

## Prepare the bed

Strawberries have shallow but dense roots. Prepare the bed two to three weeks before planting:

1. Thoroughly remove root weeds such as couch grass, groundweed and morning glories. You can hardly control them in an existing strawberry bed.
2. Loosen the soil deeply with the digging fork.
3. Work in 3 to 4 liters of mature compost per square meter. Fresh manure or large amounts of nitrogen-rich fertilizer encourage soft leaves and gray mold.
4. Allow the soil to settle and water thoroughly before planting.

## Planting step by step

1. Before planting, place the pot balls in water until no more air bubbles appear.
2. Dig planting holes 25 to 30 cm apart, with rows 50 to 60 cm apart. A wide spacing makes harvesting easier and allows the leaves to dry more quickly.
3. Position the plant so that the heart, the center from which new leaves grow, is exactly level with the soil surface. If placed too low it will rot, if placed too high the roots will dry out.
4. Press the soil firmly and water vigorously.
5. Keep the bed evenly moist in the following weeks. In hot August this often means watering every two to three days.

Before planting, cut bare-rooted plants back to a root length of around 10 cm and fan out the roots in the planting hole.

## Win offshoots yourself

Strawberries reproduce via runners, at the end of which daughter plants grow. Here's how you can get new plants from an existing bed for free:

1. During harvest, mark the healthiest and highest-yielding mother plants.
2. In June or July, choose only the first daughter plant from each runner, i.e. the one closest to the mother. She is the strongest.
3. Dig a small pot of soil next to the mother plant and place the daughter plant on top. Secure the tail with a wire hanger.
4. After three to four weeks it will have formed its own roots. Then separate the runner and plant the offshoot in the new bed.

Only take cuttings from healthy plants. Otherwise, viral diseases and strawberry mites will multiply. If your stocks are dwindling, it's time for certified healthy plants from specialist retailers.

## Care throughout the year

**Fall after planting:** Remove runners that weaken young plants. Keep the bed free of weeds.

**Spring:** Remove dried and diseased leaves in March. Loosen the soil shallowly and add some compost between the rows.

**Flowering:** If there is a late frost, a fleece protects the open flowers overnight. You can recognize frozen flowers by their black center.

**From fruit setting:** Place straw under the plants so that the fruits do not lie on the damp soil. Water in the morning and directly to the ground.

**After harvesting:** Remove any suckers you don't need for propagation. Many gardeners cut old foliage about 2 to 4 inches above the heart in late July or August to remove pathogens. Be careful not to hurt the heart. The plants then sprout fresh. A small dose of compost or organic berry fertilizer after the harvest supports the flower production for the next year.

## Gray mold and other problems

| problem | Identification mark | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Gray mold | gray, dusty mushroom turf on fruits | Plant in an airy place, place straw underneath, do not water over leaves, remove rotten fruits immediately |
| snails | eaten, hollowed out fruits | collect, snail fence, straw instead of soft mulch |
| Birds | picked fruits | Net over a simple frame without it resting |
| Strawberry mite | curled, small heart-shaped leaves, stunted growth | healthy planting material, remove infected plants |
| Leaf spot diseases | red, purple or brown spots | Remove old leaves and lay the bed in good time |

Garlic, onions or leeks between the rows of strawberries are traditionally considered good neighbors. Borage on the edge of the bed attracts bumblebees, which pollinate the strawberry flowers and thus ensure evenly shaped fruits.

## Harvest

Strawberries do not ripen after picking. Harvest them when they are fully colored, preferably in the morning when they are dry but still cool. Pick them with the green calyx so they last longer. Wash them just before eating.

## The strawberry patch after the harvest

For single-bearing varieties, the next harvest year begins immediately after the harvest. In late summer, the plants lay their flower buds for the coming year, and for this they need strength and healthy leaves. After harvesting, remove the straw, cut off old, spotted leaves and any suckers you don't need for propagation, and be careful not to damage the heart of the plant.

Then carefully loosen the soil between the rows and add a thin layer of mature compost or an organic berry fertilizer. Continue watering during dry late summer weeks, as drought stress during this time means fewer blooms next spring. On the other hand, you can continue to cultivate varieties that bear more than once into the fall, just as you would during the harvest.

## Strawberries and crop rotation

Strawberries shouldn't sit in the same place for years. After three to four years, the yield decreases and pests accumulate in the soil. Therefore, create a new bed in a different location and wait about four years before planting again in the old location.

Unfavorable previous crops are potatoes and tomatoes because they can transmit the same wilt disease that also affects strawberries. Areas that previously had green manure, onions or lettuce on them are well suited. More about this in the guide [Planning crop rotation] (/gartenwissen/fruchtfolge-planen/).

## Strawberries in raised beds

In the raised bed, the soil warms up more quickly in spring, the fruit lies more neatly, and snails have a further route. It is best to plant strawberries on the edge so that the fruit can hang over the edge, and water regularly, as raised beds dry out more quickly than ground-level beds. The guide [Creating a raised bed] (/gartenwissen/hochbeet-anlegen/) explains how to build a raised bed.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I plant strawberries in spring?

Yes, especially multi-bearing varieties and so-called frigo plants that have been stored in a cool place and bear quickly. However, with single-bearing varieties, the harvest in the first year after spring planting is low.

### How long does a strawberry patch last?

The yield is usually highest in the second year, still good in the third, after which it declines and diseases increase. Therefore, create a new bed in a different location every three to four years.

### Why are my strawberries small and misshapen?

This is often due to incomplete pollination, for example after cold days during flowering, or to late frost that has damaged some of the flower organs. Lack of water during fruit formation also makes the fruits small.

### Do strawberries need a layer of straw?

Not necessarily, but it helps. Straw keeps the fruit clean and dry and prevents gray mold. Don't put it out until the first green fruits appear. Applied too early, it keeps the soil cold and delays ripening.

### Can I plant strawberries among other vegetables?

Yes, strawberries go well with garlic, onions, chives, lettuce and spinach. According to the experience of many gardeners, garlic between the rows of strawberries is said to reduce fungal diseases. Cabbage and strong-growing neighbors that deprive the strawberries of light are unfavorable.
