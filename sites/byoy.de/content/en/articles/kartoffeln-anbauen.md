Hardly any other vegetable produces as much yield in such a small area as the potato, and hardly any other harvest is as much fun: anyone who drives a digging fork under a perennial in late summer and lifts the yellow, red or blue tubers out of the ground feels like they are on a treasure hunt. Homegrown potatoes taste different than store-bought ones, especially if you choose varieties that are rarely available in stores. Cultivation is easy, but requires some planning: the right planting material, patience when laying, regular hilling and a watchful eye for late blight.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Use certified seedlings, not table potatoes from the supermarket. They can transmit diseases and are often germ-inhibited.
> - Pre-germination from the beginning of March in a bright, cool place gives a two to three week head start.
> - Planting takes place when the soil is around 8 to 10 degrees warm, mid-April to early May, depending on the region.
> - Spacing: 30 to 40 centimeters in the row, 60 to 70 centimeters between rows. Hill up several times.
> - Do not grow potatoes after tomatoes or next to tomatoes. Both share late blight and brown rot.

## Planting material and varieties

Buy **seed potatoes** from garden shops, Raiffeisen markets or from seed retailers. Certified planting material is tested for viral diseases and pathogens. Stored potatoes are not intended for this: they are often treated with germination inhibitors and can introduce diseases into your soil that you won't be able to get rid of for years.

Varieties differ according to ripening time and type of cooking:

| Maturity group | Harvest time | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| Very early | from June | New potatoes with thin skin, cannot be stored |
| Early | July | good for new potatoes, often escape late blight |
| Mid-early | August to early September | All-rounder, partially storable |
| Mid-late to late | September | best storage potatoes, longer susceptible to disease |

**Early varieties** are particularly interesting for small gardens. They are harvested before late blight strikes in the wet mid-summer, and the bed is freed up for subsequent crops such as lamb's lettuce, spinach or winter salads. [Pre-culture, main culture, post-culture](/gartenwissen/vor-und-nachkultur/) explains how you use a bed several times a year.

The following applies to the cooking type: waxy varieties for salad and fried potatoes, predominantly waxy varieties as all-rounders, floury varieties for puree and dumplings. Many old and colorful varieties are only available as seedlings from specialized conservation companies.

## Pre-germination

Pre-germinated potatoes grow faster, can be harvested earlier and are therefore protected from diseases for longer.

1. Starting in early March, place the seed potatoes in shallow boxes or egg cartons with the “navel,” the part with the most eyes, facing up.
2. Place it bright and cool, at around 10 to 15 degrees, for example in a bright basement, a winter garden or a cool room by the window. No direct, hot sun.
3. After four to six weeks, short, strong, green or purple germs measuring one to two centimeters have emerged. Long, white, thin germs show that it was too dark and too warm. They break easily when laid.

## Soil and location

Potatoes like loose, humus-rich, rather light soil in full sun. Heavy, wet clay soils promote rot and make harvesting difficult. Potatoes are heavy feeders: add ripening compost in autumn or spring, around three to five liters per square meter. Fresh manure is unfavorable because it can promote scab. Potatoes do not need lime; scab occurs more frequently on freshly limed soils.

Change the location every year. Potatoes and other nightshade plants such as tomatoes should only be grown again in the same area after three to four years at the earliest. How this works in a small garden can be found in [Plan crop rotation](/gartenwissen/fruchtfolge-planen/).

## Lay

The ground should have warmed up to around 8 to 10 degrees. In mild regions this is often mid-April, in cool regions it is early May. You can plant early varieties a little earlier and protect them with fleece if there is a risk of late frost.

1. Make furrows about 10 to 15 centimeters deep.
2. Place the tubers with the sprouts facing up, 30 to 40 centimeters apart. Early varieties require less space than late ones.
3. Leave 60 to 70 centimeters of space between rows so that you can pile up later.
4. Cover the tubers with soil to create a shallow mound.

## Hilling and care

As soon as the leaves are around 15 to 20 centimeters high, use a hoe to pull soil from both sides of the plants until only the upper leaves are sticking out. Repeat two to three times, spaced two to three weeks apart, until the plants close up.

Hilling has several reasons:

- The tubers form on the foothills in the dam. More loose soil means more space and usually more yield.
- Tubers that are exposed to light turn green and then contain more solanine. They are no longer suitable for eating.
- The dam warms up faster and allows rain to drain more easily.

Instead of piling up, you can also mulch thickly, for example with grass clippings or straw. More about this can be found in [Mulching in the vegetable garden](/gartenwissen/mulchen-im-gemuesegarten/).

**Watering:** Potatoes need consistent moisture, especially during flowering and tuber formation. If it is dry, water the ground thoroughly in the morning, not over the foliage.

## Late blight

The most feared disease is late blight, caused by *Phytophthora infestans*. It appears as brown, rapidly spreading spots on the leaves, often with a white coating on the underside in wet weather. In warm, humid weather it can destroy a bed in just a few days.

Prevention without poison:

- choose early varieties and pre-germinate them so that the tubers are ripe before the main infestation period,
- prefer tolerant varieties,
- keep a wide distance so that the leaves dry quickly,
- just pour on the ground,
- Keep your distance from tomatoes
- Remove affected leaves immediately and do not add them to the compost.

If the leaves are severely infested, cut them off and dispose of them in the trash. Leave the tubers in the ground for another two weeks so that the shell becomes firm and spores do not get onto the tubers. More about fungal diseases can be found in [Preventing fungal diseases](/gartenwissen/pilzkrankheiten-vorbeugen/).

## Colorado potato beetle

The yellow and black striped beetles and their red larvae eat the leaves. In small gardens, **collecting** is the most effective method: check the plants every few days from May onwards, collect beetles and larvae in a container and crush the yellow eggs on the undersides of the leaves. A net over the rows after budding keeps out migrating beetles. Beneficial insects such as ground beetles and predatory bugs help, as described in [Beneficial insects in the garden](/gartenwissen/nuetzlinge-im-garten/).

## Harvesting

- You harvest **new potatoes** as soon as the plants bloom and the first tubers are large enough. Dig carefully and only remove what you will eat in the next few days.
- You harvest **storage potatoes** when the leaves are completely dead and the skin is firm. Rub a tuber with your thumb: If the peel can no longer be rubbed off, they are ripe.

Use a digging fork to dig far away from the plant so you don't puncture any tubers. Harvest on a dry day and let the potatoes dry on the bed for one to two hours, no longer in the sun.

## Store

Only store undamaged, healthy tubers. You eat damaged potatoes first. A dark, cool, airy place with 4 to 8 degrees, such as a basement, in jute boxes or bags is ideal. Light makes potatoes green, warmth makes them germinate, cold below four degrees makes them sweet. Check the supply regularly and sort out any rotting tubers.

## Potatoes in crop rotation

Potatoes belong to the nightshade family, like tomatoes, peppers and eggplants. These plants share diseases and pests, such as late blight. Therefore, do not grow potatoes where tomatoes grew the previous year and wait three to four years before nightshade plants come back to the same area.

As heavy feeders, potatoes need well-supplied soil, ideally with ripe compost. After harvesting, they leave behind loose, weed-free soil that is well suited for crops such as lamb's lettuce, spinach or green manure. More about this in the guide [Planning crop rotation] (/gartenwissen/fruchtfolge-planen/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I grow potatoes in a pot or bag?

Yes, in large pots or plant bags with at least 40 liters. You gradually add soil, like when hilling. The yield is lower than in the bed, but well suited for early potatoes on the terrace.

### Can I eat the potato berries?

No. The green, tomato-like berries that some varieties produce after flowering are poisonous. Make sure children don't eat them.

### Why are my potatoes scabby?

Potato scab appears as rough, corky spots on the skin. It occurs primarily in dry, lime-rich, light soils. The tubers are edible if you peel them. Uniform moisture during tuber formation and no fresh lime help prevent this.

### Why do potatoes turn green?

When tubers are exposed to light, they produce leafy greens and at the same time more solanine, a bitter substance that is poisonous in large quantities. Therefore, pile up the plants well and store harvested potatoes in a dark place. Cut out small green areas generously; you should not eat very green tubers.

### Can I plant table potatoes from the supermarket?

This is not recommended. Table potatoes are sometimes treated with germination inhibitors and can bring diseases with them. Certified seed potatoes are tested for health and produce more reliable harvests.

### When can I harvest early potatoes?

Early potatoes are usually ready to harvest when the plants have flowered. Try digging up a plant: if the tubers are large enough, you can harvest as needed. New potatoes have a thin skin and should be eaten soon as they cannot be stored for long.
