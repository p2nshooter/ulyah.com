A bed in which there is only one type of vegetable in a row is a set table for pests. On the other hand, if you cleverly combine different crops, you make better use of space, light and nutrients, confuse insect pests and reduce the risk of a disease affecting the entire bed. That is the basic idea of ​​mixed culture. Here you will find the tried and tested combinations, a clear table and an honest classification of which rules are based on experience and which are well founded.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Good neighbors complement each other in terms of root depth, height, nutrient requirements and duration of cultivation.
> - You shouldn't put plants from the same family next to each other because they share the same pests and diseases.
> - Classic pairings: carrots and onions, tomatoes and lettuce, beans and savory, cabbage and celery.
> - Unfavorable: Beans or peas next to onion plants, tomatoes next to potatoes.
> - Many tables come from the garden tradition. Observe for yourself what works in your garden.

## Why mixed culture works

There are several mechanisms behind the recommendations, some of which are well understood:

- **Different root zones:** Shallow-rooted lettuces get water and nutrients from the upper centimeters, deep-rooted carrots or parsnips from deeper layers. They hardly compete.
- **Different growth heights:** Tall plants such as tomatoes or runner beans shade low crops. For lettuce or spinach, light shade is even welcome in midsummer.
- **Different cultivation times:** Fast-growing radishes or lettuce have long been harvested when slower crops such as leeks or parsnips need the space.
- **Pest Confusion:** Many insect pests find their host plants by smell and shape. This is more difficult for them in mixed beds. For the carrot fly and the cabbage fly, there is evidence that mixed planting can reduce the infestation, but the effect is not reliable.
- **Less spread of diseases:** If there is another plant between two plants of the same species, fungal spores and pests are less likely to spread.
- **Nutritional partnership:** Legumes bind atmospheric nitrogen with nodule bacteria. Subsequent cultures in particular benefit from this.

## The most proven pairs in detail

### carrots and onions

The most famous mixed culture couple. The idea: The smell of the onions should keep the carrot fly away, and the smell of the carrots should keep the onion fly away. Studies show that the effect is only noticeable when the onions are in large numbers and closely mixed with the carrots. Regardless, both go well together: They grow slim, hardly take any light and use the soil at different depths. For example, alternately sow a row of carrots and a row of onion sets 20 to 25 cm apart.

### Undercropped tomatoes

A lot of space remains free under tomatoes until July. Sow lettuce, spinach or radishes there that are harvested before the tomatoes shade the ground. Marigolds and marigolds on the edge of the bed attract pollinators and hoverflies. Tagetes secrete substances through their roots that inhibit certain root nematodes, but this is particularly effective when planted densely over an entire season.

### Beans and savory

Savory between bush beans is said to deter black bean aphids. Here too, protection is not guaranteed, but the herb is used for cooking the beans anyway and flowers for insects.

### Cabbage and celery

Celery and tomatoes are said to confuse the cabbage white butterfly with their smell. What is certain is that cabbage is a long crop that requires a lot of space and lettuce or spinach are well suited as an intermediate crop in the first few weeks. A fine-meshed cultural protection net helps more reliably than any neighborhood against cabbage white butterfly and cabbage fly.

## Unfavorable combinations and why

- **Beans and peas next to onions, leeks and garlic:** Gardeners have long observed that legumes grow less well next to onion plants. Sulfur-containing root exudates are suspected to be the cause.
- **Tomatoes next to potatoes:** Both are nightshade plants and share late blight and brown rot. This rule is well founded.
- **Plants in the same family:** Beetroot, chard and spinach belong to the foxtail family; Cabbage, radishes, rocket and mustard to the cruciferous vegetables. The same family means the same pests, such as flea beetles on cruciferous vegetables.

## An example bed

For a bed 1.2 m wide and 3 m long, a mixed culture could look like this, with the rows running lengthwise:

1. Row of bush beans with savory at the ends
2. Row of lettuce, later endives
3. Row of kohlrabi alternating with celery
4. Row of spinach in spring, then beetroot
5. Row of carrots
6. Row of onion sets

The rows are each about 20 to 25 cm apart. Beans and onions are deliberately placed on opposite edges.

## The method according to Gertrud Franck

Starting in the 1950s, the gardener Gertrud Franck developed a system that many organic gardeners still use today. Its core: A row of spinach is sown every 50 cm across all the beds. The spinach shades the ground early in the year, is cut off later and left as mulch. Between the rows of spinach there are alternating high and low, long and short cultures. The system requires some planning but provides consistent ground coverage and good use of space.

## Mixed culture in raised beds and small areas

In raised beds or in a narrow vegetable strip, mixed culture not only makes sense, but is almost unavoidable. As many different vegetables as possible should grow in 1.2 to 2 square meters. This is where the combination of fast and slow cultures particularly pays off.

An example of a raised bed of 1 by 2 m in the first year: In the middle there are two tomatoes or a zucchini as heavy feeders that use the fresh, nutrient-rich structure. Kohlrabi, lettuce and radishes grow all around and are harvested by June. Around the edges are nasturtiums that trail over the edge and some onions or leeks. After the lettuce harvest in June there is space for bush beans, which can be sown until the beginning of July.

Important in a small space:

- **Plan height:** Tall plants on the north side, low ones on the south side so that everyone gets enough light.
- **Use time:** Immediately fill any gap created by a harvest, for example with lettuce, radishes or spinach.
- **Don't overdo it:** Too many species in a small space makes care confusing. Four to six cultures per bed is a good framework.
- **Write down:** A sketch of the crops will help you with crop rotation next year.

## What the research says

Mixed cultivation is less common in commercial vegetable farming than in home gardening because machines work better with uniform rows. However, studies of mixtures, such as grains with legumes in arable farming, show that mixed crops often use land more efficiently than pure crops. For individual garden pairs such as carrots and onions, protection against pests is demonstrable, but less than many gardening books promise. The most important argument for mixed culture in the home garden therefore remains a practical one: a diverse bed is more resilient, makes better use of the space and offers more living space for beneficial insects.

## The Three Sisters: Corn, Beans and Pumpkin

One of the oldest mixed cultures in the world comes from Central America and is often called the “three sisters”. Corn, runner beans and pumpkin grow together in one bed. The corn serves as a climbing aid for the beans, the beans collect nitrogen in the soil with their nodule bacteria, and the large leaves of the pumpkin shade the soil, keep it moist and suppress weeds.

In the home garden, this combination works best if the corn has a few weeks head start before the beans are sown, otherwise the beans will pull over the young corn plants. Choose strong varieties of corn and beans that don't grow too fast. For children, a three sisters bed is a particularly clear example of how plants can help each other.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to strictly adhere to mixed culture tables?

No. Much of the information comes from observations of individual gardeners and sometimes contradicts itself between different books. The most important principles are: Separate families, combine different growth forms and keep the ground covered at all times.

### How does mixed culture fit into crop rotation?

Both complement each other. Crop rotation regulates what will be on a bed next year, mixed culture regulates what will grow next to each other this year. Arrange the beds according to the main nutrient requirements and mix suitable cultures within one bed.

### Are flowers useful in the vegetable patch?

Yes. Marigolds, nasturtiums, borage or cornflowers attract pollinators and beneficial insects and make the bed more beautiful. Just make sure they don't overgrow the vegetables.

### Does mixed culture also work in balcony boxes?

Yes, in a small way. Tomatoes with basil, salad with radishes or strawberries with chives go well together. Make sure that the container is large enough and that the plants have similar water and light requirements, because competition is stronger in the limited root space than in the bed.

### How densely can I plant in mixed culture?

Slightly denser than in pure culture, but not so dense that the plants block each other's light. Maintain the recommended spacing of the main crop and use the gaps for fast-growing partners such as radishes or lettuce.
