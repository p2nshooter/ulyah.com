For decades, digging in the fall was part of the garden ritual. Clods were turned over so that the frost would break them up, and in the spring there was a seemingly perfectly prepared bed. Today we know more about what happens in the ground. A healthy soil is built up in layers in which other living beings work. Earthworms, fungi and microorganisms create a stable crumb structure that stores water and gives roots air. Anyone who digs up every year destroys this structure again and again. Gardening without digging, on the other hand, relies on loosening, covering and feeding. It saves energy, reduces weeds and makes the soil better every year.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Digging up changes the soil layers, destroys fungal networks and earthworm burrows and brings weed seeds to the surface.
> - Without digging, you loosen the soil with a digging fork or sow tooth without turning it.
> - An annual application of two to five centimeters of compost feeds soil life and suppresses weeds.
> - Mulch and green manure keep the soil covered all year round.
> - In the case of heavily compacted soil or building rubble, a one-time deep loosening can be useful. After that, soil life takes over.

## What happens when you dig

The top few centimeters of soil are home to organisms that need oxygen, including those that can survive on less. When digging, the top layers end up at the bottom and vice versa. Many soil organisms die or take a long time to re-establish themselves. There are also further disadvantages:

- **Fungal networks tear:** Fine fungal threads connect soil crumbs and supply plant roots with water and nutrients. Every break in the spade tears this network apart.
- **Earthworm burrows disappear:** Deep-burrowing earthworms create vertical tubes over the years through which rain seeps away and roots grow.
- **Weed seeds germinate:** Thousands of seeds lie dormant in the ground. When you dig they come to light and germinate.
- **Humus is broken down:** The sudden aeration causes organic matter to break down faster than it is created.
- **Compaction below the digging depth:** If you dig the same depth every year, you can create a solid layer underneath that retains water.

## The principles

### 1. Loosen instead of turning

With a **digging fork** you poke vertically into the ground, pull the handle slightly backwards so that the ground rises and breaks, and pull the fork out again. You repeat this one step further. The layers stay where they are, but compactions break up.

A **Grelinette** or wide fork with two handles and multiple tines makes this work easier on larger areas because you are working with your body weight instead of your back.

A **sow tooth** is a single, curved tine on a handle. You pull it lengthwise and crosswise through the bed, loosening the soil to a depth of around 20 centimeters without turning it over.

### 2. Cover the floor

Open ground becomes muddy, dries out and invites weeds. Therefore, always keep it covered if possible: in summer with mulch made from grass clippings or straw, as described in [Mulching in the vegetable garden](/gartenwissen/mulchen-im-gemuesegarten/), in autumn and winter with leaves or [green manure](/gartenwissen/gruenduengung/).

### 3. Feed from above

Instead of digging compost under, put it on top. A layer of two to five centimeters of mature compost in autumn or spring is sufficient. Earthworms gradually pull it into the ground. You can sow and plant directly on this compost layer. How good compost is created can be found in [Creating compost](/gartenwissen/kompost-anlegen/).

### 4. Don't step on the beds

Solid paths and beds that are no more than 1.20 meters wide ensure that the earth is never trampled. Then it no longer needs to be loosened up. [Creating a vegetable patch](/gartenwissen/gemuesebeet-anlegen/) explains how to plan beds and paths.

### 5. Leave roots in the ground

Cut harvested plants such as lettuce, beans or cabbage just above the ground instead of pulling them out by the roots. The roots rot in the soil and leave behind fine channels. Exception: diseased plants and root weeds.

## Weed without digging

Gardening without digging initially makes many gardeners hesitate because of the weeds. In practice it becomes less after a changeover phase:

- Seeds that are deep in the ground stay there and do not germinate.
- The compost or mulch layer suppresses germinating seeds on the surface.
- Young weeds in loose compost are easy to pull out.

Root weeds such as couch grass, groundweed and field bindweed do not disappear on their own. Dig them out specifically with a digging fork or cover infested areas with cardboard and compost for an entire season.

## This is how you rearrange a bed

1. **Autumn:** Cut harvested plants just above the ground and remove large weeds.
2. **Loosening:** If the soil is compacted, loosen it once with a digging fork or wide fork.
3. **Place compost:** Spread two to five centimeters of ripe or semi-ripe compost.
4. **Cover:** Leaves or green manure on top.
5. **Spring:** Pull the remaining winter cover aside, use the rake to create a fine surface, sow and plant.
6. **Summer:** Mulch between crops, replant or cover harvested rows immediately.

After two to three years, the surface is so loose and crumbly that you hardly need to loosen it anymore.

## When digging makes sense

- **New garden on compacted soil**, for example after construction work with heavy machinery: Here, a one-off deep loosening may be necessary, possibly with subsequent deep-rooted green manure.
- **Very heavy clay soils** that stay wet for a long time during the transition: Some gardeners roughly loosen this in the fall and let the frost do its work. However, compost and green manure remain the long-term goal.
- **Areas with a lot of couch grass** that must be specifically dug up.

How to assess your soil can be found in [Determine soil type](/gartenwissen/bodenart-bestimmen/).

## One year in the bed without digging

What does this look like in practice? An example of a bed with carrots and leeks:

- **March:** The winter foliage is half gone. You pull the remains to the edge of the bed, loosen only where the soil is still firm with the digging fork and rake the surface finely. Sow carrots in rows in the autumn compost layer.
- **May:** Plant leeks between the rows of carrots. As soon as the carrots are hand-high, mulch them thinly with dried grass clippings.
- **July:** You pull the few weeds by hand, add mulch and only water when there is a long period of drought.
- **October:** The carrots have been harvested, the leeks remain until winter. A layer of compost is placed on the free rows and leaves on top.

This means the ground remains covered all year round and the work is limited to sowing, planting, mulching and harvesting.

## Tools you really need

| tool | Use |
|---|---|
| Digging fork | Loosening compacted areas, digging up root weeds, harvesting potatoes |
| Sauzahn | Loosening without turning in longitudinal and cross paths |
| Rake | Create fine seed beds and distribute mulch |
| Pendulum hoe or pull hoe | Cut off young weeds flat |
| wheelbarrow | Transport compost and mulch |

A spade remains useful for planting shrubs and trees, but is rarely used in the vegetable patch.

## What practice and research show

The idea of turning the soil as little as possible is not new. In agriculture, the effects of reduced tillage on soil structure and soil life have been studied for decades. Soils that are less disturbed usually have more earthworms, more stable crumbs and a denser network of fungal threads that supply plant roots with water and nutrients. They are less susceptible to erosion and siltation.

In the home garden, the movement of gardening without digging, often called “No Dig”, has gained notoriety, among others, through the English vegetable gardener Charles Dowding, who has compared dug and non-digged beds side by side over many years. As in any garden, the result depends on the soil, climate and care. It's best to try the method out on one or two beds first and compare for yourself.

## Typical initial problems and solutions

In the first year after the change, the same questions often arise. Snails feel comfortable under a thick layer of mulch, especially in wet springs. Then pull the mulch aside a little on young plants and collect snails in the evening. In early spring, a mulched soil warms up more slowly. So rake the mulch aside on seedbeds a few weeks before sowing so that the sun reaches the soil.

Weeds can also appear stronger at the beginning because there are many seeds in the ground waiting for their chance. A layer of compost or mulch keeps these seeds in the dark, and if you remove new seedlings early with a hoe, you will have significantly less work to do after one or two years.

## Frequently asked questions

### Won't my soil harden without digging?

Not if it is covered, receives regular compost and is not walked on. Earthworms and roots then permanently loosen it better than a spade could.

### Do mulch and compost attract slugs?

Mulch provides hiding places for snails. In gardens with lots of slugs, mulch only when the plants are vigorous and keep the mulch layer thin. More about this in [Nudibranchs without poison](/gartenwissen/schnecken-ohne-gift/).

### Do I need a lot of compost?

For 10 square meters of bed area you need around 300 liters of compost per year with a layer of three centimeters. If you don't make enough yourself, you can buy certified green waste compost from municipal composting plants.

### Does this also work on heavy clay soil?

Yes, but it takes patience. Start by loosening heavy soil deeply with a digging fork or sow tooth without turning over the layers, then keep the surface permanently covered with compost and mulch. Soil life builds up the structure year after year and the soil becomes increasingly crumbly.

### What do I do with root weeds like couch grass or groundweed?

Patience helps against stubborn root weeds. Cover affected areas with cardboard and a thick layer of mulch for an entire season to keep the plants out of light, and consistently pull out new shoots.

Then plant densely so that free areas quickly grow over.
