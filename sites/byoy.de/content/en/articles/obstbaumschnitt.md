An uncut fruit tree also bears fruit, but usually many small ones, often only every other year, and over time its crown becomes dense, dark and susceptible to fungal diseases. A well-pruned tree, on the other hand, has a light crown in which sun and wind reach every leaf, regularly bears large, aromatic fruits and remains healthy and stable for decades. Many garden owners shy away from pruning because they fear making mistakes. Pruning fruit trees follows a few clear principles that can be learned quickly. Anyone who knows them and prefers to cut regularly in moderation rather than rarely and radically will not go wrong.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Winter pruning from the end of January to March stimulates growth. Summer pruning in July and August slows it down and promotes fruit wood.
> - First you remove dead, diseased, crossing and inwardly growing branches, then vertical shoots of water.
> - Always cut on a string: do not cut too deep into the trunk and do not leave any stubs.
> - You cut cherries and peaches after harvesting in summer, apples and pears usually in winter.
> - Remove no more than about a quarter to a third of the crown per year. Rejuvenate old trees over several years.

## Why cut?

- **Light:** Fruits need sun to ripen and develop flavor. In a dense crown they remain small and sour.
- **Health:** In an airy crown, leaves and fruits dry quickly after rain. This prevents scab, mildew and fruit rot. More about this in [Preventing fungal diseases](/gartenwissen/pilzkrankheiten-vorbeugen/).
- **Stability:** A well-built framework of a few strong leading branches supports heavy harvests without breaking.
- **Regular yield:** Pruning ensures a balance of growth and fruiting and mitigates the alternation of full and absent years.
- **Accessibility:** A crown of a reasonable height can be maintained and harvested.

## Winter cut or summer cut?

| | Winter pruning | Summer cut |
|---|---|---|
| point in time | End of January to March, on frost-free days | July to mid-August |
| Effect | stimulates vigorous new growth | slows growth, promotes flower buds for next year |
| Good for | Training young trees, building the crown, weak-growing trees | strong growing trees, water shoots, cherries and peaches after harvest |
| advantage | without foliage you can see the structure clearly | Wounds close faster |

A simple rule is: little growth, cut in winter. Too much growth, cut in summer. You shouldn't cut at temperatures below minus five degrees because the wood will be brittle.

**Bird protection:** From March 1st to September 30th, heavy pruning of trees is restricted in accordance with the Federal Nature Conservation Act. Gentle care and shape cuts are permitted. Before each cut in spring and summer, check whether birds are nesting in the tree and, if so, postpone the cut.

## tool

- **Bypass secateurs** for shoots up to about two centimeters, with two sharp blades that slide past each other. It doesn't crush the wood.
- **Pruning shears** with long handles for branches up to around four centimeters.
- **Tree saw** with pull cut for thicker branches.
- **Stable ladder**, preferably a fruit tree ladder with a tapered head and a third leg.

Keep the tool sharp and clean. If you cut diseased branches, disinfect the blades with alcohol afterward to avoid spreading disease.

## The basic rules for every cut

1. **Look first, then cut:** Walk around the tree and think about which branches should form the framework.
2. **Dead, Diseased, Crossing:** First remove dead and diseased branches, branches that are crossing or rubbing against each other, and shoots that are growing inside the crown.
3. **Water shoots:** Steep, strong shoots that grow vertically from branches bear little fruit and compact the crown. Remove them at the base, preferably in summer, by pulling them off while they are still young.
4. **Competitive shoots:** Where two shoots grow next to each other in the same direction, the better placed one remains.
5. **Cut on Astring:** If you are completely removing a branch, cut directly on the outside of the Astring, the slight thickening at the base. If a stub is left standing, it dies and rots. If you cut too deeply into the trunk, you will create a large wound that will heal poorly.
6. **Cut to the bud:** If you shorten a shoot, make the cut about half a centimeter above an outward-facing bud, slightly at an angle.
7. **Heavy branches in stages:** Saw off thick branches in several steps: first cut from below, then saw further out from above, and finally remove the stub on the branch ring. This way the bark doesn't tear.

Small wounds do not require wound closure. For large cuts, a wound edge sealant can protect the edge from drying out. However, it is better to avoid large wounds through regular trimming.

## Training pruning of young trees

In the first few years after planting you build the crown. The aim for apples and pears is usually a pyramidal crown with a central shoot and three to four leading branches that protrude at an angle of around 45 degrees.

- Every winter you shorten the leading branches by about a third of the new growth, above an outward-facing bud. Their tips should be approximately at the same height, the so-called juice scale.
- The central shoot remains a little longer than the leading branches.
- If the branches are too steep, tie them with string or spread them flatter with pieces of wood. Flat branches bear earlier and more.
- Remove shoots that compete with the central shoot and the leading branches.

The basis for this is the planting cut, as described in [Planting a fruit tree](/gartenwissen/obstbaum-pflanzen/).

## Maintenance pruning for apples and pears

As soon as the tree bears fruit, it is a matter of keeping the crown light and renewing fruit wood. Apples and pears bear fruit on short, perennial shoots, the fruit skewers and fruit cakes. These wear well for a few years, then they get old and sag.

- Cut old, worn, drooping fruit branches back to a younger, outward-growing shoot. This keeps the fruit wood young.
- Light up the crown so that you can see through it from the outside. An old gardening saying says you should be able to throw a hat through the crown.
- Keep the crown height within limits by diverting the tips to a lower side branch.

## Cherries, plums and peaches

- **Sweet cherries** are best cut immediately after harvesting in summer. Cherries cut in winter are prone to gum flow. Clear out branches that are too close together and remove tips that are too high.
- **Sour cherries** such as morello cherries grow on annual shoots. After harvesting, cut down, whip-like hanging shoots back to a young shoot.
- **Plums and plums** tolerate moderate pruning in late summer after harvest or in early spring. They bear two to three year old wood.
- **Peaches** only grow on annual shoots and need vigorous pruning every year, otherwise the yield will go outwards.

## Rejuvenate old trees

An overgrown old tree cannot be brought into shape in one year. Radical pruning results in a forest of water shoots and weakens the tree. Spread the rejuvenation over three to five years: Remove only a few large branches per year, starting with dead wood and the biggest troublemakers and giving the tree time to respond. For old tree trunks, it is sometimes worth calling in a professional, especially if large, high branches need to be removed.

## Treat cuts properly

A clean cut heals best. Always cut branches on a branch, i.e. at the slightly thickened point where the branch grows from the trunk or a stronger branch. If you cut too deeply into the trunk, you will end up with a large wound that will heal poorly; If you leave a stub standing, it will die and become an entry point for fungi. You saw off large branches in several steps so that the bark does not tear when broken off.

Wound closure agents are not necessary for small and medium-sized cuts. The tree seals off its own wounds, and a thick coat of paint can even trap moisture beneath the layer. It's more important to avoid large cuts altogether by cutting regularly and moderately, rather than radically every few years.

## Cutting and bird protection

Fruit trees are popular breeding places for birds. Before every cut in spring and summer, check carefully whether there is a nest in the crown, because occupied nests are protected. Heavy pruning should be done outside of the breeding season, i.e. in late winter. In summer, only gentle care cuts make sense, such as removing water shoots.

## Keep columnar and espaliered fruit in shape

Columnar fruit and espaliers need regular, small summer pruning. In June or July, trim the new side shoots to three to four leaves. This way, the slender shape is retained and the tree forms flower buds on the short shoots for the next year. A heavy winter cut, on the other hand, would primarily trigger new shoot growth.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much can I cut away at once?

As a rule of thumb, no more than a quarter to a third of the crown per year. More stimulates the tree to produce strong new growth.

### What do I do with the clippings?

You can chop up healthy clippings and use them as mulch or for compost, as described in [Creating compost](/gartenwissen/kompost-anlegen/). Diseased branches should be disposed of with household waste or collected as green waste.

### Do I have to prune every year?

Young trees yes, to build up the crown. Older trees need a small pruning every year. Regular small procedures are better than infrequent major ones.

### Can I cut in frost?

It's better not to do this in severe frost because the wood is then brittle and can splinter at the interfaces. Frost-free or only slightly frosty days in late winter are ideal. It's better to cut stone fruits such as cherries and plums in the summer after the harvest because the wounds heal faster.

Then the wounds also drip less resin and the tree can close them before winter.
