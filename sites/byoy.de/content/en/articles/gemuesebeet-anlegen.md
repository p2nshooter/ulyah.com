The desire to grow your own vegetables often starts with a piece of lawn behind the house. Turning it into a fertile bed is not rocket science, but it does take some planning. A well-designed vegetable patch is easy to work with, forgiving of beginners' mistakes, and gets better every year because the soil becomes more vibrant with each addition of compost. A poorly planned bed, on the other hand, is too wide, is in the shade or is full of couch grass. This guide will show you how to proceed from the first sod to the first harvest, in the spirit of organic gardening: with compost, gentle tillage and no chemicals.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Choose the sunniest spot in the garden, with at least six hours of sun and water nearby.
> - Make beds no more than 1.20 meters wide so that you can reach the middle from both sides without stepping on the soil.
> - Lawn is made into a bed either by cutting off the sod or by covering it with cardboard and compost for a few months.
> - Loosen the soil instead of digging it up: The digging fork breaks up compacted layers without changing the soil layers.
> - Start with hardy crops like radishes, lettuce, beans, zucchini and chard.

## The right place

Vegetables need light. Observe your garden for a day: Where does the sun shine the longest? A place with at least six, preferably eight hours of direct sun is ideal. Further criteria:

- **Water:** a tap or rain barrel nearby. If you carry watering cans all over the garden, you water less often.
- **Windbreak:** A hedge or fence a few meters away protects against drying wind without casting shadows.
- **No root competition:** Distance from large trees and hedges. Their roots take water and nutrients from the vegetables.
- **Accessibility:** A bed close to the house is visited, watered and harvested more often.

## Size and layout

To start with, an area of 10 to 20 square meters is sufficient. Two to four beds fit on it, enough for lettuce, herbs, zucchini, beans and a few tomatoes.

| Element | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| bed width | 1.0 to 1.2 meters |
| bed length | any, often 3 to 5 meters |
| Paths between beds | 30 to 40 centimeters, for wheelbarrows 60 to 80 centimeters |
| Alignment | Long sides in a north-south direction if possible |

Divide the area into several beds of equal size. This makes [crop rotation](/gartenwissen/fruchtfolge-planen/) easier: every year the crops move one bed further.

The paths remain permanently paths. This means the bed soil is never walked on and remains loose. You can create paths with bark mulch, wood chips, lawn or stepping stones.

## Turning a lawn into a bed: two methods

### Method 1: Cut sod

This method is quick but tiring.

1. Mark the beds with string and stakes.
2. Using a sharp spade, cut the turf into strips about five centimeters deep.
3. Lift off the sod and stack it upside down in one spot in the garden. After a year it has become fine soil. They are also suitable as a layer in the [raised bed](/gartenwissen/hochbeet-anlegen/).
4. Loosen the soil underneath with the digging fork.
5. Work in three to five liters of mature compost per square meter.

So you can sow and plant straight away. Watch out for root weeds like couch grass, groundweed and bindweed. You have to pick out their roots as completely as possible, otherwise they will sprout again from every piece.

### Method 2: Cover with cardboard

This method saves energy and promotes soil life. But it takes time, it's best to start in the fall for the next spring.

1. Mow the lawn very short.
2. Place unprinted cardboard without tape on the surface in an overlapping manner so that no light comes through.
3. Water the cardboard thoroughly.
4. Cover them with 4 to 6 inches of compost, leaves, grass clippings or a mixture.
5. Leave everything over the winter. Worms pull the material into the ground and the grass underneath dies and rots.

In spring you can plant directly through the leftovers. For fine seeds such as carrots, pull the top layer aside a little and sow into the loose ground. This method is not ideal for areas with a lot of couch grass because its runners crawl out from under the cardboard.

## Get to know the soil

Before you spend a lot of money on soil, it's worth taking a look at the existing soil. Is it sandy, loamy or clayey? [Determine soil type](/gartenwissen/bodenart-bestimmen/) explains how you can determine the type of soil using simple tests. For most soils, compost improves everything. It loosens heavy soils and helps light soils retain water and nutrients. A soil test in the laboratory gives you additional information about the pH value and nutrients and prevents you from fertilizing unnecessarily.

## Loosen instead of digging

Many gardens used to be dug up to the depth of a spade every autumn. Today we know that soil life is disrupted as a result: the organisms in the upper layer need oxygen and end up at the bottom, while those from the depths end up at the top. In the organic garden, it is better to loosen the soil with a **digging fork** or a **grelinette**, a wide fork with two handles: pierce, pull slightly backwards, pull out again, one step further. This means that compacted layers break up without the soil being turned. In the case of heavily compacted soil, for example after construction work, a one-off deep loosening can be useful. More about soil-saving methods can be found in [Gardening without digging](/gartenwissen/gaertnern-ohne-umgraben/).

## What to plant first?

Cultures that grow quickly, are not very demanding and bring a sense of success are suitable for starters:

- **Radish** and **Lettuce**: ready to harvest after four to six weeks
- **Bush beans**: uncomplicated and productive, see [Growing bush beans and runner beans](/gartenwissen/buschbohnen-und-stangenbohnen/)
- **Zucchini**: one plant supports one family, see [Growing zucchini and squash](/gartenwissen/zucchini-und-kuerbis/)
- **Chard** and **Spinach**: robust and long to harvest
- **Herbs** such as parsley, chives and dill on the edge of the bed
- **Marigolds** and **Tagetes** between the vegetables, which attract beneficial insects

## A simple plan for the first year

| bed | Spring | Summer | Autumn |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bed 1 | Radishes, lettuce | Zucchini, pumpkin | green manure |
| Bed 2 | Spinach | French beans | Lamb's lettuce |
| Bed 3 | Onions, carrots | Carrots, chard | Chard stops |
| Bed 4 | Kohlrabi | Tomatoes with basil | green manure |

Next year, each row moves one bed further. This keeps the soil healthy.

## Typical beginner mistakes

- **Too large an area:** A small, well-kept bed brings more joy than a large, weedy one. Better expand next year.
- **Sown too densely:** Young plants need space. Separating it takes effort, but produces stronger plants.
- **All sown at once:** It is better to sow in several sets, then you will harvest over weeks instead of everything in one week.
- **No mulch:** Open ground dries out and becomes weedy. A thin layer of mulch saves a lot of work.

## Think about water right from the start

A vegetable patch needs regular water in summer, and tap water is expensive and often contains lime. Therefore, plan a rain barrel or a larger rainwater tank on a nearby downpipe right from the start. A garden hose with a shower head or a simple drip line along the rows saves a lot of time. It is better to water thoroughly twice a week rather than a little every day: this way the roots grow deeper and the plants survive dry phases better. Mulch between rows reduces evaporation significantly.

## The right tool to get you started

You don't need expensive equipment for a new vegetable patch. A digging fork for loosening, a rake for a fine seedbed, a hoe against weeds, a planting shovel and a watering can with a fine spray are enough to get you started. A planting line with two wooden stakes helps create straight rows, and a ruler or tape measure shows you the correct distances.

It's better to invest in a few, good tools with sturdy handles than in many cheap ones. Good tools last for decades, fit better in the hand and are easier on your back. After work, clean it roughly of any soil and keep it dry so it won't rust.

## Vegetable beds in the allotment and in the rental garden

Anyone who leases an allotment garden should know the association's rules. According to the Federal Allotment Garden Act, allotment gardens are intended for horticultural use, and a significant part of the area, according to popular interpretation at least a third, should be used to grow fruit and vegetables. A new vegetable bed is expressly desired there, but buildings and borders often have to be discussed.

In the garden of a rented apartment, ask the landlord before making any major changes. Anyone who converts a lawn into beds should clarify beforehand whether the area needs to be returned to its old state when moving out.

## Frequently asked questions

### When is the best time to plant a bed?

In the fall so that the soil can rest over the winter and you can get started straight away in the spring. In the spring it is also possible, then with the deadheading method.

### Do I need a bed border?

Not necessarily. However, a border made of wood, metal or stones keeps grass away from the bed and makes mowing the paths easier.

### Do I have to use purchased soil?

Mostly not. Mature compost worked into the existing soil is better than a thin layer of purchased soil on undisturbed subsoil.

### How much time does a vegetable patch take?

That depends on size and time of year. The effort is greatest in spring, when sowing and planting, and in midsummer, watering and harvesting are the main tasks. A small bed can be easily managed with regular short visits. Mulch, good planning and watering in the morning save a lot of time.

### Can I prepare a bed in autumn and plant it in spring?

Yes, that's even ideal. In the fall, cover the area with cardboard, compost and leaves. The soil life works over the winter, and in spring you will find a loose, weed-free bed.
