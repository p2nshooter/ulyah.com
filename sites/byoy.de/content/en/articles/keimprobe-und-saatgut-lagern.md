Almost every gardener has one: a box full of opened seed packets from the last few years. Carrots from three years ago, beans from the seed exchange, tomato seeds from our own harvest. Are they still good? Is it worth sowing them or will the row remain empty in the spring? The answer comes from a germination test that only takes a few days and costs almost nothing. And if you store your seeds correctly, you can use many varieties for significantly longer than what is written on the bag. This guide shows how long different seeds last, how the germination test works and how to organize your seed supply.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Seeds lose their ability to germinate over time. Parsnips and salsify often only last one to two years, tomatoes and pumpkins five years or more.
> - A germination sample with ten to twenty seeds on damp kitchen paper will show in a few days how many are still germinating.
> - If the germination rate is below around 50 percent, you sow more densely or buy new seeds.
> - Store seeds in a cool, dry, dark and consistent temperature, ideally airtight with a desiccant.
> - Garden sheds and kitchens with large temperature fluctuations are unsuitable storage locations.

## How long do seeds remain viable?

The information refers to well-stored seeds. After that, the germination rate usually drops gradually, not suddenly.

| vegetables | Germination capacity (years, guideline) |
|---|---|
| Parsnip, salsify | 1 to 2 |
| Onion, chives | 2 to 3 |
| Leek, parsley, dill | 2 to 3 |
| Carrot, celery, fennel | 3 to 4 |
| pea, bean | 3 to 4 |
| Lettuce, spinach, chard | 3 to 5 |
| Cabbage, kohlrabi, radishes, radishes | 4 to 5 |
| Beetroot | 4 to 6 |
| Tomato, pepper | 4 to 6 |
| Pumpkin, zucchini, cucumber | 5 to 6 |

A good principle: Only buy as many short-lived species as parsnips, onions and parsley as you need in one season. For long-lasting varieties such as tomatoes and squash, you can use a bag for several years.

**Pilled seeds and seed tapes** tend to last shorter than loose seeds because the coating can absorb moisture.

## The germination test step by step

1. **Material:** Kitchen paper or a coffee filter, a flat plate or a container with a lid, water, a pen.
2. **Wet:** Place two layers of paper towels on the plate and moisten them evenly so that they are wet but not flooded.
3. **Count:** Place exactly 10 or 20 seeds of one variety with some space between them. With 10 seeds, each seed represents 10 percent, with 20 seeds, 5 percent.
4. **Cover:** Cover the plate with a second plate, a container or cling film to prevent it from drying out. Label the sample with variety and date.
5. **Heat:** Place the sample in a warm place, usually 18 to 22 degrees. Heat-loving species such as tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers germinate better at 22 to 25 degrees.
6. **Keep moist:** Check daily, spray water if necessary and ventilate briefly to prevent mold from forming.
7. **Count:** Count how many seeds have formed a radicle after the typical germination time.

| vegetables | Germination time at the right temperature |
|---|---|
| Radishes, cress, cabbage | 3 to 6 days |
| Lettuce, beans, peas, pumpkin | 5 to 10 days |
| Tomatoes, peppers | 7 to 14 days |
| Carrots, onions | 10 to 20 days |
| Parsley, parsnips | 14 to 28 days |

**Light germinators and dark germinators:** Some seeds like lettuce and many herbs germinate better in the light, others like onions, pumpkin and peas germinate better in the dark. For the test, place light germs in a bright place, but not in direct sunlight, and cover dark germs with another layer of paper.

## Interpret result

| Germination rate | Meaning | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 80 to 100 percent | very good | sow normally |
| 60 to 80 percent | good | sow a little more densely |
| 40 to 60 percent | moderate | Sow much more densely or use as a reserve |
| under 40 percent | weak | get new seeds |

Also pay attention to the quality of the seedlings: slow, weak, stunted seedlings indicate old, weakened seeds, even if they germinate formally. You can carefully plant germinated seeds from samples of large species such as beans, peas or squash in pots instead of throwing them away.

## Store seeds properly

Seeds are alive. They breathe very slowly and use up their reserves. The warmer and wetter they are, the faster they age. The basic rules:

- **Dry:** Moisture is the biggest enemy. Store seeds in paper bags in an airtight jar or container. A bag of silica gel or a bag of dry rice absorbs residual moisture.
- **Cool:** Around 5 to 15 degrees is ideal. A cool basement, an unheated pantry or a cool bedroom are suitable.
- **Dark:** Light accelerates aging.
- **Even:** Strong temperature fluctuations, such as in garden sheds, garages or next to the stove, significantly shorten the service life.

**Fridge and freezer:** Well-dried seeds will last a long time in the refrigerator in an airtight container. After removing the container, do not open it until it is at room temperature, otherwise moisture will condense on the cold seeds. Seeds must be very dry for the freezer. For most hobby gardeners, a cool, dry room is sufficient.

## Organize the supply

A clear inventory saves money and frustration:

- Sort the bags in a box by sowing month or plant family.
- On each bag, write down the year you bought or harvested it.
- Keep a simple list in which you enter the germination rate after each germination sample.
- Check the supply every January when you make the growing plan as described in [Create bed plan](/gartenwissen/beetplan-erstellen/).

## Pests in the seed supply

Beans and peas can be affected by seed beetles, whose larvae live in the seeds. Therefore, put the legumes you have harvested yourself in the freezer for a few days after drying. Do not store seeds openly to prevent mice and moths from getting there. How to collect and dry your own seeds can be found in [Grow your own seeds](/gartenwissen/saatgut-selbst-gewinnen/).

## Swap and share seeds

A bag of carrot seeds often contains more than a home garden needs in three years, and before the rest loses its germination power, it goes into the trash. Therefore, share seeds with neighbors, friends or in community gardens. Many cities have seed exchanges in the spring, and some libraries have seed libraries where you can borrow seeds and return your own seeds in the fall. This way, your supply stays fresh and you get to know new varieties at the same time.

## Special features of the germ sample

Not all seeds germinate under the same conditions. Light germinators such as lettuce, basil or celery also need light during the germination test and must not be left in a dark drawer. Dark germinators such as tomatoes or pumpkins germinate better if the kitchen paper is rolled up or covered.

Temperature also plays a role. Heat-loving species such as tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers germinate most reliably at twenty to twenty-five degrees; peppers often need two to three weeks. Lettuce, on the other hand, germinates poorly in the warmth and prefers it cool. Anyone who carries out the test under the right conditions will get a realistic result.

## Soaking and pre-germinating

Some seeds germinate more quickly if they absorb water first. You can soak parsley, parsnips, carrots and peas in lukewarm water overnight. Then dry them briefly on kitchen paper so that they are easier to sow.

For slow-germinating or expensive seeds, pre-germination is also possible: As with the germination test, place the seeds on damp paper and carefully sow the germinated seeds as soon as the small roots are visible. The delicate radicles break easily, so work with tweezers or a damp brush.

## Preserve variety diversity

Anyone who collects and exchanges seeds themselves helps to preserve old and regional varieties. In many countries, associations and initiatives collect such varieties and pass them on to gardeners, such as the VERN association in Brandenburg or the Austrian organization Arche Noah. Some city libraries now operate seed libraries where you can borrow seeds and return your own seeds after the harvest.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I still use seeds after the expiration date?

Yes, the date on the bag is a manufacturer's guarantee of a minimum germination rate, not an expiration date. Do a germ test, then you'll know whether it's still good.

### Why do my seeds germinate poorly in the bed even though the germination test was good?

Temperature, moisture, sowing depth and soil structure play a major role in the bed. Soil that is too cold, a crusty surface or sowing too deep are common causes. More about this in [Direct sowing in the bed](/gartenwissen/direktsaat-im-beet/).

### How do I do a germination test with very small seeds?

Small seeds such as carrots or lettuce can also be laid out individually using tweezers or a moistened toothpick. White kitchen paper makes them clearly visible.

### Can I store seeds in the refrigerator?

Yes, if it is well packaged. Place the bags in an airtight jar, preferably with a small bag of dried rice or silica gel to absorb moisture. After removing the jar, let it come to room temperature before opening it, otherwise condensation will form on the cold seeds.

### How long should a germination sample take?

Most vegetables germinate within one to two weeks. Slow species such as parsley, carrots or peppers take up to three weeks. Keep the paper moist but not wet at all times, and replace it if mold develops.

### What do I do with seeds that germinate poorly?

If the germination rate is still around half, you can use it and simply sow more densely. If the results are very poor, fresh seeds are worth it, because weak seeds often produce weak plants. Old, untreated seeds go into the compost.

Treated, color-treated seeds, on the other hand, belong in the household waste.

Write down the result directly on the seed bag.
