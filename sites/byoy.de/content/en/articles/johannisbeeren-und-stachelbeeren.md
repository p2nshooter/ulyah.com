Currants and gooseberries are among the most reliable fruit bushes in the garden. They are hardy, can cope with partial shade, bear fruit in the second year after planting and provide berries for jelly, juice, red fruit jelly and cakes for many years. They were never missing in old cottage gardens. To prevent them from becoming an impenetrable thicket of old, barely bearing canes, they need regular pruning, and this varies depending on the species. This guide explains planting, care and pruning step by step.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Planting time is autumn, from October to November. Plant the currants a little deeper than they were in the pot so that new shoots form from the ground.
> - The bushes have shallow roots. A layer of mulch keeps the soil moist and protects the roots.
> - Red and white currants grow on two- to three-year-old shoots. Blackcurrants mainly grow on annual shoots and are therefore more rejuvenated.
> - Resistant varieties, airy pruning and caution with nitrogen help against American gooseberry powdery mildew.
> - Propagating is easy: cuttings cut in autumn root in the bed.

## The species at a glance

| Art | taste | fruit wood | Special feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Red currant | sour, fruity | two and three year old shoots | classic for jelly, juice and cakes |
| White currant | milder, sweeter | like the red one | good for eating fresh |
| Blackcurrant | strong, bitter-aromatic | especially annual shoots | very rich in vitamins, ideal for juice and liqueur |
| Gooseberry | sweet and sour, green, yellow or red | two to three year old shoots | Thorny, susceptible to mildew |
| Jostaberry | between currants and gooseberries | similar to the black currant | thornless, vigorous, robust |

## Location and soil

Currants and gooseberries grow best in full sun to light partial shade. The berries become sweeter in the sun. The soil should be humus, fresh and well-drained. The shrubs have shallow roots and are sensitive to dryness and chopping in the root area. Plan a distance of around 1.5 meters between the bushes, more like two meters for jostaberries.

## Plants

1. Place bare-rooted bushes in water for a few hours and submerge container goods thoroughly.
2. Dig a planting hole that is significantly larger than the root ball and loosen the base.
3. Plant **currants** about five to ten centimeters deeper than they were before. New shoots form from the buried part of the trunk, and the bush becomes dense and strong from below.
4. Plant **gooseberries** as deep as before. Real-root gooseberries may be a little deeper, but not into stems, otherwise the grafting area will take root.
5. Fill the soil mixed with some compost, water heavily and mulch the soil.
6. After planting, trim all shoots to three to four buds. This promotes strong new growth from the base.

## Care

- **Mulching:** A layer of grass clippings, leaves or compost keeps the shallow root area moist and weed-free. More about this in [Mulching in the vegetable garden](/gartenwissen/mulchen-im-gemuesegarten/).
- **Watering:** Water thoroughly during drought, especially during fruit formation in May and June. Dry bushes drop berries.
- **Fertilize:** Two to three liters of compost per bush in spring. Too much nitrogen promotes soft wood and mildew.
- **Do not hoe:** It is better to suppress weeds with mulch or pluck them by hand to avoid damaging the shallow roots.

## The right cut

The best time is right after the harvest in summer, then you can see clearly which shoots have borne. Alternatively, prune in late winter.

### Red and white currants and gooseberries

The goal is a loose bush with eight to ten strong main shoots of different ages: some one-year-olds, some two-year-olds, some three-year-olds.

1. Every year, cut off the two to three oldest shoots directly at the ground. You can recognize older shoots by their dark, cracked bark and the many branched side branches.
2. Leave two to three strong, well-placed young ground shoots as replacements. Remove excess young shoots.
3. Cut off weak shoots that are on the ground and growing inwards.
4. With gooseberries, thin out the middle well so that air can flow through the bush. This prevents mildew and makes harvesting between the thorns easier.

### Blackcurrants and jostaberries

Blackcurrants produce the best berries on the annual shoots from the previous year. That's why you cut them harder:

1. Cut off about a third of the shoots, the oldest ones, close to the ground every year.
2. Cut older shoots that are still standing back to a strong, young side shoot.
3. Promote lots of strong young shoots from the ground.

How the general pruning rules for fruit trees work can be found in [Fruit tree pruning](/gartenwissen/obstbaumschnitt/).

## Diseases and pests without poison

**American gooseberry mildew:** A white, later brown, felty coating on shoot tips and berries. Choose powdery mildew-resistant varieties, cut the bush so that it is airy, do not add too much nitrogen and cut off affected shoot tips in summer. They belong in the household waste.

**Gooseberry sawfly:** Its green, black-spotted larvae can strip a bush bare in just a few days. From May onwards, check the undersides of the leaves inside the bush and collect the larvae.

**Currant gall mite:** In the case of black currants, affected buds swell and become spherical in winter, so-called round buds. Break them out in the spring and destroy them. If the infestation is severe, cut out entire shoots.

**Aphids:** Red blisters on the leaves of the currant come from the currant leaf aphid. It looks bad, but usually does little damage to the shrub. They regulate beneficial insects such as ladybirds and hoverflies, as described in [Beneficial insects in the garden](/gartenwissen/nuetzlinge-im-garten/).

## Harvesting

You can harvest currants from the end of June until August. Red and white berries are ripe when they are fully colored and slightly translucent. Harvest the whole clusters and strip the berries with a fork at home. Blackcurrants ripen unevenly within a panicle. Pick them little by little. You can harvest gooseberries when green and firm for compotes and cakes, and let them fully ripen for fresh consumption. Red currants are particularly suitable for jelly, juice and red currants, black currants for juice, liqueur and jam, and gooseberries for compote, cakes and chutney. Fresh berries only last a few days in the refrigerator, but they freeze well.

## Propagate via cuttings

Currants are very easy to propagate:

1. In the fall, after the leaves fall, cut strong, annual shoots into pieces of about 20 centimeters, above a bud and below a bud.
2. Stick them vertically into a bed with loose soil so deep that only the top one or two buds are sticking out.
3. Keep the soil moist. Most cuttings sprout in spring.
4. Next fall, plant the young bushes in their final location.

## Stem: berries at eye level

Currants and gooseberries are also available as standard stems: the variety is grafted onto a trunk about one meter high on a vigorous base. The berries then hang comfortably at reaching height, and there is space underneath for strawberries, herbs or flowers. Stems need a permanent, stable stake because the crown becomes heavy. They are cut in a similar way to bushes: older shoots in the crown are regularly removed so that young ones grow back. Remove shoots that appear below the crown on the trunk immediately because they come from the rootstock.

## Currants and gooseberries in the kitchen

Red currants are tart and fresh, ideal for jelly, meringue cakes, red jelly and juice. They contain a lot of pectin and therefore gel particularly well. Blackcurrants have a strong, tart aroma and are particularly rich in vitamin C. They are used to make juice, liqueur, jam and syrup. White currants are milder and are good for snacking straight from the bush.

Gooseberries can be harvested in two stages of ripeness: When they are still green and firm, they are sour and are suitable for compotes, cakes and chutney. When fully ripe they become soft, sweet and aromatic and taste best fresh.

## Berry bushes in the bucket

Even if you don't have a garden, you don't have to go without currants. In a pot with at least twenty to thirty liters capacity, they thrive on balconies and terraces, especially as stems that require little floor space. Use structurally stable, humus-rich soil, water regularly, as berry bushes in pots dry out quickly, and fertilize in spring with an organic berry fertilizer. In winter, you can protect the bucket from freezing with jute or coconut mats.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why is my currant bush barely bearing fruit anymore?

Most of the time he is outdated. Young shoots are missing and old branches only support the tips. A rejuvenation cut over two to three years will bring it back.

### Why do the berries fall off before they are ripe?

Common causes include drought, late frosts during flowering, or poor pollination in cold weather. Mulch and watering help against drought.

### Can I plant currants in partial shade?

Yes, they bear well there, but the berries become more tart. Gooseberries react more strongly to powdery mildew in the shade.

### What is a jostaberry?

The jostaberry is a cross between blackcurrant and gooseberry that was bred in Germany. It grows vigorously, has no spines and is considered resistant to mildew. Its dark berries are larger than currants and taste like a mixture of both parents.

### When is the best time to plant berry bushes?

It is best to plant bare-rooted shrubs in autumn, from October to November, as long as the ground is not frozen. Then they take root until spring and sprout vigorously. You can plant plants in pots all year round as long as it doesn't freeze, but you have to water them regularly in summer.

### How long do currant bushes last?

With regular rejuvenation pruning for many years, often significantly longer than a decade. It is crucial that young shoots continue to grow back and old, aging branches are removed.
