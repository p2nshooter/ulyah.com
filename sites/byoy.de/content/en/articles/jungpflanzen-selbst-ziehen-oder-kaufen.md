Every spring, gardening enthusiasts are faced with the same question: Is it worth growing tomatoes, peppers, cabbage and herbs yourself on the windowsill, or is it easier to buy ready-made young plants in May? The answer depends on your space, your time, your requirements for varieties and also the vegetables themselves.

This guide compares both ways honestly, without romanticism and without contempt for your own cultivation. At the end you will find an overview of which vegetables are particularly suitable for growing yourself, which ones are better to buy and which ones should be sown directly into the bed anyway.

## Which speaks for your own cultivation

### variety of varieties

The strongest reason for growing your own is selection. You can usually find a handful of common types of tomatoes and peppers in the garden center. Seed suppliers, seed exchanges and exchange rings, on the other hand, offer hundreds of varieties: old local varieties, particularly robust outdoor tomatoes, unusual colors and shapes, regional specialties. If you want to grow certain varieties, you can hardly avoid growing them yourself.

### Costs

A bag of seeds often costs less than a single young plant and contains seeds for many plants. If you need a lot of plants, you save significantly. However, potting soil, pots and possibly a plant lamp are also included. The savings are therefore smaller in the first year and more significant from the second year onwards.

### Control and organic quality

If you grow your own, you know exactly which seeds, which soil and which fertilizer were used. You can consistently work peat-free and organically and choose seed-resistant varieties from which you can later obtain your own seeds. More about this in the guide [Grow your own seeds] (/gartenwissen/saatgut-selbst-gewinnen/).

### joy and knowledge

Many gardeners value growing their own plants as part of the gardening year. In February and March, when nothing is growing outside, the season begins on the windowsill. Children experience how a seed becomes a plant, and the experience grows with each passing year.

## Which speaks for purchased young plants

### Light and space

The biggest bottleneck in your own cultivation is light. In February and March, there is insufficient light in many windows and the seedlings become long, thin and pale. Without a bright, south-facing spot, a greenhouse or a plant lamp, it is difficult to grow strong young plants. Garden centers have greenhouses with ideal conditions.

### Time expenditure

Growing your own means daily checking, watering, airing, pricking out, repotting and hardening off over several weeks. If you have little time in spring or travel frequently, you are better served with purchased plants.

### Few plants

If you only need two tomato plants and a zucchini, you won't save much money by growing them yourself and still have all the effort. Purchased young plants are often the more sensible choice here.

### Grafted plants

Some young plants, such as grafted cucumbers, tomatoes or eggplants that have been grafted onto a robust base, can only be produced with a lot of experience. They can provide benefits for soil diseases or cooler locations.

## How you can recognize good young plants

Anyone who buys young plants should take a close look. Good plants have these characteristics:

- **Compact growth:** short spaces between leaves and a strong stem, no long, thin shoots.
- **Healthy Color:** Rich green leaves without yellow or brown spots.
- **Healthy roots:** bright, strong roots that run through the ball, but do not grow tightly matted in a circle.
- **No Pests:** Check the undersides of leaves for aphids, whiteflies and spider mites.
- **Not too developed:** Tomatoes with already large fruits in small pots are often stressed and grow poorly after planting.

If possible, buy young plants from a nursery or market that you have grown yourself and ask for organic quality. Plants from discount stores often sit in dark stores for a long time and have suffered from transport and drought.

## The right time

Whether grown yourself or bought: Heat-loving plants such as tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, zucchini and pumpkin only come outdoors after the Ice Saints in mid-May. If you buy young plants in April, you have to keep them bright and cool at home and protect them from frost. Robust crops such as lettuce, kohlrabi and cabbage, on the other hand, can be hardened into the bed as early as April. The guide [hardening off and planting out young plants] (/gartenwissen/jungpflanzen-abhaerten-und-auspflanzen/) explains how hardening works.

## Overview by type of vegetable

**Good for your own cultivation** (large variety of varieties, manageable effort):

- Tomatoes: easy to grow, huge selection of varieties, sowing from mid-March.
- Pumpkin, zucchini and cucumbers: large seeds, quick germination, sowing at the end of April so that they don't get too big by mid-May.
- Salad and kohlrabi: quick and uncomplicated, good for several sets.
- Herbs like basil and parsley.

**Buy better if light and heat are missing:**

- Peppers, chili and eggplants: need a lot of warmth, a long growing period from February and a lot of light.
- Celery: very long growing time and fine seeds.
- Grafted plants.

**Sow directly into the bed instead of preferring:**

- Carrots, parsnips, radishes, beets, spinach, peas and beans. Most root vegetables tolerate transplanting poorly. More about this in the guide [Direct sowing in the bed](/gartenwissen/direktsaat-im-beet/).

## A middle ground

Many gardeners do well with a middle ground: they grow the varieties themselves that are particularly important to them and that are easy to grow, such as special tomatoes and pumpkins. Plants that require a lot of light and heat, such as peppers or eggplants, are purchased as strong young plants from an organic nursery. You sow root vegetables and legumes directly into the bed. In this way, they combine diversity and the joy of growing with a manageable amount of effort.

If you're starting your own cultivation, it's best to start small: with a few tomatoes and pumpkins that work reliably. With each season the experience grows, and with it the number of plants on the windowsill.

## Practical example: Sowing tomatoes too early

A reader sowed her tomatoes on a west-facing windowsill at the end of January because she wanted to harvest as early as possible. By May the plants were over half a meter long, thin and pale, and after planting several snapped in the wind.

The following year she didn't sow until mid-March, placed the seedlings under a simple LED grow light and transplanted them into deeper pots in which she planted the stems a little deeper. The plants remained compact and strong, continued to grow quickly after being planted out in mid-May and bore just as early as the previous year. On the other hand, she bought peppers as young plants because her windowsill was not bright enough for them to grow for a long time.

## What it really costs to grow your own

The savings of growing your own are often overestimated, especially in the first year. In addition to the seeds, you will need potting soil, growing trays and pots, possibly a cover and a plant lamp for windows with poor light. But you only buy pots, bowls and lamps once and they last for many years. In addition, there is electricity for the lamp for a few weeks.

From the second year onwards, all that is needed is seeds and soil, and those who get their own seeds save here too. Growing your own is economically worthwhile, especially for larger quantities and special varieties. Buying two or three plants of a common variety is usually cheaper.

## Dress and swap together

A good way to share effort and costs is to grow them with neighbors, friends or in a community garden. A bag of seeds often contains far more seeds than a household needs. If you share seeds and swap young plants, you get more variety for less money. In many places there are plant exchanges in spring where hobby gardeners pass on surplus young plants.

## Typical mistakes when growing your own

- **Sowing too early:** The plants are too big and rot long before the planting date.
- **Too warm and too dark:** Heat without enough light will result in long, thin shoots.
- **Too wet:** Constantly wet potting soil promotes mold and damping-off disease.
- **Sown too densely:** The seedlings compete for light and become weak.
- **No hardening off:** Plants that come directly from the windowsill into the bed get sunburn and cold damage.

Anyone who knows these mistakes has already won most of the work.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much light do seedlings need?

More than most windows offer in February and March. A bright, south-facing location or a plant lamp that shines close above the seedlings for twelve to fourteen hours a day prevents long, thin shoots. Cool temperatures after germination also help keep the plants compact.

### Are growing sets with lids worth it?

For germination, yes, because the hood keeps the moisture in. After germination, the lid must be ventilated regularly and soon removed completely, otherwise there is a risk of mold and damping off.

### Are organic young plants really different?

Organic young plants are grown in peat-reduced or peat-free soil without chemical synthetic fertilizers and pesticides, and they often come from seed-resistant varieties. They are therefore the right choice for the organic garden if you don't grow them yourself.

### Can I overwinter young plants from the previous year?

For annual vegetables like tomatoes, this is usually not worth it. Chili and peppers, on the other hand, can be overwintered in a bright, cool place and often produce earlier in the second year.

### When do I sow which vegetables?

You can find the dates for sowing and planting clearly in the guide [Sowing calendar for vegetables] (/gartenwissen/aussaatkalender-gemuese/). The rule of thumb is: peppers and chili first, then tomatoes, lastly pumpkin, zucchini and cucumbers.

Count backwards from the Ice Saints in mid-May and you will find the right start date for each culture.

## Conclusion

Growing your own is particularly worthwhile for special varieties, larger quantities and gardeners with bright space and enjoy caring for them. Purchased young plants are the better choice when there is a lack of light, time or space, when only a few plants are needed or when crops that require warmth require a long period of cultivation. The smartest way is often a middle ground: grow something yourself that is easy and close to your heart, buy good organic young plants and sow root vegetables directly into the bed.
