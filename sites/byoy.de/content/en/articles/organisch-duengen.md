In an organic garden, it is not the plant that is fertilized, but the soil. Instead of quickly soluble mineral salts, soil life receives organic material, which it gradually converts into nutrients available to plants. The result is plants that grow evenly, form firm tissue and are less susceptible to pests. However, fertilizing organically does not mean fertilizing as much as possible. Many garden soils are even over-supplied. This guide explains which nutrients vegetables need, which organic fertilizers are available and how to use them in moderation.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Compost is the basis. For most crops, it is sufficient together with a targeted application of nitrogen for heavy eaters.
> - Horn shavings slowly supply nitrogen over several months. For heavy eaters around 50 to 80 grams per square meter.
> - Nettle manure is a fast-acting, free liquid fertilizer for tomatoes, cabbage and pumpkin, diluted 1 to 10.
> - Only use manure when it has been well seasoned and do not add it fresh to root vegetables.
> - Overfertilization makes plants soft and vulnerable and pollutes groundwater. A soil analysis shows what is really missing.

## What plants need

The three main nutrients are often abbreviated by their chemical symbols:

| nutrient | task | Deficiency signs | surplus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen (N) | Leaf and shoot growth | light green to yellow older leaves, weak growth | soft, masty plants, aphids, nitrates in leafy vegetables |
| Phosphorus (P) | Roots, flowers, seeds | reddish-purple leaves, weak roots | Common in gardens, blocks other nutrients |
| Potassium (K) | Water balance, fruit quality, frost hardiness | brown leaf edges, weak fruits | rarely problematic |

There are also magnesium, calcium, sulfur and trace elements such as iron and boron. A humus-rich soil with regular compost addition usually contains sufficient amounts of them.

## Compost as a base

Mature compost delivers all the nutrients in a balanced mix, slowly and in the quantities that soil life releases. It is fertilizer and soil improver at the same time. For many crops, adding compost in the spring is sufficient. How to make good compost and how much to put where is written in [Create compost](/gartenwissen/kompost-anlegen/).

## Organic fertilizers at a glance

### Horn shavings, horn semolina, horn meal

Ground cattle horns and hooves are the classic nitrogen fertilizer in organic gardens. They contain around 12 to 14 percent nitrogen. The finer the grind, the faster they work:

- **Horn flour:** works after a few weeks
- **Horn semolina:** works over a few weeks to months
- **Horn shavings:** work slowly over several months

For heavy eaters such as tomatoes, cabbage, pumpkin and potatoes, work in 50 to 80 grams of horn shavings per square meter in the spring, and for medium eaters 30 to 50 grams. Light eaters do not need horn shavings.

### Sheep wool pellets

Pressed sheep's wool provides nitrogen and potassium, stores water and decomposes over several months. It is particularly suitable for pots and raised beds. Dosage according to package instructions.

### Crap

Well-seasoned horse, cattle or sheep manure is an excellent fertilizer and soil improver. It should have been stored for at least six months, preferably a year, and should be crumbly. Fresh manure can burn roots, promote deformation in carrots and rot in onions. Apply manure in the fall to areas for heavy feeders, such as next year's squash and cabbage, and work it in shallowly in the spring. Do not use manure from animals that have been treated with medication in the vegetable garden.

### rock powder

Finely ground rock such as basalt or diabase flour provides trace elements and minerals, but works very slowly. A handful per square meter per year or a shovel in the compost is enough. It also binds odors in manure and compost.

### wood ash

Ash from untreated wood contains potassium and lime, but can also contain heavy metals that have accumulated in the wood. It increases the pH value. If you use them, then only from natural wood, sparingly, at most a handful per square meter per year, and not on lime-rich soils. Ashes from briquettes, coal or treated wood never belong in the garden.

### Be careful with castor meal

Some organic fertilizers contain castor meal. It can be dangerous to dogs and cats that eat it. If pets live in the garden, pay attention to the ingredient list and choose fertilizer without castor.

## Make your own nettle manure

Nettle manure is a free, fast-acting liquid fertilizer rich in nitrogen and potassium.

1. Cut about 1 kilogram of fresh nettles, preferably before they bloom. Wear gloves when doing this.
2. Roughly chop them and place them in a container made of wood, plastic or clay, not metal.
3. Pour 10 liters of rainwater over it.
4. Cover the container with a mesh or cloth to allow air to come in but prevent animals from falling into it. Place it away from the patio and neighbors.
5. Stir daily. A handful of rock dust softens the smell.
6. After one to two weeks, the manure no longer foams and is dark: it is ready.

**Application:** Pour 1 part manure diluted to 10 parts water onto the root area, for heavy feeders every one to two weeks from June to August. Do not use undiluted and do not pour over the leaves in full sun. Nettle manure is not necessary for peas, beans and onions.

Comfrey manure is prepared in the same way and provides a particularly high amount of potassium, good for tomatoes and potatoes.

## When and how much?

| Culture | Basic fertilization in spring | Top dressing |
|---|---|---|
| Heavy eaters: tomatoes, cabbage, pumpkin, zucchini, potatoes, celery | 3 to 5 liters of compost and 50 to 80 g of horn shavings per m² | June to August manure every 1 to 2 weeks |
| Medium consumption: carrots, lettuce, beetroot, onions, chard, fennel | 2 to 3 liters of compost, possibly 30 g of horn shavings | usually not necessary |
| Weak eaters: beans, peas, lamb's lettuce, radishes, herbs | 1 to 2 liters of compost | not necessary |

Which vegetables belong to which group and why can be found in [heavy eaters, medium eaters, weak eaters](/gartenwissen/starkzehrer-mittelzehrer-schwachzehrer/).

## Avoid over-fertilization

More fertilizer does not mean more yield. Too much nitrogen makes plants soft and fattening, attracts aphids, delays fruit production and causes leafy vegetables to store nitrate. Excess nutrients are washed out and pollute the groundwater. Therefore, have a soil analysis done every three to five years, as explained in [Determine soil type](/gartenwissen/bodenart-bestimmen/). Many gardens are so well supplied with phosphorus and potassium that they only need compost and a little nitrogen for years.

## A fertilization plan for the garden year

- **March to April:** Compost on all beds as needed, horn shavings for heavy feeders, compost as mulch around fruit bushes.
- **May:** Make manure as soon as the nettles are high enough.
- **June to August:** Water heavy feeders with diluted manure every one to two weeks, medium feeders if necessary.
- **September:** Stop fertilizing with nitrogen so that plants and trees mature before winter.
- **October to November:** Manure and semi-ripe compost on areas for next year's heavy feeders, green manure and leaves on free beds.

## Soil samples: knowing instead of guessing

Many home gardens are not under-supplied, but over-supplied. Years of fertilizing with compost, manure and complete fertilizer often lead to very high levels of phosphorus and potassium, which pollute soil life and water. A soil analysis every few years shows what your soil really needs. Agricultural testing institutes and private laboratories offer inexpensive standard analyzes for home gardens, which determine not only the nutrients but also the pH value and humus content.

For the sample, take soil from the top spade depth in several places on a bed, mix it in a clean bucket and send in a portion. The result usually contains a fertilizer recommendation. It often turns out that for a few years only nitrogen, for example from horn shavings or green manure, is necessary.

## Plant manure goes beyond the nettle

In addition to the well-known nettle manure, there are other plant extracts that have a long tradition in organic gardens. Comfrey manure is rich in potassium and is often used for tomatoes and other fruit vegetables. Field horsetail is traditionally prepared as a broth and sprayed on the leaves to prevent fungal diseases because it contains silica, which is said to strengthen the leaf tissue. Mixed manures made from different herbs are also possible.

Always use manure as fertilizer diluted, usually about one part manure to ten parts water, and pour it onto the soil, not over the leaves. If left undiluted, they can burn roots and leaves.

## Nutrients from the kitchen

Some kitchen leftovers can be used in the garden, preferably in the compost. Banana peels contain potassium, eggshells contain lime, and the cooled, unsalted cooking water of vegetables contains some minerals. Placed directly on the beds, such residues decompose slowly and can attract pests. In the compost, however, they become valuable humus.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are coffee grounds a good fertilizer?

It contains some nitrogen and attracts earthworms. In small quantities it is useful in compost or spread thinly around plants, but it is not a complete fertilizer.

### Can I use blue grain in an organic garden?

No. Complete mineral fertilizers are not part of organic gardening. They act quickly, but do not feed soil life and are easily washed out.

### When do I fertilize fruit trees and berries?

In early spring with compost as mulch around the tree disc and, if necessary, a small amount of horn shavings. More about this in [Planting a fruit tree](/gartenwissen/obstbaum-pflanzen/).

### How do I store organic fertilizers correctly?

Dry, well closed and out of reach of children and pets. Some organic fertilizers smell tempting to dogs, and some, such as castor bean meal, are toxic to animals. Moisture causes organic fertilizers to clump and mold.

### How much compost can I spread per year?

For most vegetable beds, one to three liters of mature compost per square meter per year is enough, a little more for heavy eaters and less for weak eaters and herbs. More is not better: Too much compost over the years leads to an oversupply of phosphorus and potassium. A soil analysis will show whether you should adjust the amount.

### Do lawns need organic fertilizer?

A lawn on which the clippings are left lying or which is regularly mown with a mulching mower often only needs a little additional fertilizer. A thin layer of fine compost in spring is usually enough.
