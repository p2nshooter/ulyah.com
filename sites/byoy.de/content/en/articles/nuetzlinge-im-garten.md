In the organic garden, a whole team works for you that you hardly notice. Larvae of ladybirds and lacewings clear aphid colonies, tiny parasitic wasps lay their eggs in pests, ground beetles hunt snail eggs at night. These animals replace any spray, but only if they find food, hiding places and winter quarters in the garden. Here you will find out which beneficial insects are particularly important, how you can recognize them and how you can establish them permanently.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - The most important opponents of aphids are the larvae of ladybirds, lacewings and hoverflies as well as parasitic wasps.
> - Many adult beneficial insects need pollen and nectar: ​​flowering plants are their filling station network.
> - Beneficial insects react with delay. A few days of patience pays off.
> - Piles of leaves, hollow stems and untidy corners are their winter quarters.
> - “Biological” sprays such as pyrethrum or neem also affect beneficial insects.

## Why beneficial insects arrive with a delay

A basic law of nature: First comes the prey, then the predator. In spring, aphids multiply explosively because they give birth to live young without mating. Ladybugs and lacewings specifically lay their eggs near such colonies, but it takes one to two weeks for the larvae to hatch and eat. Anyone who sprays during this time will not only destroy the aphids, but also the beginning counter-movement, and will have the same problem the next year.

Therefore, when the first infestation occurs, keep your feet still. Check the colonies every few days. You will usually soon discover larvae, eggs or aphid mummies. A sharp jet of water or wiping with your fingers only helps if the infestation on young plants is very severe.

### Ladybugs and their larvae

The best known species is the seven-spot ladybird. Adult beetles eat aphids, but the larvae do the real work. They look completely different from beetles: elongated, dark gray, with warty spots, almost like small crocodiles. A larva eats several hundred aphids during its development. Many gardeners mistakenly consider the larvae to be pests. Before you remove anything from a plant, take a close look.

The yellow eggs lie in small groups on the underside of leaves near aphid colonies.

### Lacewings

The adult lacewing is a delicate, light green insect with transparent wings that likes to fly into the light in the evening. Their larvae are called "aphid lions" and rightly so: with their suction pincers they empty large quantities of aphids during their two to three week development. Adult lacewings like to overwinter in sheds and attics. Special lacewing boxes, filled with straw and painted red, provide them with winter quarters.

### Hoverflies

Hoverflies camouflage themselves as wasps with yellow-black stripes, but do not sting. You can recognize them because they stand in the air like a helicopter. Their legless, maggot-like larvae eat aphids. The adults rely on open flowers with easily accessible pollen, such as umbellifers, marigolds, phacelia, cornflowers or yarrow.

### Parasitic wasps

Parasitic wasps are often only a few millimeters long and are hardly noticed. Many species lay their eggs in aphids. The larva develops inside, the aphid dies and remains as a brownish, bloated "mummy" from which a new parasitic wasp later hatches. When you see mummies like this in a colony, nature is already at work.

### Earworms

Earwigs hunt aphids and other small insects at night. You can relocate them specifically to fruit trees: fill a clay pot with wood shavings or straw, hang it upside down in the tree and make sure that it touches a branch. One disadvantage: Earwigs occasionally nibble on very ripe fruits, such as peaches. In fruit trees, however, the benefits outweigh the benefits.

## This is how you create a garden for beneficial insects

### Flowering plants as a source of food

Many adult beneficial insects live on pollen and nectar; only their larvae hunt. A garden with lots of flowers throughout the season is therefore the basis. Plants with open flowers and short corolla tubes that small insects can also reach are particularly valuable:

- **Umbelliferous:** Dill, fennel, coriander, wild carrot, parsnip in bloom
- **Asteraceae:** Marigold, cornflower, yarrow, daisy
- **Others:** Phacelia, buckwheat, nasturtium, borage

Allow individual carrots, parsnips or parsley to bloom in their second year. Their umbels attract parasitic wasps and hoverflies in large numbers.

### Hiding places and winter quarters

Most beneficial insects overwinter in the garden itself: ladybirds under leaves and in bark cracks, lacewings in buildings, hoverflies as pupae on the ground, ground beetles under stones and dead wood. A garden that is cleaned up too thoroughly takes away these spaces.

- Leave perennials and hollow stems standing over the winter and cut them back in spring.
- Create a pile of leaves or a dead wood hedge in a corner.
- Leave a strip of wild herbs on the edges of the bed.
- Create a pile of rocks or a dry stone wall.

### Avoiding broad-spectrum agents

Plant protection products that are approved for organic farming, such as those containing pyrethrins, neem or rapeseed oil, do not only affect pests. They also affect ladybird larvae, hoverfly larvae and parasitic wasps. In the home garden, it is almost always worth avoiding it altogether and giving time to the natural balance.

## Birds, hedgehogs and other larger helpers

In addition to insects, vertebrates are also important allies in the organic garden. A pair of tits needs many thousands of caterpillars and insects to raise their brood, a large proportion of which come from fruit trees and hedges. Nesting boxes for blue and great tits with an entry hole of 26 to 28 mm or 32 mm, hung at a height of 2 to 3 m and not in direct sunlight, are usually quickly accepted. Clean them in the fall so that parasites from the old nest don't burden the next brood.

Hedgehogs, toads, slowworms and shrews eat snails, beetle larvae and other ground dwellers. They need hiding places such as piles of leaves and brushwood, passages in the fence of around 13 x 13 cm and a garden without slug pellets containing dangerous substances. Bats hunt moths at night, including the codling moth and coal owl. They are attracted to a garden with a pond and lots of insects.

## A year in the beneficial insect garden

| season | What you can do for beneficial insects |
|---|---|
| Spring | Only cut back perennials from April when the overwinterers are active; offer the first flowers such as willow and wild fruit |
| Early summer | Observe aphid colonies instead of spraying; Allow umbelliferous plants to bloom |
| Midsummer | Lay out water points flat; Do not mow flower strips completely |
| Autumn | Create a pile of leaves, leave hollow stems, set up a lacewing box |
| Winter | Clean nesting boxes and hang new ones, leave dead wood lying around |

These small steps hardly take any time, but they change the balance in the garden in the long term. After a few years, you will notice that aphid infestations become less frequent and collapse on their own more quickly.

## Buy beneficial insects?

You can buy lacewing larvae, ladybird larvae, parasitic wasps or nematodes in specialist stores. They are very effective in the greenhouse because they stay there. In the wild, many animals migrate, and success depends heavily on the weather. An exception are nematodes against black weevil or garden beetle larvae, which work well in the soil as long as the soil temperature is right, usually above 12 °C. For the outdoor vegetable garden, it is more sustainable to promote native beneficial insects.

## Lesser-known helpers: ground beetles, spiders and predatory bugs

In addition to the well-known ladybugs, there are many inconspicuous beneficial insects. Ground beetles hunt on the ground at night and eat, among other things, snail eggs and small snails. During the day they hide under stones, boards and mulch. Spiders catch large quantities of insects, including many pests, in webs and while hunting.

Small predatory bugs, such as flower bugs, suck out aphids, thrips and spider mites and are important helpers in the fruit and vegetable garden. Soft beetles, whose larvae live in the soil, and robber flies are also beneficial insects. A garden with flowers, hiding places and no broadband means offers them all a living space.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are Asian lady beetles harmful?

The Asian lady beetle is an introduced species that can outcompete native lady beetles. It also eats aphids. You can recognize it by its variable coloring and often a black "M" or "W" on the white pronotum. Targeted control in the garden does not make sense.

### How long does it take for beneficial insects to colonize?

In a newly planted garden, often one to two years. The more structure and flowers the garden offers, the quicker balance will be achieved.

### Do insect hotels help beneficial insects?

Most insect hotels serve wild bees and some species of wasps. Leaves, dead wood and perennials are more important for ladybirds and ground beetles. A good insect hotel is still a useful addition.

### Do home remedies like soapy water also harm beneficial insects?

Yes, contact agents affect all insects that they wet, including larvae of ladybirds, lacewings and hoverflies. Therefore, only treat the affected shoots specifically and first try mechanical methods such as stripping or a strong jet of water. The beneficial insects often do the rest within a few weeks.

### Do beneficial insects need water in the garden?

Yes, many insects drink regularly. A shallow bowl with stones or marbles as landing spots, filled with rainwater, helps bees, hoverflies and other helpers, especially on hot summer days. Change the water every few days to prevent mosquito larvae from developing.

A bird bath in the garden is also used by many insects.

Place some stones inside so that the insects can land safely.
