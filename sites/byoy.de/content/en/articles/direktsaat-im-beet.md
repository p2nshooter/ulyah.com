Many vegetables grow best when they germinate where they spend their entire lives. Carrots, parsnips and radishes cannot tolerate transplanting because their taproot is damaged. Peas, beans, spinach and lamb's lettuce germinate so reliably in the bed that it's not worth it. Direct sowing saves pots, soil and space on the windowsill. But it has its pitfalls: seeds dry out, are eaten by birds, do not germinate in cold soil or remain stuck under a hard crust. This guide shows you how to prepare the seedbed, sow correctly and get the seedlings safely through the first few weeks.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Mainly root vegetables, legumes, spinach, lamb's lettuce, radishes and many herbs are sown directly.
> - The seedbed should be finely crumbly, even and lightly pressed.
> - Rule of thumb for sowing depth: two to three times as deep as the seed is thick. Light germs are only pressed on.
> - Each species needs a minimum temperature in the soil. Beans only germinate at around 10 degrees, peas at just a few degrees.
> - The surface must not dry out until germination. Then they are separated so that each plant has enough space.

## Which vegetables to sow directly?

| Direct sowing is ideal | Direct sowing possible or preferable | Better prefer it |
|---|---|---|
| Carrots, parsnips, radishes, radishes, peas, French beans, runner beans, spinach, beetroot, chard, lamb's lettuce, dill, rocket | Lettuce, kohlrabi, zucchini, pumpkin, cucumbers, onions, fennel | Tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, celery, many types of cabbage |

Heat-loving crops with a long development time such as tomatoes and peppers need a head start on the windowsill or cold frame in Germany. [Hardening off young plants and planting them out] (/gartenwissen/jungpflanzen-abhaerten-und-auspflanzen/) explains how you can then bring young plants into the bed.

## Prepare the seedbed

A good seedbed is finely crumbly on the surface and loose underneath, but not hollow. Fine seeds need close contact with moist soil.

1. **Loosening:** Loosen the soil with the digging fork or sow tooth without turning it over, as described in [Gardening Without Digging](/gartenwissen/gaertnern-ohne-umgraben/).
2. **Remove leftover mulch:** Pull leaves, mulch or leftover green manure aside. For fine seeds the surface must be free.
3. **Compost:** Work mature, fine compost flat into the top layer. Coarse compost interferes with germination.
4. **Rake:** Scrape the surface in several directions until there are no large lumps left.
5. **Let it settle:** If possible, let the prepared bed sit for a few days. Then the first weeds will germinate, which you chop off flat before sowing. This is particularly helpful for carrots and other slow-germinating crops.

Never sow in wet, greasy soil. If the soil sticks to the hoe, wait a few days.

## Soil temperature: when is it warm enough?

It's not the calendar date that decides, but the temperature in the ground. A simple soil thermometer, inserted five centimeters deep, shows whether it's ready to go.

| vegetables | Minimum temperature in the soil for germination |
|---|---|
| Peas, spinach, lamb's lettuce, radishes | about 2 to 5 °C |
| Carrots, parsnips, beets, onions | about 5 to 8 °C |
| Salad, kohlrabi | around 5 to 8 °C, germinates more poorly at over 20 °C |
| Beans, zucchini, pumpkin, cucumbers | around 10 to 12 °C, better 15 °C |

Beans in cold, wet soil will rot before they germinate. Therefore, only place them after the Ice Saints when the ground is warm. You can find the correct dates for each vegetable in the [Sowing Calendar for Vegetables](/gartenwissen/aussaatkalender-gemuese/).

## Sow correctly

### Row seeding

1. Stretch a string between two sticks to make the row straight.
2. Use the edge of a hoe or stick to make a groove to the correct depth.
3. Moisten the groove when the soil is dry.
4. Place the seeds in the groove. Mix fine seeds such as carrots with dry sand so that they are distributed more evenly.
5. Close the groove with the back of the rake and press the soil lightly.
6. Label the row with a label: variety and date.

### Sowing depth

| Seeds | Depth |
|---|---|
| Light germinators such as lettuce, dill, many herbs | just press it down or cover it very thinly |
| Fine seeds such as carrots, parsley | 1cm |
| Radishes, spinach, beetroot | 2cm |
| Peas | 3 to 5 cm |
| Beans | 3 to 4 cm |
| Pumpkin, zucchini | 2 to 3 cm |

### Marker seed

Carrots and parsnips germinate very slowly, often only after two to three weeks. Mix in a few radish seeds. The radishes germinate after a few days, show you the row when weeding and loosen the surface. When the carrots grow, the radishes have already been harvested.

## The critical phase until germination

Most failures with no-till occur in the first two weeks. Keep the seeds evenly moist: A seed that swells once and then dries out will die. Water with a fine spray so that the seeds are not washed away.

**Avoid crusting:** After heavy rain or watering on loamy soils, a hard crust forms on the surface through which fine seedlings cannot get through. Help against this:

- cover the seed groove with fine compost instead of garden soil,
- a board or a damp jute bag over the row of carrots, which you remove immediately after germination,
- a fleece over the bed that retains moisture and protects against heavy rain.

## Birds and snails

Blackbirds scratch in freshly sown beds, pigeons and pheasants peck at peas and bean seedlings. A fleece or net over arches protects the seeds until the plants are strong. Snails eat young seedlings overnight. Check fresh seeds in the evening and morning and collect snails. For more methods, see [Slugs Without Poison](/gartenwissen/schnecken-ohne-gift/).

## Isolate

Almost every no-till plant becomes too dense. So that the plants can develop, pull out excess seedlings as soon as they have two to four true leaves.

| vegetables | Distance after separation |
|---|---|
| carrots | 3 to 5 cm |
| Radishes | 3 to 5 cm |
| Beetroot | 8 to 10 cm |
| Parsnips | 8 to 10 cm |
| Spinach | 5 to 8 cm |
| Chard | 25 to 30 cm |

It is best to separate when the soil is moist and then press the remaining plants back on. When carrots are separated, the smell attracts carrot flies. Therefore, separate them in the evening, remove the pulled plants from the bed and then cover the row with a cultural protection net. You can eat the young leaves of spinach, beetroot and chard as babyleaf salad.

## Follow-up seeds

Do not sow fast-growing crops such as radishes, lettuce, spinach and French beans all at once, but in small quantities every two to four weeks. This means you have a consistent harvest throughout the entire season. [Pre-crop, main crop, post-crop](/gartenwissen/vor-und-nachkultur/) describes how you can use free areas again immediately after the harvest.

## Direct seeding and mulch

Mulch protects the soil and saves water, but is not compatible with fresh seeds. Fine seeds such as carrots, radishes or lettuce need a free, fine-crumbly surface that warms up in the sun. Before sowing, pull the layer of mulch to the side along the seed rows so that a free strip is created.

As soon as the seedlings are a few centimeters high and strong enough, you can loosely pull the mulch back into the rows. This means the soil between the rows stays moist, weeds have less of a chance, and the young plants are still bright and warm.

## Direct sowing with children

Direct sowing is ideal for getting children excited about the garden. Large seeds such as beans, peas, pumpkins and sunflowers are easy to grab and place individually in the soil. Radishes and cress germinate after a few days and quickly show success.

Give each child their own small row or square and label it with a wooden sign. Those who have sown, watered and harvested their own vegetables often eat their own vegetables with a completely different level of enthusiasm.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why aren't my carrots sprouting?

Common reasons include dryness in the first few weeks, a crusty surface, sowing too deep, cold soil or old seeds. Do a germination test as described in [Germination test and storing seeds](/gartenwissen/keimprobe-und-saatgut-lagern/) and keep the seeds consistently moist.

### Are seed tapes worth it?

They are practical for fine seeds such as carrots because they largely eliminate the need to separate them. They are more expensive and need to be kept particularly moist in the first few days.

### Can I sow directly in autumn?

Yes, lamb's lettuce, spinach and winter purslane are sown directly in late summer and autumn. Some gardeners also sow parsnips and carrots in late fall for early germination the next spring.

### How do I water fresh seeds without washing them away?

Use a watering can with a fine spray and water slowly in several passes. For carrots and other slow-germinating species, a board or a piece of fleece on the seed row helps to retain moisture. Check underneath daily and remove the cover as soon as the first seedlings appear.

### What do I do if there are gaps in a row?

Simply reseed the gaps. With quickly germinating crops like radishes or lettuce, the latecomers quickly catch up; with carrots, it's worth planting a fast-growing crop like lettuce in the gaps.

### Why do some seeds germinate poorly even though I've done everything right?

Often it is due to the seeds themselves. Older or incorrectly stored seeds lose their ability to germinate without you noticing it. A quick germination test before sowing shows whether the seeds are still good. The guide [Store germination sample and seeds] (/gartenwissen/keimprobe-und-saatgut-lagern/) explains how it works. Seeding too deep, a crusty surface after heavy rain or birds pecking out seeds are also common reasons.

A thin net over fresh seeds reliably keeps birds away until the plants are strong enough.
