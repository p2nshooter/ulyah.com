Every autumn, gardeners are faced with the same picture: lawns, paths and beds sinking under a blanket of brown, yellow and red leaves. Many people rake everything together, fill sacks and take them to the recycling center. Leaves are one of the most valuable raw materials that a garden receives free of charge. In the forest, leaves fall every year, rot and form the loose, humus-rich forest soil in which everything grows. You can imitate exactly this cycle in the garden: leaves protect the soil in winter, feed earthworms, become the finest humus and offer hedgehogs and insects a place to overwinter.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Leaves belong on beds, under bushes and in the compost, not in the residual waste.
> - Leaves of fruit trees, maple, linden, birch, hornbeam and ash rot quickly. Oak, walnut, beech and plane tree take longer.
> - A layer of leaves of five to ten centimeters protects beds from mud and frost in winter.
> - Leaf compost made from pure leaves takes one to two years and produces a fine, loose soil for sowing.
> - You should remove leaves from the lawn, otherwise it will turn yellow. A pile of leaves in a quiet corner is an ideal hedgehog roost.

## Which leaves for what?

Leaves vary greatly in how quickly they rot. This depends primarily on the tannin content and the leaf structure.

| foliage | Rotting | Usage |
|---|---|---|
| Fruit trees, maple, linden, birch, hornbeam, ash, hazelnut | quickly, often in a year | Mulch beds, compost, raised beds |
| Beech, plane tree | medium | Mulch under bushes, leaf compost |
| Oak, walnut, sweet chestnut | slow, lots of tannins | Mulch for blueberries and ericaceous beds, only in thin layers in the compost |
| Needles of pine and spruce | very slow, sour | Paths, mulch for acidic sites |

Oak and walnut leaves are not harmful, but they rot slowly and slightly acidify the soil. This is an advantage for blueberries, as described in [Blueberries in the Garden](/gartenwissen/heidelbeeren-im-garten/). In the vegetable patch you should only use it in thin layers or well mixed.

**Horse chestnut leaves** are often infested by the chestnut leaf miner, whose pupae overwinter in the leaves. If you have horse chestnuts, you collect the leaves thoroughly in the fall and do not compost them in your own garden, but rather send them to the municipal green waste collection, where they are hot composted. This is how you break the moth's cycle.

## Leaves as winter mulch for beds

In the organic garden, the soil is never left bare. A mulch cover over the winter has many advantages:

- It protects against siltation caused by heavy rain and erosion.
- It alleviates frost so that soil life remains active longer.
- Earthworms pull the leaves into the ground and turn them into valuable humus.
- In spring the soil underneath is crumbly and poor in weeds.

This is how you do it:

1. Clear harvested beds and remove diseased plant parts.
2. Spread a layer of five to ten centimeters of foliage. If you have larger leaves, chop them up with a lawnmower beforehand; then they will rot more quickly and blow away less.
3. In windy conditions, weigh down the leaves with some compost, twigs or a net.
4. In the spring, pull the residue aside or work it in flat as soon as you want to sow.

You can simply leave the leaves underneath bushes, berries and in perennial beds. It will be almost completely gone there by summer. More about mulching throughout the year can be found in [Mulching in the vegetable garden](/gartenwissen/mulchen-im-gemuesegarten/).

**A note about snails:** In gardens with lots of slugs, a thick layer of foliage provides hiding places in the spring. Therefore, pull back the leaves in good time before sowing beds with sensitive young plants. Further tips can be found in [Nudibranchs without poison](/gartenwissen/schnecken-ohne-gift/).

## Leaf compost: the best soil for sowing

Pure leaves rot differently than normal garden compost. Mushrooms in particular work here, slowly and coolly. The result is **leaf humus**: a dark, loose, fine-crumbly soil that stores water, contains few nutrients and is ideal for sowing soil, potting mixes and as a soil improver.

1. **Build a container:** A simple wire basket made of chicken wire, about a meter in diameter, will do. Alternatively, you can put leaves in large, perforated bags.
2. **Collect and moisten leaves:** Dry leaves do not rot. Pour it in as you pour it.
3. **Shredding:** Leaves shredded with a lawn mower are ready after about a year, whole leaves take two years.
4. **Let it rest:** It is not necessary to move it. Just make sure the foliage stays moist.

After a year you will have a coarse leaf compost, good as mulch. After two years it is fine and dark and can be sieved.

## Leaves in normal compost

In garden compost, leaves are an important carbon-rich partner for nitrogen-rich waste such as lawn clippings and kitchen scraps. Mix it in layers or intermixed with green material. A compost pile made only of grass clippings rots, one made only of leaves rots very slowly. Together they make a good mix. [Creating compost](/gartenwissen/kompost-anlegen/) explains how to build compost correctly.

## Leaves in the raised bed

When filling a raised bed, leaves are an ideal middle layer between branches and compost. It collapses over time and slowly delivers nutrients. You can find out more about this in [Creating a raised bed](/gartenwissen/hochbeet-anlegen/).

## Hedgehog quarters and insect protection

In autumn, hedgehogs look for dry, protected places to hibernate. A heap of leaves and brushwood in a quiet corner of the garden, preferably under a hedge, is ideal accommodation. Many beetles, spiders, butterfly pupae and other beneficial insects also overwinter in the leaves. Therefore, leave leaves lying in some corners of the garden and only carefully clear up these piles in late spring. How you can help hedgehogs all year round can be found in [Hedgehogs in the Garden](/gartenwissen/igel-im-garten/).

## Rake instead of leaf blower

Leaf vacuums and leaf blowers are loud, use energy and suck in many small animals along with the leaves, which often die in the process. A leaf rake or fan broom is quieter, gentler and completely sufficient for most gardens. On lawns, you can also pick up the leaves with a lawn mower with a collecting basket and shred them straight away.

## How much foliage is too much?

Five to ten centimeters are ideal on beds. Thicker layers of whole leaves can stick together if they get wet and poorly aerate the soil beneath. Therefore, chop large quantities beforehand or mix them with coarse material such as chopped material. The layer can be thicker under bushes and hedges, where it disappears on its own over the course of the year.

## Collecting leaves with children

Fall leaf work is a fun family project. Children love jumping into piles of leaves, sorting and identifying leaves and building hedgehog roosts. Explain why the leaves stay in the garden and what the earthworms do with them. A jar with soil, leaves and a few earthworms on the windowsill shows over a few weeks how the leaves disappear into the ground.

## Leaves on the lawn: mulch instead of removing them

You don't have to rake up a thin layer of leaves on the lawn. The leaves can be finely shredded with a lawn mower, preferably with a mulching function. The snippets slip between the blades of grass, are quickly decomposed and also fertilize the lawn. On the other hand, if the leaves are dense and wet on the lawn, they deprive the grass of light and air, and bare or moldy spots appear underneath. Then you rake it off and use it elsewhere in the garden.

## Leaves as frost protection

Foliage is an excellent, free winter protection. Delicate perennials such as artichokes or young roses get a blanket of leaves in late autumn, which you weigh down with brushwood so that the wind doesn't blow them away. You can place potted plants that overwinter outside in a box or basket and fill the spaces with leaves. This insulates the roots against frost.

In spring, pull the leaves aside again as soon as there is no longer a threat of severe frost so that the plants can sprout. The leaves then go into the compost or remain in the bed as mulch.

## Leaves on paths and sidewalks

Wet leaves quickly become a slippery trap on paths, stairs and terraces. Sweep these areas regularly, especially in the fall when it rains. Many municipalities also require property owners by statute to keep the adjacent sidewalk free of leaves. Find out what rules apply to you and then put the leaves in the garden instead of in the bin.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to remove leaves from the lawn?

Yes. Under a dense layer of leaves, the lawn does not receive light, turns yellow and is susceptible to fungal diseases. Remove the leaves regularly and use them elsewhere in the garden.

### Can I use leaves from the street?

Leaves from busy roads can contain road salt, abrasion and pollutants. For the vegetable garden, it is better to use leaves from your own garden or from quiet areas.

### Is diseased foliage a problem?

Leaves from fruit trees with scab or fungal infestation are better placed in the green waste collection or buried deeply. The spores could sprout again on the bed in spring. More about this in [Preventing fungal diseases](/gartenwissen/pilzkrankheiten-vorbeugen/).

### Can I burn leaves?

Burning yard waste is banned in most communities and it releases particulate matter. Leaves belong in the compost, in the garden or, if there is no other option, in the organic waste bin or in the community's green waste collection.

### Which leaves decompose the fastest?

Leaves from fruit trees, linden trees, maples, hornbeams and birches rot comparatively quickly. Oak, walnut and chestnut leaves contain more tannins and take significantly longer. If you want to use such leaves, shred them with a lawnmower and mix them with nitrogen-rich material such as grass clippings.

Shredded leaves generally rot faster than whole leaves.

A mixture of quickly and slowly decaying leaves makes particularly good leaf compost.
