Compost is the heart of the organic garden. In a few months, grass clippings, vegetable scraps, leaves and shrub cuttings create dark, crumbly soil that smells like the forest floor. It can do more than any store-bought fertilizer: it feeds the soil life, improves the structure, stores water and delivers nutrients slowly and evenly. At the same time, a large proportion of garden waste disappears without you having to drive to the recycling center. Good compost is no accident. It needs the right place, a balanced mix and a little care.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Place the compost in partial shade and directly on open ground so that earthworms and soil life can migrate.
> - Mix wet, nitrogen-rich “green” material with dry, carbon-rich “brown” material.
> - The compost should be moist like a squeezed sponge, never wet and never dry.
> - Removing once after three to six months accelerates rotting.
> - Ripe compost smells like forest floor, is dark and crumbly. The cress test shows whether it is suitable for sowing.

## The right place

A compost needs a place in partial shade, such as under a tree or on the north side of a hedge. In full sun it dries out, in deep shade it becomes cold and wet. It stands directly on the garden floor, not on concrete or paving. This allows excess water to seep away and earthworms, woodlice and microorganisms migrate in from below. A close-meshed wire mesh under the compost keeps voles and rats out without disturbing the worms.

Plan enough space for two to three compartments: one for filling, one for maturing and one for the finished compost. A path where you can drive the wheelbarrow to the compost makes the work easier.

## Which container?

| system | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Wooden slatted composter | good ventilation, inexpensive, expandable | Wood rots over time |
| Wire basket | very airy, ideal for foliage | dries out quickly |
| Plastic thermal composter | neat, warmer, faster, good for small gardens | less air, can become waterlogged, small volume |
| Open compost rental | for large gardens, lots of material | takes up space, looks less tidy |

In small gardens, a thermal composter or a single slatted composter is often sufficient. If you have a lot of material, build two or three wooden compartments next to each other.

## What can go in the compost

**Green nitrogen-rich material:**

- Leftover vegetables and fruits, coffee grounds, tea bags without plastic
- Lawn clippings in thin layers
- Weeds without seeds
- cleared vegetable plants, healthy
- Small animal manure from herbivores with straw or hay

**Brown carbon-rich material:**

- Foliage, as described in [Using leaves in the garden](/gartenwissen/laub-im-garten-nutzen/).
- chopped shrub cuttings, small twigs
- Straw, hay, sawdust from untreated wood
- unprinted cardboard and egg cartons in pieces

## What doesn't belong in the compost

- Cooked leftovers, meat, fish, bones, dairy products: They attract rats
- diseased plant parts, for example with late blight or club root
- Weeds with mature seeds and root weeds such as couch grass and groundweed that survive in cool garden compost
- Dog and cat feces, cat litter
- treated wood, glossy paper, coal ash or briquettes
- Citrus peels and banana peels from conventional cultivation in large quantities due to possible residues

It is best to let root weeds ferment in a bucket of water for a few weeks and then add the broth to the compost later.

## The right mix

The microorganisms in compost need carbon as a source of energy and nitrogen to build their cells. A good rule of thumb is approximately equal amounts of green and brown material, well mixed or in thin layers.

| Too much green material | Too much brown material |
|---|---|
| rots, smells like ammonia or rotten eggs, becomes slimy | hardly rots, remains dry and coarse |
| Solution: Mix in leaves, shredded material or cardboard | Solution: Add lawn clippings or vegetable scraps, moisten |

Shredded materials rot faster than whole ones. Shred shrub trimmings and cut thick vegetable stalks into small pieces.

## Moisture and air

Compost is a living system. The organisms need water and oxygen. Take a handful of the inside and squeeze it: a few drops should come out, no more. If the compost is too dry, water it when setting it up. If it is too wet, mix in dry material and cover it if it is raining constantly, for example with a board or a mat that still allows air to pass through. Coarse branches at the bottom of the pile provide ventilation from below.

## Implement

After three to six months, you turn the compost over: you dig it into the neighboring compartment with a fork so that the outer, less rotten parts come inside. You can ventilate the material and check the moisture. Relocation is not mandatory, but it speeds up the rot significantly. You don't need commercial compost accelerators. A shovel of ripe compost or garden soil provides all the necessary organisms.

## Maturity levels

- **Fresh compost**, after about two to four months: still coarse, lots of recognizable residue, rich in nutrients. It is suitable as mulch under heavy feeders such as pumpkin and zucchini, but not for sowing.
- **Ripe compost**, after around six to twelve months: dark, crumbly, smells like forest floor, raw materials can hardly be recognized anymore. It is suitable for all cultures.

### The cress test

Fill a screw-top jar with sifted compost, sow cress on top and cover it. If the cress germinates evenly after a few days and remains green, the compost is ripe. Yellow, brown or stunted seedlings show that it is still too fresh.

## How much compost in the bed?

| Culture | Amount per square meter per year |
|---|---|
| Heavy eaters (cabbage, tomatoes, pumpkin, potatoes) | 3 to 5 liters |
| Medium food (carrots, lettuce, onions, beetroot) | 2 to 3 liters |
| Weak eaters (beans, peas, herbs, lamb's lettuce) | 1 to 2 liters |
| Fruit bushes and fruit trees | 2 to 3 liters as mulch |

In the spring, work the compost shallowly into the top few inches of soil, not burying it deeply. More about the classification of cultures can be found in [Heavy eaters, medium eaters, weak eaters](/gartenwissen/starkzehrer-mittelzehrer-schwachzehrer/). Too much compost over many years can oversupply the soil with phosphorus and potassium. An occasional soil analysis, as described in [Determine soil type](/gartenwissen/bodenart-bestimmen/), provides clarity.

## Compost throughout the year

- **Spring:** Sift the ripe compost from last year and bring it to the beds. The screenings, coarse residues, go back into the new pile.
- **Summer:** A lot of grass clippings and vegetable scraps are produced. Mix them with the leaves collected in autumn or with chopped material. Keep the compost moist in hot weather.
- **Autumn:** Lots of brown material: leaves, cleared plants, shrub cuttings. A good time to set up a new pile and turn over the summer compost.
- **Winter:** The rot slows down. You can still put kitchen waste away. A cover made of leaves or a mat protects against too much moisture.

If you manage the compost like this, you have ready-made soil for the beds every spring and hardly need to buy soil anymore.

## Worm box for balcony and kitchen

If you don't have a garden or want to use your kitchen waste all year round, you can run a worm bin. Special compost worms work in it, not earthworms from the garden. They eat vegetable and fruit scraps, coffee grounds and torn cardboard and leave behind fine worm castings, an excellent fertilizer for potted plants and seeds. The box is in a place with 15 to 25 degrees, such as in the kitchen, in the basement or on a sheltered balcony. When used correctly, it smells like the forest floor. Citrus peels, onions, cooked food and meat do not belong in it. The expired liquid, the worm tea, is diluted and used as liquid fertilizer.

## Accelerate compost without additives

Commercially available compost accelerators are usually unnecessary. The right mixture and the right moisture are much more important. Shred coarse waste such as cabbage stalks, shrub stems or branches, because small pieces offer more surface area for microorganisms to attack. Always mix wet, green waste such as lawn clippings and kitchen scraps with dry, brown material such as leaves, straw or crumpled cardboard.

The compost should feel like a squeezed sponge: moist but not wet. A few shovels of ripe compost or garden soil, sprinkled between the layers, bring the necessary soil organisms with them. And if you move the pile once or twice, you supply it with oxygen and speed up the rotting process significantly.

## Thermal composter for small gardens

Closed plastic thermal composters are practical for small gardens and terraced houses. They keep the heat inside, look neat and let fewer odors escape. Because hardly any water evaporates, its contents quickly become too wet. Therefore, always add plenty of dry material and open the lid to ventilate if necessary.

In winter, rotting also slows down in the thermal composter. This is normal: it gets going again in the spring. Ripe compost can usually be removed through a flap at the bottom, while fresh material is added at the top.

## Frequently asked questions

### My compost stinks. What to do?

It is too wet or contains too much green material. Turn it over, mix in leaves, shredded material or torn cardboard and cover it if it rains constantly.

### Do compost piles attract rats?

Especially if cooked food, meat or bread gets into it. Avoid this, place a wire mesh under the compost and keep it moist. Rats like dry, quiet piles.

### Can I also compost on the balcony?

In a worm bin, yes. Compost worms turn kitchen scraps into worm castings in a closed box without smelling if the box is operated properly.

### Can citrus and banana peels be put in the compost?

Yes, in normal household quantities. Citrus peels decompose more slowly than other kitchen waste, so it's best to chop them up. Residues of treatment agents on the shells are largely broken down in the compost if they only make up a small part of the material.

### How do I know if my compost is ripe?

Mature compost is dark brown to black, crumbly and smells like forest floor. The original waste can no longer be seen, apart from individual twigs or eggshells. If you are unsure, do the cress test: If cress in a sample of compost germinates evenly and with green leaves, it is ripe enough for sensitive plants.
