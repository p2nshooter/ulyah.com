Blueberries are healthy, delicious and quite expensive in the store. In your own garden, cultivated blueberries, which come from North America and produce significantly larger berries than our native wild blueberries, often produce kilos per bush. Nevertheless, many gardeners fail with them: the leaves turn yellow, the bushes are carefree and barely bear fruit. The reason is almost always the ground. Blueberries are bog and heather plants and need very acidic, loose, humus-rich soil, which is rarely found in normal gardens. Anyone who offers them this soil, in a bed or in a bucket, will enjoy them for decades.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Cultivated blueberries need acidic soil with a pH of around 4 to 5. They will die in normal garden soil.
> - Create your own bed with bark compost, needle litter, oak or pine leaves and sand, or place them in a bucket with ericaceous soil without peat.
> - Water with rainwater. Chalky tap water raises the pH value.
> - Plant two different varieties next to each other. This improves pollination and extends the harvest season.
> - From the fourth year onwards, cut out the oldest shoots every year to keep the shrub young and productive.

## Native or cultural?

The **wild blueberry** grows in German forests as a low, dwarf shrub. Its small berries are blue inside and color the mouth and fingers. In the garden it only grows in forest conditions and produces little.

The **cultivated blueberry** comes from North American species. It grows one to two meters tall, has large berries with light flesh and is the shrub you buy from nurseries. Depending on the variety, the berries ripen from July to September. With an early, a medium and a late variety you can harvest over two months.

## Why soil is so important

Blueberries do not have root hairs like most plants. They live in a community with special soil fungi that help them absorb water and nutrients. These mushrooms only thrive in an acidic, humus-rich environment. If the pH value rises above around 5.5, the plants can no longer absorb iron. The result is yellow leaves with green veins, stunted growth and hardly any fruit.

Check the pH of your garden soil with a simple test as described in [Determine soil type](/gartenwissen/bodenart-bestimmen/). If it is over 5.5, which is the case in most gardens, you will need your own blueberry bed or container.

## Create a blueberry bed without peat

In the past, blueberry beds were planted with lots of peat. Because peat mining destroys moors, which store large amounts of carbon and are rare habitats, we do not use it in the organic garden. It also works without:

1. **Dig a pit:** about 40 to 50 centimeters deep and one meter wide per bush. Wider is better because blueberries have shallow and wide roots.
2. **Delimit laterally:** If the soil is calcareous, place a foil on the sides of the pit to prevent lime from the surrounding area from penetrating. Do not seal the bottom of the pit to allow water to drain away.
3. **Filling:** Mix bark humus or bark compost, coniferous litter from pine or spruce, oak leaves, well-rotted leaf compost, wood chips from coniferous wood and some sand. Peat-free ericaceous or rhododendron soil is also available from specialist retailers.
4. **Acidifying:** If necessary, you can lower the pH value with sulfur preparations from garden stores according to the instructions on the package. This works slowly over weeks.
5. **Planting:** Plant the bushes as deep as they were in the pot, about 4 to 5 feet apart.
6. **Mulching:** Cover the soil five to ten centimeters thick with bark mulch, needle litter or oak leaves. The mulch keeps the soil moist, acidic and cool.

[Using leaves in the garden](/gartenwissen/laub-im-garten-nutzen/) explains how to use leaves from oak and other trees sensibly.

## Blueberries in a bucket

In the bucket you can completely control the soil. This is the easiest solution, especially in gardens with very calcareous soil.

- Choose a bucket with at least 40 to 50 liters and drainage holes.
- Fill with peat-free ericaceous soil, possibly mixed with bark humus and needle litter.
- Place the pot in sunny to partially shaded areas.
- Only water with rainwater and keep the soil evenly moist without waterlogging.
- Repot the shrub in fresh soil every three to four years.
- Protect the bucket from freezing in winter, for example with jute on a protected house wall.

More ideas for fruit in a small space can be found in [Fruit for small gardens](/gartenwissen/obst-fuer-kleine-gaerten/).

## Watering and fertilizing

**Water:** Blueberries have shallow roots and cannot tolerate drought or waterlogging. When it is dry, water regularly with rainwater. In many regions, tap water contains a lot of lime and slowly increases the pH value.

**Fertilizing:** Blueberries need few nutrients and are sensitive to salt. Normal garden compost is often too calcareous. In spring, apply a small amount of special rhododendron or blueberry fertilizer in organic form or a handful of horn shavings and renew the mulch layer. More about organic fertilizers can be found in [Fertilize organically](/gartenwissen/organisch-duengen/).

## Pollination and varieties

Cultivated blueberries are usually self-fertile, but produce much better with a second variety nearby because cross-pollination results in larger and more berries. Bumblebees and wild bees are important pollinators. How you promote them is explained in [Promoting Wild Bees](/gartenwissen/wildbienen-foerdern/).

## Cut

In the first three years you hardly prune, only weak and damaged shoots. Afterwards:

1. Every year in late winter, cut out one or two of the oldest, thickest shoots close to the ground. Older shoots produce fewer and smaller berries.
2. Remove weak, thin and crowded shoots.
3. Leave strong young ground shoots as replacements.
4. Shorten worn, hanging side branches to a strong, young shoot.

This means the bush remains open and constantly renews itself, similar to [currants](/gartenwissen/johannisbeeren-und-stachelbeeren/).

## Crops and birds

Blueberries do not ripen at the same time. Pick them when they are completely blue and come off easily. Red shimmering berries at the base of the stem still need a few days. Blackbirds and thrushes love blueberries at least as much as we do. A bird protection net over the bushes, which is tightly closed at the bottom so that no birds get caught in it, protects the crop.

## Lingonberries and cranberries as neighbors

Lingonberries and cranberries also feel at home in the sour blueberry bed. Cranberries are low, evergreen dwarf shrubs that often produce twice a year and are well suited for underplanting. Cranberries, also known as large-fruited cranberries, crawl along the ground and need consistent moisture. Both go well with blueberries because they have the same soil and water requirements. This turns a single blueberry bed into a small berry corner with a harvest from July to October.

## Check and maintain the pH value

Cultivated blueberries need acidic soil with a pH of around four to five. A simple measuring set from the garden store will show you whether your soil or substrate fits in the pot. It's best to measure every spring because the pH value changes over time.

Tap water containing lime slowly raises the pH value. Therefore, water blueberries with rainwater if possible. A mulch layer of coniferous litter, pine bark or wood chips from coniferous trees keeps the soil acidic and moist. If the young leaves turn yellow while the leaf veins remain green, the pH value is usually too high and the plant can no longer absorb iron. Then acidic substrate, rainwater and a rhododendron or berry fertilizer help.

## Multiply your own blueberries

Blueberries can be propagated via cuttings. In early summer, cut semi-woody shoot tips about ten to fifteen centimeters long, remove the lower leaves and place them in a moist, acidic substrate. They will form roots after a few weeks under a hood or in a propagation box. However, it takes several years for a cutting to become a bearing shrub.

Many modern varieties are protected under plant variety protection law. Propagation is generally permitted for your own garden, but selling young plants is not.

## Preserving blueberries

Blueberries freeze well. To prevent them from freezing together into a lump, place them unwashed and dry next to each other on a tray, pre-freeze them and then fill them into bags. They are only washed before use. They are also suitable for jam, compote, cakes and as dried berries in muesli.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why are my blueberry leaves turning yellow?

The pH value is usually too high, often due to watering with lime-containing tap water. Measure the pH value, water only with rainwater, mulch with needle litter and, if necessary, lower the pH value with a sulfur preparation.

### Can I plant blueberries next to rhododendrons?

Yes, both need acidic soil. A common bed with blueberries, cranberries and rhododendrons is a nice solution.

### How many berries does a bush produce?

A well-cared-for, mature shrub can support several kilograms per year. The first notable harvests usually occur from the third year onwards.

### When do young blueberries bear?

Young bushes from the nursery often bear some berries in the first or second year. However, they only reach their full yield after several years when they have grown vigorously. On very young plants, remove some of the flowers so that they can initially gather strength for roots and shoots.

### Do blueberries need winter protection?

Planted cultivated blueberries are well hardy. However, the roots freeze more easily in the pot. Place the pots on a protected house wall in winter, wrap them with jute or coconut mats and water on frost-free days when the substrate is dry.

### Can I combine blueberries with other plants?

Yes, with plants that also like acidic soil, such as cranberries, cranberries, rhododendrons or azaleas. Together you can create a moor bed with an acidic substrate that supplies everyone.

Also water such a bed with rainwater.
