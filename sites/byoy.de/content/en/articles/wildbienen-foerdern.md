When the apple trees bloom in April, the crowns hum. A large part of this work is not done by honey bees, but by wild bees: mason bees, sand bees, bumblebees and many other species. Around 560 wild bee species live in Germany, and around half of them are considered endangered. A kitchen garden can be a valuable living space for them, and they reward it with better harvests of fruit, berries and many types of vegetables. But good intentions often go astray: many purchased insect hotels are worthless or even harmful. Here you can find out what really matters.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - About three quarters of wild bee species nest in the ground. Open, sunny areas of the ground are more important than any insect hotel.
> - Many species are specialized for certain plant families. Native wild plants are therefore irreplaceable.
> - Always drill nesting wood into the longitudinal wood, never into the end wood, and keep the holes clean and smooth.
> - Single flowers from March to October provide continuous nutrition.
> - Wild bees are peaceful. Most species can barely penetrate human skin with their stingers.

## Who are the wild bees?

Wild bees include all species of bees except the honey bee. Most live solitary lives: each female builds her own nest, lays eggs and supplies each brood cell with a supply of pollen and nectar. It then dies and the next generation often does not emerge until the following year. Only bumblebees and some furrow bees form annual colonies.

Wild bees are often more efficient at pollination than honey bees. Mason bees fly in cool weather, bumblebees even fly at temperatures just above 5 °C and in light rain, when honey bees stay in the hive. Some plants, such as tomatoes, are pollinated primarily by bumblebees, which vibrate the flowers to release pollen.

## Food: the right plants

Many species of wild bees only collect pollen from one plant family or even genus. The bluebell scissor bee, for example, needs bluebells, the viper's mason bee needs the viper's head. Without these plants the species cannot exist, no matter how good the nesting sites are. Other species are less picky, but also benefit from a diverse selection.

This is how you create a continuous range of flowers:

- **Spring:** Willow willow (male plants with pollen), cornelian cherry, lungwort, crocus, grape hyacinth, fruit trees, currants and gooseberries.
- **Early summer:** Viper's head, meadow sage, horn trefoil, bluebells, forget-me-nots, raspberries, strawberries, phacelia.
- **Midsummer:** Oregano, thyme, lavender, knapweed, wild mallow, borage, cornflower, tansy.
- **Late summer and autumn:** Ivy, sedum, asters, autumn crocuses, squash blossoms.

Important: Double flowers, such as those grown in many ornamental roses, dahlias or peonies, contain hardly any pollen and nectar. The stamens were grown into flower petals. Unfilled varieties and wild forms are much more valuable for bees.

In the vegetable garden, it is worth allowing individual plants to bloom: leeks, onions, rocket, radishes and carrots in the second year attract many wild bees. Herbs such as chives, thyme and savory are also popular bee pastures.

## Nesting places in the ground

The majority of wild bees dig nests in the ground: sand bees, furrow bees, silk bees and many others. They need sunny, open areas with only sparse vegetation, depending on the species in sandy or loamy soil. In tidy gardens with thick lawns and mulched beds, these areas are almost completely absent.

How to help ground nesters:

- Leave a few square meters of soil open in a sunny spot, such as at the edge of a path or on an embankment.
- Create a **sandarium**: a pit about 50 to 80 cm deep in a sunny spot, filled with unwashed, slightly clayey sand. The sand should be somewhat cohesive so that a moistened lump does not immediately disintegrate in your hand. Play sand is usually too fine and too clean.
- Joints between paving stones with sand instead of mortar also provide nesting sites.
- Keep the area free of thick vegetation, but do not disturb it in summer.

## Build nesting aids correctly

For species that nest above ground, nesting aids are useful if they are built correctly. Many commercial insect hotels contain materials that wild bees do not use or that endanger their brood.

### Hardwood with drill holes

Use seasoned hardwood such as ash, beech or oak. Softwood is too soft and fibrous. Drill the holes in the side of the wood, in the **longitudinal wood**, not in the end face with the annual rings. Cracks appear in the end wood into which moisture and fungi penetrate.

- Diameter 2 to 9 mm, most between 3 and 6 mm
- Depth 5 to 10 cm, correspondingly less for small diameters
- Distance between holes 1.5 to 2 cm
- Smooth the drill holes with sandpaper so that no fibers damage the wings

### Hollow stems

Reed or bamboo tubes with an inner diameter of 3 to 9 mm are popular nesting sites. Cut it just behind a knot so that one end is closed, making sure the opening is smooth and not frayed. Bundle them tightly in a can or wooden frame so birds don't pull them out.

### Pithaceous stems

Some species, such as club horn bees and certain masked bees, gnaw their nests themselves into stalks containing pith. Place cut stems of blackberries, raspberries, elderberries, mullein or mugwort vertically or at a slight angle, perhaps tied to a fence post. They will not be accepted lying down.

### What doesn't belong in a nesting aid

- Pine cones, wood shavings and straw: are not used by wild bees.
- Perforated bricks with large, rough cavities: rarely populated.
- Soft softwood and end grain drilling: cracks and mold.
- Glass tubes and plastic straws: Moisture cannot escape and the brood becomes fungal.

### The right place

Place the nesting aid in a sunny location, facing southeast to southwest, protected from rain and firmly installed. It shouldn't swing in the wind. A height of about 1 m or more above the ground is favorable. Leave them hanging all year round because the brood will develop in the closed tubes until next spring. A wire mesh with a distance of 2 to 3 cm in front of the nesting aid keeps woodpeckers and tits away.

## Further help

- **Water point:** A shallow dish with rocks or moss where bees can land and drink helps on hot days. Mason bees also need moist clay to close their brood cells. A small puddle of clay near the nesting aid is worth its weight in gold in spring.
- **Dead wood and dry stone walls:** Provide nesting places for many other insects.
- **Don't mow too often:** A part of the lawn used as a flower meadow or a strip that is only mowed two to three times a year allows clover, groundgrass and speedwell to bloom.
- **No insecticides:** Biological agents such as pyrethrum are also toxic to bees.

## Specialists: Bees with favorite plants

Many species of wild bees are picky. They only collect pollen from a specific plant family or even from a single genus. The bluebell scissor bee, for example, depends on bluebells, the viper's mason bee on the common viper's bee. If these plants are missing, the bee cannot provide for its brood, no matter how many other flowers the garden offers.

Anyone who wants to help such specialists should specifically plant native wild plants such as bluebells, viper's head, meadow sage or horn trefoil. A garden with many different native species therefore offers significantly more wild bees a basis for life than a garden with a few, albeit lushly flowering, ornamental plants.

## Wild bees throughout the year

The first wild bees fly on warm days in March, such as mason and sand bees, which visit pussy willows, crocuses and fruit blossoms. In summer there are wool bees, leaf cutter bees and many furrow bees. One of the latest species is the ivy silk bee, which uses ivy flowers almost exclusively in September and October.

It is therefore important for wild bees to have a range of flowers from early spring to autumn. Willows and early bloomers in spring, perennials and herbs in summer, and flowering ivy in fall all combine to form a continuous food chain.

## What harms wild bees

A large proportion of native wild bees nest in the ground. Sealed areas, tightly closed lawns and gravel gardens deprive them of nesting places. Plant protection products, mowing all the flowers at once and a lack of wild plants also affect them. Therefore, leave some open, sunny areas and sandy path edges and avoid using chemical agents in the garden.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do wild bees sting?

Only the females have a stinger, and most species can hardly penetrate human skin with it. They do not defend their nest like honey bees or wasps. A nesting aid on the terrace is also safe with children.

### Do honey bees compete with wild bees?

In areas with large numbers of bee colonies, competition for pollen and nectar can put wild bees at a disadvantage, especially specialized species. A rich supply of flowers alleviates the problem.

### Why is my nesting aid not colonized?

Common reasons include a place that is too shady, holes that are rough or too large, the wrong wood, or not enough food in the area. Sometimes it simply takes a year until the first bees discover them.

### How many wild bee species are there in Germany?

There are more than 550 species of wild bees known in Germany, including bumblebees. A large proportion of them are considered endangered. Every garden with native flowering plants and nesting opportunities helps to provide them with a habitat.

### Do purchased flowering mixtures help wild bees?

That depends on the mixture. Many inexpensive mixtures contain mainly non-native ornamental plants that only a few species can use. Mixtures with local wild plants from regional origin, so-called regional seeds, are better. They also help the specialized species. More about this in the guide [Creating a flowering meadow](/gartenwissen/bluehwiese-anlegen/).

When purchasing, also make sure that the mixture contains perennial species that bloom for years.

Annual mixtures, on the other hand, have to be sown again every year.
