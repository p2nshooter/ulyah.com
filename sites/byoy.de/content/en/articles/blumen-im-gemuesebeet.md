In old cottage gardens, vegetables and flowers always grew together: marigolds among the cabbages, nasturtiums on the fence, borage on the edge of the bed. It didn't just look nice. The flowers attract bees and bumblebees, which pollinate beans, cucumbers and zucchini. They feed on hoverflies and parasitic wasps, whose larvae eat aphids and caterpillars. Some flowers distract pests from vegetables, others improve the soil. A vegetable patch with flowers is therefore a lively, diverse system in which fewer problems arise than in a purely vegetable area. This guide introduces the best companion flowers and honestly explains what they can do.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Flowering companion plants feed beneficial insects such as hoverflies, lacewings and parasitic wasps, whose larvae eat pests.
> - Tagetes can suppress certain nematodes in the soil, but also attracts snails.
> - Nasturtiums attract aphids and can thus relieve pressure on other plants.
> - Marigold, borage, phacelia and cornflower are bee magnets and easy to grow.
> - Flowers belong at the edges of beds, at the ends of rows and in gaps, not so densely that they crowd the vegetables.

## What companion flowers do

**Feed beneficial insects:** Many beneficial insects are predatory as larvae, but need nectar and pollen as adults. Hoverflies, for example, lay their eggs in aphid colonies and their larvae eat hundreds of lice. The adult flies find food primarily on open, flat flowers such as marigold, chamomile, dill and phacelia. More about these helpers can be found in [Beneficial insects in the garden](/gartenwissen/nuetzlinge-im-garten/).

**Pollination:** Zucchini, squash, cucumbers, beans and many fruits require insects to produce fruit. Flowering plants in the bed attract bees and bumblebees to the right place.

**Distract:** Some plants are more attractive to certain pests than the vegetables. As trapping plants, they attract lice or snails.

**Soil:** Deep-rooted flowers loosen the soil, and some affect soil organisms.

It is important to have an honest expectation: companion flowers are not a pesticide. They shift the balance in the garden in favor of beneficial insects. The more diverse the garden, the more stable this balance becomes over the years.

### Tagetes and nematodes

Tagetes, especially the species *Tagetes patula* and *Tagetes erecta*, produce substances in their roots that can damage certain harmful roundworms, so-called nematodes, in the soil. However, the effect only occurs if Tagetes grows densely and over several months, ideally as a flat culture. A few individual plants between the tomatoes have little influence on the nematodes. At the same time, marigolds are very popular with snails. Some gardeners take advantage of this and deliberately plant them as an attractant plant at the edge of the bed, where they collect the snails every morning. More about this in [Nudibranchs without poison](/gartenwissen/schnecken-ohne-gift/).

### Nasturtium as a trap plant

Nasturtiums are often attacked by the black bean louse. When placed near beans or cabbage, it can attract lice and reduce stress on crops. Cut off severely infected shoots. It also covers the ground with its tendrils and keeps it moist. It is often planted as a ground cover under fruit trees.

## Where flowers belong in the vegetable patch

- **Bed borders:** A border of marigolds, cornflowers or nasturtiums frames the bed without taking up space.
- **Row Ends:** At the end of each row of vegetables add one or two flowers.
- **Gaps:** Where a vegetable plant has not started or space becomes available after harvest, a fast-growing flower comes into it.
- **Between heavy feeders:** There is a lot of space between squash and zucchini in early summer before the plants fill it up.
- **Own flowering strip:** A narrow strip of annual flowers next to the vegetable garden is a center for beneficial insects. [Creating a flowering meadow](/gartenwissen/bluehwiese-anlegen/) describes how you can turn it into a flower meadow.

Make sure the flowers do not shade or overgrow the vegetables. Borage and sunflowers grow large and belong on the edge.

## Edible flowers

You can eat many accompanying flowers: marigold, nasturtium, borage, cornflower, zucchini flowers, chive flowers and marigolds make salads colorful. Only eat flowers that you recognize for sure, that have not been sprayed, and that are not growing on the side of the road. Tagetes, cosmea and ornamental plants from the garden center are not intended for eating. People with allergies to daisy family should be careful with marigold and cornflower.

## Flowers throughout the year

So that beneficial insects can find food all year round, something should always be blooming. Plan companion flowers so that their bloom times overlap:

| period | Blooming Companions |
|---|---|
| April to May | Chives, forget-me-nots on the edge of the bed, self-sown marigolds sown early |
| June to July | Borage, cornflower, phacelia, dill, coriander, nasturtium |
| August to September | Marigold, Tagetes, Cosmea, Zinnia, Sunflower |
| October to frost | Marigold, late green manure phacelia, nasturtium |

Early spring is particularly valuable, when the first hoverflies and wild bees are on the move, but hardly anything is blooming in the vegetable garden. Early flowering perennials and bulbs at the edge of the vegetable garden close this gap. More about the needs of wild bees can be found in [Promoting wild bees](/gartenwissen/wildbienen-foerdern/).

## Harvest seeds yourself

Marigold, cornflower, borage, tagetes and nasturtium are seed-resistant plants whose seeds you can easily collect yourself. Allow some of the most beautiful flowers to mature, harvest the dry seed heads on a sunny day and store the seeds in a cool, dry place in paper bags. This way you have free companion flowers every year. [Grow your own seeds](/gartenwissen/saatgut-selbst-gewinnen/) explains how seed production works for vegetables.

## Flowers against pests: what is really proven

Many guides promise that certain flowers will repel pests. In some cases there is good evidence, such as marigolds, whose roots push back certain nematodes in the soil if they grow densely and for an entire season. However, many other promises that a single scent keeps pests away are hardly proven.

The actual use of flowers lies elsewhere: they attract beneficial insects. Hoverflies, lacewings and tiny parasitic wasps need nectar and pollen as adults, but their larvae eat aphids and other pests. Flowers with easily accessible nectar are particularly valuable, for example from umbelliferous plants such as dill and coriander or from simple daisy family plants such as marigold and cornflower. If you place them among the vegetables, the pest controllers will come straight to the bed.

## Flowers that don't belong in the vegetable patch

Not every beautiful flower fits next to the vegetables. Poisonous plants such as foxglove, monkshood or larkspur have no place in the vegetable patch, especially if children are gardening with them or leaves could be confused. Even very tall, overgrown species that shade the vegetables, or plants that spread over the entire bed with root runners, cause more work than they are useful.

Be careful with flowers purchased from the garden center: they have often been treated with pesticides and are not intended for consumption. If you want to harvest edible flowers, it is best to grow the plants yourself from organic seeds.

## Frequently asked questions

### Don't flowers take nutrients away from vegetables?

Annual companion flowers need little and are usually on the edge. With reasonable spacing, competition is low and the benefits from beneficial insects and pollinators are significantly greater.

### Don't the flowers seed themselves everywhere?

Marigold and borage like to do this. You can simply pull out or transplant young seedlings in unfavorable places.

### Can I use herbs instead of flowers?

Yes. Flowering dill, coriander, thyme, oregano and chives are excellent sources of nectar. Let some herbs bloom consciously instead of harvesting them all.

### Do flowers attract snails?

Some do. Marigolds, for example, are extremely popular with snails. You can use this: A few marigolds on the edge of the bed distract snails from the vegetables and make it easier to collect them in the evening. If you have a lot of snails in your garden, choose flowers that avoid snails, such as nasturtium or borage, for the inside of the bed.

### When is the best time to sow companion flowers?

You can sow marigolds, cornflowers and borage directly into the bed from April as soon as the ground is no longer frozen. Nasturtiums and marigolds are sensitive to frost and are only brought outside after the ice saints or are grown on the windowsill. If you want flowers early in the year, plant some spring bulbs like crocuses at the edge of the bed in autumn.

### How many flowers do I need in a bed?

Even a few plants make a difference. A row of marigolds at the edge or individual flowers every few meters between the rows of vegetables are enough to attract beneficial insects without taking up a lot of space for the vegetables.

But more flowers won't hurt as long as the vegetables get enough light.
