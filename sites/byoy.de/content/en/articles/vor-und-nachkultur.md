A vegetable patch where tomatoes grow from May to September and then lies fallow is only half used. The season in Germany is long enough to harvest two or even three crops one after the other on many beds. Spinach is already growing in March, beans or cabbage follow in the summer, and lamb's lettuce awaits winter in the fall. If you plan this sequence, you will harvest significantly more from the same area, and the soil will remain covered and alive almost all year round.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - **Pre-crops** grow quickly in the cool spring: spinach, radishes, lettuce, kohlrabi, peas, new potatoes.
> - **Main crops** cover the bed in summer: tomatoes, pumpkin, cabbage, leeks, beans, carrots.
> - **Subcultures** will follow from July: endives, Chinese cabbage, sugar loaf, lamb's lettuce, winter spinach, autumn beets.
> - After each harvest, till the bed again immediately, otherwise the weeds will take over.
> - Change families in the sequence: cruciferous vegetables not after cruciferous vegetables.

## The principle: time is also space

Every type of vegetable has a cultivation period, the time from sowing or planting to harvest. In good conditions, radishes need four to six weeks, lettuce six to eight weeks, and kohlrabi around ten weeks from planting. Heat-loving crops such as tomatoes or beans, on the other hand, can only be grown outdoors from mid-May. Until then, the bed will lie empty if you don't use it.

Likewise, the harvest of many summer crops ends early. New potatoes are harvested at the end of June, garlic in July, and peas at the end of June to mid-July. After that, there are still three to four months of growth time left, enough for a full-fledged after-culture.

## Pre-crops for spring

Pre-crops must meet two conditions: they can tolerate cold and light frost, and they are harvested before the main crop needs the bed.

| pre-culture | Sowing or planting | Harvest | Fits in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spinach | March to early April | End of April to mid-May | Tomatoes, zucchini, beans |
| Radishes | from the beginning of March | after 4 to 6 weeks | Cucumbers, beans, pumpkin |
| Pick lettuce, cut lettuce | March to April | from the end of April | Tomatoes, cabbage |
| Kohlrabi (early) | Planting at the end of March to April | Mid-May to June | Beans, leeks, endive |
| Peas (shell peas) | March to April | June to early July | Kale, endive, Chinese cabbage |
| New potatoes | Early to mid April | Mid-June to early July | Lamb's lettuce, kale, autumn turnips |
| Onion sets for spring onions | March | May to June | Beans, pumpkin |

A pre-culture is particularly worthwhile under tomatoes, pumpkin and zucchini. These plants come into the bed in mid-May, but are initially small. Spinach or lettuce, sown in March, can be harvested by then or left around the young plants for a few weeks.

## Main crops

The main crop occupies the bed for the longest time and determines its place in the crop rotation. Fertilization suits this: heavy feeders such as tomatoes, cabbage and pumpkin receive compost in spring, medium feeders such as carrots and beetroot receive less, and weak feeders such as beans and peas receive almost nothing.

Some main crops stand for so long that there is no time for subsequent crops: leeks for the winter, Brussels sprouts, parsnips, storage carrots or celery. A pre-culture is even more valuable on these beds.

## Re-cropping from July

Secondary crops grow into a time when the days are getting shorter. This has advantages: many leafy vegetables no longer bloom in the short days. Spinach and Chinese cabbage, for example, tend to sprout in early summer and produce beautiful leaves and heads in autumn. The disadvantage: Growth slows significantly from September onwards. The sowing dates are therefore important.

| Post-culture | Sowing or planting | Harvest | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| French beans | sow until the beginning of July | September | of potatoes, garlic, peas |
| Endive | Planting July to early August | September to November | tolerates light frost |
| Sugar loaf, radicchio | Sow June to mid-July | October to December | very frost hardy, stores well |
| Chinese cabbage | Sow mid to late July | October | If sown earlier it shoots easily |
| Bok choy | Sow late July to mid-August | September to October | fast, culture protection net against flea beetles |
| Kohlrabi | Planting by early August | October | Choose robust autumn varieties |
| Autumn turnips, May turnips | Sow July to early August | October to November | Cruciferous vegetables, not cabbage |
| Kale | Planting June to mid-July | November to February | most aromatic after frost |
| Lamb's lettuce | Sow mid-August to mid-September | October to March | Sow on firm, weed-free soil |
| Winter spinach | Sow late August to mid-September | October and from March | choose winter-hardy varieties |
| Winter purslane | Sowing September | November to April | very hardy |
| Arugula | Sow August to early September | September to November | milder in autumn than in summer |

## Tried and tested sequences

Some combinations have proven successful in many gardens:

1. **Early potatoes → lamb's lettuce:** After the potato harvest at the end of June, the soil is loose and weed-free. In August you sow lamb's lettuce, which is harvested over the winter.
2. **Peas → Kale:** The peas leave nitrogen in the soil that the kale can put to good use. Cut the peas, leave the roots in the ground and plant the kale seedlings directly between them.
3. **Spinach → Tomatoes → Winter Purslane:** A triple-use bed. The spinach is harvested in May, the tomatoes are available until the end of September, after which winter purslane grows until spring.
4. **Garlic → Endive or Chinese cabbage:** The garlic is harvested in July, after which it is exactly the right time for endives.
5. **Radish and lettuce → bush beans → lamb's lettuce:** Three crops in one year are possible on a bed with weak feeders.

## Cultivate the soil between crops

After each harvest, the bed needs a little attention before the next crop comes:

- **Harvest residue:** You can incorporate healthy leaves and roots flat or compost them. Dispose of diseased plant parts in the organic waste bin.
- **Loosening:** A digging fork or sow tooth loosens the soil without turning over the layers.
- **Refertilization:** For secondary crops with medium requirements such as endive or Chinese cabbage, 1 to 2 liters of mature compost per square meter is sufficient. Lamb's lettuce and winter purslane don't need anything.
- **Watering:** In midsummer the soil is often dry. Water thoroughly before sowing or planting and keep seeds moist until they germinate. A fleece or a board over the seed row helps prevent drying out in July and August.

## Pay attention to families

The principles of crop rotation also apply within a year. If cabbage follows radishes and then autumn turnips, there are three cruciferous vegetables in a row. This encourages club root, flea beetles and cabbage fly. Plan the sequence so that families alternate, for example cruciferous vegetables, then legumes, then umbelliferous plants or goosefoot plants.

| Family | Examples |
|---|---|
| Cruciferous vegetables | Cabbage, kohlrabi, radishes, radish, arugula, bok choy, turnips |
| legumes | Beans, peas, broad beans |
| Umbelliferous plants | Carrots, parsnips, fennel, celery, parsley |
| Foxtail family | Spinach, chard, beetroot |
| Asteraceae | Lettuce, endive, sugar loaf, radicchio |
| Nightshade family | Tomatoes, potatoes, peppers |
| Cucurbitaceae | Cucumbers, zucchini, pumpkin |
| Onion family | Onions, leeks, garlic |
| Valerian family | Lamb's lettuce |

## Plans on paper

The easiest way to plan pre- and post-crop winter crops is on paper. Draw each bed as a rectangle and divide it into three columns: spring, summer, fall. Enter the crops with sowing and harvest dates. This way you can immediately see where gaps are emerging and can bring young plants forward in good time for subsequent crops. For example, in June you sow endives, kale and sugarloaf in growing trays or in a small seedbed so that they are ready when a bed becomes available in July.

## Know cultural times

Anyone planning pre- and post-cultures must know how long a culture will occupy the bed. Radishes are often ready to harvest after around four weeks, lettuce and spinach after around six to eight weeks, and early kohlrabi after eight to ten weeks. Bush beans usually take ten to twelve weeks from sowing to harvesting. The exact cultivation period is stated on the seed bag and depends on the variety, weather and season.

Enter these times into your bed plan and calculate backwards: If a main crop is to be added to the bed in June, the previous crop must be harvested by the end of May at the latest. This way you can quickly see which combinations fit in time and where things get tight.

## Re-cropping for the winter

With well-planned subsequent crops, you can harvest well into the winter. Lamb's lettuce is sown from August to September and can be harvested throughout the winter if the weather is mild. Winter purslane, winter salads and spinach from a late summer seed survive light frosts and provide fresh greens in autumn and early spring.

Kale, Brussels sprouts and leeks are planted in early summer and last until winter. And October is the right time to plant garlic, which will root over the winter and be harvested next summer. This means that hardly any bed remains empty over the winter.

## Frequently asked questions

### Isn't the soil being depleted by so many crops?

Not if you provide it with compost and mulch and include weak feeders and legumes. A vegetated soil actually loses fewer nutrients than a fallow one because the plants hold on to them instead of rain washing them out.

### What to do if there is no time for post-culture?

Sow a green manure. Phacelia, buckwheat or mustard until the end of August, winter rye or winter vetch until October. This way the floor remains protected.

### Can I sow secondary crops directly or do I have to prefer?

Both are possible. You sow lamb's lettuce, spinach, beets and rocket directly. Endive, Chinese cabbage, kale and sugar loaf grow more securely if you prefer them and plant them as young plants because the bed is then occupied for less time.

### Is a cold frame worth it for pre- and post-crop crops?

Yes very. In the cold frame you can sow previous crops a few weeks earlier and harvest subsequent crops longer in the fall. This will help you gain time at both ends of the season. More about this in the guide [Using cold frames](/gartenwissen/fruehbeet-nutzen/).

### Do I have to fertilize between two crops?

After an undemanding previous crop such as radishes or lettuce, a thin layer of compost is often sufficient before the main crop comes. If a heavy feeder is followed by a secondary crop such as lamb's lettuce, no additional fertilization is usually necessary.

This saves fertilizer and protects groundwater.
