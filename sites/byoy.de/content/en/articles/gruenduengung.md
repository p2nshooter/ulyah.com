A bed that lies naked over the winter after the harvest loses. Rain washes away nutrients, the surface becomes muddy, and weeds are the first to grow in spring. Nature knows no open ground: as soon as an area is free, something grows on it. Green manure makes use of this principle. You specifically sow plants that cover the ground, loosen them with their roots, hold on to nutrients and later return to the ground as mulch or humus. It is one of the simplest and cheapest methods of making garden soil more fertile over the years.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Green manure covers the soil, loosens it with roots, builds humus, retains nutrients and suppresses weeds.
> - Phacelia is not related to any vegetables and therefore fits into any crop rotation.
> - Mustard and radish are cruciferous vegetables like cabbage. Before or after cabbage they can promote clubroot.
> - Legumes such as clover, vetches and lupins collect nitrogen from the air using nodule bacteria.
> - Freezing species such as phacelia, mustard and buckwheat die in winter and form a mulch cover. Hardy species such as rye and vetch continue to grow in spring.

## What green manure does

- **Ground cover:** The plants protect the soil from heavy rain, erosion, drying out and siltation.
- **Loosening:** Deep roots, such as oil radish, lupine and alfalfa, break up compacted layers. Dead roots leave tubes through which air and water enter.
- **Humus buildup:** Roots and above-ground plant matter become food for earthworms and microorganisms.
- **Retain nutrients:** Nitrogen remaining in the soil after harvest is absorbed by the green manure instead of being washed out into the groundwater. He will be released later.
- **Collect nitrogen:** Legumes bind nitrogen from the air using nodule bacteria.
- **Suppress weeds:** A dense stand leaves little light for weeds.
- **Food for insects:** Phacelia, buckwheat and clover are excellent bee pastures.

## The most important green manure plants

The quantities are guidelines. Follow the instructions on the seed package. There are also ready-made green manure mixtures whose composition you should check before buying, especially if they contain cruciferous vegetables.

## The Clubroot Trap

Mustard and radish are popular because they are quick and cheap. As cruciferous vegetables, they are related to cabbage, kohlrabi, radishes and rocket. Clubroot, a soil-borne disease that deforms the roots of cruciferous vegetables into tubers, can persist and multiply through them in the soil. Their permanent spores survive for many years. Therefore, only sow mustard and oil radish in areas where there will be no cabbage in the next few years, or go straight for phacelia and buckwheat. [Planning crop rotation](/gartenwissen/fruchtfolge-planen/) explains how families migrate in crop rotation.

## When to sow?

**Summer green manure:** Between two crops, for example after early potatoes or peas in June or July, you can sow buckwheat or phacelia for six to eight weeks. They bloom quickly and are mowed before the next crop.

**Autumn green manure:** The most common form. After the harvest from August to mid-September, sow phacelia, mustard, oil radish or crimson clover. They grow until frost, then freeze and form a mulch over the winter.

**Winter green manure:** On areas that will not be used again until late spring, sow winter rye with winter vetch until the beginning of October. They overwinter green and form a lot of mass in spring.

Cover vacated beds in late autumn, for which it is too late for green manure, with leaves, as described in [Use leaves in the garden](/gartenwissen/laub-im-garten-nutzen/).

## sowing

1. Clear the bed and remove large weeds.
2. Loosen the surface shallowly with a hoe or rake.
3. Sow widely: Mix fine seeds such as Phacelia with a little sand so that they are distributed more evenly.
4. Gently rake the seeds and press the surface down, perhaps with the back of a rake or a board.
5. Keep the area moist until the seeds emerge. In late summer this often happens after a few days.

## Incorporate green manure in spring

Frozen green manure lies on the bed as a thin layer of mulch in spring. You can plant directly through it or rake the residue to the side if you want to sow fine seeds like carrots.

You should mow hardy green manure such as rye and vetch in the spring before it forms seeds, at the latest when it blooms. Allow the clippings to wilt for two to three days and then work them flat into the top two to four inches of soil, or leave them as mulch. There should be two to four weeks between incorporation and the next sowing because rotting plant matter temporarily binds nitrogen and can inhibit germinating seeds. Planted young plants tolerate this better than direct sowing.

Deep plowing is not necessary and not desirable in an organic garden. [Gardening without digging](/gartenwissen/gaertnern-ohne-umgraben/) explains how you can garden without digging.

## Green manure in crop rotation

Green manure can be easily incorporated into a cultivation plan. An example of four beds that move on every year:

| bed | main culture | Then in late summer |
|---|---|---|
| Heavy feeder | Pumpkin, zucchini, cabbage | Winter rye with vetch if cleared early, otherwise leaves |
| middle eater | Carrots, onions, beets | Phacelia |
| weak eater | Beans, peas | Phacelia or buckwheat after peas |
| recovery bed | Herbs, strawberries or green manure | Crimson clover for the next heavy eater |

It is important to think about the green manure family. Phacelia and buckwheat fit almost everywhere, cruciferous vegetables only where there is no cabbage for a long time. More about this in [Plan crop rotation](/gartenwissen/fruchtfolge-planen/).

## Green manure in allotments and on rental areas

In allotment gardens there is often a requirement that part of the area must be used for gardening. Green manure is a recognized cultural measure and shows that a bed is consciously cared for, even if no vegetables are growing on it. Phacelia and marigolds look so beautiful that they often spark conversations over the fence. If you only have an area free for a few weeks, sow fast-growing buckwheat, which forms a dense cover after just six weeks.

## Green manure for bees and other insects

Many green manure plants bloom and are valuable food sources for insects. Phacelia, also known as bee friend, is particularly popular: It is not related to any common vegetable family, therefore fits well into any crop rotation and is heavily visited by bumblebees and bees. Buckwheat and sunflowers also provide nectar and pollen.

If you want to use the flowers for insects, let the green manure bloom for a few weeks, but cut it before mature seeds form. Otherwise the plants will sow themselves throughout the bed next year.

## Frost-free and winter-hardy species

Green manure plants can be divided into two groups. Species that freeze off, such as phacelia, buckwheat, mustard or oil radish, die with the first severe frost. Their remains remain on the bed as a layer of mulch, protect the soil over the winter and can easily be worked flat or composted in the spring. Note that mustard and radish are cruciferous vegetables and should therefore not be placed before or after cabbage.

Hardy species such as winter rye, winter vetch or certain types of clover continue to grow over the winter and keep the ground covered. In the spring you have to mow them down in time before the next crop and work them in superficially so that they can decompose. They are particularly suitable for beds that will not be replanted until late in spring.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I also use green manure in a small garden?

Yes, it's worth it even on a single vacant bed. Phacelia also looks very beautiful in flower and attracts many insects.

### What do I do if the green manure forms seeds?

Mow them beforehand. Otherwise, phacelia and buckwheat will self-sow next year. This is usually not a big problem, but can be annoying in beds with fine seeds.

### Is clover worth having in the vegetable garden?

Crimson clover and other types of clover collect nitrogen and are ideal from heavy feeders such as cabbage or pumpkin. However, they grow slower than Phacelia, so sow early.

### How many seeds do I need?

The amount required is stated on the package, usually in grams per square meter. Fine seeds like Phacelia only need a few grams per square meter, large seeds like peas or beans need significantly more. It is better to sow a little more densely than too thinly, because a dense stand suppresses weeds better.

### Do I have to dig up green manure?

No. It is enough to mow down the plants or let them freeze and leave the remains as mulch or work them in flat with a hoe. Deep digging is not necessary and disturbs the soil life. If you garden without digging, you can find out more about this in the guide [Gardening without digging] (/gartenwissen/gaertnern-ohne-umgraben/).

### Can I sow green manure between rows of vegetables?

Yes, low-growing species such as white clover or yellow clover can be sown as undersowing between tall crops such as corn or cabbage. They cover the soil, suppress weeds and collect nitrogen. Water a little more during dry periods because vegetables and underseeds compete for water.

A layer of mulch on the rows also mitigates competition.

This means that no soil is left bare between the rows.
