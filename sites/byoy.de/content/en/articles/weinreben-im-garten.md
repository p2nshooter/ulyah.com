A grape vine on the wall of the house, over the pergola or on the garden fence is a beautiful sight and delivers sweet grapes straight from the vine in late summer. Many gardeners don't dare to approach grape vines because they think pruning is a science and fear fungal diseases. With the right variety and a few basic rules, even beginners can grow table grapes in the home garden.

This guide explains which varieties are suitable, which location is ideal, how to plant and train a vine, how pruning works and how to keep the grapes healthy until harvest.

## Table grapes and wine grapes

Table grapes are usually planted in the home garden, i.e. varieties that were bred for fresh consumption. They have larger berries, often a thinner skin and some are seedless. Wine grapes for wine production are smaller, denser and more acidic, and there are specific rules for making wine for personal use. For enjoyment fresh from the vine, table grapes are the right choice.

## Fungus-resistant varieties

The biggest challenge in viticulture are fungal diseases, especially powdery mildew and downy mildew. In the home garden, where no one wants to spray regularly, fungus-resistant varieties are the best choice. They are often referred to as PIWI varieties and are the result of crosses with hardy wild vines.

Pay attention to the following points when purchasing:

- **Resistance:** The variety description should indicate good resistance to powdery mildew and downy mildew.
- **Ripening time:** In cooler regions, early ripening varieties are important so that the grapes ripen before fall.
- **Seedless or with seeds:** Seedless varieties are popular with children, while varieties with seeds are often more aromatic.
- **Color:** There are white, pink and blue table grapes. Blue varieties sometimes require a little more warmth.

Get advice from a tree nursery with a variety of grapes about which varieties are tried and tested in your region.

## The right location

Grape vines love warmth and sun. A south or southwest wall is ideal, where the heat is stored during the day and released at night. A sunny pergola or a fence in full sun is also suitable. In the shade, grapes ripen poorly and become more susceptible to disease.

The soil should be permeable, because vines cannot tolerate waterlogging. Otherwise they are undemanding and root very deeply over time. On house walls, make sure that the rain reaches the plant, or water regularly in the first few years, as the soil often remains dry under eaves.

## Plants

The best time to plant is spring when the ground has warmed, but container plants can be planted from spring to fall.

1. Dig a large planting hole and loosen the ground well.
2. Plant the vine about 8 to 12 inches from the wall and tilt it slightly toward the wall.
3. Plant them so deep that the grafting area, the thickened area on the lower trunk, is about a hand's width above the soil.
4. Fill with soil and some mature compost and water heavily.
5. Install a sturdy trellis or wires right away on which the vine will be trained over the next few years.

## Training on a trellis

Without training, a grapevine grows into a dense, confusing tangle with a few small bunches. That's why she is raised to a solid framework in the first few years. Training with a trunk and horizontal arms on wires, similar to that in a vineyard, has proven successful.

In the first year you only let the strongest shoot grow and tie it vertically. In the second year, this shoot becomes a trunk and you pull one or two horizontal arms from it at the desired height. In the following years, new fruit shoots form on these arms every year. It is important that the vine retains its structure and is pruned back vigorously every year.

## The winter cut

Vines bear on shoots that grow in the same year from the previous year's wood. So that they produce strong fruit shoots every year, they are cut back heavily in winter. The best time is late winter, around February, on a frost-free day. If you cut too late in the spring, when the sap is already rising, you will find that the vine “bleeds” heavily, i.e. it loses juice from the cuts.

A simple method for home gardens is pruning: the previous year's shoots that grew on the horizontal arms are shortened to short cones with two eyes, i.e. two buds. The new fruit shoots grow from these eyes in spring. Old, worn-out cones are gradually replaced with new ones.

## The summer cut

The vine also needs attention in summer so that the grapes get light and air:

- **Breaking out:** In the spring, remove excess, weak shoots so that only one or two strong shoots remain on each cone.
- **Shortening:** Fruit shoots are shortened a few leaves above the last cluster so that the vine puts its strength into the fruit.
- **Stingy shoots:** Shorten the small side shoots in the leaf axils to one or two leaves.
- **Deleafing:** In late summer, remove individual leaves from around the grapes so that they dry and ripen better. But don't remove too many or the berries will get sunburnt.

## Diseases and pests

Powdery mildew appears as a white-gray, mealy coating on leaves and berries that later split open. Downy mildew forms oily spots on the top of the leaves and a white fuzz on the underside and occurs in damp weather. In addition to resistant varieties, an airy location, regular summer pruning and removing infected leaves help. More about this in the guide [Preventing fungal diseases] (/gartenwissen/pilzkrankheiten-vorbeugen/).

Shortly before maturity, wasps and birds become the biggest competition. Organza bags that are pulled over the individual grapes provide reliable protection, allow light to pass through and prevent wasps from getting to pecked berries. A net over the entire vine keeps birds away.

## Harvest and utilization

Grapes do not ripen on the vine after they have been picked. Only harvest them when they have reached their typical color, the berries have become slightly soft and taste sweet. It's best to try a few berries from different parts of the grape, as ripeness can vary. Cut off the whole bunches with scissors.

Fresh grapes last a few days in the refrigerator. If you have a lot, you can make grape juice, jelly or dry seedless varieties into raisins. The young leaves are also edible and are stuffed in many kitchens.

## Grape vines in a bucket

Vines can also thrive on balconies and terraces if the pot is large enough, at least around 40 to 50 liters. Choose a compact, early-ripening variety, place the container in the sunniest spot and water regularly. In winter, the bucket needs protection from freezing, such as a cover made of jute or coconut mats and a protected place on the wall of the house.

## Practical example: The vine on the garage wall

A reader planted a fungus-resistant, early-ripening table grape on his sunny garage wall. For the first two years he trained only one trunk and two horizontal arms on taut wires and left no grapes for the vine to root strongly.

From the third year onwards, every February he cut back the previous year's shoots to cones with two eyes and removed excess shoots in the summer. In August he protected the grapes from wasps with organza bags. Since then, he has harvested sweet grapes every year without ever spraying any pesticides.

## Watering and fertilizing

Young vines need regular water during dry periods in the first two years until their roots reach deep enough. Older vines have so deep roots that they usually survive in the garden without additional watering, except in very dry summers or on house walls under a roof overhang.

The same applies to fertilizing: less is more. A layer of mature compost in spring is sufficient in most gardens. Too much nitrogen makes the vine grow too much foliage, makes the shoots soft and susceptible to fungal diseases and delays the ripening of the grapes. A layer of mulch under the vine keeps the soil moist and promotes soil life.

## Frequently asked questions

### When does a young vine produce its first grapes?

Usually in the second or third year after planting. It is worth removing inflorescences in the first two years so that the vine puts its strength into its roots and framework.

### Are grape vines hardy?

Most table grapes tolerate Central European winters well when they are planted out. You protect young vines in the first few winters with a layer of leaves or soil around the grafting area. Potted plants need protection for the root ball.

### Why are my grapes bursting?

Often after heavy rainfall shortly before ripening, when the berries quickly absorb a lot of water. Powdery mildew also causes berries to crack. An even water supply and an airy cut prevent this.

### Can I grow a vine from a cutting stick?

Yes, vines are easy to propagate from cuttings in winter. However, they then grow on their own roots and not on a support, which in some areas increases the risk of root pests. For the home garden, grafted plants from the nursery are usually the safer choice.

### Does a vine also grow in partial shade?

They are growing, but the grapes ripen poorly there and remain sour. Plan for at least a few hours of direct sun a day for a vine, preferably a spot in full sun. In shadier gardens, early maturing varieties are the only realistic choice.

## Conclusion

With a fungus-resistant table grape, a sunny, warm location and a solid framework of trunk and arms, wine growing can also be achieved in the home garden. The vigorous winter pruning to short cones and a few steps in the summer ensure healthy grapes, and organza bags protect the harvest from wasps. This means that the vine on the house wall becomes a long-lasting, productive companion in the garden.
