Anyone who plants a fruit tree is planning for decades. With good care, an apple tree can last for fifty years or longer, and a standard tree in an orchard can often last even longer. The decisions at the beginning are all the more important: the right variety, the right tree shape for the available space, a good location and careful planting. Mistakes can hardly be corrected later, while a well-planted tree bears the first fruit after just a few years and grows stronger with each passing year.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - The best time to plant is autumn, from October to early December. You can plant bare-root trees until March if the ground is frost-free, and container trees almost all year round.
> - The rootstock determines how big the tree will be. Weakly growing rootstocks and bush trees or spindles are suitable for small gardens.
> - Apples, pears and many sweet cherries need a pollinator variety nearby.
> - The grafting point must remain a hand's breadth above the ground. Place the stake on the weather side and tie the tree with a figure-eight loop.
> - A wire basket around the root ball protects against voles. After planting, water thoroughly and carry out plant pruning.

## Which tree suits your garden?

### documents

Almost all fruit trees are grafted: a noble variety such as 'Elstar' or 'Topaz' was placed on a base that forms the roots. The rootstock determines growth vigor, size, start of fruiting and location requirements. Weak-growing apple rootstocks produce small trees that bear early but need good soil and a permanent stake. Strongly growing seedling rootstocks produce large, long-lasting standard stems that can also cope with poor soils. When purchasing from the tree nursery, specifically ask about the substrate and get advice on which one suits your soil.

### Pollinator varieties

Apples and pears are generally self-infertile. They need pollen from another variety of the same fruit that is flowering at the same time. Many sweet cherries also need a suitable partner variety. If there is a suitable tree in the neighborhood within a radius of about a hundred meters, that is often enough. Otherwise you will plant two varieties that will cross-pollinate each other. Good nurseries name suitable pollinator varieties. Sour cherries, many plums and plums as well as peaches, on the other hand, are mostly self-fertile.

## The right location

- **Sun:** Fruit trees need at least half a day of sun, but more for sweet, aromatic fruits.
- **Soil:** deep, permeable, humus. Most types of fruit cannot tolerate waterlogging.
- **Late frost:** Depressions and hollows in which cold air collects are unfavorable because late frosts destroy the flowers.
- **Soil fatigue:** Don't plant an apple where an old apple just stood. Rose plants such as apples, pears and quinces often grow poorly compared to their counterparts. If there is no other option, replace a large portion of the soil.
- **Distance:** Plan the space based on the final size, not the young tree. Depending on the federal state, there are legal minimum distances for trees and bushes from property boundaries. Do your research before planting.

## When to plant?

**Autumn** is ideal: the ground is still warm, the roots grow until frost, and in spring the tree starts off with a head start. You buy **bare-rooted trees**, i.e. without soil balls, from the end of October to March and plant them on frost-free days. They are cheaper and grow very well when planted correctly. You can plant **container trees** almost all year round, except in frost and high heat. Trees planted in summer need a lot of water.

## Planting step by step

1. **Watering roots:** Place bare-root trees in a bucket of water for a few hours before planting. Dip container goods until no more air bubbles appear.
2. **Cut roots:** For bare-root trees, cut back damaged roots and shorten long roots slightly to create fresh cut surfaces.
3. **Dig a planting hole:** about twice as wide and deep as the root ball. Loosen the bottom of the hole with the digging fork.
4. **Vole protection:** Place a basket made of ungalvanized or galvanized wire mesh with a mesh width of about 13 millimeters in the hole. It protects the sensitive roots in the first few years.
5. **Setting a stake:** Drive in a stake before planting, on the side where the wind usually comes from, in Germany often the west side. This way you don't hurt any roots.
6. **Check planting depth:** Place the tree in the hole. The grafting point, the thickening on the lower trunk, must remain about a hand's breadth above the future ground level. If it lies in the ground, the noble variety forms its own roots and the effect of the rootstock is lost.
7. **Filling:** Mix the excavated soil with a little mature compost, but not more than a third, and fill it between the roots. Shake the tree slightly to avoid leaving any voids and carefully tamp down the soil.
8. **Tying:** Tie the trunk to the post using a coconut rope in a figure-eight loop so that the rope does not grow into the bark. The post ends below the crown.
9. **Watering edge and water:** Form a mound of earth around the tree disc and water ten to twenty liters, even in damp weather. The water washes the soil to the roots.

## The planting cut

Bare-rooted trees have lost roots when dug up. The planting cut restores the balance between roots and crown and determines the future crown shape. For a young bush tree or half-trunk, choose a central shoot and three to four evenly spaced side shoots as leading branches. Cut off excess shoots at the base. Shorten the leading branches by about a third, each above an outward-facing bud, so that their ends are approximately at the same height. The central shoot remains about a hand's breadth longer. [Fruit tree pruning](/gartenwissen/obstbaumschnitt/) explains what happens next.

## Care in the first years

- **Watering:** In the first two to three years, water thoroughly once a week in dry conditions, preferably rarely and a lot rather than often and little.
- **Keep the tree disc clear:** A circle of approximately one meter around the trunk remains free of grass. Grass competes strongly for water and nitrogen. Mulch the tree slice with grass clippings or compost as described in [Mulching in the Vegetable Garden](/gartenwissen/mulchen-im-gemuesegarten/).
- **Fertilize:** Add some compost to the tree disc in spring. A young fruit tree doesn't need much more.
- **Trunk protection:** A white coat of lime in autumn protects young trunks from frost cracks caused by winter sun. Wire trousers help against game browsing.
- **Check the connection:** Loosen the rope as soon as it becomes constricted and remove the stake after a few years for strong trees on strong bases. Weak rootstocks need permanent support.

## Protection against voles, game and frost cracks

Young fruit trees have many enemies. Voles eat the roots, especially of apple trees. A wire basket that you place around the root ball when planting will protect the tree in the first few years until the roots are strong enough. Where rabbits or deer come into the garden, a trunk protection sleeve prevents them from gnawing or rubbing against the bark.

In winter, the low sun can warm the bark on the south side significantly during the day, while it freezes again at night. This creates frost cracks through which pathogens penetrate. A white trunk paint made from lime or a special tree paint reflects sunlight and prevents these cracks.

## Watering in the first summer

A freshly planted fruit tree has only a few roots and depends on your help in its first summer. During dry periods, water thoroughly once a week with several watering cans instead of a little every day. This is how the roots grow deeper. A watering rim made of soil around the tree disc prevents the water from running away to the side.

A layer of mulch on the tree disc keeps moisture in the soil and suppresses grass that robs the young tree of water and nutrients. Keep the mulch a few inches away from the trunk.

## Frequently asked questions

### When will my tree bear the first fruit?

Spindles and bush trees on weak rootstocks often already in the second or third year, standard trees only after six to ten years. Remove some of the fruit in the first year or two to ensure the tree retains strength for growth.

### Which apple varieties are robust?

Varieties with low susceptibility to scab and mildew are important for organic gardens. Many old regional varieties and newer scab-resistant varieties are well suited. Get advice from a regional tree nursery about which varieties grow well in your area.

### Can I grow a fruit tree in a container?

Yes, columnar fruit and trees on very weak rootstocks grow in large pots of around 40 liters. You need regular watering, fertilizing and winter protection for the pot.

### Does my fruit tree need a stake?

In the first few years yes, often permanently for trees on weakly growing rootstock. Drive in the stake before planting to avoid damaging the roots, and tie the trunk loosely with a coconut rope in the shape of a figure eight to avoid chafing the bark.

### What is a planting cut and do I have to do it?

When pruning, the leading branches are shortened after planting so that the crown and roots are in balance and a good crown shape is formed. Bare-rooted trees absolutely need it, container trees usually need it less. The section above on planting cuts explains how it works.
