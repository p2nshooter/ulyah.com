Freshly picked raspberries, still warm from the sun, are one of the greatest joys in the garden. In the store they are expensive, delicate and often mushy after a day. In your own garden, however, they grow almost by themselves once they have grown. Raspberries are forest edge plants: They love humus-rich, loose soil, a layer of mulch and not too much heat. If you know the difference between summer and autumn raspberries and cut the canes correctly, you can harvest from June to October.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Summer raspberries bear in June and July on the previous year's canes. Autumn raspberries will be on this year's canes from August.
> - Autumn raspberries are easier: in late winter you cut off all the canes close to the ground. Raspberry beetles and cane disease occur less frequently in them.
> - Raspberries need humus-rich, slightly acidic, well-drained soil. Waterlogging leads to root rot.
> - Plant spacing 40 to 50 centimeters in the row, 1.5 to 2 meters between rows, with a wire framework.
> - Raspberries spread via runners. A mowing strip or a root barrier keeps them in their bed.

## Summer or fall raspberries?

| | Summer raspberries | Autumn raspberries |
|---|---|---|
| Harvest time | June to July | August to October, until frost |
| fruit wood | two-year-old canes (grown the previous year) | annual canes (grown in the same year) |
| Cut | After harvesting, only remove the worn canes | In late winter, cut all canes close to the ground |
| Raspberry beetle | more often because flowering occurs during its flight period | less common, flowering later |
| Tail disease | more often | less common because all the rods are removed every year |
| Care effort | medium | low |

For beginners and small gardens, fall raspberries are the easier choice. If you want to harvest early in the year, also plant some summer raspberries. In addition to red varieties, there are yellow raspberries with a mild aroma and black raspberries with an intense, blackberry-like taste.

## Location and soil

Raspberries grow best in sun to light partial shade, protected from drying wind. The soil should be rich in humus, loose and slightly acidic, similar to forest soil. They cannot tolerate waterlogging at all: wet feet lead to root rot, which can kill entire crops.

On heavy, loamy soils, plant raspberries on a ridge made of garden soil, compost and leaves about 20 to 30 centimeters high and 50 centimeters wide. This allows excess water to drain away. How to assess your soil is explained in [Determine Soil Type](/gartenwissen/bodenart-bestimmen/). Do not plant raspberries where there were raspberries or blackberries, or next to potatoes or tomatoes.

## Plants

The best time to plant is autumn, containers can also be planted in spring.

1. Loosen the soil thoroughly and incorporate plenty of mature compost or leaf compost.
2. Place the plants in a row 40 to 50 centimeters apart, only as deep as they were before. Raspberries have shallow roots.
3. Leave 1.5 to 2 meters of space between two rows.
4. After planting, shorten the canes to around 20 to 30 centimeters so that the plant puts its energy into new shoots from the ground.
5. Water thoroughly and mulch with leaves, grass clippings or straw.

## The scaffolding

Raspberry canes grow 1.5 to 2 meters long and bend under the weight of the fruit. A simple scaffolding made of posts at the ends of the rows and two to three stretched wires at 60, 100 and 150 centimeters high keeps them upright. You tie the rods loosely or run them between two parallel wires. This means the fruits stay clean, dry more quickly after rain and are easy to pick.

## Care

- **Mulching:** Raspberries love a permanent layer of mulch. It keeps the soil moist, cool and loose, as described in [Mulching in the Vegetable Garden](/gartenwissen/mulchen-im-gemuesegarten/).
- **Watering:** in dry periods, especially during fruit formation, penetrating to the ground. Do not pour over the fruit.
- **Fertilize:** Compost as a mulch layer in spring. Raspberries are sensitive to lime and too much salt in fertilizers.
- **Do not chop:** The roots are flat. It is better to suppress weeds with mulch and pluck them by hand.

## The right cut

### Autumn raspberries

In late winter, in February or March, cut all canes just above the ground. New canes sprout in spring and bear fruit from August onwards. That's all. In early summer, thin out young canes that are too close together so that around ten to twelve strong canes remain per meter of row.

One variant is the **double cut**: you leave some rods standing in the winter. They bear a second time in the lower area in early summer. Then you cut them off. This method brings an additional early harvest, but also more work.

### Summer raspberries

With summer raspberries, there are two generations of canes in the bed at the same time: the two-year-olds that are currently bearing and the annuals that will bear next year.

1. Immediately after harvesting, cut off any canes that have borne close to the ground. You can recognize them by the woody, brown bark and the dried fruit stalks.
2. Leave eight to ten strong young, green canes per meter of row and tie them to the scaffolding.
3. Remove weak, thin young canes that are too close together, as well as runners out of the row.
4. In late winter, trim the tips of the remaining canes above the top wire.

You can find the general pruning rules for fruit trees in [Fruit tree pruning](/gartenwissen/obstbaumschnitt/).

## Tame runners

Raspberries form underground runners and then suddenly appear in the middle of the lawn or vegetable patch. A mown strip around the raspberry bed keeps them in check. You can dig up young runners and replant them elsewhere or give them away. In small gardens, a root barrier made of solid plastic, buried about 30 centimeters deep, helps.

## Diseases and pests without poison

**Raspberry beetle:** Its larvae feed in the fruits, which you then find as “maggot raspberries”. The beetles fly when the summer raspberries are blooming. Autumn raspberries largely escape the beetle. With summer raspberries, it helps to wipe off the beetles in the blossoms in the morning using a light cloth.

**Tail disease:** Purple to brown spots on the young canes, from which the bark later tears open and the cane dies. Keep the stock airy, cut off infected canes immediately and dispose of them with household waste, avoid injuries and waterlogging.

**Gray Mold:** A gray fur on the fruit in wet weather. Pick regularly, remove infected fruit and provide air. More about this in [Preventing fungal diseases](/gartenwissen/pilzkrankheiten-vorbeugen/).

## Harvesting

Raspberries are ripe when they are fully colored and separate from the cone without pulling. During the peak season, pick every one or two days, preferably in the morning when the fruit is dry and cool. Lay them flat in a bowl, not piled high. Raspberries only last for one or two days in the refrigerator, but they freeze well: pre-freeze individually on a tray, then put into cans.

## Blackberries as an alternative

If you have a sunny fence or wall and already know raspberries, you can try blackberries. Thornless garden varieties are easy to care for, bear abundantly in August and September, and are less susceptible to root rot. They grow vigorously and need a stable framework. They are cut in a similar way to summer raspberries: After harvesting, remove the worn canes and tie five to eight new, strong canes in a fan shape. Blackberries also form runners and need space, but reward you with a long harvest time when the raspberries are already over.

## Process raspberries correctly

Raspberries are delicate and only last a day or two after harvest. Therefore, process them on the day of harvest if possible. Only wash them when absolutely necessary, and then very carefully in a bowl of water, as they will become mushy under the stream of water.

To freeze, place the berries next to each other on a tray, pre-freeze them for a few hours and then fill them into cans or bags. This means they remain individual and can be removed in portions. Raspberries are also suitable for jam, syrup, raspberry vinegar, cakes and desserts. If you have a lot of berries, you can puree them into a puree, strain them through a sieve and freeze the seedless puree in portions.

## Raspberries in special colors

In addition to the classic red raspberries, there are yellow varieties that often taste particularly sweet and mild and are less noticed by birds because they are less conspicuously colored. Black raspberries come from a North American species and have a strong, slightly spicy aroma. Colorful raspberries in the garden are a nice discovery for children, and cakes and desserts also look particularly festive with mixed colors. The care hardly differs from that of red varieties.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does a raspberry planting last?

With good care, eight to twelve years. After that, the yield often decreases and it is worth starting over in a new location with fresh, healthy plants.

### Can I grow raspberries in a container?

Yes, particularly compact autumn raspberries are suitable for large pots of around 30 liters with humus-rich soil. They then need regular watering.

### Where do raspberries fit in a small garden?

As a row on a fence or as a boundary between the garden and the vegetable patch. [Fruit for small gardens](/gartenwissen/obst-fuer-kleine-gaerten/) shows you how to accommodate berries in a small space.

### Why are my raspberries getting mushy or moldy?

Damp weather is usually to blame, causing gray mold to spread on the ripe fruits. Harvest every two to three days, remove moldy berries immediately and thin out overly dense stands so that the canes dry more quickly after rain. Overripe fruits also quickly become soft.

### Can I use raspberry leaves for tea?

Yes, raspberry leaf tea is a traditional home tea. To do this, pick young, healthy leaves before they bloom, dry them in the shade and store them in a dark jar. However, only harvest a small portion of a cane's leaves so that the plant remains strong.
