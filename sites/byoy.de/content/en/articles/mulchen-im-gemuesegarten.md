In nature, soil is almost never bare. In the forest it is covered by leaves and in the meadow by a dense carpet of plants. Only in the vegetable patch do we often chop it bare and are surprised when it crusts over in the summer, dries out and becomes muddy after every thunderstorm. Mulching mimics nature's protective blanket. It is one of the simplest and most effective methods in organic gardening, and yet there are a few mistakes that are better known before you tip the first wheelbarrow of grass clippings onto the bed.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Mulch keeps the soil moist, cool and crumbly, suppresses weeds and feeds earthworms and soil life.
> - Only mulch when the ground has warmed up in spring, usually from mid to late May.
> - Apply fresh grass clippings only thinly, about 2 to 3 cm, and add regularly.
> - Wood chips and bark mulch belong on paths and under bushes, not in the vegetable patch.
> - In snail years, mulch thinner and only use dried material.

## What mulch does in the soil

A layer of mulch fundamentally changes the life in the topsoil. It slows down evaporation, so you have to water much less often in dry summer weeks. It buffers the temperature: under mulch, the soil stays cooler on hot days and cools down more slowly at night. Heavy rain no longer hits the earth directly, the fine soil particles are not washed out and a hard crust does not form.

But above all, mulch is food. Earthworms gradually pull the material into their burrows, while woodlice, springtails, fungi and bacteria decompose it. This creates humus and nutrients that directly benefit the plants. If you mulch consistently for several years, you will notice how the soil is changing as the soil becomes darker, crumbly.

## Which materials are suitable?

Not every material suits every culture. Basically, the more nitrogen a material is, the faster it decomposes and the more nutrients it releases. The woodier it is, the longer it stays there and the more nitrogen the microorganisms temporarily bind as it breaks down.

|---|---|---|---|
| Lawn clippings, dried | 2 to 3 cm, add more often | Tomatoes, cabbage, zucchini, pumpkin, leek | only from unfertilized, seed-free lawn |
| Straw | 5 to 10 cm | Strawberries, potatoes, pumpkin | few nutrients, keeps fruits clean |

### Use lawn clippings correctly

Grass clippings are the most common mulching material in the home garden because they are produced every week. It is rich in nitrogen and is therefore broken down quickly. However, when freshly applied in thick layers, it rots: the layer sticks together, becomes slimy, smells and no longer allows air to reach the ground. Therefore, let the cutting dry in the sun for half a day to a whole day and then spread it thinly. It's better to have a new thin layer every week than a thick pack once.

Only take clippings from lawns that have not been treated with weed killers and mow before grasses and wild weeds go to seed. Otherwise you will sow the next weed at the same time.

### Straw for strawberries and pumpkin

Straw contains few nutrients and stays around for a long time. Under strawberries, it keeps the fruit clean and dry, which prevents gray mold. Under pumpkins and zucchini, it protects the fruit from contact with the ground. Look for organically grown straw because conventional grain is often treated with straw shorteners.

## The right time

In spring the ground is cold and wet after winter. An early layer of mulch would keep it cold like a blanket and stunt growth. Therefore, wait until the ground has warmed up. For heat-loving crops such as tomatoes, zucchini and beans, the right moment is usually from mid to late May, when the plants are established. You can start a little earlier with cabbage and leeks.

When direct sowing, you wait until the seedlings are a few centimeters high. Fine seeds such as carrots would not grow under a layer of mulch. Then mulch between the rows and later move closer to the plants.

In autumn, mulch harvested beds that no longer receive green manure with leaves, straw or plant residues. This means the soil goes into the winter protected, and in the spring you will find a lively, crumbly surface.

## This is how you mulch step by step

1. **Prepare the soil:** Thoroughly remove root weeds such as groundweed, couch grass or bindweed. Mulch suppresses germinating weeds, but not perennial root weeds.
2. **Water thoroughly:** Mulch over moist soil. When placed on dry soil, the mulch also keeps out rain for a while.
3. **Distribute material:** Lay out the layer evenly. Leave a hand's width of space around the stems to prevent them from rotting.
4. **Add regularly:** In midsummer, a thin layer of grass clippings will almost disappear in one to two weeks. Add more when the bottom shows through again.
5. **Watering under the mulch:** Water directly at the base of the plant so that the water reaches the soil and does not get stuck in the mulch.

## Mulch and snails

The most common objection to mulch: It provides shelter for slugs. That's partly true. Snails feel comfortable under moist, fresh material, and in wet springs a thick layer of mulch can make the situation worse.

This doesn't mean you have to forego mulch. Customize the method:

- In wet weeks, mulch thinner or only use dried, coarse material.
- Only mulch young, endangered plants such as lettuce when they are larger and more robust.
- Lift the mulch regularly and collect snails and eggs underneath.
- Coarse, dry material such as straw or chopped twigs is less attractive to snails than soft grass clippings.

At the same time, mulch also encourages ground beetles, shrews and slowworms, which eat snails and their eggs. In the long term, the balance often shifts in favor of the beneficial insects.

## Mulch for individual crops

Every culture has slightly different preferences. A few experiences:

- **Tomatoes:** Grass clippings or chopped nettles once plants are established. The mulch prevents splashing water and thus the transfer of brown rot from the ground to the lower leaves.
- **Cabbage:** Cabbage loves nutrient-rich mulch. Lawn clippings keep the soil evenly moist, which helps prevent cabbages and kohlrabi from bursting.
- **Potatoes:** A thick layer of straw can partially replace hilling. Tubers grown under straw remain clean, but must be completely covered to prevent them from turning green.
- **Onions and Garlic:** Mulch only thinly or not at all. The onions ripen better when the surface dries out in summer.
- **Carrots:** Mulch between rows once the foliage is about 4 inches tall. It prevents the carrot heads from turning green.
- **Berry bushes:** Leaves, straw or chopped bushes. Currants and raspberries have shallow roots and are very grateful for a permanent layer of mulch.

## Common mistakes when mulching

- **Too early in the year:** Cold ground stays cold and plants stall.
- **Too thick fresh grass clippings:** Rot, smell and lack of air.
- **Woody material in the vegetable patch:** Bark mulch and chopped wood remove nitrogen from the soil and sometimes contain growth-inhibiting substances.
- **Sick parts of plants:** Leaves with powdery mildew or brown rot do not belong as mulch on beds.
- **Seed-bearing wild herbs:** They sow themselves out of the mulch layer.

## Mulch over the winter

Mulch also protects the soil in winter. A blanket of leaves, straw or green manure residues prevents rain from muddying the surface and washing out nutrients, and it moderates strong temperature fluctuations. The soil life remains active longer under the cover and processes the organic material into humus.

In spring, pull the winter mulch aside a few weeks in advance on beds that are to be planted or sown early. This allows the sun to warm the ground. Whatever has not yet decomposed goes into the compost or remains between the rows.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use cardboard as mulch?

Unprinted brown corrugated cardboard is suitable for creating new beds in a meadow or for suppressing weeds in larger areas. Compost and a layer of mulch are added on top. Colorfully printed or coated boxes do not belong in the garden. Cardboard is usually unnecessary in a running vegetable patch.

### Do I have to add additional fertilizer under mulch?

Nutrient-rich mulch such as grass clippings or nettles provides a noticeable amount of nitrogen throughout the season. For heavy eaters it is often not enough on its own, so add additional compost in the spring. The fertilizer effect is low with straw or leaves.

### How long does it take for the soil to change?

You will notice the first differences in soil moisture and crumb structure in the first summer. A significantly higher humus content and more earthworms usually appear after two to three years of consistent soil cover.

### Can I mulch under fruit trees?

Yes, a tree slice with mulch is very beneficial for young fruit trees because grass around the trunk competes strongly for water and nutrients. Keep a distance of about 10 cm from the trunk so that voles do not nest directly on the bark.

### Does mulch attract mice?

Thick, dry layers of hay or straw provide shelter for mice and voles, especially in winter. Therefore, keep layers of mulch around fruit trees and trees thinner and a few centimeters away from the trunk so that rodents do not nibble on the bark unnoticed.

### Can I use wood chips in the vegetable patch?

Fresh wood chips bind nitrogen as they decompose and are therefore more suitable for paths, under bushes and for soft fruit. It is better to use them in the vegetable patch only after they have been composted for a year.

For the same reason, coarse bark mulch is more suitable for ornamental trees than for vegetable beds.
