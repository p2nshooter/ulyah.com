You've been tending tomatoes, peppers, cabbage and lettuce on the windowsill for weeks. They are strong and green, and it is finally May. But anyone who puts the plants directly from the warm living room into the bed often experiences disappointment: the next day the leaves are burnt white, the plants hang limply, or a cold night has damaged them. The reason is simple: plants grown indoors know neither direct sun, wind nor cool nights. You have to get used to it. This step is called hardening. It lasts one to two weeks and determines how well your young plants start in the bed.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Hardening off means gradually accustoming young plants to sun, wind and outside temperatures over seven to fourteen days.
> - Start with one to two hours in the shade on a mild day and increase the time and amount of sun each day.
> - Heat-loving crops such as tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers and zucchini only come into the bed after the ice saints and when night temperatures are above 10 degrees.
> - Plant on a cloudy day or late afternoon, water thoroughly and protect the plants for the first few days.
> - Purchased young plants from the greenhouse also need to be hardened off.

## Why hardening is necessary

Plants adapt their leaves to the light in which they grow. The amount of light under window glass and in the room is much lower than outside, and above all there is no UV radiation, which is retained by the glass. The leaves of houseplants are therefore thinner and have fewer protective substances. In full sun they get sunburned: white or brown, papery spots.

Added to this are wind and temperature. Indoors, plants grow softly and with thin stems. Outside they are moved by wind and form stronger tissue. And cold, even without frost, can weaken heat-loving plants like tomatoes, cucumbers and basil to the point where they stop growing for weeks.

## A ten day hardening plan

| day | Location | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 2 | outside in the shade, protected from the wind | 1 to 2 hours in the warmest part of the day |
| 3 to 4 | Partial shade | 3 to 4 hours |
| 5 to 6 | Morning sun, then partial shade | half a day |
| 7 to 8 | Sun until early afternoon | all day, bring in at night |
| 9 to 10 | full sun | Outside day and night if there is no threat of a cold night |

Adjust the plan according to the weather. On cold, windy days, the plants stay indoors or in a closed [cold frame](/gartenwissen/fruehbeet-nutzen/). A cold frame is the ideal place for hardening off: you open the lid a little wider and longer every day.

**Cabbage, lettuce, onions and kohlrabi** are more robust. They tolerate cool nights above zero degrees and can be hardened off and planted as early as April. **Tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, cucumbers, zucchini, pumpkin and basil** require heat. They need temperatures above 10 to 12 degrees when hardening.

## When in the bed?

The **Ice Saints** from May 11th to 15th are the best known rule of thumb. Until then, there may still be night frost in many regions of Germany. But the actual temperatures are more important than the date:

| Culture | Conditions for planting out |
|---|---|
| Lettuce, kohlrabi, cabbage, onions, leeks | from mid-April, no more severe frosts, have fleece ready |
| Celery | from early to mid-May, cold nights can cause him to shoot |
| Tomatoes, peppers, eggplant | after the Ice Saints, night temperatures permanently above 10 °C |
| Cucumbers, zucchini, pumpkin | according to the Ice Saints, soil at least 12 to 15 °C |
| Basil | only when the weather is warm at the end of May or June |

In cool locations such as low mountain ranges or in the north, it may make sense to wait until the beginning of June. You can find the sowing and planting dates for each vegetable in the [Sowing Calendar for Vegetables](/gartenwissen/aussaatkalender-gemuese/).

## Recognize healthy young plants

When buying young plants, pay attention to:

- strong, stocky plants with lush green leaves,
- thick stems instead of long, thin, withered shoots,
- a well-rooted but not matted root ball,
- no yellow or spotted leaves, no lice on the undersides of the leaves,
- Tomatoes have no or only the first flowers yet.

Plants from the garden center are often kept in the greenhouse. You also have to harden them for a few days. Young plants from organic farming are available at weekly markets, in organic nurseries and at plant exchanges.

## Plant correctly

1. **Time:** Plant on a cloudy, windless day or late afternoon. This gives the plants the night to recover.
2. **Water:** Water the young plants thoroughly an hour before planting or submerge the pots in a bucket of water.
3. **Planting Hole:** Dig a hole slightly larger than the root ball. For heavy feeders, add a handful of mature compost as described in [Heavy feeders, medium feeders, weak feeders](/gartenwissen/starkzehrer-mittelzehrer-schwachzehrer/).
4. **Planting depth:** You plant most plants as deep as they were in the pot. If you plant **tomatoes** deeper, they will form additional roots on the buried stem. You plant **cabbage** up to the first leaves so that it is stable. **Lettuce** should only be deep enough that the root collar remains free, otherwise it will rot.
5. **Pressing and watering:** Press the soil around the ball firmly and water vigorously so that the soil adheres to the roots.
6. **Mulching:** A thin layer of mulch will retain moisture once the soil is warm.

## Protection in the first few days

- **Sun:** If the weather is very sunny, shade the freshly planted plants for two to three days with fleece or an inclined board.
- **Cold nights:** A fleece over the bed provides a few degrees of protection. Keep it handy until mid-June.
- **Wind:** Tall plants like tomatoes get their rod or spiral right when they are planted.
- **Snails:** Freshly planted lettuce and zucchini are particularly tempting for snails. Check in the evening and morning. More about this in [Nudibranchs without poison](/gartenwissen/schnecken-ohne-gift/).
- **Flea beetles and cabbage flies:** A crop protection net immediately after planting protects cabbage, kohlrabi and radishes.

## Your own young plants: time on the windowsill

If you prefer, you can lay the foundation for strong young plants on the windowsill. Three points decide:

- **Light:** A south window is ideal. Too little light causes the plants to rot; they become long, thin and light green. A plant light helps in February and March.
- **Temperature after germination:** Tomatoes and peppers need warmth to germinate. Once the cotyledons are there, put them cooler, around 16 to 18 degrees. So they grow stocky.
- **Pricking:** When the first true leaves appear, place each plant in its own small pot. You plant tomatoes a little deeper.

A gentle breeze, such as from a fan on a low setting or gently stroking the plants, strengthens the stems before they harden off.

## Planting times at a glance

Robust crops such as lettuce, kohlrabi, cabbage, chard and onions tolerate cool nights and can be hardened into the bed from April, a little later in rough locations. Frost-sensitive plants such as tomatoes, cucumbers, zucchini, pumpkin, peppers and basil wait until after the ice saints in mid-May. Peppers and cucumbers also like warm soil and often struggle to grow for weeks if they are planted too early in cold soil.

Leeks, celery and winter vegetables such as kale or Brussels sprouts are planted from May to July, depending on the variety. If you plant your plants in several sets, you harvest over a longer period of time and spread the risk if a late frost or a hot summer affects a planting.

## The correct planting depth

Not every plant is planted at the same depth. You can plant tomatoes deeper than they were in the pot, because they form additional roots on the buried stem and are then more stable. Cabbage is planted up to the first leaves. Lettuce, on the other hand, is planted so flat that the root ball is just covered; An old gardening rule says that lettuce should “flutter in the wind” after planting. Set too low, his heart easily rots.

You plant leeks in deep planting holes that you gradually fill with soil. This creates a long, light shaft. You plant cucumbers and zucchini at the same height as in the pot, preferably on a small hill so that water drains away from the sensitive base of the stem.

## Frequently asked questions

### What do I do if the leaves get sunburned?

The burned areas do not recover, but the plant produces new, adapted leaves. Shade them for a few days. You can remove severely damaged leaves later.

### My tomatoes have grown long and thin on the windowsill. What to do?

Plant them deep, down to the first leaves. The buried stem forms roots and the plant becomes stable. Next year, sow a little later or keep the plants brighter and cooler.

### How long can I leave young plants in the pot?

Not too long. If the roots fill the pot and the plant has yellow leaves, it needs more space. Repot it into a larger pot or plant it out when the weather is right.

### What do I do if frost is predicted after planting?

Cover sensitive plants with fleece, upside-down buckets or plant covers in the evening and remove the cover again the next morning. Water the soil beforehand because moist soil retains heat better than dry soil. If there is severe frost, bring plants in pots into the house overnight.

### Do I have to fertilize freshly planted young plants?

Not immediately. If you have provided the bed with compost beforehand, the nutrients will be enough for the first few weeks. Heavy feeders such as tomatoes, cabbage or pumpkins receive their first dose of organic fertilizer after two to three weeks, when they continue to grow visibly.

Weak feeders such as lettuce and herbs usually do not require any additional fertilizer.

Too much nitrogen makes young plants soft and vulnerable to aphids.
