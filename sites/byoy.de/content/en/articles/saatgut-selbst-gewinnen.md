Harvesting your own seeds closes a circle: the bean you planted in May will become next year's beans in the fall. Over the years, the plants adapt to your garden, your soil and your climate, saving you money and helping to preserve old varieties that are hardly available in stores anymore. Some vegetables make it easy for you, others require knowledge about pollination, spacing and biennial cultures. This guide explains the basics and shows you where to start.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Only seed-resistant varieties produce offspring that resemble the mother plant. F1 hybrids produce different, often weaker plants.
> - Easy to start with: beans, peas, tomatoes, lettuce and peppers. They are predominantly self-pollinating.
> - More difficult: pumpkin, zucchini, cucumbers, corn, carrots, cabbage, beets. They interbreed easily and some only bloom in their second year.
> - Harvest seeds only from the healthiest, most typical plants, or from several plants in the case of cross-pollinators.
> - Seeds must be completely dry before storing them in a cool, dark, labeled place.

## Seed Festival or F1?

The most important requirement is a **seed-resistant variety**. The offspring of this plant resemble the mother plant if they have not been crossed with another variety. **F1 hybrids**, on the other hand, arise from crossing two parental lines. They are uniform in the first generation and often very productive, but their seeds split into very different plants in the next generation. On seed bags there is usually “F1” after the variety name. More on this explained [Seed Festival or F1 Hybrids](/gartenwissen/samenfest-oder-f1/).

## Self-pollinators and cross-pollinators

| group | vegetables | What you need to consider |
|---|---|---|
| predominantly self-pollinators | Beans, peas, tomatoes, lettuce, peppers | simple, small distances between varieties are usually sufficient |
| Cross-pollination by insects | Pumpkin, zucchini, cucumbers, cabbage, carrots, onions, radishes | cross with other varieties of the same species, large spacing or hand pollination required |
| Cross-pollination by wind | Corn, spinach, beetroot, chard | Pollen flies far, large distances are necessary |

**Crossing** means: Pollen from another variety of the same species fertilizes your plant. The fruit this year looks normal, but the seeds within bear a mix of both varieties. In the case of zucchini, this can even lead to bitter fruits in the next generation. Garden squash, zucchini and many ornamental pumpkins belong to the same species and interbreed with each other. More about bitter substances can be found in [Growing zucchini and pumpkin](/gartenwissen/zucchini-und-kuerbis/).

## The easy entry

### Beans and peas

Leave some of the strongest plants standing and do not harvest green pods from them. The pods ripen on the plant, becoming yellow, dry and parchment-like, and the beans within rustle. Harvest them in dry weather. If wet weather is imminent, pull out the entire plants and hang them upside down in an airy, dry place to dry. Then open the pods and let the seeds dry for another week or two. Beans are dry enough when they no longer give in when you bite into them. How beans are grown can be found in [Growing bush beans and pole beans](/gartenwissen/buschbohnen-und-stangenbohnen/).

**Tip against bean beetles:** Stored beans can be infested with seed beetles that hatch from small round holes. Place the dried seeds in the freezer for a few days before storing them.

### Tomatoes: the wet method

Tomato seeds are embedded in a gel-like coating that prevents germination within the fruit. You loosen this shell through fermentation:

1. Choose a fully ripe, healthy fruit of a hard-seed variety.
2. Halve the tomato and scrape the seeds and juice into a glass.
3. Add a little water and leave the jar at room temperature for two to three days. A film of mold forms on the surface. This is intentional.
4. Fill the glass with water and stir. Good seeds sink to the bottom, pulp and dead seeds float to the top and are drained. Repeat until the seeds are clean.
5. Pour the seeds through a fine sieve and spread them thinly on a plate or coffee filter. Kitchen paper is unfavorable because the seeds stick to it.
6. Allow them to dry in a warm, airy place out of direct sun for one to two weeks.

Tomatoes are predominantly self-pollinating. If you have several varieties next to each other, keep some distance or harvest plants in the middle of the row to avoid rare insect crossings. More about growing tomatoes can be found in [Tomatoes in the field](/gartenwissen/tomaten-im-freiland/).

### Salad

Let some lettuce plants sprout. They form a tall flower stalk with many small yellow flowers and then white feathery umbrellas, similar to dandelions. Cut the stems when about half of the flowers have feathers and let them ripen upside down in a paper bag. Then you shake out the seeds.

## For advanced users

### Pumpkin, zucchini, cucumbers

To get pure seeds, pollinate individual flowers by hand: the night before, close one male and one female flower, which will open the next morning, with tape or a clip. In the morning you pick the male flower, remove the petals and spread the pollen on the stigma of the female flower. Then you close the female flower again and mark the fruit. Allow it to fully ripen until hard, and remove the seeds after a few weeks of storage. Wash them and let them dry thoroughly.

### Biennial vegetables

Carrots, cabbage, beetroot, chard, onions, leeks and parsnips only bloom in the second year. You overwinter the most beautiful plants in the bed or as turnips in the cellar and plant them out again in the spring. Carrots are at risk of cross-breeding with wild carrots, which grow along many roadsides. When it comes to cabbage, all types of cabbage such as white cabbage, savoy cabbage, broccoli, kohlrabi and kale interbreed with each other. Here you need large distances or insect nets and lots of plants.

## Choice and diversity

Only harvest seeds from plants that are healthy, have grown well and show the typical characteristics of the variety. Whoever takes the earliest or most beautiful fruits improves the variety over the years for their own garden. For cross-pollinators such as pumpkin, corn or cabbage, you should harvest and mix seeds from several plants so that the variety does not become weaker due to inbreeding.

## Dry, clean, label

- Seeds must be completely dry before you package them. Damp seeds mold or germinate in the bag.
- Remove husks, husks and dust by sieving or gently blowing over a bowl.
- Put the seeds in paper bags or small jars and label them with the variety, harvest year and special features.

How long seeds remain capable of germinating and how to store them correctly can be found in [germ sample and storing seeds](/gartenwissen/keimprobe-und-saatgut-lagern/).

## Seeds of herbs and flowers

Seeds for many herbs and summer flowers are particularly easy to obtain. Dill, coriander, marigolds, nasturtiums, cornflowers and sunflowers produce abundant seeds that you can easily harvest when the seed heads are brown and dry. Place a paper bag over ripening seed heads to prevent the seeds from falling out or being eaten by birds.

Allow the harvested seed heads to dry in the bag for a few days, then shake the seeds out and remove any large parts of the plant. Herb and flower seeds make nice gifts and are easy to exchange at seed exchanges.

## Enough plants for healthy varieties

If you want to obtain seeds from cross-pollinators such as pumpkin, cabbage, carrots or corn, you should not just use a single plant. If only the seeds of a few plants are propagated over the years, the variety loses genetic diversity and the plants can become weaker over time. Therefore, take seeds from as many healthy plants as possible and mix them.

This is less critical for self-pollinators such as beans, peas, tomatoes and lettuce, but here too it is worth taking seeds from several plants. This means the variety remains resilient and slowly adapts to your garden.

## Don't pass on diseases

Some diseases are transmitted via seeds, such as certain fungal and viral diseases in beans. Therefore, only get seeds from healthy, strong plants and mark the best specimens with a ribbon during the season. Sick or weak plants are taboo for seed production.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I pass on seeds I have grown myself?

You can multiply seeds freely for your own garden. Passing on in a private context, for example when swapping between amateur gardeners, is common. Commercial sales, on the other hand, are regulated by law and special rules apply to protected varieties.

### Can I take seeds from store-bought tomatoes?

Many supermarket tomatoes are F1 hybrids. Plants with unpredictable properties grow from their seeds. It's exciting to try, but for a reliable harvest, use solid seeds.

### How many seeds do I need?

Usually much less than a single plant provides. One tomato produces hundreds of seeds, one bean plant enough for a whole row. The rest is great for swapping.

### How long do home-grown seeds last?

As long as purchased seeds of the same species if they are well dried and stored in a cool, dry and dark place. Check older seeds with a germination sample before sowing. More about this in the guide [Store germination sample and seeds] (/gartenwissen/keimprobe-und-saatgut-lagern/).

### Do I have to label the seeds?

Absolutely. On each bag, write down the species, variety, year of harvest and special features such as origin or observations about the plant. After a year, no one else knows which tomato is in which bag. Keep the bags sorted by type in a box so you can quickly find what you're looking for in the spring.

A small piece of paper with notes about the plant will help you choose next year.

This will gradually create a seed supply that suits your garden perfectly.
