The soil is the foundation of every garden. Whether carrots grow long and straight, whether lettuce rots after every rain or whether tomatoes wilt in July often depends less on the seeds than on the soil. If you know your soil, you water, fertilize and loosen more specifically and don't waste money on products that aren't necessary. You don't need a laboratory for this: with your hands, a screw-top jar and a little observation, you can figure out what you're dealing with. This guide shows you the simple tests and what you do with the results.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Soils consist of sand, silt and clay in varying proportions. The mixture determines how well water, air and nutrients are retained.
> - The finger test reveals the type of soil in two minutes: sand crunches, silt feels mealy, clay is sticky and malleable.
> - In the slurry glass, the components settle in layers so that you can see their proportions.
> - Most vegetables grow well at a pH of around 6 to 7.
> - Compost improves any soil: it loosens heavy soils and helps light ones hold water.

## What soil is made of

The mineral components of the soil are classified according to their grain size:

| component | grain size | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| Sand | 0.063 to 2mm | loose, permeable, heats up quickly, holds little water and nutrients |
| Silt | 0.002 to 0.063mm | mealy, stores water well, tends to become muddy |
| Tone | less than 0.002mm | binds water and nutrients strongly, heavy, compacts easily, becomes hard when dry |

There is also **humus**, i.e. decomposed organic matter, and **soil life**: earthworms, woodlice, fungi and countless microorganisms. Humus and soil life turn mineral grains into fertile soil with a crumb structure.

**Loam** is a mixture of sand, silt and clay in approximately balanced proportions. A sandy loam with a good humus content is considered ideal garden soil.

## The finger test

Take a handful of soil from about four to eight inches deep, remove stones and roots, and moisten it until it behaves like playdough but doesn't drip. Rub it between your thumb and fingers and try to roll a sausage.

| Observation | Soil type |
|---|---|
| Grainy, crunches audibly, cannot be shaped, falls apart | Sandy soil |
| A little grainy, can be shaped, the sausage breaks easily | loamy sand |
| Feels velvety and floury, stays in the grooves of the skin and is not very sticky | Silt soil |
| Malleable, sausage about as thick as a pencil is possible, slightly sticky, slightly grainy | Clay soil |
| Very sticky, shiny cut surface, can be rolled into thin sausages | clay soil |

Do the test in several places in the garden. Especially in new development areas, areas can differ greatly, for example when building excavation has been distributed.

## The sludge sample in the glass

The sludge sample shows you the proportions in more detail.

1. Fill a tall, straight screw-top jar about a third full with soil without stones or roots.
2. Add water until the glass is three-quarters full. A teaspoon of dish soap or salt will help dissolve the crumbs.
3. Seal the jar and shake vigorously for several minutes.
4. Put it in a quiet place and wait.

After a few minutes the sand has settled to the bottom. After a few hours the silt follows on top. Clay often takes one or more days for the water to clear. Humus particles float at the top. Measure the height of the layers and compare them: This way you can see whether your soil is sandy, silty or clayey.

## Indicator plants

Wild herbs reveal a lot about the soil because they grow where the conditions suit them:

- **Stinging nettle, chickweed, groundweed:** nutrient-rich, nitrogen-rich soil
- **Coltsfoot, field horsetail:** compacted, often loamy and moist subsoil
- **Buttercups, rushes, moss:** Waterlogging, poor ventilation
- **Small sorrel, heather:** acidic, often sandy soil
- **Fire mustard, corn poppy:** rather calcareous soil

Individual plants say little. Only when a species occurs in large numbers is it a clear indication.

## Measure the pH value

The pH value indicates how acidic or basic the soil is. Most vegetables grow best at a value of around 6 to 7. Blueberries, on the other hand, need acidic soil around pH 4.5, as described in [Blueberries in the Garden](/gartenwissen/heidelbeeren-im-garten/).

For an initial assessment, test strips or simple test sets from garden shops are sufficient. A **soil test in the laboratory** is more precise, which tells you not only the pH value but also nutrients such as phosphorus, potassium and magnesium. By the way, many gardens are oversupplied rather than undersupplied, especially with phosphorus. An analysis every three to five years protects against unnecessary fertilization.

Lime only if a measurement shows that the soil is too acidic, and then with a slow-acting carbonated lime in the recommended amount. Too much lime does more harm than good.

## Improve any soil

### Sandy soil

Sandy soils are easy to work and warm up quickly in the spring, but dry out and lose nutrients with the rain. Improve them with:

- lots of mature compost, three to five liters per square meter every year,
- Mulch that slows down evaporation, see [Mulching in the vegetable garden](/gartenwissen/mulchen-im-gemuesegarten/),
- Green manure that builds up root mass and humus, see [Green manure](/gartenwissen/gruenduengung/),
- regular small fertilizer applications instead of one large one.

### Loam and clay soil

Heavy soils hold water and nutrients well, but are cold and wet for a long time in spring and compact easily. Improve them with:

- Compost and coarse organic material that loosens the structure,
- Green manure with deep-rooted plants,
- the principle of never entering or working on wet earth,
- permanent paths so that the beds are not trampled on.

Mixing sand into clay soil is often recommended, but it requires large amounts and can even worsen the structure if there is too little sand. Compost is the safer route.

## Spade test: look at the ground from the inside

A look beneath the surface reveals more than any test in the glass. Use the spade to make a hole about 12 inches deep in a typical spot and lift out a block of soil without breaking it. Put it on a tarp and look at it:

- **Color:** A dark top layer shows humus. Gray or rusty areas further down indicate waterlogging.
- **Structure:** Good soil breaks down into round, porous crumbs. Sharp-edged, flat chunks are a sign of compaction.
- **Roots:** Do they grow straight down or do they bend to the side at one layer?
- **Life:** Earthworms, burrows and woodlice indicate an active soil. Ten or more earthworms in one sod is a very good sign.
- **Smell:** Healthy soil smells pleasantly of the forest. A foul smell indicates a lack of air.

Do the spade test in spring and fall when the soil is moist but not wet. Over the years you will see how your soil changes with compost and mulch.

## Humus: the invisible treasure

In addition to sand, silt and clay, humus determines the quality of a garden soil. Humus is created when soil organisms decompose dead plant residues and compost. It turns the soil dark, stores water and nutrients like a sponge, sticks the soil particles together to form stable crumbs and feeds earthworms and microorganisms.

Humus cannot be bought and incorporated once; it has to be created again and again. Mature compost, mulch made from leaves or grass clippings, green manure and leaving plant residues feed the soil life and slowly build up humus. A soil that has been cared for for years becomes darker, more crumbly and is much easier to work with, regardless of whether it was originally sandy or clayey.

## Observe soil life

You can check for yourself how alive your soil is using simple means. In spring or fall, when the ground is moist, dig a spade deep into the soil and count the earthworms in it. Many worms indicate active, healthy soil, almost none indicate compaction, drought or low organic matter.

Worm solutions, small piles of crumbly soil on the surface, and a pleasant smell of forest floor are also good signs. On the other hand, if the soil smells putrid or acidic, it is lacking air, usually because it is compacted or permanently wet.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best soil for vegetables?

A humus-rich, sandy loam: loose enough for roots and air, firm enough to hold water and nutrients. With compost you can get closer to this ideal on almost any soil.

### How do I recognize soil compaction?

Water remains standing for a long time after rain, the earth is hard and gray deep down, roots grow flat to the side. A groundbreaking shows the compacted layer. Loosening with a digging fork and deep-rooted green manure help.

### Do I really need a laboratory analysis?

It is very helpful for a new garden or if plants are suffering despite good care. For a well-maintained organic garden, every few years is enough.

### How often should I measure pH?

For most gardens, one measurement every two to three years is sufficient. It is often worth caring for plants despite good care after you have limed them or when you create a new bed. Measure in several places and mix the samples so that you get a reliable average value.

### How do I improve a very rocky soil?

You gradually collect larger stones, especially in beds for root vegetables such as carrots, which grow crookedly in stony soil. Smaller stones hardly disturb most plants and even improve permeability. For sensitive crops, a raised bed or a bed with filled, humus-rich soil is often the simplest solution. More about this in the guide [Creating a raised bed](/gartenwissen/hochbeet-anlegen/).

### Can I buy garden soil from a bag for an entire bed?

This is possible for small beds and raised beds, but for large areas it is expensive, and purchased soil is no substitute for living soil. It is better to improve the existing soil step by step with compost, mulch and green manure.
