Pumpkins are among the most rewarding crops in the vegetable garden. Two zucchini plants will feed a family all summer long, and a handful of Hokkaido pumpkins will provide soup well into winter. To do this, they require warmth, plenty of space and nutrient-rich soil. Anyone who meets these requirements and knows a few typical mistakes will be rewarded with yields that hardly any other culture can achieve.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Sow or plant only after the Ice Saints, when the soil is at least 12 °C warm.
> - Space requirement: Zucchini about 1 m², climbing pumpkins 2 to 3 m² per plant.
> - Heavy feeders: plenty of compost, mulch and a consistent water supply.
> - Never eat bitter-tasting fruits; they may contain poisonous bitter substances.
> - Only harvest pumpkins when the stem is woody, then allow them to ripen and store them in a cool place.

## Zucchini or pumpkin: what's the difference?

Botanically, zucchini and many pumpkins belong to the same species, the garden pumpkin (*Cucurbita pepo*). Zucchini are pumpkins that are harvested unripe. There is also the giant pumpkin (*Cucurbita maxima*), which includes the Hokkaido, and the musk pumpkin (*Cucurbita moschata*), which includes butternut and nutmeg pumpkin. This is important for the garden because pumpkins of the same species cross with each other, which plays a role in your own seeds.

| Culture | growth | space per plant | Harvest time | Shelf life |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Zucchini | bushy | 1 m² | July to frost | a few days in the fridge |
| Hokkaido | climbing, up to 3 m | 2 to 3 m² | September to October | until around February |
| Butternut | ranking | 2 to 3 m² | End of September to October | often until March or longer |
| Nutmeg pumpkin | strong ranking | 3 to 4 m² | October | very good, until spring |
| Spaghetti squash | ranking | 2 m² | September | a few months |

Butternut and nutmeg squash require a long, warm summer. In cool climates, Hokkaido is the safer choice because it ripens earlier.

## Sowing and planting

Cucurbits are sensitive to frost and don't like cold feet. You can sow them directly into the bed from mid-May as soon as the ground has warmed to around 12 to 14 °C. To do this, place two to three seeds 2 to 3 cm deep in each planting spot and later leave only the strongest seedling.

You can harvest safer and earlier with early plants. They are sown individually in pots with a diameter of 8 to 10 cm about three to four weeks before planting out. They should not be left in the pot for a longer period of time because pumpkin plants react sensitively when the roots fill the pot. Harden off the plants for a week and plant them out after the Ice Saints.

An old and proven method is planting on a compost heap or on the edge of it. There the plants find warmth and nutrients in abundance. In the bed, work two to three shovels of ripe compost into the planting hole per planting site.

## Care in summer

### Water and nutrients

The large leaves evaporate a lot of water on hot days. Water thoroughly, twice a week rather than a little every day, and always directly to the root area, never over the leaves. A layer of mulch made from grass clippings or straw keeps the soil moist and protects the fruit from contact with the ground.

If the plants weaken from July onwards, a dose of diluted nettle or comfrey liquid every two to three weeks will help.

### Pollination

Cucurbits have male and female flowers. You can recognize female flowers by the small ovary beneath the flower, which looks like a mini zucchini. At the beginning of the season, many plants initially only produce male flowers, which is normal.

If young fruits turn brown and soft from the tip and fall off, they have usually not been pollinated or have been pollinated insufficiently. This happens in cool, rainy weather when few bumblebees and bees are flying. You can then pollinate yourself in the morning: pluck a male flower, remove the petals and dab the pollen on the stigma of the female flower.

### Steering pumpkins

Climbing pumpkins wander over paths and into neighboring beds. You can carefully redirect the shoots while they are young. To ensure that pumpkins grow large and ripe, some gardeners only allow two to four fruits to form per shoot and then cut off the shoot a few leaves behind the last fruit. You should remove new flowers from the end of August because they will no longer produce ripe fruit until frost.

## Powdery mildew

From mid-summer onwards, a white, mealy coating appears on many pumpkin leaves: powdery mildew. Unlike many fungal diseases, it occurs especially in dry, warm weather when the plants are under drought stress. It can hardly be prevented completely, but you can slow it down:

- Water and mulch evenly so that the plants do not suffer from a lack of water.
- Do not plant too closely so that the leaves dry out.
- Remove severely affected leaves early and do not compost them.
- Choose mildew-tolerant varieties; many modern zucchini varieties are specifically bred this way.
- A spray with diluted milk (1 part milk to 8 to 10 parts water) is often used in practice and can slow down the infestation.

From September onwards, powdery mildew on pumpkins is no longer a big problem because the fruits will then ripen anyway.

## Be careful, bitter substances

Cucurbits can produce cucurbitacins, bitter substances that, even in small quantities, cause nausea, vomiting and diarrhea and are dangerous in severe cases. Commercially available varieties are bred free of it. Problems arise when you recreate seeds from your own plants that have crossed with ornamental gourds. Severe stress can also increase salary.

The rule is simple: **Taste a tiny piece raw before cooking. If it tastes bitter, spit it out and throw the whole fruit away.** Cooking does not destroy the bitter substances.

## Harvest

**Zucchini** is best harvested when it is 15 to 20 cm long. Then they are tender and aromatic. The more regularly you harvest, the more fruit the plant produces. Forgotten giant zucchini are edible, but watery and slow down the new growth.

**Squash** is ripe when the stem is woody and cracked, the shell sounds hollow when tapped and can hardly be scratched with a fingernail. Cut it off with a piece of stem, never without a stem, otherwise rot pathogens will penetrate at the base of the stem. Harvest before the first heavy frost.

## Store

After harvesting, let pumpkins ripen in a warm, dry place at 20 to 25 °C for about two weeks. The shell hardens. Then store them at 10 to 15 °C, dry and airy, preferably on wooden slats or a shelf, not on concrete and not on top of each other. A cold cellar below 10 °C is too cold for pumpkins and causes them to rot more quickly. Check supplies regularly and use damaged fruits first.

## Varieties for the home garden

The selection of pumpkin plants is huge. Some proven examples:

- **Zucchini:** 'Black Beauty' is an old, seed-resistant variety with dark green fruits. 'Defender' F1 is considered tolerant to viral diseases, 'Gold Rush' bears yellow fruits that are easy to find between the leaves. Round varieties such as 'Tondo di Piacenza' are suitable for stuffing.
- **Hokkaido:** 'Uchiki Kuri' is the classic orange Hokkaido, the peel of which can be eaten. 'Blue Kuri' has a grey-blue skin and firm flesh.
- **Butternut:** 'Waltham Butternut' is common but needs heat. Compact varieties like 'Butterbush' ripen a little earlier and require less space.
- **Spaghetti squash:** The flesh falls apart into strands after cooking.

If you want to collect seeds yourself, grow only one variety per species or pollinate by hand and seal the flowers. Otherwise, zucchini will cross with ornamental pumpkins or other garden squash, and the offspring may contain bitter substances.

## Cucurbits in crop rotation and mixed culture

As heavy feeders, zucchini and pumpkin are at the beginning of the crop rotation, on a bed that has received plenty of compost. This is followed by middle feeders such as carrots or onions. Good neighbors are corn and runner beans, which together with the pumpkin form the traditional "Three Sisters" mixed culture: the corn serves as a climbing aid, the beans provide nitrogen, and the pumpkin shades the ground. Nasturtiums, marigolds and borage also work well because they attract pollinators that are essential for fruit setting. It is unfavorable to have potatoes in the immediate vicinity, as they also require a lot of water and nutrients.

## Zucchini and pumpkin in the kitchen

Zucchini taste best when harvested young, around 15 to 20 centimeters long. Then they are tender, have hardly any seeds and are suitable raw for salads, grilling, roasting or stuffing. If you have too many, grate them and freeze them in portions for soups, cakes or pancakes.

Pumpkins are versatile: as soup, from the oven, as puree or in curry. The seeds of many varieties can be washed, dried and roasted. Oil pumpkins whose seeds do not have a hard shell are particularly practical.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many zucchini plants do I need?

Two plants are usually enough for a four-person household. In good summers, each plant produces several fruits per week.

### Can I grow pumpkin after pumpkin?

Better not. For cucurbits, allow three to four years of cultivation on the same bed to prevent diseases in the soil.

### Why does my zucchini only have male flowers?

This is normal at the start of the season. If it lasts for a long time, the plant is often too cool, too shady or receives too much nitrogen.

### Can I eat zucchini flowers?

Yes. In particular, the male flowers, which sit on a thin stem without fruit, can be harvested without significantly reducing the yield. They are stuffed, fried in dough or added raw to salads. Always leave a few male flowers so that the female flowers can be pollinated.

It is best to harvest the flowers in the morning when they are freshly opened.

This is when they are most tender and easy to stuff.
