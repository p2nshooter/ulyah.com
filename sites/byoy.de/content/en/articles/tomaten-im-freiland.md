Tomatoes are the Germans' favorite vegetable, and hardly anything tastes as good as a sun-warmed fruit straight from the vine. But in the field they have a serious opponent: late blight. In wet summers it can destroy an entire crop within two weeks. The good news: With the right location, a simple rain cover and suitable varieties, you can reliably get ripe tomatoes until October, even without spraying.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Only plant out after the Ice Saints, when the nights no longer fall below 10 °C.
> - Spacing: 60 to 80 cm in row, 80 to 100 cm between rows.
> - The foliage must remain dry: rain cover, water only at the base, remove lower leaves.
> - Grow stick tomatoes on one or two shoots and root them out weekly.
> - Plant far away from potatoes because both share the same pathogen.

## The right place in the garden

Tomatoes come from the highlands of South America and need a lot of warmth and light. Choose the sunniest spot your garden has, ideally in front of a south or southwest wall. A house wall stores heat during the day and releases it at night, and a roof overhang also keeps out rain. Many gardeners harvest healthy tomatoes in such places without any additional protection.

The soil should be deep, humus-rich and well supplied with nutrients. Tomatoes are heavy eaters. In the spring, work in about 3 to 5 liters of mature compost per square meter. If you want to add additional nitrogen, sprinkle 50 to 80 grams of horn shavings per square meter, which will slowly decompose over several weeks.

An important point for planning: keep your distance from potatoes. Both are nightshade plants, and the pathogen that causes late blight, *Phytophthora infestans*, affects both. Potato populations usually become diseased earlier and then provide masses of spores that the wind carries to your tomatoes. Tomatoes are considered self-tolerant and can be left in the same place for several years as long as you thoroughly remove all plant residue in the fall.

## Planting time and planting technique

The Ice Saints from May 11th to 15th are the best known rule of thumb. What is more important, however, is that the nights no longer fall below around 10 °C. Below this temperature, tomatoes hardly grow and cold nights permanently weaken them. In mild locations on the Rhine you can plant from mid-May, in cool regions the end of May to the beginning of June is often the better date. The young plants should be hardened off for about a week beforehand.

This is how you plant correctly:

1. Place the potted balls in a bucket of water an hour before planting.
2. Dig a planting hole that is deeper than the pot.
3. Plant the plant 5 to 10 cm deeper than it was in the pot. Tomatoes form additional roots on the buried stem, which make the plant more stable and tap water better.
4. Insert the stick or spiral right away before you could injure the roots later.
5. Water heavily and cover the soil with mulch after a few warm days.

| Growth habit | Distance in the row | Distance between rows | Support |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stick tomatoes (salad, meat, cocktail tomatoes) | 60cm | 80 to 100 cm | Spiral rod or cord, 1.8 to 2 m |
| Bush tomatoes | 60 to 80cm | 80cm | short rod or ring |
| Wild tomatoes, currant tomatoes | 80 to 100 cm | 100cm | Scaffold or let it climb freely |

## Understanding late blight and brown blight

Late blight is not a fungus in the strict sense, but an egg fungus. Its spores only germinate when the leaves are wet for several hours. This is exactly where prevention comes in: If the leaves remain dry, the pathogen can hardly gain a foothold.

Typical signs include grey-green to brown spots on the leaves that spread quickly, brown spots on the stem and hard, brown marbled fruits. In damp weather you can often see a whitish coating on the underside of the leaves.

### The rain roof

A rain roof is the most effective measure in the home garden. A simple construction made of wooden slats or metal rods with a translucent film or double-wall sheets about 2 m high is sufficient. It is important that the pages remain open. A closed polytunnel without ventilation creates condensation on the leaves and can even make the situation worse. The roof should extend 30 to 40 cm above the plants all around to keep out slanting rain.

### Further measures against brown rot

- **Watering at the base:** Water directly onto the ground in the morning, never over the leaves.
- **Remove lower leaves:** Cut the leaves to a height of about 30 to 40 cm as soon as the first fruits appear. This means that soil with spores does not splash onto the leaves.
- **Mulching:** A layer of grass clippings or straw prevents splashing water.
- **Plant airily:** It is better to plant one less plant than too densely.
- **Remove affected leaves immediately:** Dispose of them in household waste, not in the compost.
- **Disinfect rods:** Clean spiral rods with hot water or vinegar water before the new season.

## Varieties for the outdoors

The choice of variety makes a big difference. There are now varieties that are resistant to brown rot. None of them are completely immune, but they last significantly longer.

- **Hybrids with resistance breeding:** 'Philovita' F1, 'Phantasia' F1 and 'Crimson Crush' F1 are considered very resistant.
- **Seed-resistant outdoor varieties:** Varieties such as 'Primabella', a robust cocktail tomato, come from the ecological breeding project at the University of Göttingen.
- **Wild tomatoes and currant tomatoes:** Small fruits, many shoots, but surprisingly robust against brown rot. They don't need to be maxed out.

If you grow seed-resistant varieties, you can collect your own seeds and select plants that have proven successful in your garden over the years.

## Thinning, tying and trimming

Stingy shoots grow in the leaf axils, i.e. in the angle between the main shoot and the leaf. For stick tomatoes, remove them once a week while they are still small. They can then simply be broken off to the side with your fingers. Small wounds dry faster than large cuts. You should wear it on dry mornings so that the wounds have dried by the evening.

You can also grow stick tomatoes with two shoots by leaving a strong stinger shoot directly under the first inflorescence. This results in more fruit for the same number of plants, but requires a little more space.

Tie the main shoot loosely to the stick, or wind it along the spiral. With strings, rotate the plant around the string once every week.

Around the beginning to mid-August, cut off the shoot tip above the last inflorescence that is still producing fruit. Flowers that appear afterward no longer produce ripe fruit until fall. The plant then puts its power into the existing tomatoes.

## Watering and fertilizing in summer

Tomatoes need a lot of water, but especially evenly. Strong fluctuations between drought and lots of water lead to burst fruits and promote blossom end rot. The fruits become black and leathery on the underside. The cause is usually not a lack of lime in the soil, but rather a disturbed calcium transport with irregular water supply. In hot weeks, 5 to 10 liters per plant per week, divided into two to three doses, is a good guideline. A layer of mulch keeps moisture in the soil.

Once the fruit has set, you can fertilize every two to three weeks with diluted nettle or comfrey manure (around 1 part manure to 10 parts water). Comfrey provides a lot of potassium, which promotes fruit quality. Apply the manure to the moist soil, not the leaves.

## Harvest and end of season

The first fruits usually ripen outdoors from mid to late July. Harvest them when they are fully colored and give slightly under light pressure. Green collar at the base of the stem is normal for many old varieties and is not a ripening defect.

When night frost threatens in October, you harvest all the fruits that are already showing some color. They ripen at room temperature in a box with newspaper. Whole green fruits contain the alkaloid solanine and should not be eaten raw. After the harvest, pull out the plants, dispose of diseased plant parts with household waste and sow green manure or lamb's lettuce in the bed.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I grow tomatoes outdoors without a roof?

Yes, this works in dry summers and with robust varieties. But in wet years you have to expect failures from August onwards. Wild tomatoes and resistant varieties are then the safest choice.

### Does nettle manure or milk help against brown rot?

There are practical reports of home remedies such as milk or horsetail broth, but no reliable protection. They do not replace dry leaves. Once the infestation has occurred, the best you can do is slow it down by removing diseased leaves immediately.

### Why are my tomatoes dropping flowers?

Most of the time it is too hot or too cold. Pollen is poorly transmitted above about 30 °C and below 13 °C. Gently shaking the inflorescences on warm mornings helps with pollination, but in the open field this is usually done by wind and bumblebees.

### Can I compost sick tomato plants?

Better not. Normal garden compost rarely gets hot enough to kill all pathogens. Dispose of infected plant parts with household waste or the organic waste bin, as municipal composting plants reach higher temperatures.

### When do I harvest the last tomatoes in autumn?

At the latest before the first frost. Green tomatoes that have already reached their full size ripen indoors at room temperature. Wrap them individually in newspaper or put them in a box with an apple to make them color faster. Small, completely unripe fruits, on the other hand, hardly ripen any further.

### How far apart do I plant tomatoes?

About 50 to 60 centimeters in a row and 80 to 100 centimeters between rows. The distance ensures that the leaves dry quickly after rain and thus prevents fungal diseases.

Low companions such as basil or marigolds fit between the rows.
