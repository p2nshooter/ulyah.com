Anyone who stands in front of the seed shelf or leafs through a seed catalog in spring will come across terms that are initially confusing: “F1”, “seed-proof”, “organic seeds”, “old variety”, “organically bred”. These names cover different types of breeding with real consequences for your garden. They determine whether you can harvest your own seeds, how uniformly and productively the plants grow and how well they adapt to your location. This guide explains the differences clearly and helps you make the right choice for each vegetable.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - **Seed-resistant varieties** pass on their characteristics to their offspring. You can harvest your own seeds.
> - **F1 hybrids** arise from crossing two parental lines. They are uniform and often productive in the first generation, but their offspring split.
> - For hobby gardeners, non-seed varieties are usually the better choice: robust, tasty, propagable and with a harvest that is spread over weeks.
> - Hybrids can be useful for certain problems such as disease resistance.
> - Organic seeds come from plants that were grown organically. But that doesn't say anything about whether it is seed-resistant or a hybrid.

## What is a non-seed variety?

Seed-resistant varieties were created through selection over many generations. The plants of a variety pollinate among themselves, and the offspring resemble their parents in essential characteristics: shape, color, taste, ripening time. If you harvest its seeds and sow them the next year, you'll get the same variety again, assuming it hasn't been crossed with another variety.

Seed resistant varieties are not completely uniform. There is some diversity within a variety. This is often an advantage for hobby gardeners: not all plants ripen on the same day, the harvest is spread out, and individual plants cope with extreme weather better than others. Over the years, you can adapt a variety to your garden by selecting the best plants, as described in [Grow your own seeds](/gartenwissen/saatgut-selbst-gewinnen/).

## How is an F1 hybrid created?

For an F1 hybrid, seed companies first breed two very pure parental lines, often over many generations of inbreeding. These lines are usually weak in their own right. If you cross them, the first daughter generation, the F1, produces a particularly strong and uniform offspring. This effect is called heterosis.

The crossing must be carried out again for each seed production. If you sow the seeds of an F1 plant, the F2 generation is created. According to the rules of inheritance, it is divided into many different types: some are similar to one parent, some to the other, many are in between, often with weaker growth. That's why you buy new F1 seeds every year.

For some vegetables, such as cabbage, hybrids are created using techniques that produce male-sterile plants, partly by fusing cells in the laboratory. Some organic farming associations exclude such varieties. If this is important to you, look for seeds that say “seed proof” or suppliers who disclose their breeding methods.

## Advantages and disadvantages in comparison

| | Seed resistant varieties | F1 hybrids |
|---|---|---|
| Own seed production | possible | doesn't make sense, descendants split up |
| Uniformity | natural diversity within the variety | very uniform in shape and maturity |
| yield | good, often a little lower | often high |
| Harvest period | spread over weeks | often concentrated, everything ripens at the same time |
| Adaptation to the location | possible through selection over the years | not possible |
| Disease resistance | Depending on the variety, they are often robust landraces | Targeted resistance is often present |
| Seed price | usually cheaper | usually more expensive |
| taste | often aromatic, great variety | very different, partly bred for transportability |

## Organic seeds, old varieties, conservation varieties

**Organic seeds** means that the mother plants from which the seeds were harvested were grown according to the rules of organic farming. Organic seeds can be seed resistant or a hybrid. However, many organic seed dealers mainly offer seed-resistant varieties.

**Organically bred varieties** go one step further: They were bred under ecological conditions, i.e. selected for gardens without chemical plant protection and with organic fertilization.

**Heirloom varieties** are varieties that have often been grown for decades or centuries. Many are regional landraces with a special taste. Some are approved as **preservation varieties** in a simplified procedure so that they can even be offered commercially. Anyone who grows and propagates old varieties helps to preserve the diversity of our crops.

## Which choice for which vegetables?

| vegetables | Recommendation for the hobby garden |
|---|---|
| Tomatoes | Seed-resistant varieties with a wide variety of flavors, possibly tolerant varieties if there is strong late blight pressure |
| Beans, peas | seed-resistant, making your own seeds is very easy |
| Salad | seed-resistant, many proven varieties |
| Carrots, beetroot | Proven to be seed resistant, hybrids more uniform, but not necessary |
| Cabbage | Seed-resistant varieties are readily available; hybrids dominate commercial cultivation |
| Cucumbers | Greenhouse cucumbers are often hybrids with only female flowers; in the open field, good seed-resistant varieties |
| Zucchini, pumpkin | both possible, seed-resistant varieties deliver throughout the season |
| corn | Sweet corn often available as hybrid, non-seed varieties for hobby gardeners |

## Recognize seeds

You will find the necessary information on the seed bag:

- **“F1”** after the variety name stands for a hybrid.
- **“seed-resistant”** or **“non-hybrid”** indicates seed-resistant varieties. If there is no information, it is often still a seed-resistant variety, but you can only be sure with the information.
- **Organic seals** show ecological reproduction.

Many specialized organic seed companies and associations for the preservation of crop diversity only offer seed-resistant varieties. You can also find rare varieties at seed exchanges and seed festivals in spring.

## An experiment in your own garden

If you want to experience the differences yourself, make a small comparison: Plant a seedless tomato variety and a hybrid next to each other in the same bed, note growth, diseases, start of harvest, yield and taste. Harvest both seeds and sow them again next year. The seedless variety will resemble its mother plant, the offspring of the hybrid will differ significantly in growth, fruit shape and ripening time. Such experiments are educational and fun, especially with children. Sometimes you even discover a plant with particularly good characteristics among the split offspring, from which you could develop your own seed-resistant line over many years of selection.

## Maintain diversity

Today, commercial cultivation is dominated by a few varieties, often hybrids that have been bred for yield, storability and transport. Many old varieties have disappeared or can only be found in gene banks and among dedicated hobby gardeners. Any variety grown and propagated in a garden remains vibrant and can adapt to changing conditions such as hotter summers. Anyone who chooses seed-resistant varieties, collects and exchanges seeds, therefore makes a small but real contribution to the biological diversity of our cultivated plants.

## Why F1 hybrids are so consistent

For an F1 hybrid, two parent lines are inbred over several generations until they are each very uniform. If you cross these two lines, the offspring of the first generation are almost identical to one another and are often particularly vigorous. This effect is called heterosis.

The properties split again in the next generation. The plants from home-grown seeds of an F1 hybrid therefore differ greatly from each other and from the mother plant. This is not a problem for professional growers who need uniform harvests on a fixed date because new seeds are purchased every year. For hobby gardeners who want to grow seeds themselves, seed-resistant varieties are the better choice.

## Adapt seed-resistant varieties to your own garden

A big advantage of seed-resistant varieties is that they can continue to develop in your own garden. If you select seeds from the healthiest, most aromatic and best-adapted plants every year, you will get a line over the years that goes well with the soil and climate of your own garden. In the past, many regional varieties were created this way.

Today, hobby gardeners, initiatives and organic breeders are once again working together on such varieties. Anyone who exchanges seeds and passes on their experiences contributes to this diversity.

## Hybrids from cell fusion

In some vegetables, such as cabbage, hybrids are created using laboratory-created male sterility by fusing cells from different plants. This technology is not legally considered genetic engineering, but is controversial. Some organic growing associations therefore do not allow such varieties. If you want to avoid them, pay attention to seed-resistant varieties or seeds from suppliers who expressly forego them.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are F1 hybrids genetically modified?

No. Hybrid breeding is classic cross breeding. Genetically modified vegetables are not permitted in the home garden trade in the EU.

### Are hybrids worth it for hobby gardeners?

In certain cases yes, for example if a variety has important disease resistance. For most vegetables, seed-resistant varieties offer everything a home garden needs.

### How long do seeds last?

Depending on the species, between one and six years. [Germination test and storing seeds](/gartenwissen/keimprobe-und-saatgut-lagern/) explains how to test germination and store seeds correctly.

### Can I breed my own variety from an F1 hybrid?

Basically yes, but it takes patience. Over many generations, you repeatedly select the plants that match your ideas until the offspring are uniform. It usually takes many years for it to become a stable, seed-resistant variety.

### How do I recognize solid seeds in stores?

F1 hybrids are usually marked with “F1” after the variety name on the seed packet. If this addition is missing, it is usually a seedless variety. Many organic seed suppliers also expressly identify seed-resistant varieties.

### Are old varieties always seed-proof?

Yes, historical varieties come from a time when seeds were propagated and are therefore seed resistant. Conversely, not every non-seed variety is old: new non-seed varieties are also being bred today, especially for organic cultivation.

Their plants are often particularly well adapted to organic cultivation without artificial fertilizers.

They are therefore an excellent choice for the home garden, especially if you want to grow your own seeds later.
