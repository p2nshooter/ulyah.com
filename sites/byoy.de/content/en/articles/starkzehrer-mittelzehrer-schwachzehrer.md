In gardening books and on seed bags you will often come across three terms: heavy feeders, medium feeders and weak feeders. They describe how many nutrients, especially nitrogen, a vegetable needs to grow well. Classification is a simple but very practical tool. It helps you give each vegetable the right amount of compost and fertilizer, plan beds sensibly and use the soil in a balanced way over the years. Anyone who knows them fertilizes less and more specifically and avoids both starving cabbages and over-fertilized lettuce.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - **Heavy feeders** such as cabbage, pumpkin, zucchini, tomatoes, potatoes, celery and leeks need lots of nutrients and a generous amount of compost.
> - **Medium feeders** such as carrots, beetroot, chard, fennel, lettuce, onions and spinach get by with a medium amount of compost.
> - **Low eaters** such as peas, beans, lamb's lettuce, radishes and many herbs hardly need any fertilizer.
> - Legumes fix nitrogen from the air and leave the soil richer.
> - The classification is the basis of the four-field economy: heavy eaters, then medium eaters, then weak eaters, then recovery.

## What “drain” means

In old garden parlance, “Zehren” means something like consuming. A heavy eater consumes a lot, a weak eater consumes little. This primarily refers to nitrogen, which is responsible for the growth of leaves and shoots, but also potassium and other nutrients. Plants that produce a lot of mass in a short time, large leaves, heavy fruits or thick tubers have a high demand. Plants that remain small or supply themselves with nitrogen have a small amount.

## The three groups at a glance

| group | Typical vegetables | Compost per m² and year | Additionally |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heavy feeder | White cabbage, red cabbage, Brussels sprouts, broccoli, cauliflower, pumpkin, zucchini, cucumbers, tomatoes, peppers, potatoes, celeriac, leeks, corn | 3 to 5 liters | 50 to 80 g horn shavings, manure in summer |
| middle eater | Carrots, parsnips, beetroot, chard, spinach, fennel, kohlrabi, lettuce, endive, onions, garlic, radish | 2 to 3 liters | if necessary 30 g horn shavings |
| weak eater | Peas, bush beans, runner beans, lamb's lettuce, radishes, cress, rocket, many herbs | 1 to 2 liters | none |

The boundaries are fluid and some vegetables are classified differently depending on the source. Kohlrabi, for example, is sometimes considered a medium or heavy eater, and onions are sometimes considered a medium or weak eater. The table shows proven classifications for gardening practice.

## Properly care for heavy eaters

Heavy feeders are on the best-supplied bed in the first year of crop rotation. You get:

- **in autumn or spring** a generous amount of compost, preferably also well-seasoned manure,
- **for planting** a handful of horn shavings or compost in the planting hole for pumpkins and tomatoes,
- **in summer**, regular doses of diluted nettle manure because your need increases as the fruit sets.

Pumpkin and zucchini grow particularly well right next to or on the compost heap. In addition to nitrogen, cabbage needs soil that is not too acidic. How organic fertilizers work and how you make manure can be found in [Organic fertilization](/gartenwissen/organisch-duengen/).

**Signs of deficiency:** Heavy feeders who get too little remain small, their older leaves turn light green to yellow, and the fruits or heads remain puny.

## Properly provide for middle-eaters

Medium feeders are on the heavy feeder bed in the second year. They still find enough nutrients there. A light addition of compost in the spring is usually enough.

For some moderate eaters, too much nitrogen leads to problems:

- **Carrots** with fresh manure or a lot of nitrogen become leggy, branchy and more susceptible to the carrot fly.
- **Onions** become soft and store poorly.
- **Spinach, chard and lettuce** can store nitrates, especially in low light in spring and fall.

## Properly care for weak eaters

Weak feeders are on the bed in the third year and get by with the remaining nutrients. At most you get some compost.

The **legumes** play a special role: peas, beans and clover live together with nodule bacteria on their roots. These bacteria bind nitrogen from the air and make it available to the plant. If the roots remain in the ground after harvest, part of them is freed for the next crop. Therefore, cut peas and beans just above the ground instead of pulling them out. [Growing bush beans and pole beans](/gartenwissen/buschbohnen-und-stangenbohnen/) describes how beans are grown.

**Mediterranean herbs** such as thyme, sage, rosemary and oregano are also weak eaters. In soil that is too nutrient-rich, they grow fattened and lose their aroma.

## The division in practice

### Crop rotation

The three groups form the basis of four-field farming: a bed with heavy feeders is followed next year by medium feeders, then weak feeders and finally a year of recovery with green manure before heavy feeders come again with fresh compost. This means that each bed is regularly fed and then used gradually. [Planning crop rotation](/gartenwissen/fruchtfolge-planen/) explains what this actually looks like.

### Mixed culture

In mixed culture, plants with different needs are placed side by side, such as cabbage and lettuce or tomatoes and basil. Make sure that heavy eaters do not stand too close together, otherwise they will compete for nutrients. A combination of a heavy eater with a medium or light eater usually works well. More about this in [Mixed culture: good and bad neighbors](/gartenwissen/mischkultur-gute-und-schlechte-nachbarn/).

### raised bed

A freshly filled raised bed is very nutrient-rich. In the first year you set heavy feeders, in the second year medium feeders and from the third onwards you set weak feeders until you refill. Details can be found in [Creating a raised bed](/gartenwissen/hochbeet-anlegen/).

| year | cultures | Supply |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Zucchini, pumpkin, nasturtiums in between | Before autumn, manure or 5 liters of compost per m², in the summer manure |
| 2 | Carrots and onions in alternating rows, followed by lamb's lettuce | 2 liters of compost in spring |
| 3 | Bush beans with savory, then phacelia | no fertilization |
| 4 | Green manure or strawberries, compost again in autumn for heavy feeders | Compost in autumn |

## How to recognize nutrient status

Plants show quite clearly whether they are well cared for. Look out for these signs:

| Observation | Possible cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| older leaves light green to yellow, weak growth | Nitrogen deficiency | diluted nettle manure, horn meal, compost |
| lush green, soft, masty plants, lots of aphids | too much nitrogen | Reduce fertilization, use less compost next year |
| yellow leaves with green veins on young leaves | Iron deficiency, often due to too high a pH value | Measure the pH value, do not lime |
| brown, dried leaf edges on tomatoes or potatoes | Potassium deficiency or drought stress | Pour comfrey manure evenly |
| Fruits with black spots on the underside (tomatoes) | Blossom end rot caused by uneven water supply | water regularly, mulch |

Many symptoms have multiple causes, and dryness, cold or waterlogging sometimes look like nutrient deficiencies. Before you fertilize, always check the soil, water and weather. A soil analysis provides clarity, as described in [Determine soil type](/gartenwissen/bodenart-bestimmen/).

## Why legumes need so little

Peas, beans, lentils and clover are low-eaters because they obtain their most important nutrients themselves. Nodule bacteria live on their roots and bind nitrogen from the air and make it available to the plant. In return, the plant supplies the bacteria with sugar. If you carefully dig up a bean or pea plant, you will see the small, rounded nodules on the roots.

After harvesting, it is worth leaving the roots in the ground and only cutting off the above-ground parts. This means that some of the bound nitrogen remains in the bed and benefits the following crop. That's why legumes are placed directly before more demanding vegetables in many crop rotations.

## Nutrient requirements in the bucket and raised bed

The root space in pots and containers is limited and the soil's nutrients are used up more quickly than in garden soil. Heavy feeders such as tomatoes, peppers or zucchini need regular replenishment from around four to six weeks after planting, for example an organic liquid fertilizer every one to two weeks.

In the raised bed it is initially the other way around: the fresh filling is very nutrient-rich, which is why heavy eaters are included in the first year, and medium and weak eaters gradually in the following years. More about this in the guide [Creating a raised bed](/gartenwissen/hochbeet-anlegen/).

## Sign of oversupply

Too much fertilizer is just as damaging as too little. Plants that get too much nitrogen produce soft, dark green leaves, are more susceptible to aphids and fungal diseases, and produce flowers and fruit later. Nitrate also accumulates in leafy vegetables such as spinach or lettuce when overfertilized.

What the plants don't absorb is washed out with the rain and can end up in the groundwater. Therefore, fertilize as needed and preferably in small doses, and if in doubt, have the soil examined every few years.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I provide for heavy eaters without crop rotation?

Yes, with enough compost and fertilizer they will grow anywhere. But crop rotation also has a second purpose: it stops the proliferation of diseases and pests in the soil.

### Is corn a heavy feeder?

Yes. Sweet corn needs a lot of nutrients and water. In the classic “Three Sisters” mixed culture, it grows with beans, which provide nitrogen, and pumpkin, which covers the ground.

### Do strawberries need a lot of fertilizer?

Strawberries are considered a medium nutritious food. Too much nitrogen promotes leaves instead of fruit and makes them susceptible to gray mold. A little compost after harvest is enough. More about this in [Planting strawberries](/gartenwissen/erdbeeren-pflanzen/).

### Are herbs weak eaters?

Most Mediterranean herbs such as thyme, rosemary, sage and oregano are extremely undemanding and thrive most aromatically in lean soil. Basil and parsley, on the other hand, need a little more nutrients and are more likely to be medium consuming.

### Is there a binding classification?

No. Depending on the source, some vegetables are classified differently, such as kohlrabi or onions. The classification is a guide, not a hard and fast rule. Monitor your plants and adapt fertilization to their growth.

### How do I know if a heavy eater is starving?

Starving heavy eaters grow slowly, the older leaves turn light green to yellow from below, and fruits remain small. Then give a dose of organic fertilizer or mature compost, water well and observe whether the new leaves sprout strong green again.
