Bokashi is a method of fermenting kitchen waste in an airtight bucket instead of allowing it to rot in the air like in compost. The word comes from Japanese and means “fermented organic material”. Bokashi is popular in apartments, on balconies and in small gardens because the bucket can stand in the kitchen, hardly smells and processes waste for the soil after just a few weeks.

At the same time, Bokashi is often associated with expectations that it does not fulfill. A Bokashi bucket does not replace compost and does not provide finished soil, but rather an intermediate product that only becomes humus in the soil or compost. This guide explains how the method works, how you use it in everyday life and what mistakes you should avoid.

## How Bokashi works

In compost, bacteria, fungi and soil animals decompose organic material with the help of oxygen. In the Bokashi bucket, however, fermentation takes place without air, similar to sauerkraut. Lactic acid bacteria and other microorganisms, which are usually added as a starter culture, acidify the waste. They are preserved and pre-digested, but do not rot.

After fermentation, the waste looks almost the same as before, but is softer, sour and covered with a fine white fungal coating, which is a good sign. Only when the ferment gets into the soil or compost do soil organisms break it down into humus within a few weeks. Because the waste is pre-digested, this often happens faster than with fresh kitchen leftovers.

## What you need

- **A Bokashi bucket:** an airtight bucket with a sieve insert and a tap at the bottom through which the resulting liquid is drained. Many gardeners work with two buckets alternately: while one is fermenting, the other is filled.
- **A starter culture:** Bokashi bran or a liquid containing microorganisms added after each layer. It ensures that the desired bacteria have the upper hand right from the start.
- **A masher or plate:** to press the scraps together and force out air.

## What can go in the bucket

The Bokashi bucket mainly contains raw kitchen waste: leftover vegetables and fruits, peels, coffee grounds, tea leaves, eggshells, wilted flowers and leftover bread. Because the fermentation takes place without air and under acidic conditions, many bokashi systems, according to the manufacturer, can also tolerate small amounts of cooked food scraps, which would attract rats in open compost.

Liquids such as soup or milk, moldy food, large bones, ash, large quantities of paper, plastic and garden waste such as leaves or branches should not be placed in the bucket. Large amounts of meat, fish and fat are also unfavorable because they disturb the balance and can later attract animals in the ground.

## Start step by step

1. **Shredding:** Cut larger waste into small pieces. The smaller they are, the faster they ferment.
2. **Fill:** Add a layer of waste to the bucket.
3. **Add starter:** Sprinkle some bokashi bran on top or spray the layer with the starter liquid according to the manufacturer's instructions.
4. **Pressing:** Press the layer firmly together with the masher so that as little air as possible remains.
5. **Closing:** Immediately close the lid airtight again.
6. **Repeat:** Add a new layer daily or every few days until the bucket is full.
7. **Let it rest:** Let the full, sealed bucket sit at room temperature for about two weeks without opening it.

A healthy bokashi smells sour, a bit like pickled vegetables. If, on the other hand, it smells foul, air has usually gotten in or it was too wet.

## The Bokashi juice

During fermentation, a liquid collects at the bottom of the bucket. Drain it through the tap every two to three days, otherwise the contents will become too wet and begin to rot. The sap is acidic and should never be given to plants undiluted.

Highly diluted, often in a ratio of around 1 to 100 with water, it is used as a liquid fertilizer for house and garden plants. Fresh juice has a short shelf life and should be consumed within a few days. Poured undiluted into the drain, many users say it reduces deposits in pipes; However, a reliable effect has not been proven and it is not a substitute as a cleaning agent.

## Put the ferment into the ground

After the rest period, the content is ready for the next step. You have several options:

- **Bury in the bed:** Dig a trench or hole about 20 to 30 centimeters deep, pour the ferment into it, mix it with some soil and cover it well. After about two to four weeks it will be largely broken down and you can plant in this spot. Do not plant directly into the acidic ferment immediately, otherwise young roots may be damaged.
- **In the compost:** Mix the ferment into the compost pile. It accelerates rotting and provides lots of nutrients.
- **In a soil factory:** If you don't have a garden, mix the ferment in a large bucket or box with old potting soil, cover it and let it sit for a few weeks. This then creates a nutrient-rich soil for balcony boxes.

## Bokashi and compost in comparison

Bokashi and compost complement each other rather than replacing each other. The Bokashi bucket is ideal for kitchen waste in the home and for people who don't have space for a compost heap. It also absorbs waste that would cause problems in compost and hardly any nutrients are lost because nothing evaporates or is washed out.

Compost, on the other hand, processes large amounts of garden waste such as leaves, lawn clippings and shrub residues, ultimately delivers ripe, crumbly soil and does not require a starter culture or special buckets. Many gardeners use both: kitchen waste goes into the bokashi bucket, the ferment and garden waste go into the compost. ## Limitations of the method

Bokashi has some limits that you should be aware of. The starter culture has to be purchased regularly, which causes ongoing costs. If you have large amounts of kitchen waste, you will need several buckets. And the ferment still needs soil or compost to turn into humus. Without this second step, all that's left is a bucket full of acidic waste.

Many promises about effective microorganisms, such as that they cure diseases or quickly transform any soil, are not scientifically proven. However, it has been proven that organic material added to the soil feeds soil life and builds humus. That's exactly what Bokashi reliably does.

## Typical mistakes

- **Too wet:** The juice is not drained regularly, the contents rot and stink. Drain more often and add dry waste such as leftover bread or some bran.
- **Too much air:** The bucket is not pressed firmly enough or remains open. Always press firmly and close immediately.
- **Too large pieces:** Whole heads of cabbage or large bowls will ferment slowly and unevenly.
- **Impatience:** The ferment is buried too early directly under plants, whose roots are then damaged.
- **Black or green mold:** A sign that air has entered or fermentation has failed. Such a bucket goes on the compost, not in the bed near plants.

## Practical example: Bokashi in the city apartment

A family in a city apartment without a garden no longer wanted to dispose of their kitchen waste in the organic waste bin in the yard, but instead wanted to use it for the balcony boxes. She placed two bokashi buckets under the sink. While one was being filled, the other was fermenting. They diluted the juice and used it to water their house plants and tomatoes.

For the finished ferment, they set up a large box of old potting soil on the balcony, into which they mixed and covered each full bucket. After a few weeks it had turned into dark, crumbly soil, which they used to freshen up their balcony boxes in the spring. The box barely smelled and the tomatoes grew more vigorously than last year.

## Bokashi, dogs and wild animals

Fermented waste smells tempting to dogs, foxes and raccoons. Therefore, bury the ferment deep enough, at least two spade-holes, and cover the area with a board, a grid or a few stones for the first few days. Mix the ferment well into the compost and cover it with a layer of leaves or soil. Always keep the bucket itself closed so that pets cannot get to the contents.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a bokashi bucket smell in the kitchen?

As long as it's closed, hardly. When opened, a healthy bokashi smells sour, but not putrid. A strong smell of rot is a sign that something has gone wrong, usually too much moisture or air.

### Can I plant bokashi in winter?

Yes, Bokashi works all year round in a warm apartment. In winter, keep the finished ferment sealed until spring or mix it into the compost, because you cannot dig up frozen ground.

### Do I absolutely need a purchased starter culture?

For reliable results, a starter culture is recommended. Some gardeners make their own bokashi bran, but this requires experience. If you work without a starter, you risk that putrefactive bacteria will gain the upper hand.

### Is Bokashi better than a worm bin?

Both methods are suitable for apartments. The worm box provides ready-made worm humus, but requires care and does not tolerate all waste. Bokashi processes more types of waste and faster, but requires soil or compost afterwards. Some gardeners combine the two.

### How much starter culture do I need?

Follow the manufacturer's instructions. As a rough guide, a handful of bokashi bran per layer is usually sufficient. For waste that is high in protein or if the bucket starts to smell, add a little more.

## Conclusion

Bokashi is a practical method of processing kitchen waste in the home in a space-saving and almost odorless manner. When firmly pounded, mixed with starter culture and sealed in an airtight container, the waste ferments in about two weeks. The diluted juice serves as a liquid fertilizer, and the ferment becomes humus in the bed, compost or in a soil box. Bokashi does not replace compost, but is an ideal supplement, especially where there is no space for a compost heap.
