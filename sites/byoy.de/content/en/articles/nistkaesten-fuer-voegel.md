Cave-nesting birds such as tits, sparrows and nuthatches no longer find natural breeding holes in many gardens. Old trees with woodpecker holes and rotten knotholes have become rare, and modern houses hardly offer any niches. A well-built and properly hung nesting box can close this gap while bringing a piece of nature right outside the window.

In order for a nesting box to actually be used and for breeding to be successful, a few details are important: the right size of the entry hole, the right place, protection from cats and martens and regular cleaning. This guide explains the most important things.

## Which birds use nesting boxes

Nesting boxes are primarily used by birds that nest in caves or semi-caves. The most common guests in gardens include the great tit, blue tit, coal tit, house sparrow, tree sparrow, nuthatch and starling. Semi-cave nesters such as black redstarts, robins, spotted flycatchers and wagtails prefer boxes with a large, open front.

Birds that nest freely in hedges and trees, such as blackbirds, greenfinches or chaffinches, on the other hand, do not use closed nesting boxes. Dense hedges and bushes are the best help for them.

## The entrance hole decides

The size of the entry hole determines which species can use a box. Small holes keep larger competitors and some predators away. These values serve as orientation:

- **26 to 28 millimeters:** Blue tit, coal tit, marsh tit.
- **32 millimeters:** Great tit, nuthatch, tree sparrow, also blue tit.
- **32 to 35 millimeters:** House Sparrow.
- **Approximately 45 millimeters:** Star.
- **Half-open boxes:** Black redstart, robin, spotted flycatcher.

The entrance hole should not be too low at the bottom of the box so that cats or martens cannot reach into it. As a rule of thumb, there is a distance of at least 17 centimeters between the bottom edge of the hole and the bottom of the box.

## Material and construction method

Nesting boxes should be made of untreated wood or wood concrete. Wood concrete, a mixture of wood chips and concrete, is particularly durable, breathable and protects well against woodpeckers and martens. Wooden boxes are easy to build yourself and work well as a project with children.

Pay attention to these points:

- **Untreated wood:** Do not use paint, glaze or wood preservatives inside. The outside of the box can be painted with an environmentally friendly paint.
- **Wood thickness:** At least about two centimeters so that the box buffers heat and cold.
- **Small holes in the bottom:** They allow water to drain out.
- **No perch in front of the entrance hole:** The birds don't need it, but predators use it as a support.
- **Openable front or lid:** so that the box can be cleaned in the fall.
- **Rough inside under the entrance hole:** Young birds can pull themselves up on it when flying out.

## The right place

Hang nesting boxes about two to three meters high, on a tree trunk, a house wall or on a post. The entrance hole should face east or southeast, i.e. away from the weather side, from which wind and rain usually come in Central Europe. The blazing midday sun heats up the box considerably and can endanger the young birds.

The approach should be clear, but nearby trees or bushes should provide cover. Hang the box slightly tilted forward so that no rain runs into the entrance hole. Several boxes for the same species need to be spaced apart because many birds defend a territory. Sparrows, on the other hand, like to breed socially; colony boxes with several chambers are suitable for them.

## Protection against cats and martens

Cats, martens and magpies are among the most common predators of nestlings. Therefore, choose places for the boxes that cats cannot easily reach, such as free-standing trunks without branches at jumping height or house walls without protrusions. A smooth metal cuff ring around the trunk, a thorny rose at the base of the tree or a trellis below the box make climbing difficult.

Many wood-concrete boxes have a deep interior or a porch at the entrance hole that prevents paws from reaching the nest. Don't hang boxes over trash cans, shed roofs, or window sills that serve as springboards for cats.

## When to hang up?

The best time is autumn or early winter. Many birds also use nesting boxes as a place to sleep on cold nights, and in spring they already know the box. A box hung up in February is often moved in the same spring, because tits start building nests in March, depending on the weather. Boxes that are hung later are also occasionally used for a second brood.

## Cleaning in the fall

After the breeding season you should clean the boxes, because bird fleas, mites and other parasites often live in old nests, which burden the next brood. The right time is autumn, around September to November, when breeding is no longer in progress.

Open the box, completely remove the old nest and sweep out the interior. Wear gloves when doing this. Chemical cleaning products and insect sprays do not belong in nesting boxes. If the box is very dirty, you can brush it out with hot water and let it dry thoroughly. If you find a nest with unbrooded eggs or dead young birds, dispose of it too. If the box is inhabited by dormice, bumblebees or hibernating insects in the fall, leave it alone until late winter.

## Do not disturb during breeding season

As exciting as it is: Do not open an inhabited box during the breeding season and keep your distance. Frequent disturbances can cause parents to abandon the nest. Observe what is happening from a distance, for example from the window with binoculars. The feeding flights of the adult birds and at the end the fledging of the young are an experience for the whole family.

Young birds that have fledged and cannot yet fly properly and are sitting on the ground are usually not abandoned. The parents continue to feed them. Leave them alone and keep cats indoors during this time if possible.

## More than a box

Nesting boxes help, but they work best in a garden that also provides food for the birds. Native bushes with berries, perennials with seed heads, a place with insects and a shallow bird bath make the garden a living space. Many garden birds feed their young with caterpillars and insects. A garden without insecticides and with flowers for insects is therefore the most important support. ## Practical example: The tit box on the apple tree

A family hung two nesting boxes in November: one with a 26-millimeter hole for blue tits on the apple tree and one with a 32-millimeter hole for great tits on the east side of the shed. Both boxes hung at a height of a good two meters with a clear approach, the apple tree was given a smooth cuff to protect against cats.

In March, blue tits brought moss into the first box, and a pair of great tits moved into the second. The children watched from the kitchen window as their parents brought in countless caterpillars in May, including from the apple tree, which was hardly infested with caterpillars this year. In October they cleaned the boxes together and found two neatly built nests made of moss, hair and feathers.

## Build your own tit box

A simple tit box is a nice weekend project, even with children. You need a rough, untreated board about two centimeters thick, screws, a saw, a drill with a Forstner bit for the entrance hole and two hinges or a twist lock for the cleaning flap.

The interior should have a footprint of around 12 by 12 centimeters and be a good 25 centimeters high. The roof protrudes slightly at the front so that rain doesn't run into the entrance hole. Drill the entrance hole into the front wall so high that there is at least 17 centimeters of space between the bottom edge of the hole and the floor. There are a few small holes in the bottom for drainage. Screw the parts together instead of gluing them, and roughen the inside under the entrance hole a little with a saw.

Wire hangers with a piece of garden hose to protect the bark or aluminum nails are suitable for hanging on the tree, as they do less damage to the wood and do not cause problems later when sawing.

## Help for building breeders

In addition to tits and sparrows, many birds breed on and in buildings, such as swifts, house martins, barn swallows and black redstarts. They often lack niches and cavities in modern or renovated houses. There are special nesting aids for them: swift boxes that are installed high under the eaves, artificial nests for house martins under the roof overhang and open niches for black redstarts.

Nests of building nesters are protected by law, even outside of the breeding season when the birds use them every year. Anyone who is renovating their house should check beforehand whether breeding places are affected and create replacements. Nature conservation associations often provide advice on this free of charge.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why is my nesting box not covered?

Common reasons are an unfavorable location, for example in full sun, on the weather side or too low, an entrance hole that is too large or too small, too much disturbance or competition from too many boxes close together. Sometimes it just takes a year of patience. Move the box to a better location in the fall.

### Can I hang nest boxes during breeding season?

Yes, this is allowed at any time and sometimes makes sense for late breeders. However, boxes that hang in autumn or winter are accepted much more often.

### Do birds need nesting material?

Most of the time they find enough. In spring, a few undyed sheep's wool flakes or chemical-free animal hair in a wire basket will help. Avoid using synthetic fibers, threads or cords that young birds can get caught in.

### Do I have to remove old nests in spring?

No, cleaning takes place in autumn. In spring, birds may have already started building nests and any disruption would be unfavorable.

### How many nesting boxes fit in a garden?

This depends on the size of the garden and the species. For titmice, it is better to have a few boxes at a sufficient distance than many close together, because the pairs defend their territory. In a terraced garden, two to three boxes are usually enough for different species.

## Conclusion

A nesting box helps cavity-nesting garden birds if it is made of untreated wood or wood-concrete, has the right entrance hole and hangs at a height of two to three meters with an entrance hole facing east or southeast. Protection from cats, rest during the breeding season and cleaning in autumn ensure breeding success. Together with native shrubs, insects and water, the garden becomes a real habitat for birds.
