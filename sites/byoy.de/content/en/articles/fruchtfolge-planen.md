Anyone who plants tomatoes in the same place year after year, always puts cabbage in the same corner or constantly grows peas in the same bed often experiences this after just a few years: the plants suffer, diseases increase, and the harvest becomes smaller, even though fertilization and watering have been carried out. The reason is simple. Plants in the same family get the same nutrients from the soil and have the same pests and pathogens that accumulate in the soil. Crop rotation is agriculture's oldest answer to this: crops change places every year so that the soil has time to recover. With a little planning, this is easy to implement in your home garden.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - The plant family is crucial: cabbage, radishes and rocket are cruciferous vegetables, tomatoes and potatoes are nightshades, carrots and celery are umbellifers.
> - Plants from the same family should only be back in the same place after three to four years at the earliest, peas and cabbage often even longer.
> - The four-field economy divides the garden into four areas: heavy feeders, medium feeders, weak feeders and a recreational bed. Every year the groups move one further.
> - Keep a simple gardening book with one sketch per year. After three years, nothing can be understood without notes.
> - Crop rotation and mixed culture complement each other: change over the years, diversity in the same year.

## Why crop rotation works

**Diseases and pests in soil:** Many pathogens survive in the soil, some for many years. The clubroot only attacks cruciferous vegetables, certain nematodes mainly affect potatoes and tomatoes, the carrot fly overwinters as a pupa in the soil of the carrot bed. If the appropriate host plant is missing for a few years, the pathogens decline.

**Nutrients:** Heavy feeders such as cabbage and pumpkin remove a lot of nitrogen from the soil, weak feeders such as peas and beans even enrich it with nitrogen. The change uses the ground in a balanced way.

**Roots at different depths:** Shallow-rooted plants such as lettuce and onions use the upper layer, deep-rooted plants such as carrots, parsnips and tomatoes reach deeper layers.

**Soil fatigue:** Some species tolerate themselves poorly. Peas, for example, show significant growth problems after just a few years in the same area.

## The most important plant families

| family | vegetables | Cultivation break at the same place |
|---|---|---|
| Cruciferous vegetables | Cabbage, kohlrabi, broccoli, radishes, radishes, rocket, mustard | 4 years, significantly longer with clubroot |
| Nightshade family | Tomatoes, potatoes, peppers, eggplant | 3 to 4 years |
| Cucurbitaceae | Pumpkin, zucchini, cucumbers, melons | 3 years |
| Umbelliferous plants | Carrots, parsnips, celery, fennel, parsley, dill | 3 to 4 years |
| legumes | Peas, bush beans, runner beans, broad beans | Peas 4 to 5 years, beans 3 years |
| Foxtail family | Spinach, chard, beetroot | 3 years |
| Allium family | Onions, garlic, leeks, chives | 3 to 4 years |
| Asteraceae | Lettuce, endive, chicory, Jerusalem artichoke | 2 to 3 years |
| Valerian family | Lamb's lettuce | 2 years |

The periods are proven guidelines from gardening practice. For specific illnesses such as clubroot, longer breaks may be necessary. Tomatoes are considered to be comparatively self-sustainable and are often grown in the same place for several years if all plant residue is removed in autumn. In the case of illness problems, switching helps here too. More about tomatoes in the bed can be found in [Tomatoes in the field](/gartenwissen/tomaten-im-freiland/).

## The four-field economy

The most proven system for home gardens divides the vegetable area into four areas of approximately equal size. Every year the groups move one area further.

| area | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 | Year 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Heavy feeder | middle eater | weak eater | recovery |
| b | recovery | Heavy feeder | middle eater | weak eater |
| C | weak eater | recovery | Heavy feeder | middle eater |
| D | middle eater | weak eater | recovery | Heavy feeder |

- **Heavy eaters:** Cabbage, pumpkin, zucchini, cucumbers, tomatoes, potatoes, celery, leeks. This area receives the most compost.
- **Medium eaters:** Carrots, beetroot, chard, fennel, lettuce, onions, spinach.
- **Low eaters:** Peas, beans, lamb's lettuce, radishes, many herbs.
- **Recreation:** Green manures, strawberries, perennial herbs or flowers for insects.

[Heavy eaters, medium eaters, weak eaters](/gartenwissen/starkzehrer-mittelzehrer-schwachzehrer/) explains what the classification means and what exceptions there are. Plants from [green manure in the vegetable garden](/gartenwissen/gruenduengung/) are suitable for the recreational bed.

**A note about the family:** Classifying according to nutrient requirements alone is not enough. Additionally, make sure that cabbage does not follow a bed that had mustard as a green manure or radishes the previous year, and that potatoes do not grow where tomatoes previously stood.

## Crop rotation in a small garden

Not everyone has space for four large areas. You can also rotate on two raised beds or four square meters:

- **Rows instead of beds:** Divide a bed into rows and let families move down one row each year.
- **Buckets for problem crops:** Tomatoes or potatoes can grow in a bucket with fresh soil when the bed needs a break.
- **Limit Families:** Don't grow something from every family. If you hardly eat cabbage, you save on the cruciferous vegetables and have more leeway.
- **Use mixed culture:** Many families in a small area spread the risk, as described in [Mixed culture: good and bad neighbors](/gartenwissen/mischkultur-gute-und-schlechte-nachbarn/).

## Include pre-, main- and post-culture

Over the course of a year, there are often several crops on one bed one after the other. Also plan this sequence according to family: Early kohlrabi should not be followed by radish or winter radish in late summer, both are cruciferous vegetables. Lamb's lettuce or spinach are better. How you use a bed three times a year can be found in [pre-culture, main culture, post-culture](/gartenwissen/vor-und-nachkultur/).

## The Garden Book

The most important part of crop rotation is journaling. Each year, draw a simple sketch of your beds and record what was where, including pre- and post-crops and green manure. Also note diseases and particularly good or bad harvests. After a few years you will recognize patterns that no guide can reveal to you. [Create a bed plan](/gartenwissen/beetplan-erstellen/) explains how to design a plan step by step.

## Common mistakes in crop rotation

- **Forget green manure:** Mustard or radish as green manure are cruciferous vegetables. Anyone who plants cabbage afterwards will interrupt the crop rotation without noticing it.
- **Plan only according to nutrients:** Changing heavy feeders, medium feeders and weak feeders is not enough if there is cabbage in the heavy feeder bed every year. The plant family has to migrate with you.
- **Herbs overlooked:** Parsley in the carrot bed is one more umbelliferous plant in the same place.
- **Potatoes from the previous year:** Forgotten tubers will sprout again next year in the middle of the bean bed. Remove them consistently, otherwise nightshades will remain in place.
- **No notes:** Without notes, after two years no one knows what was written where.

## Heavy, medium and weak eaters alternate

In addition to the plant families, nutrient requirements play an important role. A tried-and-tested scheme allows heavy, medium and weak feeders to alternate on one area: In the first year, nutrient-hungry crops such as cabbage, tomatoes or pumpkins are planted on soil that has been freshly supplied with compost. In the second year, middle feeders such as carrots, onions, lettuce or chard follow, which live on the remaining nutrients. In the third year, frugal, weak eaters such as peas, beans and herbs come.

In the fourth year, green manure can regenerate the soil before the cycle starts again. Legumes and green manure also enrich the soil with nitrogen. The guide [heavy, medium and weak eaters] (/gartenwissen/starkzehrer-mittelzehrer-schwachzehrer/) shows which vegetables belong to which group.

## Diseases that persist in the soil for a long time

The most important reason for crop rotation is diseases and pests that survive in the soil. Clubroot, a disease of the cabbage family, forms permanent spores that remain viable in the soil for many years. Certain nematodes reproduce on potatoes, and carrot fly larvae and other pests find their host plants more easily if they are always in the same place.

Crop rotation cannot completely prevent such problems, but it breaks the cycle: If a pathogen does not find a suitable plant for several years, its number in the soil decreases. That's why it's so important to let not just individual vegetables migrate, but entire plant families.

## Crop rotation in raised beds and buckets

It's also worth changing the raised bed, even though the soil there is richer in nutrients. Divide a large raised bed into sections and let families move one field each year. With tomatoes and other vegetables in pots, it is easiest to replace the soil every year and add the old one to the compost if the plants were healthy.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to strictly follow the crop rotation?

The stricter the better for soil health. Small variations are not a problem as long as you don't grow the same family in the same place for years.

### What do I do if I have clubroot?

Do not grow any cruciferous vegetables on the affected area for many years, nor any green manure made from mustard or oilseed radish. Remove the roots of infected plants and do not add them to the compost. Calcareous soils with a pH value above 7 inhibit the pathogen.

### Do herbs count as part of crop rotation?

Yes. Parsley and dill belong to the umbelliferous family, and chives belong to the leek family. Perennial herbs such as thyme and sage are better placed in their own bed.

### What do I do with perennial crops?

Rhubarb, asparagus, strawberries, horseradish and perennial herbs stay in the same place for years and are removed from the crop rotation. It's best to give them their own permanent bed at the edge of the vegetable garden so that they don't disturb the change in the other beds.

### How do I keep track of things over the years?

The easiest way is with a garden book or a sketch each year in which you write down which family was where. Whether on paper or in a gardening app is a matter of taste. It is only important that the records go back at least four years.

### Does crop rotation also apply to green manure?

Yes. Green manure plants also belong to plant families. Mustard and radish are cruciferous vegetables like cabbage and should therefore not be grown on cabbage beds, especially if clubroot has already appeared there. Phacelia is not related to any common vegetable family and fits almost anywhere.

For each green manure, write down which family it belongs to.

This way you prevent cabbage diseases from creeping in through the green manure.
