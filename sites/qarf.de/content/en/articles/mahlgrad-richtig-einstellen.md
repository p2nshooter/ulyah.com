The grinding degree is the setting screw on which most coffee problems depend. A coffee that tastes sour and thin is often ground too coarsely. One that is bitter, dry and scratchy, often too fine. Because each method of preparation brings the water into contact with the coffee for a different amount of time and in a different way, each needs its own grinding level. And because every grinder is scaled differently, a number on the dial alone is of little help. This guide shows which grind goes where, how to find it on your grinder and how to adjust using the taste as a compass.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - The **shorter** time water and coffee are in contact, the **finer** the grind will be. The longer, the coarser.
> - Rule of thumb: Turkish dust fine, espresso fine, espresso maker fine to medium, hand filter medium, French press coarse, cold brew very coarse.
> - The numbers on mills are not comparable. Orientate yourself on appearance, turnaround time and taste.
> - **Sour and thin** usually means too coarse, **bitter and dry** usually means too fine.
> - Change one thing at a time and in small steps.

## Why the degree of grinding matters so much

When brewing, water releases aromatics from the ground coffee. How quickly this happens depends on the surface: fine flour has a much larger surface area than coarse flour, which is why water dissolves more quickly and more. At the same time, fine flour slows down the flow of water because it is denser. Coarse flour allows the water to pass through more quickly and releases less.

For a preparation to be in balance, the contact time and the degree of grinding must match. An espresso only takes about 25 to 30 seconds, so it needs very fine flour. A French press brews for four minutes, so grind coarsely or the coffee will be bitter and muddy. [Understanding Extraction](/ratgeber/extraktion-verstehen/) explains how water dissolves aromas and what under- and over-extraction mean.

## The grinding table

| Preparation | Grinding degree | Touch-and-touch comparison | Contact time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Turkish mocha | fine as dust | Flour, powdered sugar | in the jug for a few minutes |
| Espresso | fine | fine sand, slightly lumpy | 25 to 30 seconds |
| Espresso maker | fine to medium fine | Semolina, fine sand | a few minutes |
| AeroPress | fine to medium | depending on the recipe | 1 to 3 minutes |
| Hand filter | medium fine to medium | granulated sugar to coarse sand | 2.5 to 4 minutes |
| Filter coffee machine | medium | coarse sand | 4 to 6 minutes |
| French press | roughly | coarse sea salt | about 4 minutes |
| Cold brew | very rough | coarse salt, ground pepper | 12 to 24 hours |

The comparisons are indicative. Two mills that are set to the same “sand” can still produce different amounts of fines. That's why taste and lead time are better measuring devices.

## Find the starting point

1. **Use manufacturer information:** Many grinders have a scale with symbols or areas for espresso, filter and French press. Start there in the middle.
2. **Calibrate:** With many hand mills you count clicks from zero. Carefully turn the grinder all the way until the grinder just stops turning, and count from there. Record the number of clicks for each preparation.
3. **Visual inspection:** Rub some flour between your fingers and compare it to the table.
4. **Test brews:** Brew broth according to a fixed recipe and measure the time.

## Adjust to taste

Record everything else, i.e. amount of coffee, amount of water, temperature and method, and only change the grind.

| taste | pass | Probably | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| sour, salty, thin, short finish | quickly | too coarse, under-extracted | grind finer |
| sweet, balanced, juicy | in the target area | fits | maintained |
| bitter, dry, astringent | slowly | too fine, over-extracted | grind coarser |
| sour and bitter at the same time | uneven | uneven grinds or channeling | Check the mill, distribute it better |

With the hand filter, the throughput time is a good indication: If a cup with 15 grams of coffee and 250 milliliters of water runs through in significantly less than two and a half minutes, grind finer. If it takes significantly longer than four minutes, grind more coarsely. How to brew using the hand filter is described in [Hand filter: Brew coffee by hand](/ratgeber/handfilter-kaffee-anleitung/).

With espresso you work with dose, yield and time. The details explained [Portafilters and espresso: the basics](/ratgeber/siebtraeger-espresso-grundlagen/).

## The influence of the mill

Not only the setting, but also the mill itself determines the result. Good mills grind **evenly**, with little dust and few coarse chunks. Beater knife “grinders,” on the other hand, chop the beans randomly. The flour contains dust and large pieces at the same time, the extraction is uneven, and the coffee tastes sour and bitter at the same time. If you're just starting out, a simple hand mill with a cone grinder is a big improvement. The difference between a cone and disc grinder is explained in [coffee grinder: cone or disc grinder](/ratgeber/kaffeemuehle-kegel-oder-scheibenmahlwerk/), and whether a hand or electric grinder is a better fit can be found in [hand grinder or electric grinder](/ratgeber/handmuehle-oder-elektrische-muehle/).

## Fine particles and coarse chunks

No coffee grounds consist of particles of the same size. In addition to the desired main size, there is always a **fine portion**, tiny particles that leach out very quickly and bring bitterness and cloudiness, and some **coarse chunks** that hardly release anything and leave acid. The tighter a grinder holds both edges, the clearer the coffee tastes. If you're curious, you can shake your ground coffee through a fine tea strainer: what falls through is the fines. Some filter fans specifically remove it using special coffee sieves to get particularly clear cups. This is not necessary for everyday use, but it clearly shows why the quality of the mill makes such a difference.

## Grinding degree or amount of coffee?

Not every problem can be solved with the grind size. It helps to separate two things: the **strength**, i.e. how concentrated the coffee is, and the **extraction**, i.e. how much has been dissolved from the flour. If your coffee tastes balanced and sweet, but just too watery, use more coffee with the same grind. If it tastes strong but sour, grind it finer. If it is too strong and pleasant, dilute it with hot water. If you differentiate between the two adjusting screws, you will find your goal more quickly instead of turning the grinding degree back and forth.

## Other factors that influence the degree of grinding

- **Roast level:** Dark roasts are more porous and dissolve more easily. They often need a slightly coarser grind than light roasts of the same preparation. Light roasts are denser and tolerate finer flour. More in [roast levels explained](/ratgeber/roestgrade-erklaert/).
- **Freshness:** Very freshly roasted coffee still releases a lot of gas and runs differently than coffee that is two weeks old. If the taste changes over the weeks, slight adjustment may be necessary.
- **Quantity:** With the hand filter, a larger quantity of coffee with a higher bed requires a slightly coarser grind.
- **Water:** Very soft or hard water will alter the extraction. Before you despair about the grinding level, check the water, see [Water hardness for coffee and tea](/ratgeber/wasserhaerte-fuer-kaffee-und-tee/).
- **Humidity:** In very humid weather, espresso grinders sometimes behave differently and need a little correction.

## Practical tips for everyday life

- **Notebook:** Write down the setting you liked for each bean. This will help you find your way back quicker next time.
- **Adjusting without beans:** With many electric grinders, you should only adjust the finer settings while the motor is running so that no beans get stuck in the grinder. Follow the instructions for your device.
- **Residual flour:** In many grinders some coffee gets stuck after grinding. After a change, grind away a few grams before brewing.
- **Two preparations, one grinder:** If you often switch between espresso and filter, you need a grinder that can be reliably switched back and forth, or two grinders.

## An exercise that sharpens your intuition

The quickest way to get to know the grind level is to deliberately prepare the same coffee incorrectly. Brew three small cups using the hand filter or French press, everything the same except for the grind: one much finer than usual, one as usual, one much coarser. Try them side by side when they have cooled slightly. The fine cup usually tastes bitter and dry, the coarse cup tastes sour and thin, and the medium cup tastes balanced. Anyone who has consciously tasted these three directions will later recognize them in every cup and immediately know in which direction they should turn the grinder. [Tasting coffee](/ratgeber/kaffee-verkosten/) shows you how to taste it systematically.

## Without your own mill

You can also compensate for a lot with ready-ground coffee. Purchase coffee ground for your method, then adjust the time and quantity. If the flour is too fine for the French press, shorten the brewing time to three minutes and press the stamp very slowly. If the hand filter runs through too quickly with filter coffee, pour more slowly and in several portions. Many roasters will grind their beans for your preparation upon purchase upon request. Then buy small quantities, because ground coffee quickly loses its aroma.

## When nothing fits at all

Sometimes you can't find a setting that tastes good. Then it's worth looking at other causes: Is the coffee old or very darkly roasted? Is the grinder dull or dirty? Grinders wear out after many kilograms and then grind more unevenly. Cleaning can work wonders, as described in [Cleaning the Coffee Grinder](/ratgeber/kaffeemuehle-reinigen/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use one grind for everything?

With a medium grind you will get usable results in filter machines and hand filters. It is far too coarse for espresso and too fine for French press and cold brew. If you use multiple methods, you should grind yourself.

### Why does ground coffee taste different?

It is tailored to a typical preparation, usually filter machine or espresso, and quickly loses aroma after opening. More about this in [Roasting date and freshness](/ratgeber/roestdatum-und-frische/).

### How often do I have to adjust the grind?

Almost always with a new bean, occasionally with the same bean if it ages or the weather changes significantly. Small steps are enough.

## Conclusion

The right grind is not secret knowledge, but the result of a few conscious attempts: choose the starting point according to the table, change one thing at a time and follow the taste. Sour means finer, bitter means coarser, and if you write down your settings you will find them more quickly with each new bean.
