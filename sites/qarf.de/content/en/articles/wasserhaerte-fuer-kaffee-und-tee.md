A cup of filter coffee consists of over 98 percent water, a cup of tea even more than 99 percent. Nevertheless, when making coffee, most people think about the beans, grinder and machine, but hardly about what comes out of the tap. The same bean can taste as different with two different waters as if they were two different coffees. In this guide you will find out what water hardness actually is, why it affects taste and devices, how to find out the values ​​of your tap water and when a filter is worthwhile.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Water hardness primarily describes dissolved calcium and magnesium. In Germany it is given in degrees of German hardness (°dH) or in mmol/l.
> - Moderately soft water is ideal for coffee; The Specialty Coffee Association specifies a target value of around 4 °dH total hardness.
> - The carbonate hardness buffers acids: too much makes coffee flat, too little can make it appear sharply sour.
> - Hard water causes tea to become cloudy and form a skin on the surface.
> - You can find out your hardness range from your water supplier, usually on their website.

## What water hardness actually is

Tap water is never pure H₂O. On its way through soil and rock, it dissolves minerals, especially calcium and magnesium, as well as sodium, potassium, hydrogen carbonate, sulfate and chloride. The sum of calcium and magnesium is called **total hardness**. The portion that is bound to hydrogen carbonate is called **carbonate hardness** or temporary hardness because it precipitates as lime when heated.

The common units in Germany:

| Unity | Meaning | Conversion |
|---|---|---|
| °dH (degree of German hardness) | 10 mg calcium oxide per liter | 1 °dH = 0.179 mmol/l |
| mmol/l | Millimoles of alkaline earth ions per liter | 1 mmol/l = 5.6 °dH |
| mg/l CaCO₃ | internationally common information | 1 °dH ≈ 17.8 mg/l CaCO₃ |

Since 2007, the Detergents and Cleaning Agents Act has divided water into three hardness ranges:

| Hardness range | mmol/l | °dH (approximately) |
|---|---|---|
| soft | below 1.5 | below 8.4 |
| medium | 1.5 to 2.5 | 8.4 to 14 |
| hard | over 2.5 | over 14 |

Hardness varies greatly in Germany. Regions with granite and sandstone subsoil often have soft water, while regions with limestone, such as parts of southern Germany, Franconia or Thuringia, have significantly harder water. Even within a city, districts can be supplied by different waterworks.

## Why water changes taste

When brewing, the water dissolves hundreds of substances from the coffee grounds or tea leaves. The minerals in the water influence which substances it removes, how well, and how we subsequently perceive them.

### Magnesium and calcium as flavor carriers

Calcium and magnesium ions can bind certain aromatic substances and thus promote extraction. Completely mineral-free water, such as distilled water, therefore produces a flat, empty coffee. A certain degree of hardness is therefore desirable. In experiments, magnesium is often said to have a particularly good effect on fruity and sweet notes.

### Carbonate hardness as an acid brake

Hydrogen carbonate is a base. It neutralizes some of the fruit acids in coffee. A little of this is good because it rounds out sharp acidity. But too much of it almost completely swallows up the lively acidity, and the coffee tastes dull, chalky and flat. That's why a light, fruity roast with very hard water often seems disappointing, while a dark espresso roast is more forgiving.

### The Target Values of the Specialty Coffee Association

The Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) has formulated recommendations for brewing water:

| Parameters | Target value | Acceptable range |
|---|---|---|
| Total hardness | 68 mg/l CaCO₃ (around 4 °dH) | 17–85 mg/l (around 1–5 °dH) |
| Alkalinity (carbonate hardness) | 40 mg/l CaCO₃ (around 2.2 °dH) | around 40 mg/l |
| Dissolved Solids (TDS) | 150 mg/l | 75-250 mg/l |
| pH value | 7,0 | 6,5–7,5 |
| Sodium | 10 mg/l | as low as possible |
| Chlorine | 0 mg/l | odorless |

Many roasters and baristas in Germany still consider slightly higher values of up to around 8 °dH to be good. What is more important than an exact value is that your water is not extremely hard and does not smell of chlorine.

## Water and tea

When it comes to tea, you can even see the effects of hard water with the naked eye. Black tea with hard water becomes darker and cloudier, and an iridescent film often forms on the surface. This film consists primarily of calcium carbonate and oxidized tea polyphenols. It is harmless, but unpleasant, and the tea tastes duller.

Green tea and white tea are particularly sensitive: their fine, grassy and floral notes quickly disappear with hard water. It's not without reason that the East Frisians swear by their comparatively soft water, and in tea countries like Japan the tap water is very soft in many places.

## This is how you find out your water values

1. **Ask water suppliers:** Every supplier is obliged to provide information about the hardship. You can usually find an analysis on the website with total hardness, often also with calcium, magnesium and hydrogen carbonate. Enter your zip code or check your water bill.
2. **Test Strips:** Total hardness and carbonate hardness strips are available at drugstores and aquarium stores. They are less accurate than the utility's analysis, but are useful if you want to test filter water.
3. **Drip Test:** Aquarium drip tests for GH and KH are more accurate than strips and inexpensive.

You can roughly convert hydrogen carbonate data in mg/l: Divide the value by 21.8, then you get the carbonate hardness in °dH.

## What you can do if your water is hard

### Table water filter

Can filters with ion exchange cartridges primarily reduce carbonate hardness and thus also limescale deposits. They noticeably improve the taste of coffee and tea with hard water. It is important to change the cartridge regularly, the manufacturers usually say about four weeks, and to keep the filter clean because germs can multiply in standing water. Filtered water belongs in the refrigerator if it sits for more than a few hours.

### Still mineral water

A bottled water with a low mineral content can be a good alternative. Look for low levels of hydrogen carbonate on the label (roughly less than 100 mg/l) and moderate calcium and magnesium content. Medicinal waters that are very rich in minerals are not suitable. However, for everyday use, bottled water is expensive and generates a lot of waste.

### Mixing

A simple method: Mix your hard tap water with distilled or very soft water, for example in a 1:1 ratio. This way you halve the hardness but keep enough minerals for the taste.

### Make your own brewing water

Some coffee lovers go one step further and mix their water themselves. The starting point is distilled or demineralized water, to which food-grade salts are added in small, measured amounts: such as magnesium sulfate, known as Epsom salt, for hardness and baking soda for buffering. If you like it easier, use ready-made mineral packets for brewing water, which are dissolved in a certain amount of water. This allows very precise, repeatable values ​​and is exciting for experiments. However, a good table water filter is sufficient for everyday use in most households.

### Fixed installations

For portafilter machines with a fixed water connection or high consumption, there are filter systems directly on the line. It's worth getting advice from your machine dealer because the setting depends on your water values.

## A little taste test at home

If you want to know how much your water affects your coffee, do a simple comparison. Brew the same coffee twice with the same recipe, i.e. same amount, same grind, same temperature and same time. Sometimes you use tap water, sometimes you use still mineral water with a little hydrogen carbonate or filtered water.

1. Brew both cups quickly one after the other so that the beans are equally fresh.
2. Let both cool to drinking temperature.
3. Taste alternately, paying attention to acidity, sweetness, bitterness, and aftertaste.

When it comes to hard tap water, the difference is often astonishingly clear: the cup with soft water tastes livelier and fruitier, the one with hard water is rounder but also flatter. By the way, some people prefer this rounder cup, especially with dark roasts. The test shows you whether a filter is actually worth it for your taste.

## Why limescale also affects your devices

When heated, the carbonate hardness precipitates as lime. It settles on heating elements, in pipes and valves. In kettles, it insulates the heating element and extends the heating time. In espresso machines it can narrow lines, block valves and distort the brewing temperature. Soft or filtered water also extends the life of your devices. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is very soft water always better?

No. Water with almost no minerals extracts poorly and tastes empty. It can also corrode metals in espresso machines. Moderately soft water with some buffer is ideal.

### Is hard water unhealthy?

No. Calcium and magnesium are vital minerals, and tap water in Germany is one of the best controlled foods. Hard water is a taste and limescale issue, not a health risk.

### Why should I use fresh water?

Water that has been left in the boiler for a long time or has been boiled several times has less dissolved oxygen and can taste stale. In the morning, let the water run briefly until it comes out of the tap until it is cool, especially in old buildings. For hot drinks, you should always use cold tap water, not warm tap water.

### Do I need different water for tea than for coffee?

The demands are similar. Tea is even more sensitive to carbonate hardness because it has little acid of its own. What's good for your coffee is also good for your tea.

### Does it help to boil the water first?

Only to a limited extent. When cooking, part of the carbonate hardness precipitates as lime, so the hardness decreases slightly. The water loses oxygen and tastes stale when you heat it a second time, and the limescale collects in the cooker. A filter is the better solution.

## Conclusion

Water is the largest ingredient in every cup. If you look up your values ​​from your supplier, filter or mix hard water and always use fresh, cold water, you can taste the difference straight away: more lively acidity in coffee, clearer color in tea and devices that last longer.
