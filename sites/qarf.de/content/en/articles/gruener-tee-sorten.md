Green tea has an ambivalent reputation for many people. Some love it for its freshness, others have once poured it too hot, got a bitter, astringent cup and never tried it again. Green tea is one of the most diverse tea categories of all. A steam-cooked Japanese Sencha tastes of freshly mown grass and sea breeze, a pan-roasted Chinese Long Jing tastes of chestnut and roasted beans. In this guide you will find out how green tea is made, what types there are and how you can recognize a good green tea.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Like black tea, green tea comes from the tea plant *Camellia sinensis*. The difference lies in the processing.
> - With green tea, the oxidation of the leaves is stopped by heat shortly after harvest.
> - Japan usually heats with steam, China usually heats in a pan. This has a strong influence on the taste.
> - Green tea does not tolerate boiling water: Depending on the variety, 60 to 85 °C is correct.
> - Green tea is sensitive to freshness and tastes best within about a year of harvest.

## How a leaf becomes green tea

All real teas, whether green, black, white or oolong, come from the same plant, *Camellia sinensis*. As soon as a tea leaf is picked, the plant's own enzymes begin to react the ingredients with oxygen. The leaf turns brown, similar to a cut apple. This process is called oxidation, colloquially often also known as fermentation.

With green tea, oxidation is interrupted right from the start. The fresh leaves are briefly heated, which causes the enzymes to become inactive and the leaves remain green. It is then rolled, shaped and dried. The most important steps:

1. **Picking:** usually a bud with two or three young leaves, with simpler qualities also older leaves.
2. **Heating:** in Japan usually by steaming for around 20 seconds to around 2 minutes, in China usually by roasting in hot pans or drums.
3. **Rolling and Shaping:** The sheets are rolled, twisted, pressed or flattened. The shape determines how quickly the tea releases its aroma.
4. **Drying:** to a residual moisture content of a few percent so that the tea can be preserved.

## Japanese green teas: steamed and grassy

Japanese green teas are almost always steamed. This gives it a bright green color and aromas reminiscent of grass, spinach, seaweed and fresh peas. Many Japanese teas also have a pronounced umami note, a savory spice that comes primarily from the amino acid L-theanine.

### Sencha

Sencha is the everyday tea in Japan and makes up the majority of green tea production there. The leaves are rolled in a needle shape. Depending on the steaming time, a distinction is made between **Asamushi** (steamed briefly, clear infusion, fine taste) and **Fukamushi** (steamed for a long time, the leaves break down more, the infusion is cloudy-green and full-bodied). The first harvest in spring is called **Shincha** or Ichibancha and is considered particularly aromatic.

### Gyokuro

For Gyokuro, the tea bushes are shaded for about three weeks before harvesting. Less light means the leaf retains more L-theanine and converts less of it into bitter catechins. The result is a deep green, sweet and very umami-rich tea. Gyokuro is brewed at a low temperature of 50 to 60 °C and is one of the most expensive teas in Japan.

### Kabusecha

Kabusecha lies between Sencha and Gyokuro: The bushes are shaded for a shorter period of time, usually around one to two weeks. It is milder than Sencha and cheaper than Gyokuro.

### Bancha, Hojicha and Genmaicha

- **Bancha** is made from later harvests and larger leaves. It is stronger, less delicate and usually lower in caffeine.
- **Hojicha** is roasted bancha or sencha. Roasting makes it brown, tastes of caramel and nuts and contains less caffeine than Sencha.
- **Genmaicha** is green tea with roasted rice grains. It tastes mild and nutty, almost like popcorn.

### Matcha

Matcha is a special case: the shaded leaves are made into tencha and then finely ground. You drink the whole leaf. More about this in the guide [Preparing Matcha](/ratgeber/matcha-zubereiten/).

## Chinese green teas: roasted and nutty

China is the largest tea producer in the world, and the majority of Chinese tea is green tea. The leaves are usually heated in hot pans or drums. This creates roasted aromas, the taste is softer and nuttier, and the color of the infusion is more yellowish-green.

| variety | Origin | Leaf shape | taste |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long Jing (Dragon Fountain) | Zhejiang, Hangzhou area | pressed flat | Chestnut, roasted beans, sweet |
| Bi Luo Chun | Jiangsu, Taihu Lake | rolled finely in a spiral | floral, fruity, delicate |
| Huang Shan Mao Feng | Anhui, Yellow Mountains | fine buds with hairs | mild, floral, slightly sweet |
| Gunpowder | especially Zhejiang | rolled into balls | strong, slightly smoky |
| Chun Mee | different provinces | curved like eyebrows | strong, slightly sour |

**Gunpowder** is also the basis of North African mint tea, which is served heavily sweetened with fresh mint.

## Green tea from other countries

Korea, Vietnam, India (such as Darjeeling Green), Nepal, Sri Lanka, Kenya and even Georgia and the Azores also produce green tea. Manufacturers often orientate themselves on either the Japanese or Chinese style. A look at the dealer's description usually tells you whether the tea was steamed or roasted.

## What's in green tea

Green tea contains caffeine, traditionally often called teain in the tea world; chemically it is the same substance. A cup of green tea provides roughly 20 to 50 mg of caffeine, depending on the amount of leaf, brewing time and variety. There are also catechins, especially epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), which are responsible for the bitter, astringent note, and L-theanine, which provides umami.

The health effects of green tea are much discussed. As a normal drink it is considered harmless. High-dose green tea extracts in dietary supplements, on the other hand, are something different: The EFSA has expressed concerns that high doses of EGCG from such preparations can put strain on the liver. This applies to capsules and extracts, not the cup of tea.

## This is how you recognize good green tea

- **Color:** Leaves should look vibrant green or olive green, not brown or gray.
- **Scent:** Fresh, grassy, ​​nutty or floral. If the tea smells like hay or cardboard, it is old.
- **Evenness:** Evenly sized, undamaged leaves indicate careful processing.
- **Harvest year:** Good traders indicate the harvest. Green tea is not a lager tea and should be drunk as fresh as possible.
- **Packaging:** Light, air and heat are harmful. Light-tight, resealable packaging is best.

## Which green tea is right for you?

The selection seems confusing at first. This overview will help you choose based on taste and situation rather than by name.

| You like... | Try first | Why |
|---|---|---|
| it mild, without bitterness | Genmaicha, Hojicha | Rice and roasting round off the taste |
| nutty, warm notes | Long Jing | Pan roasted instead of steam, hardly grassy |
| Freshness and grass | Asamushi Sencha | clear, typically Japanese character |
| full body and strong color | Fukamushi Sencha | long steamed, denser and rounder |
| Sweetness and umami | Kabusecha, later Gyokuro | Shading brings sweetness, Kabusecha is the cheaper way to start |
| floral, fine cups | Bi Luo Chun, Huang Shan Mao Feng | young buds, tender and fragrant |
| Mint tea | Gunpowder | strong enough for mint and sugar |
| a cup in the evening | Hojicha, Bancha | less caffeine than Sencha |

If you have had bad experiences with green tea, start with Hojicha or Genmaicha. Both are infused with hotter water and are less delicate than a fine Sencha. They show that green tea doesn't have to be bitter.

## Harvest times: why spring is so popular

Tea bushes are picked several times a year and the harvests vary significantly. In Japan, the first harvest in spring, around mid-April to mid-May depending on the region, is called **Ichibancha**. The young shoots have stored nutrients over the winter and contain a particularly high amount of L-theanine; they taste sweet and fresh. The second harvest in early summer, **Nibancha**, is stronger and bitter, later harvests are often used to make bancha or tea bags.

The same applies in China. Teas that are picked before the Qingming Festival in early April are particularly sought after. They are called **Ming Qian** and consist of very young, tender buds. A little later, before the “Grain Rain” period around April 20th, the **Yu Qian** teas follow, which are stronger and cost less.

For you as a buyer, this means: Spring harvests cost more, but also taste much better. Summer harvests are perfectly fine for everyday use, especially if you brew the tea with slightly warmer water and for a shorter time.

## Briefly about the preparation

Green tea is unforgiving. Boiling water literally burns the leaves and dissolves too many bitter substances. The following apply as guideline values:

| Type of tea | Temperature | Brewing time |
|---|---|---|
| Sencha | 70-80°C | 1-2 mins |
| Gyokuro | 50-60°C | 1½–2½ mins. |
| Bancha, Hojicha | 80-90°C | 1-2 mins |
| Chinese green tea | 75-85°C | 2-3 mins |

Most green teas can be steeped two to three times. You can find further values ​​in the guide [Tea: Brewing times and temperatures](/ratgeber/tee-ziehzeiten-und-temperaturen/) and in our [Brewing table](/bruehtabelle/).

## Tasting plan: Japan and China side by side

The quickest way to understand the difference between steamed and roasted green tea is to compare them directly:

1. **Choose two teas:** a Sencha and a Long Jing, both from a current spring harvest if possible.
2. **Dose immediately:** 3 grams per 200 milliliters, in two equal jugs or cups with a sieve.
3. **Pour appropriately:** the Sencha at 75 °C for one minute, the Long Jing at 80 °C for two minutes.
4. **Smell first, then drink:** Pay attention to the color, scent and feel in your mouth. Is the tea grassy-fresh or nutty-round, dense or light?
5. **Second infusion:** Infuse both with slightly hotter water and for a shorter time and see which tea lasts longer.

Sencha usually seems greener, fresher and heartier, Long Jing is softer and sweet and nutty. Such a comparison sharpens the taste more than any description. If you want to explore a single tea over many short infusions, you will find the right method in [Gongfu Cha](/ratgeber/gongfu-cha/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does my green tea taste bitter?

The water is almost always too hot, the brewing time is too long or the amount of leaves is too large. Try 70°C and one minute. Very hard water can also make the taste dull and bitter.

### Does green tea have less caffeine than black tea?

Often, but not always. The caffeine content depends more on the leaf and preparation than on the color. Young shoots and shaded teas such as Gyokuro and Matcha even contain a particularly high amount of caffeine.

### Can I brew green tea cold?

Yes, in Japan it is known as Mizudashi. Cold water only slowly dissolves bitter substances, making the tea soft and sweet. Add about 8 to 10 grams of Sencha to a liter of cold water, let it steep in the fridge for a few hours, strain it and drink it within a day. More variations are available in [Make your own iced tea](/ratgeber/eistee-selbst-machen/).

### Why is my Sencha cloudy?

This is normal with Fukamushi-Sencha. Due to the long steaming, the leaves break down into fine particles, which turn the infusion green. Use a fine sieve and pour out the pot completely so that the next infusion doesn't follow suit.

### How long does green tea last?

If unopened and stored in a cool place, it remains edible for a long time, but loses its freshness. Once opened, you should use it within about two to three months. High-quality Japanese teas are often stored refrigerated by retailers.
