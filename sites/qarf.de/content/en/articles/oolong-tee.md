Oolong is perhaps the most versatile tea family. Some oolongs taste fresh, floral and creamy, almost like a green tea with a lilac scent. Others are deeply roasted and reminiscent of cocoa, wood, honey and stone fruit. There is an enormous range between these poles, and a good oolong can be infused many times, with the flavor changing from infusion to infusion. In Germany, oolong is less well-known than green and black tea, but it's worth getting started, especially for those who are curious. This guide explains what oolong is all about, introduces the best-known varieties and shows you how to prepare it.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Oolong is **partially oxidized**, somewhere between green and black tea. The degree of oxidation ranges from light to strong.
> - Many oolongs are additionally **roasted** after processing, which brings notes of caramel, nuts and wood.
> - The main origins are the Chinese provinces **Fujian** and **Guangdong** as well as **Taiwan**.
> - Oolong is often rolled into balls or made into long, twisted leaves.
> - Pour at 90 to 95 degrees. Ideal for several short gongfu style infusions.

## What sets Oolong apart from other teas

All real teas come from **Camellia sinensis**. With Oolong, oxidation is neither stopped immediately, as with green tea, nor completely allowed, as with black tea. After picking, the leaves are withered and then carefully moved, shaken or rolled in bamboo drums. The edges of the leaves are slightly injured and oxidize, while the inside remains greener. Once the tea master sees the desired level reached, he stops the oxidation with heat. It is then rolled and dried; many varieties are then roasted for hours or days.

An oolong can therefore be very different:

| Type | Oxidation | Roasting | Typical taste |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slightly oxidized, unroasted | low | none or very light | floral, creamy, fresh, lilac, butter |
| Agent oxidized | medium | light to medium | Orchid, honey, peach, pastry |
| Heavily oxidized | high | different | Honey, dried fruits, muscat |
| Roasted | different | strong | Cocoa, caramel, wood, roasted nuts, minerals |

## Name and history

The name Oolong is the old transliteration of the Chinese **Wulong**, which means “black dragon”. Where it comes from is not clear; there are several legends, but no reliable source. It is likely that processing developed in the 17th century in Fujian, in the Wuyi Mountains and in Anxi. In the 19th century, immigrants from Fujian brought tea plants and knowledge to Taiwan, and the “Formosa Oolong” produced there became a sought-after export tea. To date, Fujian, Guangdong and Taiwan are the most important regions.

## Balls or twisted leaves

Oolong is sold in two basic forms. **Rolled into balls** teas such as Tieguanyin, Gao Shan or Dong Ding are repeatedly pressed and rolled in cloths until the leaves are formed into solid beads. They look compact, but open up to many times their volume in water. So give them enough space in the pot. You can find **long-twisted** leaves in the rock teas from Wuyi, Dancong and Oriental Beauty. They are loose and voluminous, so the gaiwan looks much fuller with the same number of grams. A scale prevents you from taking too little tea with twisted leaves and too much tea with balls.

## The most famous oolongs

### Tieguanyin from Fujian

Known as the “Iron Goddess of Mercy,” the tea comes from Anxi in Fujian Province. The leaves are rolled into tight balls. Modern, slightly oxidized Tieguanyin are light green, floral and creamy. Traditional versions are more oxidized and roasted, with warm, nutty notes.

### Rock teas from the Wuyi Mountains

In the Wuyi Mountains in northern Fujian, **Yancha**, or rock teas, grow on mineral soils between steep rock faces. They are heavily roasted and taste of dark caramel, wood, cocoa and stone fruit with a typical mineral note. The most famous is **Da Hong Pao**, the “Great Red Coat”. There are also Shui Xian and Rou Gui with cinnamon spice.

### Dancong from Guangdong

The Dancong oolongs from the Phoenix Mountains in Guangdong are known for their intense, natural scents reminiscent of orchid, honey, peach or almond. They can become bitter quickly, so short steeping times.

### Highland teas from Taiwan

Taiwan has its own highly valued oolong tradition. **Gao Shan**, highland teas from regions such as Alishan or Lishan, grow at high altitudes, are lightly oxidized, rolled into balls and taste floral, buttery and sweet. **Dong Ding** is medium oxidized and roasted, with notes of honey and roasted nuts.

### Oriental beauty

This highly oxidized oolong from Taiwan has a special feature: tiny leafhoppers suck on the leaves in summer. The plant reacts with defensive substances that, when processed, produce a distinctive aroma of honey, ripe fruit and muscat. The gardens therefore usually do not use insecticides, because without the cicadas there would be no such taste.

### Milky Oolong

Two things are sold under this name: the natural **Jin Xuan** from Taiwan, a tea plant variety with a naturally creamy, slightly milky taste, and flavored oolongs to which a milk flavor has been added. A look at the ingredients list shows what you are buying.

## Prepare oolong

### West in the pot

| Quantity per 250 ml | Temperature | Brewing time | Infusions |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 to 4 g | 90 to 95 degrees | 3 to 4 minutes | 2 to 3 |

Rolled balls take a little longer for the first infusion because they are just opening. Pour the tea completely so that it doesn't bleed.

### Gongfu style

Oolong develops particularly beautifully in a small pot or in a gaiwan with lots of tea and short infusions.

1. Preheat gaiwan or pot with hot water.
2. Add about 5 to 7 grams of tea for 100 to 120 milliliters of water.
3. Optional: briefly pour hot water over it and drain immediately to awaken the leaves.
4. First infusion 20 to 30 seconds, then a little longer each time.
5. Depending on the tea, five to ten infusions are possible.

[Gongfu Cha: Chinese-style tea](/ratgeber/gongfu-cha/) explains exactly how Gongfu Cha works.

### Poured cold

Slightly oxidized oolongs make an excellent cold extract. Add about 8 to 10 grams of tea to one liter of cold water and let everything steep in the refrigerator for eight to twelve hours. Then strain. The tea tastes sweet, floral and barely tart, and the balls can often be brewed again while warm. You can find more ideas in [Make your own iced tea](/ratgeber/eistee-selbst-machen/).

## What the roasting does

At Oolong, roasting is its own craft. The leaves are heated over charcoal or in electric ovens at low temperatures for many hours, sometimes in several batches with rest periods in between. This reduces the residual moisture, the tea lasts longer and warm roasted aromas are created. Freshly roasted oolongs may initially taste a bit burnt or smoky. Many tea dealers therefore let them rest for a few weeks or months until the roasted notes settle and combine with the fruit notes. A well-roasted oolong does not taste burnt, but rather round, sweet and deep, with a pleasant warmth in the finish.

## Oolong for eating

Lightly oxidized, floral oolongs pair well with light foods like steamed fish, vegetables, or fresh fruit. Roasted oolongs tolerate heavier dishes, fried foods, mushrooms and dark chocolate. In many Chinese restaurants, a simple oolong is served with dinner because it balances out fatty foods without overpowering the flavor. For dessert, an Oriental Beauty with its honey notes is a nice accompaniment.

## How do you recognize good oolong?

- **Leaves:** After the infusion, whole leaves, often with stems, unfold. Slightly oxidized oolongs show green leaves with reddish edges.
- **Fragrance:** The dry tea has an intense scent. If it smells dusty, flat or musty, it is old or poorly stored.
- **Infusions:** A good oolong lasts several infusions and changes as it goes.
- **Clarity:** The cup is clear and bright.

## Storage

Slightly oxidized green oolongs behave like green tea: They lose their fresh aroma quite quickly and should be stored airtight, cool and dark and drunk within months. Heavily roasted oolongs are more stable and will partially develop further if stored correctly. More in [Store coffee and tea properly](/ratgeber/kaffee-und-tee-lagern/).

## Information about origin and price

Good oolongs come at a price, especially highland teas from Taiwan and rock teas from the core area of ​​the Wuyi Mountains. Because well-known names like Alishan or Da Hong Pao sell well, they are also used for teas from other locations or for simple blends. Reputable dealers therefore say more than just the name: region, harvest time, degree of oxidation and roasting and often the harvest year. In Taiwan, the spring and winter harvests are particularly valued. A very cheap tea with a big name deserves skepticism, but an honestly described tea from a less famous location can be excellent.

## For starters

If you're new to oolong, try two contrasting teas side by side: a lightly oxidized highland oolong from Taiwan or a modern Tieguanyin and a roasted rock tea like Da Hong Pao. The difference immediately shows you how far this tea family extends. After that, a Dong Ding or Oriental Beauty for the middle of the spectrum is worth it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does oolong have more or less caffeine than green tea?

This cannot be said in general terms. The caffeine content depends on the plant, leaf age, quantity and steeping time, not the tea type. More about this in [Caffeine in Coffee and Tea](/ratgeber/koffein-in-kaffee-und-tee/).

### Does Oolong help you lose weight?

Oolong is sometimes advertised this way. There is no reliable evidence of a significant effect. Enjoy it as a drink, not as a slimming aid.

### Can I drink oolong with milk?

It is possible, but the delicate aromas are lost. Oolong is traditionally drunk pure.

### Why is the first infusion sometimes poured away?

In Gongfu style, the tea is often briefly poured with hot water and this infusion is immediately discarded. This warms the leaves and allows rolled balls to rise faster. It is not necessary; With high-quality teas, many people also drink the first infusion.

## Conclusion

Ranging from floral-fresh to deeply roasted, oolong rewards curiosity more than almost any other tea. With enough leaf, hot water, and short infusions, you can get to know many faces of the same tea in a single tea hour.
