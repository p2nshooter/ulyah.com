Why does the same coffee taste sweet and round one day and sour or bitter the next day? The answer lies in a word baristas use all the time: extraction. This refers to the process in which water dissolves soluble substances from the ground coffee. Not all substances dissolve equally quickly, and not all taste good. If you understand the order in which acids, sweetness and bitter substances enter the cup, you can improve almost any coffee in a targeted manner, whether using a hand filter, a portafilter machine or a French press. This guide explains the principle without formulas and shows which adjustment screws you can adjust.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - **Extraction** describes how much of the ground coffee dissolves in the water. **Strength** describes how concentrated the drink is. Both are not the same.
> - First acids and fruity notes are released, then sweetness and body, and finally tart and bitter substances.
> - **Under-extracted** tastes sour, salty, thin, with a short finish. **Over-extracted** tastes bitter, dry, hollow and astringent.
> - The adjusting screws are grind size, contact time, water temperature, brewing ratio, agitation and water quality.
> - Change one thing at a time and try side by side.

## What happens when brewing

Roasted coffee beans consist largely of insoluble plant material. Only a smaller part, often around a third, is soluble in water at all. But we don't want to have all of it in our cup. In the coffee world, a range of approximately 18 to 22 percent extraction is considered balanced. This means that out of 15 grams of ground coffee, around 2.7 to 3.3 grams should end up in the water.

You don't have to measure these numbers. The order in which the substances dissolve is more important:

| phase | What comes loose | How it tastes |
|---|---|---|
| early | Acids, salty and fruity compounds | bright, sour, fresh |
| middle | Sugar and caramel substances, body | sweet, round, full |
| late | tart phenols, bitter and dry compounds | bitter, dry, woody |

If the extraction ends too early, the sweetness that balances the acidity is missing and the coffee tastes sour and unfinished. If it runs for too long, too many late substances are added that mask the sweetness and the coffee becomes bitter and dry. The goal is the point where acidity, sweetness and bitterness are in balance.

## Extraction and strength are two different things

Many people confuse “strong” with “bitter”. The **strength** describes how much dissolved coffee is in the cup, i.e. how concentrated the drink is. An espresso is very strong, a filter coffee is weaker. The **Extraction** describes how thoroughly the coffee grounds have been leached.

A coffee can be strong and under-extracted, such as a coffee that has been brewed too briefly but has a very high dosage: intense and sour. Or weak and over-extracted, when too little coffee is brewed for a long time with too much water: thin and bitter at the same time. This is exactly what often happens in the filter coffee machine when too little coffee is dispensed, as described in [Use the filter coffee machine correctly](/ratgeber/filterkaffeemaschine-richtig-nutzen/).

**You control the strength** primarily via the ratio of coffee to water. **You control the extraction** primarily via the grinding degree, time and temperature.

### Adjust the strength later

Because starch and extraction are separate things, you can still change the strength after brewing. If a coffee is well extracted, i.e. sweet and balanced, but too intense, add some hot water. Baristas call this bypass because this water bypasses the ground coffee. The Americano, an espresso made with hot water, follows the same idea. It doesn't work the other way around: you don't make a thin, sour coffee stronger by keeping it warm for longer, just more bitter. The only thing that helps here is to use more coffee or grind it finer the next time you brew it.

## The six adjusting screws

### 1. Grinding degree

Finer flour has more surface area and extracts faster and more powerfully. It also slows the flow of water, which increases contact time. The degree of grinding is therefore the most effective adjustment screw. Details in [Set the grinding level correctly](/ratgeber/mahlgrad-richtig-einstellen/).

### 2. Contact time

The longer water and coffee are in contact, the more it dissolves. In the French press you control the time directly, on the hand filter via the grinding degree and pouring speed, with espresso via the grinding degree and yield.

### 3. Water temperature

Hotter water dissolves faster. For filter coffee, around 92 to 96 degrees is considered a good range. Light, dense roasts tolerate the upper limit, dark roasts often taste better when brewed a little cooler. Cold brew shows the extreme: With cold water, the extraction takes many hours and dissolves other substances, see [Make your own cold brew](/ratgeber/cold-brew-anleitung/).

### 4. Brewing ratio

The ratio of coffee to water primarily determines the strength, but also influences the extraction. More water per gram of coffee leaches the flour more thoroughly. For filter coffee, around 60 grams per liter is a good starting point, for espresso a ratio of around 1 to 2.

### 5. movement

Stirring, swirling and vigorous watering bring fresh water to the flour and speed up the extraction. Gently swirling the hand filter after pouring makes the coffee bed more even. However, too much movement can stir up fine particles that clog the filter and make the coffee bitter.

### 6. water

Minerals in the water bind aromatic substances and help dissolve them. Very soft water can make the coffee appear flat and sharp, very hard water can make it appear dull and chalky because carbonate dampens the acid. More in [Water hardness for coffee and tea](/ratgeber/wasserhaerte-fuer-kaffee-und-tee/).

## Percolation and immersion: two ways to extract

There are basically two ways to brew coffee. With **percolation**, water runs through a coffee bed, for example in a hand filter, a filter machine or an espresso. Fresh water is constantly being supplied, which hardly contains any dissolved substances and therefore continues to dissolve vigorously. This is efficient, but depends heavily on how evenly the water flows through the bed.

With **immersion**, the coffee and water are together all the time, like in the French press or cold brew. The more that has already dissolved, the slower it will continue because the water is already enriched with coffee substances. This makes immersion more error-tolerant: A minute more or less in the French press is usually less important than a significantly different run on the hand filter.

A practical rule follows from this. When it comes to percolation, the grind size is your main tool because it determines both the surface area and the flow time. With Immersion you can adjust the grinding level and time independently of each other.

## Where do you start first with which method?

| Method | Principle | First adjustment screw | Typical stumbling block |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hand filter | Percolation | Grinding degree | Uneven pouring, crooked coffee bed |
| Filter machine | Percolation | Dosage, then grinding degree | too little coffee, warming plate too long |
| Portafilter | Percolation under pump pressure | Grinding degree with fixed dose and yield | Channeling through uneven distribution and tamping |
| Espresso maker | Percolation driven by steam pressure | Heat, then grind | too much heat, bitter aftertaste at the end |
| French press | Immersion | Brewing time, then grinding level | Fine fraction, coffee remains in the pot after pressing |
| Cold brew | Immersion in cold water | Brewing time | set too short or ground too finely |

You can find the appropriate instructions in [Hand filter](/ratgeber/handfilter-kaffee-anleitung/), [Espresso with the portafilter](/ratgeber/siebtraeger-espresso-grundlagen/), [Using the espresso maker correctly](/ratgeber/espressokocher-anleitung/) and [French Press](/ratgeber/french-press-anleitung/).

## Uniformity: the hidden factor

Even if the grind, time and temperature are correct, coffee can taste sour and bitter at the same time. Then the extraction was **uneven**: some of the flour was leached too much, another too little. Causes are:

- a grinder that produces a lot of dust and coarse pieces at the same time
- a crooked or uneven coffee bed in the filter
- Water that shoots through in one place, called “channeling” in espresso
- dry lumps that have not been wetted

A good grinder, a flat surface, and careful pre-brewing will improve uniformity more than any other modification.

## The error compass

| What you taste | Diagnosis | First Amendment | Second Amendment |
|---|---|---|---|
| sour, salty, thin, short | under-extracted | grind finer | hotter water or longer time |
| bitter, dry, scratchy, hollow | over-extracted | grind coarser | cooler water or shorter time |
| thin and watery, but not acidic | too weak | more coffee per water | |
| heavy, overwhelming, but balanced | too strong | more water or less coffee | |
| sour and bitter at the same time | uneven | Check mill, distribution and casting technology | fresher beans |

## This is how you really try it

Let the coffee cool down a bit before you judge. All coffees taste similar when hot, but only lukewarm do the sweetness, acidity and bitterness become clear. Try two versions side by side with only one thing different. In addition to the taste, pay attention to the mouthfeel: overextraction often appears as a furry dryness on the teeth and gums, underextraction as a short, sharp impression that quickly disappears. [Tasting Coffee](/ratgeber/kaffee-verkosten/) shows you how to taste systematically.

## A practical example

Let's say your hand filter coffee tastes sour and thin and runs through in two minutes. Then it is probably under-extracted. You grind two levels finer. The next cup goes through in three minutes and tastes sweeter and rounder, but still a bit sharp. You grind another level finer and reach three and a half minutes. Now the coffee is sweet and juicy with a long finish. If it had become dry and bitter at four minutes, you would know you had gone a step too far. This way you can work your way up to the point that suits your taste in small steps.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a refractometer?

No. Baristas use a refractometer to measure the dissolved substances in the cup and use this to calculate the extraction. A scale, a watch and a tongue are sufficient for use at home.

### Why does my coffee always taste bitter no matter what I do?

Common causes are very dark roasts, a dirty grinder or machine, coffee that has been left on the warming plate for too long, or too little coffee with a lot of water. Check these points before turning the grinder.

### Can I try with milk and sugar?

Better not to adjust it. Milk dampens bitterness and acidity, sugar masks the acidity. An under-extracted coffee with sugar quickly seems okay. Try the first few sips black, decide what to change next time, and only then add milk or sugar.

### Does this also apply to tea?

Yes, in principle yes. In tea, too, some substances dissolve early and others late, and brewing time and temperature determine sweetness and bitterness. You can find the values ​​for tea in [Tea: Brewing times and temperatures](/ratgeber/tee-ziehzeiten-und-temperaturen/).
