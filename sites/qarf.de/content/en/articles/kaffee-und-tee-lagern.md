Good coffee and good tea cost money, and both lose their flavor faster than many people think. An opened coffee package that is closed with a clip next to the stove tastes much flatter after two weeks. Green tea in a glass jar on the sunny windowsill becomes hay-like and dull in a few weeks. Proper storage is not complicated. If you know the four enemies of aroma and have a few suitable containers, you can enjoy your beans and leaves at their peak for much longer. This guide shows how to do it, with special features for coffee, the different types of tea and herbs.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - The four enemies of aroma are **oxygen**, **light**, **heat** and **moisture**. There are also **foreign smells**.
> - Store coffee and tea in **airtight, opaque** containers in a **cool, dry** place, away from the stove, dishwasher, and spices.
> - Buy **small quantities** that you will use in a few weeks.
> - **Refrigerator** is usually not a good idea for opened packages. **Freezing** works on coffee if you do it right.
> - Green tea and matcha are more delicate than black tea. Pu-erh, on the other hand, needs some air to ripen.

## The four enemies of aroma

| enemy | What it does | How you protect |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen | Oils and flavorings oxidize, coffee goes rancid, tea flat | airtight containers, little air in the container |
| Light | destroys colorings and delicate aromas, especially in green tea | opaque cans, dark cupboard |
| Warmth | accelerates all chemical changes | cool place, not next to stove or oven |
| Moisture | Lumps, mold, musty taste | Store in a dry place and only remove with a dry spoon |
| smells | Tea and coffee absorb odors easily | away from spices, onions and cleaning products |

## The right containers

- **Cans made of metal or ceramic** with a tight-fitting lid are light-tight and robust. Cans with an inner lid, like those usually used for tea, are ideal.
- **Vacuum cans** for coffee push the air out or have a movable inner lid that rests on the beans.
- **Original packaging with aroma valve:** A good coffee packaging with a valve and zip closure is already a usable container if you squeeze out the air before sealing.
- **Glass** is only suitable if it is dark or in a cupboard. Clear glasses on the countertop look nice, but damage the contents.
- **Wood** is only suitable if the can is coated on the inside. Untreated wood gives and takes away odors.

Tip: Choose the can that matches the quantity. There is a lot of oxygen in a half-empty, large can. Several small doses are better than one large one.

## The right place

A wall cabinet or drawer on the side of the kitchen that is not next to the stove, oven, dishwasher or refrigerator ventilation grille is well suited. These devices give off heat and moisture. The spice cabinet is also inconvenient because tea absorbs odors. The pantry, hallway or a cool cupboard in the dining room are often better than the kitchen itself.

## Storing coffee

Roasted coffee is most delicate once ground. Whole beans retain their flavor significantly longer.

| Coffee | Best taste after opening | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Whole beans, freshly roasted | about 2 to 4 weeks | in vacuum or valve can |
| Ground coffee | a few days to 2 weeks | airtight, consume quickly |
| Capsules, pads | stable for a long time as long as it is closed | packed under protective gas |
| Green coffee | many months | cool, dry, airy in jute or paper |

[Roasting date and freshness](/ratgeber/roestdatum-und-frische/) explains when coffee tastes best after roasting, how to recognize old coffee and how to properly freeze beans.

**Refrigerator:** The refrigerator is usually not a good choice for opened coffee. Every time you open it, moisture condenses on the cold beans and coffee absorbs the smells of other foods.

**Freezer compartment:** Whole beans freeze well if they are packed in portions and airtight and you only defrost each portion once. Allow it to come to room temperature when closed before opening it.

## Store tea

Tea is dry and has a long shelf life, but loses its aroma. How quickly depends on the variety.

| tea | Best taste, well stored | Special features |
|---|---|---|
| Matcha | a few weeks after opening | very delicate, store in a cool place, see [Preparing Matcha](/ratgeber/matcha-zubereiten/) |
| Japanese green tea | approximately 3 to 6 months after opening | Fresh is best, often in the fridge in an unopened package |
| Chinese green tea | about 6 to 12 months | airtight, dark |
| Light oolong | about 6 to 12 months | treat like green tea |
| Roasted Oolong | one to several years | stable, can develop further |
| Black tea | about 1 to 2 years | robust |
| White tea, loose | about 1 to 2 years | pressed longer, see [White Tea](/ratgeber/weisser-tee/) |
| Pu-erh | many years | needs some air, see [Pu-erh tea](/ratgeber/pu-erh-tee/) |
| Flavored teas | about 6 to 12 months | Aroma disappears, store separately |
| Herbs, fruits, rooibos | about 1 year | essential oils evaporate |

These details are guidelines for the best taste. Tea won't be harmful afterwards as long as it's dry and mold-free, it just tastes less fresh.

**Flavored teas** like Earl Gray or fruit blends release their scent to everything nearby. Store them separately from natural teas, preferably in their own cans.

**Green tea and matcha in the fridge:** Unopened packs can be stored in a cool place. Let them come to room temperature before opening. Don't constantly switch open tea between the fridge and the kitchen.

## How much you should buy

“Small quantities” is easy to say. With a simple calculation you can find the pack size that suits your consumption. For coffee, you start from your recipe, for example 15 grams per cup of filter coffee:

| consumption per day | coffee per week | About 250 g is enough | About 500 g is enough |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 cup | a good 100 g | about 2 weeks | about 4 weeks |
| 2 cups | a good 200 g | about 1 week | about 2 weeks |
| 4 cups | a good 400 g | about 4 days | about 1 week |

Anyone who drinks one cup a day is well served with 250 grams. A household with four cups a day, on the other hand, will consume a kilo in just over two weeks, which is still the best time possible, especially if you divide it up as described below.

For tea, expect around 2 to 3 grams per cup. A 100 gram pack is enough for a good 30 to 50 cups. For robust black tea, the pack can be a little larger, but for matcha and Japanese green tea it can be: only buy as much of it as you actually drink in the recommended period of time from the table above.

## Storage in special situations

| situation | The problem | This is how you do it |
|---|---|---|
| Midsummer, warm attic apartment | Heat accelerates aging and cabinets on outside walls heat up | Buy smaller quantities and put supplies in the coolest place in the apartment, such as a cupboard in the hallway |
| Vacation, away for several weeks | Opened packs continue to age | Use up the coffee beforehand or freeze whole beans in portions, leave the tea tightly sealed in the cupboard |
| office | Tea kitchen with warmth, smells and lots of hands | own small container for a week, not next to the microwave or coffee machine |
| Travel and camping | Heat in the car, humidity in the tent | Daily portions in small, tight bags or cans, stored in the shade |
| Cool basement | cool, but often too humid | only in really tight cans, check regularly for smells and lumps |

## Divide supplies sensibly

Sometimes a larger pack is worth it, such as a kilo of beans from your favorite roaster or a large bag of black tea. Then it helps to divide up the stock immediately after purchase. Pour a portion into your everyday container for the next one to two weeks and seal the rest as airtight as possible. For coffee, you can freeze the rest in weekly portions; for tea, pour it into smaller, well-sealed cans or bags and store it in a dark place. This means you don't open the main supply every day, and only a small part comes into regular contact with air. Label each can with the variety and date. Especially when it comes to tea, you quickly lose track of which type has been in the cupboard since when, and some fine green tea is forgotten until it has lost its aroma. A small list on the cupboard door helps you use up supplies sequentially.

## Spices, rock candy and accessories

Chai spices, cardamom, cinnamon and vanilla also lose their aroma and tend to give it off to tea and coffee. Store them separately and in tight jars. Rock candy and sugar have a long shelf life as long as they stay dry. More about the spice mixture for chai can be found in [Make your own chai](/ratgeber/chai-selbst-machen/).

## Common mistakes

- **Coffee next to the coffee machine:** The heat and steam are harmful.
- **Tea in clear glass cans on the window:** Light destroys color and aroma.
- **Bulk packs in stock:** The rest will go old before you drink it.
- **Use a wet spoon:** Moisture leads to lumps and, in the worst case, mold.
- **Coffee can that smells like old coffee:** Old oils build up in cans. Rinse cans occasionally in hot water and allow them to dry completely.

## The five-minute check for your supply

It's worth taking a quick look in the cupboard once a month:

- **Order:** What is open the longest? That's next.
- **Smell:** Open each can briefly. If a black tea only smells faintly, it is still suitable for chai, where spices and milk set the tone.
- **Heat:** Place your hand on the wall of the cupboard while the oven or dishwasher is running. If it's warm, find another place.
- **Seals:** Do all lids still close tightly? Replace worn out seals or change the can.
- **Self-dried herbs:** If condensation forms on the inside of the glass, they were not dry enough. Spread them out again to dry and then check them carefully for mold. How to dry it is described in [Herbal tea and fruit tea](/ratgeber/kraeutertee-und-fruechtetee/).
- **Quantities:** If you regularly have leftovers, buy smaller packs next time.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I recognize spoiled tea or coffee?

A musty, moldy or rancid smell, visible deposits or lumps are warning signs. You should no longer use such tea and coffee. Most of the time, old tea only has a weak aroma and is not spoiled.

### Can I leave tea in the original bag?

Yes, if the bag is light-tight and easy to close. Squeeze out the air and place in a can or dark cupboard.

### Should I freeze tea?

As a rule not. Tea is dry and lasts long enough if stored in a dark and airtight place. When thawing, moisture can condense on the leaves, causing more harm than good. For sensitive green teas and matcha, it is sufficient to store unopened packs in the refrigerator, as described above.

### Do tea bags last longer than loose tea?

Rather not. The finely broken tea in bags has a large surface area and loses aroma more quickly. Individually wrapped bags are slightly better protected.
