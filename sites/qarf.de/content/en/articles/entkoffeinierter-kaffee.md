Decaffeinated coffee has long had a bad reputation: flat, papery, a stopgap solution for those who can't drink “real” coffee. That has changed. Specialty roasters today offer decaffeinated coffees from good origins that taste of chocolate, caramel and fruit and are often difficult to distinguish from their caffeinated siblings in a blind test. If you want to enjoy an espresso in the evening, want to consume less caffeine during pregnancy or have difficulty tolerating caffeine, you can find real alternatives today. This guide explains how coffee is decaffeinated, what makes the processes different and what to look out for when buying.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - **Green coffee** is decaffeinated, i.e. the green bean before roasting.
> - Common processes work with **water**, **carbon dioxide (CO2)**, **ethyl acetate** or **dichloromethane**. All remove most of the caffeine.
> - In Germany, decaffeinated roasted coffee may contain a maximum of **0.1 percent caffeine** in the dry matter. One cup usually only contains a few milligrams.
> - Modern processes protect the aroma. The quality of the green coffee and appropriate roasting are crucial.
> - Decaffeinated beans are often slightly more porous and **age faster**. Buy small quantities.

## A brief look into history

The Bremen merchant **Ludwig Roselius** developed the first commercial process for decaffeination at the beginning of the 20th century. The beans were steamed and washed with a solvent that is no longer used today for health reasons. Since then, the procedures have continued to improve. Today, decaffeination companies work with methods that specifically remove the caffeine and preserve as many flavors as possible.

## How decaffeination works

Caffeine is water-soluble, as are many other substances in the coffee bean that are important for taste. The trick is to just extract the caffeine. All processes therefore begin similarly: The green coffee is **pre-swollen** with water or steam so that the pores open and the caffeine becomes mobile. The caffeine is then removed using a solvent or a filter. Finally, the beans are cleaned, steamed and **dried** again before being roasted like regular green coffee.

## The procedures in comparison

| Procedure | This is how it works | Special features |
|---|---|---|
| Water processes, such as Swiss Water | Green coffee is steeped in water that is already saturated with coffee solubles, just not caffeine. The caffeine migrates into the water and is filtered out using activated carbon. | without solvents, often used in specialty coffee |
| CO2 process | Carbon dioxide under high pressure behaves like a solvent and dissolves caffeine in a very targeted manner. | is considered particularly gentle and is used on a large scale |
| Ethyl acetate | Ethyl acetate, a substance that also occurs naturally in fruits, dissolves the caffeine. Often obtained from sugar cane. | is often advertised as “naturally decaffeinated” or “sugarcane decaf” and can have a slightly fruity note |
| Dichloromethane | A solvent containing chlorine dissolves the caffeine very selectively. It is then almost completely removed by steam and roasting. | very efficient and aroma-preserving, residual levels are strictly limited by law |

Which procedure was used is not always stated on the package. Specialty roasters usually specify this. There are strict maximum values ​​for solvent residues in the EU, which are checked during controls. If you generally want to avoid solvents, choose coffees made using the water or CO2 process.

## How much caffeine is left?

Decaffeinated coffee is not completely caffeine-free. In Germany it can be sold as decaffeinated if the roasted coffee contains a maximum of **0.1 percent caffeine** in the dry matter. For comparison: Arabica roasted coffee usually contains around 1 to 1.5 percent, Robusta significantly more. A cup of decaffeinated coffee typically only contains a few milligrams of caffeine, while a normal filter coffee contains many times that. [Caffeine in coffee and tea](/ratgeber/koffein-in-kaffee-und-tee/) shows how much caffeine is in which drink.

Anyone who has to strictly avoid caffeine for medical reasons should remember that even small amounts can add up over several cups and, if in doubt, discuss this with a doctor.

## Why decaf used to taste bad

There were reasons for the bad reputation. For a long time, cheap, mediocre green coffees were often used for decaffeination. The processes were less gentle, and the decaffeinated beans were often roasted very dark to mask defects. In addition, decaffeinated coffee from roasters is often sold less often, sits on the shelf longer and therefore arrives old to customers.

Today, good roasters specifically select high-quality green coffees for decaffeination, for example from Colombia, Peru or Ethiopia, and roast them carefully. More about origins can be found in [Coffee-growing countries and their taste](/ratgeber/kaffee-anbaulaender/).

## Roasting and preparing

Decaffeinated raw beans look browner before roasting and behave differently in the roasting machine: They brown faster and are more porous. For you as a coffee drinker this means:

- **Freshness is particularly important.** Decaffeinated beans lose their flavor more quickly. Buy small quantities and pay attention to the roasting date, see [Roasting Date and Freshness](/ratgeber/roestdatum-und-frische/).
- **Check the grinding level.** For espresso you often need a slightly different setting than your normal bean, often a little coarser because the flour extracts more easily. More in [Setting the grinding level correctly](/ratgeber/mahlgrad-richtig-einstellen/).
- **Temperature:** For very dark roasted decafs, a slightly lower brewing temperature helps to avoid bitterness.

## Who is decaf useful for?

- For anyone who wants to enjoy coffee **evening** without disturbing their sleep.
- During **pregnancy and breastfeeding** when the amount of caffeine should be limited. The general recommendation is no more than 200 milligrams of caffeine per day from all sources.
- For people who react to caffeine with **palpitations, restlessness or stomach problems**.
- For everyone who wants to **reduce their caffeine consumption** but doesn't want to miss the taste and the ritual. A blend of regular and decaffeinated coffee, often called “half café,” is a good intermediate step.

## Half Café: Reduce caffeine gradually

If you want to drink less caffeine, you don't have to change overnight. It's easier to gradually mix the usual bean with decaf. Because almost all of the caffeine comes from regular coffee, the amount of caffeine drops in approximately the same proportion as its proportion in the mixture.

| step | Regular coffee | Decaf | Caffeine compared to before |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 75 % | 25 % | about three quarters |
| 2 | 50 % | 50 % | about half |
| 3 | 25 % | 75 % | about a quarter |
| 4 | 0 % | 100 % | only traces |

Stick with each step for about a week or until you get used to the new amount. Weigh out the whole beans and mix them in the storage container for the next few days instead of putting together each portion individually. This works best with two coffees of similar roasting level, ideally from the same roastery, because then they taste together and behave similarly in the grinder. Those who give up abruptly sometimes experience headaches or fatigue in the first few days; the gradual approach usually makes the transition easier. If you need to reduce caffeine for medical reasons, discuss the procedure with your doctor.

## Decaf and regular coffee in the same household

Anyone who drinks both or offers decaf to guests should keep an eye on the mill. In almost every grinder, some coffee remains after grinding, with some grinders just a touch, with others a few grams. If you grind a portion of decaf immediately after your normal coffee, this residue ends up in the cup. For most people this is irrelevant. However, if you want to consistently avoid caffeine, grind a few grams of decaf beforehand and throw them away or use your own small hand grinder for the decaffeinated coffee, see [hand grinder or electric grinder](/ratgeber/handmuehle-oder-elektrische-muehle/).

With fully automatic machines with bean containers, decanting is laborious. Many devices therefore have a filling slot for ground coffee, which you can use to prepare a single cup of decaf. How much powder you can put in there is stated in the instructions.

Clear identification helps with invitations: a separate, labeled thermos flask for the decaffeinated coffee or a small sign next to it. This means that guests who avoid caffeine in the evening, during pregnancy or for other reasons do not have to ask.

## Try decaf fairly

Many people think they can recognize decaffeinated coffee straight away. A little test at home often shows something different. Buy a decaffeinated coffee and a regular coffee from a similar origin and roast level, preferably from the same roaster. Have a second person prepare both coffees using the same method, quantity and grind setting and pour them into equal cups without telling you which is which. Taste both while they cool and note sweetness, acidity, body, and aftertaste. Often the differences are smaller than expected, sometimes the decaffeinated coffee turns out to be a little softer and rounder, sometimes a little flatter. [Tasting Coffee](/ratgeber/kaffee-verkosten/) shows you how to taste systematically.

## If you want it to be completely caffeine-free

If you want or need to avoid caffeine completely, you will find other options besides decaf. Grain coffee made from roasted barley, rye or chicory has a coffee-like, malty taste and contains no caffeine. Rooibos, honeybush, herbal and fruit teas are naturally caffeine-free. For the evening, a warm rooibos chai is a nice alternative to espresso, see [Make your own chai](/ratgeber/chai-selbst-machen/).

## What to look for when buying

1. **Roasting date** instead of just the best-before date.
2. **Origin and process** stated, which speaks for care.
3. **Whole beans** if you have a grinder.
4. **Roasting** suitable for the preparation, rather lighter for filters, medium for espresso.
5. **Small pack** that you use in two to three weeks.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is decaffeinated coffee unhealthy?

No. Decaffeinated coffee is subject to the same food regulations as regular coffee and strict limits apply to possible residual solvents. It also contains many other ingredients in coffee.

### Does the limit also apply to soluble coffee?

Decaffeinated soluble coffee has its own maximum value of 0.3 percent caffeine in the dry matter. Because only a small amount of powder is needed for one cup, it only contains a few milligrams of caffeine.

### Are there coffee plants without caffeine?

There are of course low-caffeine coffee plants that are being researched, but they hardly play a role in the trade. Almost all decaf is made using one of the methods described.

### Can I also decaffeinate tea?

Yes, decaffeinated black and green tea is available commercially, usually treated using the CO2 process. Alternatively, rooibos and herbal teas are naturally caffeine-free, see [Rooibos and Honeybush](/ratgeber/rooibos-und-honeybush/).
