Almost every coffee package says: “100% Arabica”. The sentence sounds like a promise of quality, and Robusta seems like its cheap cousin. It's not quite that simple. Both types have their strengths, both are available in excellent and mediocre quality, and for some preparations a mixture is even the better choice. In this guide you will find out where the two types of coffee come from, how they grow, why they taste so different and what you can pay attention to when buying.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Arabica (*Coffea arabica*) and Robusta (*Coffea canephora*) are two different plant species in the genus Coffea.
> - Arabica usually grows at altitudes above around 600 meters, Robusta can tolerate heat and lower altitudes.
> - Robusta contains about twice as much caffeine: around 2 to 2.7% compared to around 1 to 1.5% for Arabica.
> - Arabica usually tastes more complex and acidic, Robusta tastes earthier, bitter and heavier.
> - “100% Arabica” is not proof of quality. A carefully processed Robusta can be better than a cheap Arabica.

## Two species, one genus

Botanically, both coffee plants belong to the genus *Coffea* from the red family. There are over a hundred described Coffea species, but only two play a major role in world trade. *Coffea arabica* originally comes from the mountain forests in southwest Ethiopia. *Coffea canephora*, almost always called “Robusta” in trade, is native to the lowland forests of West and Central Africa.

There is an exciting detail in the genes: Arabica is tetraploid, meaning it has four sets of chromosomes with a total of 44 chromosomes. It is believed to have arisen from a natural cross between *Coffea canephora* and *Coffea eugenioides*. Robusta is diploid and has 22 chromosomes. This has practical consequences. Arabica is predominantly self-pollinating, so the plants are genetically quite similar and are susceptible to the same diseases in many places. Robusta relies on cross-pollination and is therefore genetically more diverse.

There are also smaller varieties such as *Coffea liberica* and the Excelsa variety, which are drunk regionally but rarely appear in German supermarkets.

## Cultivation: mountain versus lowland

Coffee grows in the so-called coffee belt between the Northern and Southern Tropics. Within this belt, however, the two species have very different preferences.

| feature | Arabica | Robusta |
|---|---|---|
| Typical height | around 600 to over 2,000 m | about 0 to 800 m |
| Ideal temperature | around 18-22°C | around 22-30°C |
| Pollination | predominantly self-pollination | Cross-pollination necessary |
| Vulnerability | sensitive to coffee rust and pests | significantly more robust |
| Yield per plant | lower | higher |
| Bean shape | rather oval, curved furrow | rather round, furrow straight |
| Important countries | Brazil, Colombia, Ethiopia, Central America | Vietnam, Brazil, Indonesia, Uganda |

Cherries ripen more slowly at higher altitudes. The beans become denser and can store more sugar and acids. This is why highland coffees are often considered particularly aromatic. Robusta, on the other hand, thrives where Arabica is too hot and delivers more yield with less care. The name says it all: the plant is robust.

A little more than half of the coffee harvest worldwide comes from Arabica, the rest predominantly from Robusta. The proportion varies from year to year because weather and prices have a strong influence on harvests. Vietnam is by far the largest producer of Robusta, and Brazil grows both types on a large scale.

## Ingredients: caffeine, sugar and oil

The most well-known difference concerns caffeine. On average, Arabica beans contain around 1.2% caffeine based on dry matter; the usual range is around 1 to 1.5%. Robusta comes to around 2 to 2.7%. For the plant, caffeine is a natural repellent against insects, which partly explains Robusta's greater resistance.

The types of other substances also differ:

- **Sugar:** Arabica contains significantly more sucrose than Robusta. When roasted, the sugar caramelizes, contributing sweetness and complex flavors.
- **Fats:** Arabica has a higher fat content at around 15 to 17% than Robusta at around 10 to 12%. Fats carry flavors and influence the mouthfeel.
- **Chlorogenic acids:** Robusta has more of them. They contribute to bitterness and astringency, i.e. the slightly dry feeling in the mouth.

These numbers are averages. Variety, cultivation, ripeness and processing vary in individual cases.

## Taste: What you notice in the cup

A good Arabica shows a lively acidity that is reminiscent of fruit, as well as sweetness and often a wide range of aromas: from berries and citrus to chocolate and nuts to floral notes. How these aromas turn out depends largely on the origin, variety and preparation.

Robusta tends to taste earthier, woodier and more bitter, with less acid and more body. Poorly processed Robusta can appear rubbery or strawy, which has shaped its reputation. High-quality Robusta, which is carefully picked and prepared, shows notes of dark chocolate, grains and spices. In recent years there has been an increasing number of so-called Fine Robustas, which are assessed according to similar quality criteria as specialty Arabica.

### The crema question

Robusta has a visible advantage in espresso: it forms a denser, more stable and often darker crema. The crema is created because carbon dioxide dissolved under pressure forms bubbles as it exits the sieve, which are stabilized by oils and other substances. That's why many classic Italian espresso blends, especially from the south of Italy, contain a portion of Robusta. However, a beautiful crema is not a proof of taste, but rather a visual feature.

## Arabica varieties: diversity within a species

If a package says Typica, Bourbon, Caturra, Catuaí, SL28 or Geisha, these are varieties of Arabica. They differ in growth, yield, disease resistance and taste. A few examples:

- **Typica and Bourbon** are considered the historical original forms of many of today's varieties.
- **Caturra** is a short-statured mutation of Bourbon that can be planted more densely.
- Made famous by competition coffees from Panama, **Geisha** (also Gesha) is known for floral, tea-like flavors.
- **Catimor and Sarchimor** are crosses with the Timor hybrid, which carries Robusta genetic material. They are more resistant to coffee rust.

The last group in particular shows how closely the species are related: many modern Arabica plants carry a small piece of robusta in them to protect them against diseases.

## Blends: Why Roasters Combine

A blend of Arabica and Robusta is not a sign of austerity if it is deliberately put together. Roasters combine the species to achieve certain characteristics:

1. **More body and crema** in espresso, especially for cappuccino where the coffee has to prevail over milk.
2. **Less acid** for people who prefer a round, chocolatey taste.
3. **More caffeine** per cup.
4. **More stable price** because Robusta is cheaper.

Typical blends contain 10 to 30% Robusta. The proportion can also be higher for southern Italian roasts. For filter coffee, French press or hand filters, where fine acidity and a variety of aromas are the focus, pure Arabica coffees are usually the better choice.

## Where you encounter Robusta in everyday life

Even those who consciously buy “100% Arabica” often drink Robusta more often than they think. A large proportion of soluble coffee is made entirely or partly from Robusta because the type is cheap and still tastes strong even after industrial processing. Espresso blends that advertise terms such as “crema”, “strong” or a high level of intensity also often contain a Robusta component.

In Vietnam, Robusta is the everyday coffee par excellence. It is vigorously roasted, brewed in a small metal filter directly over the glass and drunk with sweetened condensed milk, hot or over ice. The sweet, heavy milk absorbs the bitterness and turns the bitter coffee into an almost dessert-like drink. You can get close to this at home if you pour a strong coffee from the [espresso maker](/ratgeber/espressokocher-anleitung/) onto one or two tablespoons of sweetened condensed milk, stir well and fill the glass with ice as desired.

## How you recognize quality

Since “100% Arabica” alone doesn’t mean much, it’s worth taking a closer look at the packaging:

- **Roasting date:** A specific date is more meaningful than just a best-before date. Coffee tastes best in the first few weeks after roasting.
- **Origin:** Country, region or even farm indicate traceability.
- **Processing:** Information such as “washed” or “natural” shows that the roaster has dealt with the green coffee.
- **Whole beans:** Ground coffee loses its aroma much more quickly.
- **Appearance:** Uniform size and color are a good sign for whole beans. Many broken or very unevenly roasted beans indicate less careful sorting.

A look at the beans themselves also helps: Arabica beans are more elongated-oval with an S-shaped central furrow, Robusta beans are smaller, rounder and have an almost straight furrow.

## What that means for your preparation

If you use Robusta or a blend rich in Robusta, you can tame the bitterness somewhat through preparation. A slightly lower water temperature, for example 90 to 92 °C, and not too long a contact time help. Robusta shows its strengths in the espresso maker or portafilter. For bright, fruity Arabica roasts, however, hotter water around 94 to 96 °C and methods such as the hand filter that emphasize clarity are suitable.

## Climate change: why Robusta and wild species are becoming more important

Arabica is sensitive to rising temperatures, irregular rainy seasons and heavy rain. Numerous studies assume that the areas that are currently suitable for high-quality Arabica will become smaller in the coming decades or will have to move to higher altitudes. As a result, Robusta is becoming more important: it tolerates heat better, is more resistant to diseases and, in many places, is cultivated and processed more carefully than before. But he isn't completely insensitive either. It needs abundant rainfall and suffers from long droughts and heat waves.

At the same time, researchers are looking for solutions among wild coffee species. An example is *Coffea stenophylla* from West Africa, which was considered lost for decades and was only rediscovered a few years ago. Initial studies indicate that it can tolerate higher temperatures than Arabica and has a similar taste. Liberica and Excelsa are also receiving more attention again. It will take some time before such coffees play a significant role in retail. For you as a buyer, this already means: Robusta deserves an unbiased look, and the line between good and mediocre coffee is becoming less and less based on the species name.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Arabica healthier than Robusta?

No, it cannot be said in general terms. Robusta contains more caffeine and more chlorogenic acids, Arabica more sugar and fats. For most people, the difference is primarily a question of the amount of caffeine per cup. Those who are sensitive to caffeine are more likely to choose pure Arabica or decaffeinated coffee.

### Does Robusta always taste bitter?

Robusta is naturally more bitter than Arabica, but the bitterness depends heavily on quality, roasting and preparation. A properly processed Robusta with a medium roast can taste pleasantly chocolaty and strong.

### Why is Arabica more expensive?

Arabica requires altitude, is more susceptible to diseases and produces less yield per plant. In addition, high-quality Arabica is often selectively picked by hand. All of this costs. The stock market prices of both types also fluctuate greatly with harvests and weather.

### Can I mix Arabica and Robusta myself?

Yes. If you have two coffees at home, you can combine, for example, 80% Arabica and 20% Robusta and see how the crema and taste change in the espresso. Mix whole beans and grind them together, then everything will be evenly distributed.
