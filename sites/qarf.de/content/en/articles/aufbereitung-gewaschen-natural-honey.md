On many coffee packages from small roasters, next to the country and region, there is a word that initially seems puzzling: “washed”, “natural” or “honey”. This information describes the processing, i.e. the path from the ripe coffee cherry on the vine to the green raw bean, which is shipped and roasted. The preparation shapes the taste almost as much as the origin. The same variety from the same farm can taste clean and lemony when washed and, when natural, taste like blueberries and red wine. If you know the differences, it will be easier to find the coffees that you like.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - The coffee bean is the seed of a fruit, the coffee cherry. During processing, the peel, pulp and mucus are removed and the bean is dried.
> - **Washed:** Pulp is removed early. Result: clear, clean, lively acidity.
> - **Natural:** The whole cherry dries with the bean in it. Result: fruity, sweet, full-bodied, sometimes winey.
> - **Honey:** The pulp is removed, the sticky fruit mucus remains on the bean as it dries. Result: between the two, with a rounded sweetness.
> - **Wet Hulling** from Indonesia produces earthy, herbal coffees with little acidity.

## The structure of the coffee cherry

A ripe coffee cherry is red, yellow or orange and about the size of a cherry. From the outside to the inside it consists of:

1. the thin **shell**,
2. the sweet **pulp**, the pulp,
3. the sticky, sugary **fruit mucilage**, also called mucilage,
4. the firm **parchment skin** that encloses each bean,
5. the fine **silver skin**,
6. usually two **beans** with their flat sides touching each other.

The aim of the processing is to remove all layers from the beans and dry them to a moisture content of around ten to twelve percent so that they can be stored.

## Natural: dry processing

Natural or dry processing is the oldest method. The harvested cherries are sorted, washed and then dried whole in the sun, on concrete surfaces or on raised drying beds made of nets. They are turned regularly over two to four weeks so that they dry evenly and do not become moldy. The dry, hard shell is then removed mechanically.

Because the bean lies in the sweet pulp for weeks, it absorbs the aromas of the fruit. Natural coffees often taste like berries, stone fruit, dried fruits or wine and have a full body and a sweet note. The method requires little water and is common in dry regions such as parts of Ethiopia and Brazil. However, it requires a lot of care: irregularly dried cherries can ferment or mold and then produce defective, musty or alcoholic notes.

## Washed: wet processing

During wet processing, the cherries are depulped shortly after harvest: a machine squeezes the beans out of the shell and pulp. What remains is the bean in its parchment skin, surrounded by the sticky fruit mucus. This is dissolved through fermentation in water tanks, which takes about half a day to several days, depending on the climate and method. The beans are then washed thoroughly and dried on drying beds or in dryers.

Washed coffees taste clear and clean. The characteristics of the variety and the growing region clearly stand out, often with a lively, fruity acidity, such as citrus, green apple or blackcurrant. Many coffees from Colombia, Kenya and Central America are washed. The disadvantage: The method requires a lot of water, and the wastewater must be treated carefully so as not to pollute rivers.

## Honey: the middle ground

During honey processing, also called pulped natural, the pulp is removed, but the fruit mucilage remains on the bean in whole or in part. It dries with this sticky, honey-like layer, hence the name. Coffee has nothing to do with honey.

Depending on how much mucilage remains on the bean and how slowly the bean is dried, producers often differentiate between **White, Yellow, Red and Black Honey**. The more slime and the slower the drying, the darker the name and the fruitier and sweeter the coffee. Honey coffees combine the clarity of washed coffees with more sweetness and body. The method is particularly common in Costa Rica and El Salvador and saves water compared to wet treatment.

## Wet Hulling: the Indonesian method

In Sumatra and other Indonesian islands there is a special process called Giling Basah. The beans are peeled from the parchment skin while they are still wet and then dried further without this protective layer. This happens faster in the humid climate. The result is coffees with low acidity, heavy body and earthy, spicy, herbal notes. Many people like this character in espresso or strong blends.

## Experimental fermentations

In recent years, many producers have been experimenting with controlled fermentations: **Anaerobic** means that the cherries or beans ferment in sealed tanks without oxygen. During **carbonic acid maceration**, which is known from viticulture, whole cherries are immersed in an atmosphere of carbon dioxide. Such coffees can have very intense, unusual aromas, such as tropical fruits, cinnamon or liqueur. They are exciting, but polarizing and are often more expensive.

## After processing: drying, resting time and dry mill

No matter which method a producer chooses, the final steps determine whether the care taken during harvest and fermentation reaches the cup. Drying should be even and rather slow. On concrete surfaces, cherries and beans dry faster and are heated more strongly from below; on raised drying beds, the air also circulates under the drying material. Larger companies dry completely or partially in drum dryers, especially if the harvest falls during a rainy period. Drying too quickly or unevenly can cause a coffee to later taste flat or age more quickly.

The coffee then usually rests in its parchment skin or in the dried cherry shell for a few weeks so that the moisture in the beans equalizes. It only goes into the dry mill shortly before export. There, the parchment or casing is removed, the beans are sorted according to size and density and defective beans are sorted out, by machine using a color sorter and, for top-quality coffees, by hand. Beans that are unripe, black, fermented or eaten by insects would otherwise spoil the taste of the entire batch.

## Decipher terms on the package

Roasteries often use the English names of the producers. The most important ones mean:

| Information | What's behind it |
|---|---|
| Washed, fully washed | classic wet processing with fermentation and washing |
| Double washed, Kenya process | two fermentation steps with washing in between, often followed by soaking in clear water, typical of Kenya |
| Pulped natural | Common name in Brazil for a honey-like method |
| Semi-washed | used inconsistently, in Indonesian coffees it is usually another name for wet hulling |
| Natural, dry process | dry preparation in the whole cherry |
| Anaerobic Natural or Anaerobic Washed | first fermented without oxygen, then dried or washed as natural |
| Yeast fermentation | Fermentation is controlled with specifically added yeasts |
| Infused, co-fermented | During fermentation, fruits, spices or aromas are added; controversial because the taste does not come from cherry and bean alone |

If it only says “Natural” or “Washed” without any addition, the classic version is usually meant. When it comes to infused coffees, it's worth checking whether the roasting company openly states what has been added.

## Preparation and taste at a glance

| Processing | Acid | Sweetie | body | Typical notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Washed | high, clear | medium | light to medium | Citrus, apple, flowers, tea |
| Honey | medium | high | medium | Caramel, stone fruit, honeydew melon |
| Natural | medium | high | full | Berries, dried fruits, wine, chocolate |
| Wet Hulled | low | medium | difficult | earthy, spicy, herbs, dark chocolate |
| Anaerobic, experimental | variable | high | variable | tropical fruits, spices, liqueur-like |

The table describes trends. Variety, altitude, harvest care and roasting influence the result just as much. ## What that means for your cup

- **For filter coffee and hand filters** washed coffees show their clarity particularly well, naturals their fruit. Try both side by side using the same method as described in [Hand Filter](/ratgeber/handfilter-kaffee-anleitung/).
- **For espresso**, naturals and honeys are popular because they bring sweetness and body. Very acidic washed coffees can appear sharp as espresso.
- **For French Press and Cold Brew** Naturals work well with their full body and fruity notes.

## Recognize preparation in the cup

You'll learn the most if you make the comparison specifically: two coffees from the same country and with a similar roast, which differ primarily in the preparation. Ethiopia is a good choice because washed coffees and naturals are equally available from there.

1. Prepare both using the same method, dose, grind and temperature, for example in two small French presses.
2. First smell the dry coffee grounds. Naturals often smell clearly of berries or dried fruit.
3. Taste in several sips while the cups cool. Pay attention to how clearly the flavors are distinguished from each other, how heavy the coffee is on the tongue and how long the aftertaste lasts.

At the same time, you train your eye for mistakes. A natural can be fruity and winey, but should not smell like vinegar, nail polish remover or stable. Such notes indicate fermentation that has gotten out of control. A washed coffee with a pungently sour aftertaste may have been fermented too long or improperly. [Tasting coffee](/ratgeber/kaffee-verkosten/) shows how you can systematically record such impressions.

## Processing and sustainability

The choice of processing also has ecological consequences. Wet processing requires a lot of water, and the acidic wastewater from fermentation can pollute rivers if not cleaned in settling ponds. Modern systems save water through circulation systems and mechanical degumming. Natural and honey processes require significantly less water, but require a lot of space, manpower and stable, dry weather. Many farms use the cherry skins and pulp as compost on their orchards, and dried cherry skins are brewed into a fruity, tea-like drink called cascara.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is one preparation better than the other?

No. Each can produce great or flawed coffees. Care during harvesting, sorting, fermentation and drying is crucial.

### Why isn't the preparation stated on every package?

When it comes to supermarket coffee, many origins are often mixed and processing takes a back seat. Specialty roasters specify it because it explains the character of an individual coffee.

### Can you taste the preparation even when roasted dark?

The darker a coffee is roasted, the more the roasting aromas mask the differences in preparation. If you want to taste the fruit of a natural or the clarity of a washed coffee, choose light to medium roasts. More about this in [Roasting levels explained](/ratgeber/roestgrade-erklaert/).

### What is Pergamino?

This is what many producers call the bean in its parchment skin after processing. Coffee is often stored in this state before being shelled just before export.
