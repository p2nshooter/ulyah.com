Pu-Erh is a tea with a history and its own character. It comes from Yunnan Province in southwest China, is often pressed into solid cakes and can age for years and decades, similar to wine. Young Pu-Erh tastes fresh, tart and fruity, matured soft, sweet and earthy. A second type, Shou Pu-Erh, is made dark and mild through an accelerated process and tastes of forest floor, cocoa and dates. For beginners, Pu-Erh often seems mysterious, with Chinese names, vintages and factories. This guide explains the basics and shows you how to enjoy it.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Pu-Erh comes from **Yunnan** and is made from the large-leaved tea plant **Camellia sinensis var. assamica**.
> - **Sheng** (raw) matures slowly over years. **Shou** (matured) becomes dark and mild in a few weeks due to accelerated wet fermentation.
> - Pu-Erh is often pressed as a **cake**, brick or nest, but is also available loose.
> - Before the first infusion, the tea is usually washed briefly. This is followed by many short infusions with hot water.
> - Store Pu-Erh dry, clean, odorless and with some air.

## What makes Pu-Erh special

Most teas are drunk as unchanged as possible after production. Pu-Erh, on the other hand, is one of the **dark teas**, Heicha in Chinese, which continues to mature after production. Microorganisms, enzymes and oxygen slowly act on the tea. This secondary fermentation changes the color, taste and mouthfeel: tart and bitter notes become softer, sweet, earthy and woody notes emerge.

The name comes from the city **Pu'er** in Yunnan, which was once an important tea trading place. Pressed tea was transported to Tibet via the old tea horse road. Pressing made the tea compact and sturdy for long journeys.

## Sheng and Shou in comparison

| | Sheng Pu-Erh (raw) | Shou Pu-Erh (aged) |
|---|---|---|
| Manufacturing | Leaves are wilted, briefly heated, rolled, dried in the sun and pressed | additionally moist and fermented in a controlled manner for weeks |
| Color young | golden yellow to orange | dark red to almost black |
| Taste young | fresh, tart, floral, fruity, sometimes strongly bitter | earthy, soft, cocoa, dates, forest floor |
| Development | becomes darker, softer and sweeter over the years | changes less, becomes rounder over time |
| For whom | Curious people who like development and complexity | Beginners, lovers of mild, dark teas |

**Shou Pu-Erh** was developed in the 1970s to achieve the flavor of aged tea more quickly. The leaves are moistened and piled up into large piles, similar to compost. Microorganisms warm the pile, which is turned regularly. After a few weeks the tea is dark and mild. Young Shou may have a slightly musty, “cellar-like” smell that disappears after some time in storage.

## Forms and names

- **Bing Cha:** round, flat cake, classic 357 grams.
- **Tuo Cha:** small nest or bowl, often 100 grams, also as a mini Tuo for a jug.
- **Zhuan Cha:** rectangular brick.
- **Loose:** unpressed Mao Cha, the raw tea before pressing.

The packaging often shows the **year of pressing**, the **factory** or producer, the **region** such as Menghai, Lincang or Pu'er and sometimes a recipe number. Old trees, “gushu,” and certain mountains fetch high prices. Unfortunately, there are also counterfeits and exaggerated age statements in this market. Buy from dealers who clearly describe the origin and storage.

## Open a cake

1. Place the cake flat side down on a clean board.
2. Place a **pu-erh pick** or a blunt knife at an angle along the edge, not in the middle.
3. Carefully pry off layers and try to leave the leaves intact if possible.
4. Weigh out the portion and store the remaining cake in its paper.

Broken leaves and dust release bitterness more quickly. So you shouldn't crumble the cake.

## Prepare Pu-Erh

### Gongfu style

Pu-Erh is made for small pots and lots of infusions.

1. Preheat gaiwan or pot with boiling water.
2. Add approximately **5 to 7 grams** of tea for 100 milliliters of water.
3. **Washing:** Pour boiling water over it and drain after a few seconds. This opens the pressed leaves and rinses away dust. For tightly pressed tea or shou, wash twice.
4. First infusion **10 to 15 seconds**, with boiling or almost boiling water.
5. Each subsequent infusion takes a little longer. Good Pu-Erh delivers ten infusions and more.

For young, strong sheng, you can lower the temperature slightly, to around 90 degrees, and infuse briefly to tame the tartness. More about the technique in [Gongfu Cha: Chinese-Style Tea](/ratgeber/gongfu-cha/).

### Simply in a glass

In China, many people drink their tea in everyday life without any ceremony: a few leaves in a large glass or mug, hot water on top, drink and then pour again as soon as the glass is a third empty. **Shou Pu-Erh** is particularly suitable for this type, as it hardly becomes bitter even if it is steeped for a long time. Use only a small amount of tea, about 2 grams for a large mug. Young sheng can quickly become very bitter this way.

### In the pot

If you don't have gaiwan, use about 3 to 4 grams per 250 milliliters, wash the tea briefly and let it steep for one to three minutes. Pour off completely and brew several times.

## Storage and maturation

Pu-erh requires different conditions than most teas. It only ripens if it has some air and a certain humidity, but under no circumstances should it become moldy.

- **Dry, but not bone dry:** Normal living rooms are usually suitable. No cellars, no bathrooms.
- **Odourless:** Tea absorbs odors very easily. Store away from spices, coffee, perfume and cleaning products.
- **Dark** and without direct sun.
- **With some air:** Cakes in their paper in a cardboard box or clay container, not sealed airtight, if the tea is to mature.

Check the tea occasionally. White or green, fluffy coating and a musty smell are signs of mold. You shouldn't drink tea like that. A general overview of storage is given in [Store coffee and tea correctly](/ratgeber/kaffee-und-tee-lagern/).

## Dry and damp storage

Among lovers, the question of **where** a pu-erh has matured plays a big role. Teas that have been stored for years in very humid, warm regions such as Hong Kong or southern China ripen more quickly. They become darker and earthier, often with notes of camphor, wood and old leather. However, this **moist storage** carries the risk of mold and musty tones if not done carefully. When stored **dry**, such as in Kunming or European homes, maturation is slower and the tea remains clearer and fruitier. Neither is fundamentally better. Good dealers indicate how and where a tea was stored.

## The returning sweetness

A term you'll often come across with pu-erh is **Huigan**, the returning sweetness. What is meant is an effect that good Sheng teas have: the sip itself can be tart or slightly bitter, but shortly afterwards a lasting sweetness spreads through the mouth and throat. Many connoisseurs value this change more than an initially sweet taste. Together with a dense, almost oily mouthfeel, it is a sign of tea made from good raw materials.

## How to recognize good Pu-Erh

- **Infusion:** clear, bright, not cloudy.
- **Taste:** Young sheng may be tart, but should show sweetness in the aftertaste. Shou should be soft and earthy, not smelling like a wet cellar or fish.
- **Endurance:** Many infusions without rapid loss of flavor.
- **Leaves:** After the infusion, whole, elastic leaves instead of broken and dust.

## Other dark teas from China

Pu-erh is the most famous, but not the only, dark tea. Other provinces have their own traditions, which are also fermented and often pressed. **Liu Bao** from Guangxi is often stored in baskets and tastes soft, woody and slightly of betel nut and camphor. **Fu Cha** from Hunan, also called Fu Zhuan, is pressed as a brick in which a golden-yellow noble mushroom grows, which forms small yellow dots in the tea and gives it a sweet note reminiscent of dried fruit. These mushroom spots are intentional and not mold. If you like Shou Pu-Erh, you will find exciting relatives in these teas that can be prepared in a similar way.

## For starters

Start with a simple shou pu-erh in mini-tuo or loose form. It is cheap, forgives mistakes in preparation and immediately shows you the typical earthy, sweet character. Afterwards, it's worth trying a young Sheng from a well-known region to get to know the fresh, bitter side. If you have the chance, try a sheng with ten or more years of aging at some point. The comparison with young tea shows you impressively what ripening does. Buy small batches or samples rather than whole cakes at first until you know what you like.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does Pu-Erh help you lose weight?

Pu-erh is often advertised this way. Reliable evidence of a relevant effect in humans is lacking. Enjoy it as a special tea, not as a slimming product.

### Does Pu-Erh have a lot of caffeine?

Pu-erh contains caffeine, often at a similar level to other teas. How much ends up in the cup depends largely on the amount and type of infusion. More in [Caffeine in Coffee and Tea](/ratgeber/koffein-in-kaffee-und-tee/).

### How long does Pu-Erh last?

If stored well, many years. Sheng continues to develop, Shou remains more stable. For legal reasons, there is still a best-before date on the package.

## Conclusion

Pu-Erh invites you to discover: Shou for a soft, earthy introduction, young Sheng for freshness and strength, mature Sheng for depth and sweetness. With briefly washed leaves, hot water, many short infusions and clean, airy storage, every piece of cake becomes a little trip to Yunnan.
