White tea is the least processed of all teas. The leaves and buds are picked, wilted and dried, nothing more. No rolling, no heating to stop oxidation, no roasting. What sounds so simple requires a lot of experience, because the weather, temperature and humidity determine how the leaf changes as it slowly wilts. The result is a light, delicate tea with notes of hay, honey, melon and flowers that tastes gentle and sweet and is hardly bitter. By the way, the name does not come from the color of the infusion, but from the fine, silvery-white hairs on the young buds.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - White tea is just **withered and dried**. It oxidizes easily without being specifically rolled or heated.
> - The classic origin is the Chinese province **Fujian**, especially the Fuding and Zhenghe regions.
> - The main varieties: **Silver Needle** (buds only), **Bai Mu Dan** (bud with two leaves), **Shou Mei** and **Gong Mei** (more leaves, stronger).
> - White tea is brewed at **80 to 90 degrees** and can withstand longer steeping times and multiple infusions.
> - Pressed white tea can mature for years and becomes darker and sweeter.

## How white tea is made

For high-quality white tea, young buds and the top leaves are picked in spring. They are laid out thinly on bamboo mats or trellises and wilt slowly, traditionally outdoors in the shade and sun, but today often in rooms with controlled air. Wilting takes one to three days depending on the weather. The leaves lose water and slight natural oxidation occurs. Finally, the tea is dried at a low temperature.

Because the leaf is not rolled, the cells remain largely intact. This explains the delicate, gentle taste and why white tea can steep longer without becoming bitter. In comparison, green tea is heated quickly to prevent any oxidation, and black tea is deliberately rolled and completely oxidized. More about this in [Green tea: varieties from Japan and China](/ratgeber/gruener-tee-sorten/) and [Black tea varieties](/ratgeber/schwarztee-sorten/).

## The varieties

| variety | Chinese name | picking | taste |
|---|---|---|---|
| Silver Needle | Bai Hao Yin Zhen | just buds | very tender, sweet, honey, melon, hay |
| White Peony | Bai Mu Dan | Bud with one or two leaves | floral, fruity, a little fuller |
| Tribute Eyebrow | Gong Mei | smaller leaves, few buds | stronger, nutty, warm |
| Longevity Eyebrow | Shou Mei | later, larger leaves | full, autumnal, dried fruit, wood |

### Silver Needle

The finest form of white tea consists exclusively of the thick, fluffy buds of the tea plant. They are only picked on a few days in early spring. The infusion is very light, almost colorless, with a delicate, sweet taste of honey, cucumber and fresh hay. Silver Needle is expensive because it takes a lot of buds per kilogram.

### Bai Mu Dan

The “white peony tea” contains one or two young leaves in addition to the bud. It is slightly stronger and more floral than Silver Needle, often with notes of peach and meadow blossoms. For many it is the best introduction to white tea because it has character and is still delicate.

### Shou Mei and Gong Mei

These varieties consist primarily of leaves that are harvested later in the year. They are darker, bolder and earthier, with notes of dried fruit, dates and wood. They are cheaper and are good for everyday use and for maturing.

## White tea from other countries

In addition to Fujian, Yunnan, India, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Kenya and Malawi also produce white tea. Yunnan white tea, often made from large-leaved plants, is fuller and fruitier. Darjeeling and Nepal white teas are floral and light. Since the term “white tea” is not used the same way everywhere, it is worth taking a look at the description: What is important is that the tea has only been withered and dried.

## Pressed and aged white tea

In recent years, pressed white tea has become popular. Shou Mei and Bai Mu Dan are pressed into cakes, similar to Pu-Erh. When stored dry, clean and odorless, the tea changes over the years: it becomes darker, sweeter, softer, with notes of honey, dates and herbs. In Fujian there is a saying that white tea is tea in the first year, medicine in the third year and a treasure in the seventh. This is a saying, not a health statement. How Pu-Erh matures is explained in [Pu-Erh Tea](/ratgeber/pu-erh-tee/).

To remove a piece from the cake, place a blunt knife or a special tea pick at the edge and carefully pry off layers without breaking the leaves.

## Prepare white tea

| variety | Quantity per 250 ml | Temperature | Brewing time | Infusions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Silver Needle | 3 to 4 g | 75 to 85 degrees | 3 to 5 minutes | 2 to 3 |
| Bai Mu Dan | 3g | 80 to 90 degrees | 3 to 4 minutes | 2 to 3 |
| Shou Mei, pressed | 3g | 90 to 95 degrees | 2 to 4 minutes | 3 to 4 |

Because white teas are voluminous and light, the right amount in the spoon often goes a long way. A scale helps. Silver Needle is forgiving for longer steeping times, but will become flat if the water is too hot. Shou Mei and aged teas tolerate hotter water.

**In the glass:** Silver Needle is particularly beautiful when poured in the glass. The buds first float on the surface and slowly sink vertically downwards.

**In Gaiwan:** With more tea and short infusions, white tea develops over many rounds. How to do this can be found in [Gongfu Cha: Chinese-style tea](/ratgeber/gongfu-cha/).

**Cold infused:** White tea is great for brewing cold overnight in the fridge. It will be sweet and soft. More ideas in [Make your own iced tea](/ratgeber/eistee-selbst-machen/).

## What's in white tea

Like all teas, white tea contains caffeine and polyphenols. It is often said that it hardly contains any caffeine. That's not true: teas rich in buds like Silver Needle can certainly contain caffeine because young buds produce a lot of it. How much ends up in the cup depends on the quantity, temperature and brewing time. More in [Caffeine in Coffee and Tea](/ratgeber/koffein-in-kaffee-und-tee/). Health claims that promote white tea as particularly effective should be treated with caution. Above all, it is a fine, enjoyable tea.

## Storage

Loose white tea keeps well for about one to two years if stored airtight, dark, dry and away from odors. Pressed white tea can be stored for longer, but then needs some air and must not become moist. More in [Store coffee and tea properly](/ratgeber/kaffee-und-tee-lagern/).

## Recognize quality

You can recognize good white tea by the dry leaf. For Silver Needle, the buds should be plump, evenly sized and densely covered with silvery hairs; A lot of breakage and dust indicate rough workmanship or poor treatment during transport. Bai Mu Dan features a mix of silvery buds and green to olive leaves. Brown leaves are normal for Shou Mei because it is harvested later and wilted more heavily. The scent of the dry tea should be clean, sweet and hay-like. If it smells musty, damp or like smoke, it has been stored poorly. The infusion is clear and shiny, not cloudy, and the taste remains pleasant in the mouth for a long time.

## Harvest time and vintage

The finest buds are picked in early spring, in Fujian usually in March and early April. Later pickings in summer and fall produce stronger leaf teas like Shou Mei. Pressed cakes often indicate a vintage, but sometimes the year of pressing, which does not have to correspond to the year of harvest. Older age alone does not guarantee better quality: what matters is how clean and dry the tea was stored. A young tea that has been carefully stored is better than an old one that has absorbed moisture.

## Typical mistakes

- **Too little tea:** The light leaves are deceptive. It's better to weigh instead of dosing by eye.
- **Sparkling boiling water for buds:** Silver Needle loses its sweetness. Let the water cool briefly.
- **Crumble pressed tea:** Fine fragments extract too quickly and make the infusion cloudy and tart. It is better to remove entire layers of leaves.
- **Wrong neighborhood:** In addition to spices, coffee or perfume, white tea takes on foreign smells.
- **Forgot the infusion:** If the leaves stay in the water for a long time, white tea will eventually become flat and bitter. Pour off as soon as you like it.

## White tea and food

Because of its delicate taste, white tea goes well with light dishes. Silver Needle and Bai Mu Dan taste ripe pears, melon, peaches, mild pastries with little sugar or fresh goat cheese. Strong spices and spicy dishes, on the other hand, mask it. Matured Shou Mei can tolerate more accompaniment: nuts, dates and dried fruits pick up its warm, sweet notes.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why doesn't my white tea taste like anything?

You probably used too little tea or let it brew for too short a time. White tea needs more leaves than you think, and it can steep longer.

### Is white tea the same as white tea flavored tea?

No. Some flavored blends contain only a small amount of white tea and a lot of flavor. Pure white tea tastes sweet without any additives.

### Can I drink white tea with milk?

This is not recommended. The fine taste is lost. White tea is drunk pure.

### How often can I brew white tea?

In the pot with 3 grams per cup, two to three infusions can usually be achieved, but you can extend the brewing time slightly each time. In gaiwan with more leaves and short infusions of 20 to 40 seconds, six or more rounds are possible, especially with pressed shou mei.

## Conclusion

White tea rewards patience: enough leaves, not too hot water and a little time. Anyone who starts with Bai Mu Dan quickly gets to know the entire spectrum, from the delicate sweetness of a Silver Needle to the warm depth of an aged Shou Mei. Stored clean and dry, it remains a pleasure for a long time.
