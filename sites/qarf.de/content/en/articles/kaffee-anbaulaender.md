Coffee grows in more than fifty countries, all in a broad band around the equator called the coffee belt. And yet a coffee from Kenya tastes completely different than one from Brazil, and one from Ethiopia tastes different from one from Sumatra. Soil, climate, altitude, plant varieties and the type of processing characterize each origin. If you know the typical profiles, you can find your way around the roasting companies' offerings more quickly and search specifically for coffees that suit your own taste. This overview presents the most important growing regions.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Coffee grows between the tropics, usually at altitudes where it is warm but not hot.
> - The higher the cultivation, the slower the cherries ripen, the denser the bean becomes and the more complex and acidic the coffee.
> - **East Africa** (Ethiopia, Kenya, Rwanda): floral, fruity, lively acidity.
> - **Central and South America** (Colombia, Guatemala, Costa Rica, Brazil): balanced, chocolate, caramel, nut, from fresh to mild.
> - **Asia** (Sumatra, India, Vietnam): earthy, spicy, heavy body, low acidity. Vietnam is the largest producer of Robusta.

## The coffee belt and the role of height

Arabica coffee needs a balanced climate without frost and without extreme heat, regular rainfall and a dry season for flowering. These conditions are found in the tropics, especially at altitudes between around 1,000 and 2,200 meters. Robusta grows at lower, hotter altitudes. More about the two species can be found in [Arabica and Robusta](/ratgeber/arabica-und-robusta/).

At altitude the nights are cool. The cherries ripen more slowly, the plant stores more sugar and acids, and the beans become denser and harder. Highland coffees therefore usually taste more complex and lively. Coffees from lower altitudes are milder, softer and have less acid. On packages you will often find an altitude in meters above sea level.

## Africa

### Ethiopia

Ethiopia is considered the country of origin of Arabica coffee. It still grows wild in the forests of the southwest today. Thousands of local varieties make the coffees from regions such as Yirgacheffe, Sidama, Guji or Harrar exceptionally diverse. Washed Ethiopian coffees often taste floral like jasmine and bergamot, with citrus notes and a tea-like body. Naturals are reminiscent of blueberries, strawberries and red wine. Coffee is deeply rooted in Ethiopia's culture, with its own coffee ceremonies where the beans are roasted in front of guests.

### Kenya

Kenyan coffees are famous for their intense acidity and fruitiness: blackcurrant, grapefruit, sometimes a spice reminiscent of tomato. Most are washed and come from small farms that supply their cherries to cooperatives. Kenya sorts its beans by size into quality levels such as AA and AB.

### Rwanda and Burundi

The coffees from the highlands of Rwanda and Burundi are often fruity with red berries, hibiscus and a tea-like elegance. Occasionally an off-taste occurs that smells like raw potatoes and is caused by an insect. Good roasters sort out such batches carefully.

## Central America

**Guatemala:** full coffees with chocolate, caramel and often spicy or smoky notes from volcanic regions such as Antigua and Huehuetenango.

**Costa Rica:** clear, sweet coffees, known for careful honey processing that brings round sweetness and stone fruit notes. More about this in [Preparation: washed, natural, honey](/ratgeber/aufbereitung-gewaschen-natural-honey/).

**Honduras, El Salvador, Nicaragua:** balanced coffees with caramel, nuts and mild fruit, often good value for money.

**Panama:** known for the Geisha variety, which commands record prices with floral, jasmine-like aromas.

## South America

### Colombia

Colombia has been supplying washed coffee in large quantities and quality for decades. Balanced cups with caramel, red apple, cherry and a pleasant acidity are typical. In some regions, thanks to the climate, there are two harvests a year, which means fresh produce is available almost all year round.

### Brazil

Brazil is the largest coffee producer in the world. Many plantations are located at lower, flatter altitudes where harvesting can be done mechanically. Brazilian coffees are usually mild, nutty and chocolaty, with little acid and full body. Natural and pulped-natural preparations are common. They form the basis of many espresso blends.

### Peru and Bolivia

Coffees from Peru are often mild and sweet, with notes of chocolate and nuts, and are often organic. Bolivia only produces small quantities of highland coffee, but some of it is very fine.

## Asia and Arabia

**Indonesia:** Coffees from Sumatra, Sulawesi and Java are earthy, spicy, herbal and heavy in body due to the wet-hulling processing that is widespread there. Many people like them in espresso and dark roasts.

**India:** known for Monsooned Malabar: Green coffee is exposed to moist winds during the monsoon season, swells and loses acidity. The result is a very mild, heavy, spicy coffee.

**Vietnam:** Second largest coffee producer in the world and largest producer of Robusta. Vietnamese coffee is often heavily roasted and traditionally drunk with sweetened condensed milk.

**Yemen:** one of the oldest coffee exporters. The port mocha gave its name to an entire type of preparation. Yemeni coffees are rare, expensive and often taste winey, spicy and of dried fruits.

## Overview of typical profiles

| region | Acid | body | Typical aromas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ethiopia | high | light to medium | Jasmine, bergamot, citrus, berries |
| Kenya | very high | medium | blackcurrant, grapefruit, tomato |
| Colombia | medium | medium | Caramel, red apple, cherry |
| Guatemala | medium | medium to full | Chocolate, spice, caramel |
| Costa Rica | medium | medium | Honey, stone fruit, citrus |
| Brazil | low | full | Nut, chocolate, caramel |
| Sumatra | low | difficult | Earth, herbs, spices, dark chocolate |

The profiles are tendencies. Variety, farm, preparation and roasting change the taste greatly. How roasting levels work is explained [Roasting levels explained](/ratgeber/roestgrade-erklaert/).

## This is how you use the knowledge when shopping

- If you like coffee **mild, chocolaty and without much acid**, try Brazil, Peru or Sumatra.
- If you are looking for **fruity, bright, lively** coffees for the hand filter, try Ethiopia, Kenya or Rwanda.
- For a **balanced all-rounder**, Colombia, Guatemala or Honduras are suitable.
- Compare two origins side by side as described in [Tasting Coffee](/ratgeber/kaffee-verkosten/). This is the quickest way to get to know your taste.

## How exactly is the origin? From the land to the plot

Information about origin on packages varies in precision. The more accurate they are, the easier it is to understand where the coffee comes from and the easier it is for you to find it later.

| Information | Example | What she tells you |
|---|---|---|
| just the country | “Colombia” | rough direction, often a mixture of many parts |
| region | Huila, Sidama, Minas Gerais | Climate and altitude can be narrowed down |
| Cooperative or Washing Station | Processing station of a village | Cherries from many small farmers, processed together |
| farm | a specific finca or fazenda | one company, one manuscript |
| Microlot | a plot or a harvest day | small, separately processed quantity, often particularly pronounced |

How fine the information is also depends on the structure in the growing country. In Ethiopia, many small farmers usually deliver their cherries to a washing station, so an individual farm is rarely mentioned. In Kenya, cooperatives process their members' harvest in wet mills, which are called “factories” there. In Brazil, however, many coffees come from individual, sometimes large, farms. Precise information is not a must for quality, but it is a good sign that the roaster knows its supply chain. The section on Arabica varieties in [Arabica and Robusta](/ratgeber/arabica-und-robusta/) explains which variety is specified.

## Origin and preparation: what goes with what

Not every origin shows its best side in every preparation. The following overview is a starting point, not a law.

| Preparation | Matching origins | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Hand filter | Ethiopia, Kenya, Rwanda, Costa Rica | the paper filter emphasizes clarity, floral and fruity notes |
| French press | Brazil, Guatemala, Sumatra | full-bodied and chocolate match the oily, dense brew |
| Pure espresso | Brazil, Colombia, Peru | little to medium acidity, sweetness and chocolate remain pleasant even when concentrated |
| Espresso with milk | Brazil, Guatemala, Sumatra | Strong nut and cocoa notes prevail over milk |
| Espresso maker | Brazil, India, Sumatra | little acid, which makes the strong preparation appear more rounded |

Very acidic coffees from Kenya can quickly appear sharp in espresso, while earthy Sumatra can sometimes appear heavy in a hand filter. Both can be attractive if you like it that way. You can find information about the respective method in [French Press](/ratgeber/french-press-anleitung/) and [Espresso with the portafilter](/ratgeber/siebtraeger-espresso-grundlagen/).

## Harvest time and freshness of the green coffee

Green coffee has a long shelf life, but not indefinitely. Over the months it loses liveliness and can develop woody or papery notes. Good roasters therefore buy coffee from the current harvest and roast origins seasonally. When a roaster announces new coffees from Ethiopia or Kenya in the spring and fresh harvests from Central America in the fall, he is following this rhythm. What this means for you is that the best time for a particular origin coffee also depends on when it was harvested.

## Why the same coffee doesn't taste the same every year

Coffee is an agricultural product. Rain at the wrong time, a long dry period during flowering or unusual heat alter ripening, yield and taste. The work involved in harvesting also varies: Are only ripe cherries picked, or are unripe cherries included? Is it raining while the beans are drying? The same farm can therefore produce outstanding coffee one year and just decent coffee the next. If you particularly liked an origin coffee, the new harvest is a re-encounter, not a copy.

In the long term, climate change will put cultivation under pressure. Arabica is sensitive to heat, warmer conditions promote pests and diseases such as coffee rust, and suitable locations move to higher altitudes where there is not space for new plantings everywhere. Breeders and producers are therefore increasingly relying on more resilient plants, shade trees and adapted cultivation methods.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does “Single Origin” mean?

A coffee from a single origin, such as a country, region, cooperative or farm. In contrast, mixtures, so-called blends, which combine several origins, often represent espresso.

### What is a Microlot?

A small batch that is harvested and prepared separately, for example from a specific plot, a single variety or a special harvest day. Microlots are usually more expensive and sell out quickly. They're worth it if you want to try a coffee with a very unique style.

### When is coffee harvested?

That depends on the country. In many countries north of the equator the harvest occurs between October and March, and in Brazil between May and September. It usually takes a few months for green coffee to be roasted by us.

### Is expensive coffee automatically better?

Not automatically, but specialty coffees with traceable origins, careful harvesting and fresh roasting cost more because producers are paid more for quality. More about seals and fair prices can be found in [Seal for coffee and tea](/ratgeber/siegel-fairtrade-bio/).
