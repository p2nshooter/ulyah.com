Peppermint, chamomile, fennel, rose hip, hibiscus: There are more herbal and fruit teas in German tea cupboards than real teas. Strictly speaking, they are not teas at all because they do not come from the tea plant. Experts therefore speak of tea-like products or infusions. This doesn't matter for everyday life, but it does for preparation: herbs and fruits need different water and different steeping times than green or black tea. This guide explains the most popular varieties, how to infuse them safely and how to put together your own mixtures, including herbs from the garden or balcony.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Herbal and fruit teas do not come from the tea plant and naturally contain **no caffeine**, with exceptions such as mate.
> - Always pour over **sparkling boiling water** and let it steep **covered** for at least **5 to 10 minutes**. This dissolves aromas and renders possible germs harmless.
> - The following applies to **children** and pregnant women: variety instead of always the same type, and if you have any questions, seek medical advice.
> - Fruit tea often relies on **hibiscus** and **rose hips**, which give it color and acidity.
> - Store dried herbs in an airtight, dark and dry place and use them within about a year.

## Why boiling water is important

Herbs and fruits are natural products that grow in the field and are dried without being heated. As with all plant-based foods, germs can be found on them. In studies, pathogens such as salmonella have occasionally been detected in dried herbs and fruits. Consumer protection authorities therefore recommend pouring **sparkling boiling water** onto herbal and fruit teas and letting them steep for **at least five minutes**. This kills possible germs.

Hot water is also good for taste reasons: many aromatic substances in roots, seeds and dried fruits only dissolve at high temperatures and after a long time. **Covering** keeps the essential oils in the cup instead of letting them escape with the steam. You can find an overview of all brewing times in [Tea: Brewing times and temperatures](/ratgeber/tee-ziehzeiten-und-temperaturen/).

For **cold herbal or fruit tea**, which is popular in summer, the following applies: first brew with boiling water and then cool, instead of just putting the herbs in cold water. More about this in [Make your own iced tea](/ratgeber/eistee-selbst-machen/).

## Quantities and brewing times at a glance

How much you take and how long the infusion takes depends primarily on the part of the plant. Delicate leaves and flowers release their aroma more quickly than hard seeds, peels or roots.

| plant part | Examples | Quantity per 250 ml | Brewing time |
|---|---|---|---|
| leaves | Peppermint, lemon balm, nettle | 1 to 2 teaspoons dried | 5 to 10 minutes |
| flowers | Chamomile, lime blossoms | 1 to 2 teaspoons | 5 to 10 minutes |
| seeds | Fennel, anise, caraway | 1 teaspoon, lightly crushed | about 10 minutes |
| fruits and peels | Rosehip, apple, hibiscus | 1 heaped tablespoon | 8 to 12 minutes |
| roots | Ginger, freshly sliced | 4 to 6 thin slices | about 10 minutes |

The values are starting points. If a tea is too strong for you, use less herb instead of shortening the brewing time to less than five minutes.

Herbal teas are traditionally drunk for various conditions. If you want to drink tea because of health problems, discuss this with a doctor or at the pharmacy. Medicinal teas from the pharmacy are tested differently than food teas from the supermarket.

## Variety instead of constant consumption

Some herbs contain natural substances that are undesirable in large quantities over a long period of time. Plant poisons such as **pyrrolizidine alkaloids**, which can come from wild herbs that were harvested, have also occasionally been found in herbal teas. Authorities and manufacturers are working to reduce levels. For you this means above all: **Alternate.** Don't drink liters of just one type for months, especially not with children, during pregnancy or while breastfeeding. Alternating between different herbs, fruits and real tea reduces possible risks.

## fruit tea

Fruit tea usually consists of dried apple pieces, rose hips, hibiscus flowers, elderberries, orange peels and other fruits. Many also contain flavors.

- **Hibiscus:** The sepals of the hibiscus flower color the tea bright red and give a strong acidity reminiscent of cranberry. In West Africa, Egypt and Mexico, a drink is made from it, often cold and sweetened.
- **Rose hip:** fruity-sour, slightly mealy, a classic German tea.
- **Apple:** provides sweetness and body, often the base of fruit blends.

Pay attention to the list of ingredients: In some fruit teas, the eponymous fruit is placed far back and the taste comes primarily from the aroma. A tea without added flavors tastes more natural but more reserved.

## Loose, in a bag or in a pyramid

Herbs and fruits are available loose, in classic paper bags and in larger pyramid bags. For bags, the plants are usually cut finer so that they grow out quickly. This is practical, but the fine pieces lose their aroma more quickly. Loose goods with recognizable leaves, flowers and pieces of fruit show you better what you are buying and can be dosed to suit your taste. Pyramid bags are in between: they often contain coarser pieces and give them room to swell. The same thing is important for all forms: boiling water, a lid on and enough time.

## Put together your own mixtures

Mixing is fun and easy. Start with a basic ingredient that makes up about half of it and supplement it with two to three companions.

| base | companion | idea |
|---|---|---|
| Peppermint | Melissa, lemon verbena | fresh and lemony for the summer |
| Chamomile | Lavender in small quantities, lemon balm | gentle for the evening |
| Apple | Cinnamon, orange peel, clove | warm for the winter |
| Hibiscus | rosehip, ginger | strong, red, sour |
| Rooibos | Vanilla, orange | sweet, see [Rooibos and Honeybush](/ratgeber/rooibos-und-honeybush/) |

Write down the quantities. This way you can repeat a successful mixture.

## Dry herbs yourself

Herbs from the garden or balcony taste wonderful when freshly brewed. You can dry them for the winter:

1. **Harvest** late morning on a dry day when the dew has dried off, just before or at the beginning of flowering.
2. **Bundle** and hang upside down in an airy, shady place or spread the leaves out on a trellis. No direct sun, otherwise they will lose color and aroma.
3. They are **dry enough** when the leaves rustle and break easily.
4. **Fill** into dark, airtight jars. Grind whole leaves just before infusion.

Only collect wild herbs if you can identify them with certainty. Some poisonous plants look very similar to edible herbs.

## Pour fresh herbs directly

In summer you don't need tea bags: a handful of fresh mint, lemon balm or lemon verbena, rinsed and lightly pressed, makes a fragrant tea. Use about twice to three times as much fresh herb as dried, because fresh leaves contain a lot of water. Pour boiling water over the herbs in the pot, cover them and let them steep for five to ten minutes. Fresh mint tastes softer and greener than dried mint, which is stronger and more menthol-like. You should only use herbs from the balcony if they have not been treated with pesticides that are not approved for edible plants.

## Typical mistakes

| problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Tea doesn't taste like much | too little herb, pulled too short, herbs old | Take more, steep for ten minutes, buy fresh produce |
| Tea tastes bitter | Sage, thyme or peels have been pulled for too long | steep shorter or mix with milder herbs |
| Aroma evaporates | Cup not covered | Place saucer or lid on |
| Fruit tea very sour | lots of hibiscus | Mix with apple or rooibos, a little honey |
| Herbs musty | stored moist | Throw it away and store it airtight and dry in the future |

## Storage

Dried herbs and fruits gradually lose their essential oils. Stored airtight, dark, dry and away from spices, they last well for about a year. If they hardly smell anymore, it's time for new ones. More in [Store coffee and tea properly](/ratgeber/kaffee-und-tee-lagern/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I brew herbal tea with water below 100 degrees?

For safety reasons, boiling water is recommended. The risk is lower with fresh herbs from your own garden that you have washed thoroughly, but hot water also brings the best aroma.

### Does herbal tea contain caffeine?

Pure herbal and fruit teas are caffeine-free. Exceptions are mate, guayusa and blends that contain real tea. More in [Caffeine in Coffee and Tea](/ratgeber/koffein-in-kaffee-und-tee/).

### Can I brew herbal tea several times?

A second infusion is possible, but usually tastes much weaker. Fresh herbs produce a good second infusion more often than dried ones.

### Does acidic fruit tea damage your teeth?

Hibiscus and rose hips make fruit tea quite sour. Drinking it sips throughout the day often exposes tooth enamel to acid. It is better to drink it at fixed times, then rinse with water and wait about half an hour before brushing your teeth.

### When do I add honey to tea?

This is primarily a matter of taste. If you want to taste the delicate aroma of honey, only stir it in when the tea is warm to drink. Babies in their first year of life should generally not be given honey, not even in tea.

## Conclusion

Herbal and fruit teas are versatile, caffeine-free and easy to mix yourself. With boiling water, a covered cup, sufficient steeping time and variety between the varieties, you can get the best out of them, whether from the pack, from the balcony or from your own garden.
