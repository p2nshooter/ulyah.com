The hand filter is perhaps the most underrated coffee method of all. A porcelain or plastic cone, a paper filter and hot water, that's all you need. And yet a carefully brewed filter coffee can taste so clear and complex that you can almost taste the origin of the bean. The method has a tradition in Germany: in 1908, Melitta Bentz in Dresden had her coffee filter protected with a paper insert. Today there are many shapes, from the classic Melitta trapezoid to the conical V60 to the flat bottom filter. In this guide, you'll learn how to brew a good cup with each of them.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Starting recipe: 15 g coffee to 250 g water, i.e. 60 g per liter.
> - Water temperature 92 to 96 °C, grind medium fine, roughly like coarse sand.
> - First brew with twice the amount of coffee water and let it soak for 30 to 45 seconds.
> - The total brewing time is usually 2½ to 4 minutes, depending on the filter shape and quantity.
> - Sour and thin: grind finer. Bitter and dry: grind more coarsely.

## What you need

The basic equipment is manageable and inexpensive:

- **Filter holder:** conical (for example V60 or similar cones with a large hole), trapezoidal with small holes (the classic Melitta type) or with a flat bottom (for example Kalita Wave and similar).
- **Matching paper filters.** White, oxygen-bleached filters release less of their own flavor than unbleached brown ones.
- **Freshly roasted coffee** in whole beans and a grinder with grinder.
- **Kettle,** ideally with a gooseneck spout for a thin, easily controllable stream of water.
- **Kitchen scale** with 0.1 gram increments and a clock or timer.
- **jug or cup** as a collecting vessel.

Scales and gooseneck kettles aren't mandatory, but they do make your coffee repeatable. If you measure coffee with spoons, you are measuring inaccurately because, depending on the degree of roasting and the size of the beans, a heaped spoon weighs between 5 and 8 grams.

## The basic recipe

For a large cup:

| Value | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Coffee | 15g |
| water | 250g (ml) |
| ratio | 1:16.7 (60g/l) |
| Grinding degree | medium fine |
| Temperature | 92-96°C |
| Pre-infusion | 30-45g water, 30-45 seconds |
| Total time | about 2½–3½ min. (cone), 3–4 min. (trapezoid, flat bottom) |

The Specialty Coffee Association cites a golden range for filter coffee of around 55g per liter, with room for improvement up and down. Many roasters recommend 60 to 65 g per liter. For two cups, simply double: 30 g of coffee to 500 g of water. The brewing time is a little longer because there is more ground coffee in the filter.

## Step by step

1. **Heat water.** Bring a little more water to a boil than you need. If your stove does not have a temperature selection, let the water stand briefly after boiling.
2. **Insert and rinse the filter.** Insert the paper filter, fold over the seams on the trapezoidal filter beforehand, and pour hot water through it. This rinses out any paper taste and preheats the filter and pot. Throw away this water.
3. **Grind coffee.** Grind 15g of beans right before brewing. Ground coffee loses volatile aromas within minutes.
4. **Pour in ground coffee.** Pour the flour into the filter and tap the holder lightly so that the surface is level. Put everything on the scales and tare them.
5. **Pre-scalding (blooming).** Pour about 30 to 45 g of water in a circular motion so that all the flour is wetted. The coffee bed rises and bubbles: freshly roasted coffee releases carbon dioxide. Wait 30 to 45 seconds.
6. **Main infusion.** Pour in slow circles from the center outwards and back again until you reach 250g. Don't pour directly onto the edge of the paper, otherwise water will run past the coffee. You can water in one go or in two to three stages.
7. **Let it run through.** At the end, gently swirl the filter or gently stir once to form a flat bed of coffee. Then let everything play out.
8. **Try.** Remove the filter, swirl the jug briefly and pour. Let the coffee cool for a minute or two; At around 60 °C you can taste acidity and sweetness most clearly.

### Why pre-infusion is important

Freshly roasted coffee contains a lot of carbon dioxide. If you pour the entire amount of water straight away, the escaping gas will displace the water and some areas of the coffee grounds will be less leached. During pre-infusion, a large part of the gas escapes, after which the water can extract more evenly. With very old coffee there is hardly any bubbling, this is a simple test for freshness.

## A watering plan to follow

A set routine helps, especially at the beginning, so that you can concentrate on watering. This is what a plan looks like for a cup in a cone filter, with 15 g of coffee and 250 g of water. The scale runs from the first drop.

| time | What you do | Libra shows |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | Pre-scald, wet all the flour | 40g |
| 0:40 | first infusion, slowly circling from the inside out | 150g |
| 1:10 | second infusion as soon as the water level has dropped slightly | 250g |
| about 1:30 | Swirl the filter gently to level the bed | 250g |
| 2:30 to 3:00 | last drop, remove filter | – |

Two infusions instead of one keep the water level in the filter even. The coffee grounds are never dry, but neither do they float in an overfilled cone. With a trapezoidal filter, the water drains more slowly; There you pour in three smaller stages and expect a total time of around three to four minutes. It's less important that you hit the seconds exactly, but rather that you repeat the process the same way every time. Only then can changes to the grinding level be meaningfully assessed.

## Filter shapes in comparison

The shape of the filter determines how quickly the water drains and how much control you have.

| shape | drain | Typical character | For whom |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tapered, large hole (V60 type) | fast, depends heavily on the casting technique | clear, bright, emphasizes acidity | who likes to fine-tune |
| Trapeze with small holes (Melitta type) | braked, evenly | rounder, slightly more body | who likes it uncomplicated |
| Flat bottom (wave type) | evenly, forgives mistakes | balanced, sweet | Beginners and frequent brewers |

For the trapezoidal filter you can grind a little coarser than for the V60 because the small holes slow down the flow anyway.

## Adjust the taste

The most important lever is the grind. It determines how much surface area the water can attack and how quickly it passes through.

- **Sour, salty, thin, brewing time very short:** The coffee is under-extracted. Grind a little finer.
- **Bitter, dry, scratchy, brewing time very long:** The coffee is over-extracted. Grind a little coarser.
- **Balanced but too weak:** Use more coffee, for example 16 or 17g to 250g.
- **Balanced but too intense:** Use less coffee or dilute the finished cup with a little hot water.

Change one thing at a time and write down what you did. After three or four tries you will know your bean.

### Example: in three attempts to find the right setting

This is what setting up a new, light roasted bean can look like. The recipe and watering plan remain the same, only one value is changed at a time.

| Try | Change | pass | impression | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Starting recipe, medium grinding level | 2:05 | sharply sour, watery | grind finer |
| 2 | two levels finer | 2:50 | sweeter, round, but a little weak | Increase dose |
| 3 | 16 g instead of 15 g, grinding level as with 2 | 2:55 | sweet, clear, full body | maintained like this |

The first attempt went through too quickly, a typical sign of flour that was too coarse. With the finer grind, the extraction was correct, only the starch was missing. If the second cup had tasted bitter and dry, the correction would have been a step back, not less coffee. Write down the setting for each bean so that you hardly have to readjust it the next time you buy the same variety.

### Temperature as a finishing touch

Light roasts are denser and harder to separate. They tolerate hot water well around 94 to 96 °C. Dark roasts release their bitter substances more easily; Here you often get a rounder cup at 88 to 92 °C.

## More than a cup

The same principle works for two to four cups, you just have to adjust a few things:

| cups | Coffee | water | Pre-infusion | Total time (guideline) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 15g | 250g | 30-45g | 2½–3½ mins. |
| 2 | 30g | 500g | 60-75g | 3½–4½ mins. |
| 3 | 45g | 750g | 90-100g | 4-5 mins |

With larger quantities, the coffee bed becomes higher and the water takes longer to flow through. Therefore, grind one to two levels coarser than for a single cup, otherwise the brewing time will be too long and the coffee will be bitter. Pour in several stages, about three to four equal portions, and keep the water level in the filter as constant as possible. Also make sure that your filter holder is large enough: a size 01 cone is intended for one to two cups, for three to four you need size 02.

## The most common mistakes

- **Grinding too fine:** The flour clogs the filter, the water stands for minutes, the coffee becomes bitter.
- **Boiling water to dark roast:** The result is often a sharp bitterness.
- **Water on the edge of the filter:** It runs past the coffee and the cup becomes thin.
- **Old ground coffee:** Pre-ground coffee from a package that has been open for weeks loses a lot of flavor.
- **Hard tap water:** Very hard water makes coffee dull. More about this in the guide [Water hardness for coffee and tea](/ratgeber/wasserhaerte-fuer-kaffee-und-tee/).

## Hand filter or filter machine?

A good filter machine delivers consistent results on large quantities and requires no attention. However, many cheap machines do not reach the ideal temperature or distribute the water unevenly. Machines with a certification from the European Coffee Brewing Center or the Specialty Coffee Association are tested for brewing temperature and brewing time. For one or two cups, the hand filter is more flexible, cheaper and allows you to optimally adjust each bean. The basic ratio of around 60g per liter applies to both.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I really have to rinse the paper filter?

It is not mandatory, but recommended. Unbleached filters in particular can give off a papery aftertaste. Rinsing also preheats the filter holder and pot so that the coffee does not cool down too quickly.

### Which coffee is suitable for the hand filter?

Basically everyone, but light to medium roasts show their strengths here: acidity, fruit and sweetness come through particularly clearly. Many roasters offer their own filter roasting. Espresso roasts also work, but often taste stronger and bitterer when filtered.

### How fine is “medium fine”?

Something like coarse sand or table sugar. If the flour feels like powder, it is too fine; If it looks like coarse sea salt, it's more for the French press. More about this in the guide [Setting the grinding level correctly] (/ratgeber/mahlgrad-richtig-einstellen/).

### Is it possible without a gooseneck boiler?

Yes. First pour the hot water from the regular kettle into a milk jug or measuring cup with a pointed spout. This makes it much easier to direct a thin stream. Since the water cools down slightly when decanting, use it immediately after boiling. It's better to pour in more small portions rather than one large one. Trapezoidal and flat-bottom filters are more forgiving of an unsteady hand than a cone with a large hole.

### Can I keep filter coffee warm?

Best in a preheated thermos. Coffee on a warming plate loses a lot of its flavor after around 20 to 30 minutes and becomes bitter.
