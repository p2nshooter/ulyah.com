Anyone who visits East Frisia will sooner or later be offered a cup of tea, and with it a little ritual: a piece of rock candy crackles in the cup when the hot tea runs over it, a spoonful of cream sinks down the edge and rises again as a white cloud. There is no stirring. East Frisia is one of the regions with the highest tea consumption per capita in the world, and East Frisian tea culture has been on the nationwide register of intangible cultural heritage since 2016. This guide explains how the ceremony works, what tea is included and how you can recreate it at home.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - A very **strong black tea blend** is drunk, mainly from **Assam**.
> - First a piece of **Kluntje**, white rock sugar, goes into the empty cup, then the hot tea, then a spoonful of **cream** on the edge of the cup.
> - **Do not stir.** You can taste the mild cream, the strong tea and finally the sweetness of the rock candy one after the other.
> - It is polite to drink **at least three cups**. If you've had enough, put the spoon in the cup.
> - The pot stands on a **warmer** with a tea light and stays warm.

## How tea came to East Frisia

Tea came to East Frisia, which was culturally and economically closely linked to the Netherlands, in the 17th century via Dutch traders. At first it was an expensive drink for the wealthy, but over time it became an everyday drink for everyone. In the damp, windy coastal landscape, hot, strong tea became an integral part of the daily routine. Over the centuries, people developed their own blends, their own dishes and their own way of drinking tea. Tea trading houses in East Frisia still put together their own East Frisian blends today.

## Tea and authority

A chapter from the 18th century shows how much tea belongs to the region. After East Frisia fell to Prussia, the Prussian administration tried several times to restrict tea consumption because a lot of money was flowing out of the country for imported tea; the population should rather drink local beer. The attempts failed due to resistance from the East Frisians, and the tea remained. Stories like these are still often told on the coast today when it comes to why tea is more than just a drink here.

## The tea

An East Frisian blend is strong, malty and dark. It consists predominantly of **Assam**, often supplemented by teas from other growing regions. It is important that the tea can contain cream and rock candy and also tastes good with the typical water of the region. The cream would cover a fine Darjeeling. More about the tea types can be found in [Black tea types](/ratgeber/schwarztee-sorten/).

## The dishes

- **Teapot** made of porcelain, often with the well-known pattern of the **East Frisian rose**, a red rose decoration.
- **Tea pot:** a small frame with a tea light on which the pot stays warm.
- **Small, thin-walled cups** with a saucer, because East Frisian tea is drunk in small quantities.
- **cream jug** and a **small cream spoon**, sometimes a cream ladle, the “Rohmlepel”.
- **Kluntjedose** with pliers.

Traditionally, the pot is preheated before pouring hot water. Many families do not wash their teapots with detergent so that the taste of the tea does not suffer. More about pots and accessories can be found in [Tea Accessories](/ratgeber/teezubehoer/).

## The preparation step by step

1. **Preheat the pot:** Rinse the teapot with hot water.
2. **Pour the tea:** Take about **one heaped teaspoon per cup** and an extra “for the pot”. East Frisian tea is prepared vigorously.
3. **Pour:** First pour just enough boiling water to cover the leaves and let them swell briefly. Then fill with boiling water.
4. **Let it steep:** About **three to four minutes**, depending on the mixture and taste.
5. **Pouring:** Many people pour the tea through a sieve into a second, preheated pot so that it doesn't become bitter. This pot goes on the warmer.

[Tea: Brewing Times and Temperatures](/ratgeber/tee-ziehzeiten-und-temperaturen/) explains how long black tea should steep and why it becomes bitterer the longer it is steeped.

## The ceremony in the cup

1. **Kluntje:** Use the tongs to place a piece of white rock candy in the empty cup.
2. **Tea:** Pour the hot tea over the Kluntje. It crackles and cracks because the rock candy cracks due to the sudden heat. Don't fill the cup completely.
3. **Cream:** Use the cream spoon to carefully slide a spoonful of liquid cream into the tea along the edge of the cup, **counterclockwise**, as many East Frisians say, so that “time stands still”. The cream first sinks and then rises as a cloud, the “Wulkje”.
4. **Do not stir:** Drink the tea in layers. First the mild cream, then the strong tea, and finally the sweet rest.
5. **Refill:** The rock candy is often enough for several cups. The hostess refills while the cup is empty.

## Tea times and customs

In many East Frisian households there are fixed tea times, for example in the morning and afternoon, as well as tea for breakfast and in the evening. Afternoon tea often includes cakes or pastries.

A few unwritten rules apply to guests:

- **Three cups are East Frisian law.** Drinking less can be considered impolite, but more is always welcome.
- **Spoon in the cup:** If you don't want any more tea, put the spoon in the empty cup. Otherwise it will be refilled.
- **Do not stir:** The spoon is not for stirring.
- **Take your time:** Drinking tea is conversation time, not a quick drink in between.

## East Frisian tea at home

You don't need all the dishes to try it out. A jug, small cups, white rock candy and liquid cream are enough. A suitable, strong mixture is important. If the water is very hard, a film easily forms on black tea and the aroma becomes duller. Filtered or softer water helps, see [Water hardness for coffee and tea](/ratgeber/wasserhaerte-fuer-kaffee-und-tee/). Use real cream with around 30 percent fat. Coffee cream or milk doesn't form a nice cloud.

## Plan tea for guests

When visitors come, some planning is worthwhile. The classic cups are small, often well under 150 milliliters, and every guest drinks at least three of them. So for four people you will quickly need one and a half liters of tea. It's better to prepare it in two batches instead of leaving a huge pot on the warmer for a long time, because even poured tea becomes flatter over time. Have kluntje, cream and a small bowl for used spoons ready, and have hot water in reserve in case someone wants the tea a little milder.

## East Frisian tea today

The tradition is alive, but changing. Many households today drink East Frisian blends from tea bags, and in offices there is more of a thermos than a warmer. At the same time, there are tea rooms and cafés in the region that consciously practice the ceremony, and many younger East Frisians carry the habit with them to other cities. At festivals, on Sundays and when there are visitors, rose porcelain, Kluntje and cream are still on the table.

## What's for tea

Afternoon tea in East Frisia often includes something sweet or baked. Popular is **Krintstuut**, a raisin mare that is sliced ​​and eaten with butter. There are also butter cakes, rusks, biscuits and, in winter, pastries with spices. In some places, at family celebrations, **Bohntjesopp** is served, raisins soaked in brandy and rock candy and spooned out of small bowls. However, they are more for special occasions than for everyday life, and of course not suitable for children.

## Experience tea culture

If you want to find out more about the history of tea on the coast, you will find tea museums in East Frisia, for example in **Norden** and **Leer**. The history of the tea trade is told there, historical tableware is shown, and you can often take part in a tea ceremony. Many cafés in the region also serve East Frisian-style tea with warmers, Kluntje and cream. Once you have experienced the ceremony on site, it will be even easier to do at home.

## Typical mistakes

| problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| no cloud of cream | Milk or coffee cream instead of cream, stirred | Use liquid whipped cream, do not stir |
| Tea bitter | Steeped too long, pot not drained | After three to four minutes, pour into a second jug |
| Tea too thin | too little tea, fine instead of strong mixture | more tea, Assam blend |
| Rock candy doesn't crackle | Tea not hot enough | Pour tea fresh and hot |
| Tea cold | no warmer | Use a warmer or tea warmer |

## Caffeine and rock candy

East Frisian tea is strong, but the cups are small. If you drink several cups throughout the day, you will still get a considerable amount of caffeine. More about this in [Caffeine in Coffee and Tea](/ratgeber/koffein-in-kaffee-und-tee/). The rock candy also adds up. If you want less sugar, take smaller pieces and serve them over several cups.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why shouldn't you stir?

Because the tea is drunk in layers. The cream at the top, the strong tea in the middle and the sweet rock candy at the bottom create a different taste with every sip.

### Can I use brown rock candy?

Traditionally, white rock candy is used, the Kluntje. Brown rock candy tastes more caramel and changes the aroma, some people like that too.

### Is there East Frisian tea without caffeine?

There are decaffeinated black teas that are similarly strong. They don't taste the same, but they are an option for the evening.

### Is East Frisian tea in a tea bag suitable for the ceremony?

Yes. Tea bags usually contain finely broken leaves that extract faster and more powerfully. Therefore, let them grow for a shorter time and take them out in good time. Loose leaf tea gives you more control over strength and steeping time, but the ceremony itself works with both.

## Conclusion

East Frisian tea is a ritual of hospitality: strong tea, crackling Kluntje, a cloud of cream and time for each other. With a good Assam mixture, real cream and small cups, you can bring a piece of the coast into your home, no matter where you live.
