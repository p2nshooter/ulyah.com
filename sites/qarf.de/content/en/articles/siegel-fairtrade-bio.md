Coffee and tea grow almost exclusively in countries in the global south, often on small farms and tea gardens whose families depend on fluctuating world market prices. At the same time, cultivation has consequences for soil, water and forests. When purchasing, many people therefore want to pay attention to the conditions under which their coffee and tea were produced. Promise help seal. But there are many logos on the packs, and not all of them mean the same thing. Some concern organic farming, others fair prices and working conditions, others are purely marketing terms. This guide explains the most important seals, what they guarantee and what their limits are.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - **Organic seals** stand for ecological cultivation without chemical-synthetic pesticides and mineral fertilizers. The **EU organic logo** is mandatory for packaged organic food in the EU. **Growing associations** such as Naturland or Demeter have stricter rules.
> - **Fairtrade** primarily stands for a **minimum price**, an additional **premium** for community projects and standards for working conditions.
> - **Rainforest Alliance** focuses on environmental protection, agriculture and working conditions, but does not guarantee a minimum price.
> - **Direct Trade** is not a protected term. It describes direct relationships between roasters and producers and thrives on transparency.
> - Terms like “fair”, “sustainable” or “responsible” without a seal are **not protected**.

## Why seals play a role in coffee and tea

The price of green coffee is formed on the stock exchange and fluctuates greatly. In some years it is below what many small farmers have to spend on cultivation. In tea cultivation, wages on plantations are also very low in some regions. There are also ecological questions: Coffee is sometimes grown on cleared forest areas, and intensive cultivation puts a strain on the soil and water. Seals are intended to help consumers recognize products that meet certain standards. They are controlled by independent bodies, although with varying degrees of rigor.

## Organic seal

### EU organic logo and German organic seal

The green **EU organic logo** with the stars in the shape of a leaf is mandatory for packaged organic food in the EU. The voluntary hexagonal **German organic seal** may stand next to it. Both mean the same thing: the product meets the requirements of the EU organic regulation. For coffee and tea this means, among other things:

- no chemical-synthetic pesticides
- no mineral nitrogen fertilizers
- no genetic engineering
- regular checks by approved inspection bodies whose code is on the packaging

Imported organic goods must also meet these or equivalent standards.

### Growing Associations

Associations such as **Naturland**, **Demeter** or **Bioland** have their own guidelines, which are stricter in many respects than the EU requirements. Naturland is particularly widespread internationally in coffee and tea cultivation and partly combines ecological requirements with social criteria. Demeter stands for biodynamic agriculture.

**What organic does not guarantee:** a fair price for the producers. There are separate seals for this.

## Fairtrade

The **Fairtrade seal** is the best-known social seal in Germany. For coffee and tea it essentially means:

- **Minimum price:** Producer organizations receive at least a fixed price, even if the world market price falls below it. If the market price is higher, the market price applies.
- **Fairtrade premium:** In addition to the price, a premium is paid, which the cooperative or the employees decide democratically on how to use it, for example for schools, health care, quality improvement or switching to organic.
- **Standards:** Prohibition of exploitative child labor and forced labor, freedom of assembly, environmental regulations.
- **Organization:** Coffee comes predominantly from small farmer cooperatives, tea also comes from plantations with employed employees.

Many Fairtrade coffees and teas are also certified organic. Then they both bear seals.

**Limits:** The minimum price protects against low points, but does not automatically make you rich. Critics point out that part of a cooperative's production is often sold under normal conditions because there is not enough Fairtrade demand. Nevertheless, Fairtrade is considered one of the more reliable social seals.

## Rainforest Alliance

The green frog seal is very common on coffee and tea, even among large brands. The **Rainforest Alliance** merged with the UTZ organization in 2018. Your standards concern:

- Protecting forests and ecosystems, preventing deforestation
- sustainable agriculture, soil and water conservation
- Working conditions and rights of employees
- Improving living conditions in the farming communities

**Difference to Fairtrade:** The Rainforest Alliance guarantees **no minimum price**. However, there is a mandatory payment that producers receive in addition to the market price. The seal is not an organic seal; chemical plant protection is restricted but not completely prohibited.

## Direct trade and specialty coffee

Many small roasters buy their green coffee directly from farms or cooperatives or through specialized importers with whom they work long-term. This is often what the term **Direct Trade** stands for. It is not protected and does not have a uniform seal. Good roasters therefore disclose which farm the coffee comes from, what they paid for it and what the partnership looks like. Some publish the prices paid in transparency reports. Due to its quality, specialty coffee often achieves significantly higher prices than stock market coffee. You can only judge whether this is received by the producers through transparency. More about origins and quality can be found in [Coffee-growing countries and their taste](/ratgeber/kaffee-anbaulaender/).

## The seals in comparison

| Seal | Focus | Minimum price | Organic | Control |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| EU organic, German organic seal | organic farming | no | yes | state-approved inspection bodies |
| Naturland, Demeter | ecological cultivation, partly social criteria | depending on the association | yes, stricter | Association controls |
| Fairtrade | fair prices, bonuses, labor rights | yes | not mandatory | independent certification |
| Rainforest Alliance | Environment, agriculture, labor rights | no | no | independent certification |
| Direct Trade | direct trading relationship | individually | not mandatory | no uniform control |

## Terms without seals

Words like “fairly traded”, “sustainably grown”, “responsibly sourced” or “controlled cultivation” sound good, but are **not protected** without an independent seal. For example, “from controlled cultivation” does not mean organic. Company logos and programs can be useful, but are usually not controlled by independent bodies. If a brand advertises sustainability, it is worth taking a look at whether it provides specific information.

## This is how you shop consciously

1. **Set priorities:** If environmental protection is particularly important to you, pay attention to organic and association seals. If you care about income and working conditions, Fairtrade. Both together are also often available.
2. **Use combinations:** Organic plus Fairtrade covers ecological and social aspects.
3. **Reward transparency:** Roasteries and tea dealers who disclose origin, farm and prices allow you to make your own assessment.
4. **Less but Better:** High quality coffee and tea cost more. If you enjoy things consciously and throw away less, you can partially compensate for this. How not to waste anything can be found in [Store coffee and tea properly](/ratgeber/kaffee-und-tee-lagern/).

## Special features of tea

In tea cultivation, in addition to small farmers, many employed pickers work on large plantations, for example in India, Sri Lanka or Kenya. For such companies, Fairtrade has its own standards for employees, which regulate, among other things, co-determination of the bonus. Tea plays a special role in organic cultivation: unlike coffee, whose beans grow in a fruit and are roasted, the tea leaf is brewed directly. Many tea drinkers therefore pay particular attention to organic quality when it comes to tea. When it comes to herbal and fruit teas, it is also worth taking a look at the origin of the individual ingredients, which often come from many countries.

## What's in a pack of coffee

Part of the price has nothing to do with the cultivation. In Germany, a coffee tax of 2.19 euros per kilogram is levied on roasted coffee, and 4.78 euros per kilogram on soluble coffee, plus VAT. For a 500 gram pack, that's around 1.10 euros in coffee tax alone. If you deduct taxes, transport, roasting, packaging and trading, there is little left for the green coffee and therefore for the producers with very cheap offers. This explains why fairly paid coffee is rarely available at discount prices.

## New rules against deforestation

Coffee is one of the raw materials covered by the EU regulation on deforestation-free products. Companies that market coffee in the EU will in future have to prove that it does not come from areas that were deforested after the end of 2020. The start of application has already been postponed and details are still being discussed. For consumers, the regulation does not replace seals: it primarily concerns deforestation, not prices or working conditions. Tea is not covered by this regulation.

## Capsules and pads with seal

Capsules and pads are also available with an organic or fair trade seal. The seals then refer to the coffee, not the packaging. References such as “compostable” often mean that the capsule is broken down in industrial composting facilities. Many municipalities do not accept such capsules in the organic waste bin. So check what your municipality allows.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does organic coffee taste better?

Not automatically. The taste depends on the origin, variety, harvest, preparation and roasting. Organic says something about the cultivation method, not about the taste quality.

### Why is Fairtrade coffee sometimes not more expensive?

The minimum price is particularly effective when the world market price is low. Many fair trade coffees in the supermarket also compete on price. The premium is often only a small amount per pack.

### Are there seals for rooibos and herbal tea?

Yes, rooibos and herbal teas are also available with organic and fair trade seals, see [Rooibos and Honeybush](/ratgeber/rooibos-und-honeybush/).

### How do I recognize empty sustainability promises?

Caution is advised if a package only uses general terms such as “sustainable” or “responsible” but does not carry an independent seal or provide specific information. Credible providers name the origin, cooperative or farm, explain their purchasing practices and have themselves checked by independent bodies.

### Is a seal worthwhile even for small quantities?

Yes. Every pack counts, and especially with products that you drink every day, the effect adds up over the year. If you only buy coffee or tea occasionally, you can also choose particularly transparent providers.

## Conclusion

No seal covers everything. Organic stands for cultivation, Fairtrade for prices and labor rights, the Rainforest Alliance for environmental and social standards without minimum prices, Direct Trade for transparency in the supply chain. If you know what a logo stands for, you can choose consciously and support providers who back up their promises.
