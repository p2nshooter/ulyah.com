Hardly anything determines whether a tea is successful as temperature and brewing time. The same Sencha can taste sweet and fresh or bitter and scratchy, depending on whether you let it steep for one minute at 70°C or for five minutes with boiling water. With herbal tea it is the other way around: boiling water not only ensures taste, but also safety. In this guide you will find the guideline values ​​for all important types of tea and find out why they are so different.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - The less oxidized a tea is, the cooler the water: green tea 60 to 80 °C, white tea 75 to 90 °C, oolong 85 to 95 °C, black tea 95 to 100 °C.
> - Always pour herbal and fruit teas with boiling water and let them steep, covered, for at least 5 minutes.
> - The basic amount is around 1 teaspoon or 2 to 3 grams of tea in 200 to 250 milliliters of water.
> - Bitterness is caused primarily by water that is too hot and the steeping time is too long.
> - You can brew high-quality green teas, oolongs and pu-erhs several times.

## Why temperature is so important

Tea leaves contain different groups of substances that dissolve at different speeds:

- **Flavourings** and **amino acids** such as L-theanine dissolve well even at low temperatures. They provide sweetness, freshness and umami.
- **Caffeine** dissolves quickly, especially in hot water and in the first few minutes.
- **Catechins and tannins** are responsible for tartness, bitterness and the dry feeling in the mouth. They go into the infusion with hot water and increasingly as time goes on.

Green and white teas contain many unoxidized catechins, which quickly become bitter. That's why they need cooler water. In black tea, the catechins have been converted into larger molecules such as theaflavins and thearubigins through oxidation. They taste less spicy and the tea needs hot water to develop its full aroma.

## Herbal and fruit tea: boiling and covered

A separate rule applies to herbal and fruit teas. These teas consist of dried plant parts that can come into contact with germs in the field and during drying. In rare cases, salmonella has been found in herbal teas. The Federal Institute for Risk Assessment therefore recommends always pouring boiling water into herbal and fruit teas and letting them steep for at least five minutes. This is especially true if small children, pregnant women, older people or people with weakened immune systems drink.

A lid on a cup or pot has a second advantage: essential oils, such as peppermint or chamomile, otherwise evaporate with the steam. Put any drops that collect on the lid back into the cup.

## Without a thermometer: it still works

Not everyone has a kettle with a temperature selector. These tricks help:

1. **Let it cool:** Boil the water and let it stand with the lid open. How quickly it cools depends heavily on the quantity and container. A simple kitchen thermometer shows you how long your cooker needs, then you know.
2. **Pouring:** In Japan, boiling water is poured into the cups first and then into the pot. Each pour into a cold vessel lowers the temperature by approximately 5 to 10 °C. This way you can preheat the cups at the same time.
3. **Add cold water:** One part cold to four parts boiling water gives a rough temperature of 80°C. This is a simple mixed calculation and works surprisingly well.
4. **Don't guess by bubbles:** The old rules with fisheye and crabeye bubbles are charming, but inaccurate because they depend on the pot.

## Pour cold

Real tea can also be prepared cold, and the result is a discovery: because cold water hardly dissolves tannins, the tea becomes sweet, soft and almost never bitter. Add about 8 to 10 grams of green tea, white tea or light oolong to one liter of cold water and let everything steep in the refrigerator for six to twelve hours. Then strain and drink within one to two days. This method expressly does not apply to herbal and fruit teas: they are first infused while boiling and then cooled. You can find more recipes in [Make your own iced tea](/ratgeber/eistee-selbst-machen/).

## Water doesn't boil at 100 degrees everywhere

The boiling point depends on the air pressure. It sinks with each height, roughly by about one degree per 300 meters. In the flatlands this doesn't matter, but in the mountains it does: at 1,500 meters water boils at around 95 degrees. For black tea, the result can be a little flatter. A slightly longer steeping time or a little more tea compensates for this. With green tea, however, nothing changes because it is brewed with cooled water anyway.

## The quantity: rather weigh

A teaspoon is an imprecise measurement. A spoonful of fine broken tea weighs significantly more than a spoonful of voluminous white tea like Bai Mu Dan. If you want it precisely, use a fine scale: 2 to 3 grams per 250 milliliters is a good start for most teas. For tea with milk, such as Assam or East Frisian mixture, it can be more because milk weakens the taste.

## Brewing time and effect: an old legend

It has long been said that short-drawn black tea has a stimulating effect and long-drawn black tea has a calming effect. It's not quite like that. Caffeine largely dissolves in the first few minutes. With a longer brewing time, more tannins are released into the infusion. These make the tea bitter and can delay the absorption of the caffeine slightly, so that the effect may seem milder. However, this doesn't make the tea calming, and the caffeine is still in it.

## Several infusions

Good green teas, oolongs, white teas and pu-erhs do not completely release their aroma in the first infusion. You can usually infuse the leaves two to three times, and often more often with oolong and pu-erh. Rules of thumb:

- Keep the second infusion shorter with Sencha, around 20 to 30 seconds, because the leaves are already open.
- From the third infusion, increase the time again and increase the temperature slightly.
- Do not leave wet leaves standing for hours. There should be no more than one to two hours between two infusions.

Broken or Fannings quality black tea and tea bags, on the other hand, release almost all of their aroma in the first infusion.

## Separate the tea from the leaves in good time

The best brewing time is of no use if the tea continues to steep afterwards. That's why it's worth taking a look at your accessories:

- **Large sieve or filter insert in the jug:** The leaves have room to unfold and you simply lift them out after the steeping time. This is the most practical solution for everyday life.
- **Small tea eggs:** They are often too tight. The leaves swell and cannot fully release their aroma. They work for fine broken teas, but less so for large-leaf varieties.
- **Paper filter:** Clean and simple, but the leaves need enough space in the filter.
- **Two pots:** The tea steeps loosely in the first pot and is poured through a sieve into the second, preheated pot. So the last cup tastes like the first.

If you want to keep tea warm for longer, pour it into a preheated thermos flask after the steeping time or place the flask on a warmer. Only the finished tea without leaves should be left on the warmer, otherwise it will be bitter.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I pour boiling water into tea?

Black tea, pu-erh, rooibos and all herbal and fruit teas: yes, even expressly. On the other hand, it is better to pour green and white tea and light oolongs with cooler water.

### Do I have to squeeze the tea bag?

Better not. When pressed, additional tannins get into the cup and the tea becomes bitter. Simply take the bag out and let it drain briefly.

### Why does my tea still get stronger after steeping?

Because the leaves are still in the water. Remove the strainer, bag, or filter after steeping time. If there is loose tea in the pot, pour the tea through a sieve into a second, preheated pot.

### Can I brew tea in the microwave?

Technically yes, but water can be heated above boiling point in the microwave and suddenly foam up when you add the tea leaves. The kettle is safer and more accurate.

## Conclusion

Temperature, brewing time and quantity are the three adjustment screws of every cup of tea. The less oxidized a tea is, the cooler the water, always boil herbs and fruits and cover, and separate the tea from the leaf in good time. With these basic rules and a scale, you can make any variety, from the delicate Gyokuro to the strong Assam.
