Black tea is the classic tea in Germany: for breakfast with milk, in the afternoon with rock candy, as Earl Gray with bergamot or as East Frisian tea with cream. But behind the simple word “black tea” lies a great diversity. A malty Assam tastes completely different to a floral Darjeeling from the first picking, a fresh Ceylon different to a smoky Lapsang Souchong. Origin, plant variety, altitude, harvest time and processing characterize every tea. This guide introduces the most important varieties, explains the puzzling letters on the pack and helps you find the right tea.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Black, green, white and oolong tea all come from the same plant, **Camellia sinensis**. The difference lies in the processing.
> - Black tea is **completely oxidized**. This causes the leaf to darken and malty, fruity and spicy aromas emerge.
> - **Assam:** strong, malty, ideal with milk. **Darjeeling:** light, floral, muscat-like. **Ceylon:** fresh, citrusy, versatile. **Keemun:** soft, slightly smoky, with cocoa and fruit notes.
> - Abbreviations such as **FTGFOP** or **BOP** describe the leaf size and sorting, not directly the quality of taste.
> - Pour black tea with bubbling hot water and let it steep for three to five minutes, depending on the type.

## How a green leaf becomes black tea

Freshly picked tea leaves are first **withered**, which means they sit for a few hours and lose water. They are then **rolled** or broken. Cell walls are damaged and plant juices escape. In contact with oxygen, a reaction begins that tea makers call fermentation, although it is actually an **oxidation** by the plant's own enzymes. The leaves turn copper brown, similar to a cut apple. Finally, drying with hot air stops oxidation and the leaf becomes dark and durable.

With green tea, this oxidation is prevented right from the start by heat, while with oolong it is only partially permitted. More about this in [Green tea: varieties from Japan and China](/ratgeber/gruener-tee-sorten/) and [Oolong tea](/ratgeber/oolong-tee/).

## Orthodox or CTC?

Black tea is processed in two ways:

- **Orthodox:** The leaves are gently rolled and remain largely whole or roughly broken. This results in complex aromas and several degrees of leaf. High-quality leaf teas are almost always processed in an orthodox way.
- **CTC** (Crush, Tear, Curl): Machines crush, tear and roll the leaves into small balls. The tea steeps very quickly, becomes strong and dark and is suitable for tea bags and milk tea.

## The most important growing areas

### Assam, India

Assam is located in northeast India in a warm, humid river plain on the Brahmaputra. A large-leaved variety grows here, the **Camellia sinensis var. assamica**. Assam teas are strong, malty, full-bodied and dark in the cup. They tolerate milk and sugar very well and form the basis of many breakfast mixes and East Frisian tea, see [East Frisian tea culture](/ratgeber/ostfriesische-teekultur/).

### Darjeeling, India

Darjeeling lies on the slopes of the Himalayas in West Bengal, at altitudes of over 2,000 meters. The gardens predominantly grow the small-leaved Chinese variety. Darjeeling is lighter and brighter than other black teas, often floral and fresh. The harvest time has a strong influence on the character:

| picking | time | taste |
|---|---|---|
| First flush | Spring, around March to April | bright, fresh, floral, greenish, sometimes slightly tart |
| Second flush | Early summer, around May to June | full, round, with the typical muscat tone |
| Autumnal | Autumn | soft, mild, darker, nutty |

First Flush Darjeeling is often only partially oxidized and tastes somewhere between black and green tea. It is better to pour it a little cooler, around 85 to 90 degrees.

Darjeeling is registered as a **Protected Geographical Indication** in India and the European Union. A tea sold under this name must come from the region's registered tea gardens. Because the harvest is limited and demand is high, it is still worth taking a closer look at noticeably cheap offers: good dealers name the garden, picking and harvest year.

### Ceylon, Sri Lanka

In Sri Lanka, formerly Ceylon, tea is grown at different altitudes. **Highland teas** from regions like Nuwara Eliya or Uva are bright, fresh and citrusy, sometimes with a subtle spice. **Lowland teas** are darker, stronger and sweeter. Ceylon teas are versatile, are suitable pure and with milk and are a popular base for iced tea, see [Make your own iced tea](/ratgeber/eistee-selbst-machen/).

### Kenya and East Africa

Kenya is one of the largest tea exporters in the world. The majority is processed using the CTC process and ends up in tea bags and breakfast mixes. Kenyan tea is strong, dark and brewed quickly. But there are also Orthodox specialties.

### China

China is the country of origin of tea. There, black tea is called **Hongcha**, red tea, after the color of the infusion. Known varieties:

- **Keemun** from Anhui province: soft, with notes of cocoa, dried fruit and a hint of smoke.
- **Dian Hong** from Yunnan: golden leaf tips, sweet, malty, honey-like.
- **Lapsang Souchong** from the Wuyi Mountains: smoked over a pine wood fire, intensely smoky, a tea you love or hate.

### Other origins

Nepal, Vietnam, Indonesia, Georgia, Turkey and other countries also produce black tea. Nepalese teas are often similar to Darjeeling. In Turkey, black tea from the region around Rize on the Black Sea is prepared in a double pot and drunk in a tulip-shaped glass.

## Decoding the leaf grades

The letter combinations on black tea packages primarily describe the leaf size and sorting.

| Abbreviation | Meaning | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| OP | Orange Pekoe | whole, elongated leaf |
| FOP | Flowery Orange Pekoe | whole leaf with young leaf tips |
| GFOP, TGFOP, FTGFOP | Golden, Tippy, Finest | increasingly more golden tips, higher sorting |
| BOP | Broken Orange Pekoe | broken leaf, pulls faster, more powerfully |
| Fannings, Dust | small particles | very fast steeping, usually in tea bags |

By the way, “orange” has nothing to do with oranges. The origin of the term is not clearly understood. The important thing is: broken tea is not worse, but different. It brews faster and more powerfully and goes well with milk.

## From the auction to the packaging

A large proportion of black tea is traded through auctions, for example in Kolkata and Guwahati in India, in Colombo in Sri Lanka and in Mombasa for East African teas. Before the lots are auctioned, tea testers taste samples from each delivery. Trading houses use it to buy the teas for their blends. This is an art in itself: Because every harvest is different, blenders always put together their mixtures from different batches so that an English breakfast or an East Frisian tea tastes the same all year round. [Tasting Coffee](/ratgeber/kaffee-verkosten/) shows how professionals taste, because the methods are similar.

## The classic mixtures

- **English Breakfast:** strong mixture, often from Assam, Ceylon and Kenya, intended for milk.
- **Irish Breakfast:** usually even stronger and maltier, with a high proportion of Assam.
- **Earl Grey:** Black tea flavored with bergamot oil, a citrus fruit. Lady Gray often also contains orange or lemon peel.
- **East Frisian blend:** very strong, predominantly Assam, made for rock candy and cream.
- **Russian blends:** often with Keemun and sometimes a hint of smoked tea, traditionally from the samovar.

## Black tea with milk, lemon or sugar

Strong, malty teas such as Assam, breakfast blends and CTC teas tolerate milk excellently: the milk protein binds some of the tannins and the tea becomes rounder and softer. On the other hand, fine, floral teas such as Darjeeling First Flush or Highland Ceylon are better drunk straight, otherwise their delicate aromas will be lost. Lemon brightens the infusion because acid changes the colors and gives freshness. You shouldn't use it with milk because the milk will flocculate.

## Prepare black tea correctly

| Tea | Quantity per 250 ml | Temperature | Brewing time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assam, Ceylon, Breakfast | 2 to 3 g | bubbly hot | 3 to 5 minutes |
| Darjeeling Second Flush | 2g | about 95 degrees | 3 minutes |
| Darjeeling First Flush | 2g | 85 to 90 degrees | 2 to 3 minutes |
| Keemun, Dian Hong | 2 to 3 g | about 95 degrees | 3 to 4 minutes |
| Broken and CTC | 2g | bubbly hot | 2 to 3 minutes |

Longer steeping makes black tea more bitter because more tannins are released. You can find the complete overview for all types of tea in [Tea: Brewing times and temperatures](/ratgeber/tee-ziehzeiten-und-temperaturen/). Hard water can form an iridescent film on the tea and dull the aroma, see [Water Hardness for Coffee and Tea](/ratgeber/wasserhaerte-fuer-kaffee-und-tee/).

## Typical mistakes

| problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Tea bitter and astringent | Steeped too long, too much tea | Shorten the brewing time, weigh |
| Tea thin and flat | Water not hot enough, not enough tea | boiling hot water, increase quantity |
| Darjeeling First Flush tastes grassy and tart | poured too hot | Allow to cool to 85 to 90 degrees |
| iridescent film on the tee | hard water | filtered or softer water |
| Milk flocculates | Lemon and milk combined | use only one of the two |

## Frequently asked questions

### How much caffeine does black tea have?

One cup usually contains less caffeine than one cup of filter coffee; the exact value depends on the type, quantity and brewing time. More about this in [Caffeine in Coffee and Tea](/ratgeber/koffein-in-kaffee-und-tee/).

### Milk first or tea first?

This is an old question with no clear answer. Whoever gives milk first heats it more gently. If you pour tea first, you can better adjust the amount of milk to the strength.

### How long does black tea last?

If stored well sealed, dry and dark, black tea usually retains its aroma for one to two years, longer than green tea. More in [Store coffee and tea properly](/ratgeber/kaffee-und-tee-lagern/).

### Which black tea is suitable for chai?

A strong, malty tea is best, such as Assam or a CTC tea from India or Kenya. It holds its own against milk and spices without appearing thin. Fine Darjeelings would be a shame for that. You can find a recipe in [Make your own Chai](/ratgeber/chai-selbst-machen/).

## Conclusion

Black tea is a whole world of its own: malty Assam for milk and rock candy, floral Darjeeling for quiet moments, fresh Ceylon for every day and Chinese Hongcha for explorers. If you pay attention to the origin, leaf grade and brewing time, you will find the right tea for any time of day.
