Gongfu Cha roughly means “tea with skill” or “tea with care”. This refers to a method of preparation that originated in southern China and Taiwan: lots of tea in a small vessel, little water and many short infusions, often only lasting a few seconds. Instead of a large pot that brews once, you experience tea in many stages. The first infusion is fragrant and light, the middle infusions are full and sweet, the late infusions are gentle and mineral. Gongfu Cha is not a rigid ceremony, but a method to get to know good tea in depth. You need little equipment and a little attention.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Lots of tea, little water, short infusions: around **5 to 7 grams of tea per 100 milliliters** of water, infusions of **10 to 30 seconds**.
> - Classic vessels include the **Gaiwan**, a lidded bowl, and small **clay pots** from Yixing or Chaozhou.
> - Particularly suitable for **Oolong**, **Pu-Erh**, **white** and **dark tea**, with adjustments also for green and black tea.
> - Preheat the containers, awaken the tea briefly, then let it steep a little longer, one infusion at a time.
> - A good tea lasts five to fifteen infusions.

## Why this method shows so much

In Western preparation, a few grams of tea are steeped in a lot of water for several minutes. All the flavors release and mix at the same time. With Gongfu Cha, however, each short infusion only dissolves some of the substances. Light, fleeting scents appear early, sweetness and body in the middle, mineral and woody notes at the end. You taste the tea in layers. Because the tea to water ratio is high, each infusion is intense without steeping for long. This means that bitter substances largely remain in the leaf. The principle of early and late dissolved substances is explained in [Understanding Extraction](/ratgeber/extraktion-verstehen/) using coffee as an example; it also applies to tea.

## Start without special equipment

If you want to try the method out first, you can even get by without buying anything new. What is crucial is a small container, a lot of tea in proportion to the water and complete pouring.

- **Small teapot:** A 150 to 250 milliliter pot works if you adjust the amount of tea to the volume and empty it completely after each infusion.
- **Cup with saucer:** If necessary, a thick-walled cup can replace the gaiwan. The saucer serves as a lid and is poured through a fine sieve.
- **Milk jug or measuring cup** as a carafe so that all cups have the same strength.
- **Kitchen scales and cell phone timer:** Gram increments are enough to start with.

If you like the method, a porcelain gaiwan is the most sensible first purchase: neutral in taste, easy to clean and open enough to watch the leaves rise.

Tightly rolled oolong balls take a little longer in the first infusion because they are first opening. You can find background information about the tea types in [Oolong tea](/ratgeber/oolong-tee/), [Pu-erh tea](/ratgeber/pu-erh-tee/) and [White tea](/ratgeber/weisser-tee/).

## Step by step with the Gaiwan

1. **Preheat:** Pour hot water into gaiwan, carafe and cups and pour away after a few seconds. A warm container keeps the temperature stable.
2. **Pour tea:** Pour the measured tea into the warm gaiwan. Put the lid on, shake briefly and smell. The scent of the warm, dry leaves is the first impression of the tea.
3. **Wake up:** For oolong, pu-erh and pressed teas, pour hot water over the leaves and pour it off after a few seconds. This opens the leaves and rinses away dust. This is usually not necessary with green and white tea.
4. **First infusion:** Pour water to just below the rim, put the lid on and wait the specified time.
5. **Draining:** Hold the gaiwan with your thumb and middle finger on the edge, with your index finger on the lid button, and tilt the lid slightly to leave a gap. Pour the tea completely into the carafe. If water remains in the gaiwan, the next infusion will follow suit uncontrollably.
6. **Distribute and drink:** Distribute the tea from the carafe among the cups. Also smell the inside of the lid; scents collect there.
7. **More infusions:** Extend each infusion by a few seconds. If one brew tastes too strong, shorten the next one. If it becomes thin, lengthen it significantly.

Be careful: the edge of the gaiwan will be hot. Don't fill it all the way to the top, and practice with a little cooler water at the beginning.

## Example: a roasted oolong session

To make the numbers from the table tangible, here is a typical process with a roasted rock tea, 6 grams in a gaiwan with 100 milliliters and water just below the boiling point. Times are a starting point, not a law.

| Infusion | time | What you pay attention to |
|---|---|---|
| Wake up | 3 to 5 seconds | Drain, do not drink, smell the wet leaf |
| 1 | 10 seconds | Roasted aroma, initial sweetness, still light body |
| 2 | 10 seconds | Leaves are open, the cup is getting fuller |
| 3 | 15 seconds | often the peak of body and sweetness |
| 4 | 20 seconds | Roasted notes recede, minerals come forward |
| 5 | 30 seconds | the tea becomes calmer, the aftertaste counts |
| 6 | 45 seconds | check whether there is still power in the blade |
| 7 and 8 | 1 to 2 minutes | extract the last sweetness |

The first two infusions are often the same length because the leaves haven't fully opened the first time. From the middle onwards you lengthen in larger increments because there is less left in the sheet. If the third infusion becomes scratchy, use a gram less tea next time instead of reducing the time to two or three seconds. Such short times can hardly be controlled because the pouring itself takes several seconds. Therefore, always start pouring shortly before the time is up, as the tea will continue to steep.

## The right water

Because Gongfu Cha makes a lot of tea with little water, differences in the water are particularly noticeable. Very hard water dampens the aroma and makes the cup appear dull and dull; a film often forms on the surface. Very soft water, on the other hand, can make the tea thin and spiky. A table water filter or still mineral water with a low mineral content often brings a significant improvement. Boil the water freshly and do not leave it in the boiler for long. More about this in [Water hardness for coffee and tea](/ratgeber/wasserhaerte-fuer-kaffee-und-tee/).

## Clay pots from Yixing

Jugs made from the red or purple clay of the **Yixing** region of Jiangsu Province are unglazed and porous. Over many years they absorb aromas and form a patina. Tea lovers therefore only use a pot for one type of tea, for example only for roasted oolong or only for Pu-Erh. Rinse clay pots only with hot water, without detergent, and let them dry open. When buying, pay attention to reputable dealers, because the market is full of pots of unclear origin and composition.

## Gongfu Cha in everyday life

Gongfu Cha doesn't have to be celebratory. Many people drink this way on a quiet afternoon, while reading or with friends. You can also brew a tea throughout the day, as long as the leaves are not left moist and warm in the container for hours. When you take a break, drain all the water completely and leave the lid open. After a few hours you should discard the leaves.

## Compare infusions consciously

The biggest benefit of Gongfu Cha is really getting to know a tea. This is easier to achieve if you briefly note what changes from infusion to infusion.

1. **Dry leaf:** Capture the first scent impression from the preheated gaiwan: roasty, floral, fruity or earthy?
2. **Cup:** Write down two or three words about sweetness, body and tartness for each infusion. Nothing more is needed.
3. **Aftertaste:** How long does the tea stay in the mouth after swallowing? A long, pleasant aftertaste speaks for a good tea.
4. **Wet leaves:** At the end, spread out a few leaves on a light plate. Whole, elastic leaves are evidence of careful processing; lots of breakage explains why a tea quickly wears off.

You'll learn particularly quickly if you brew two teas next to each other with identical quantities and the same times. How you put impressions into words shows [tasting coffee](/ratgeber/kaffee-verkosten/); the procedure can be easily transferred to tea.

## Typical mistakes

| problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| bitter, scratchy | Too long infusions, too much tea, too hot | Shorten times, reduce quantity, lower temperature |
| thin, watery | too little tea, infusions too short | Increase quantity to 5 to 7 g, steep longer |
| Tea tastes empty after just a few brews | Tea broken too finely, inferior quality | whole leaves, better tea |
| burnt fingers | Gaiwan too crowded, too hot | Fill less, practice grip |
| Infusions vary in strength | Tea not completely drained | always pour completely into the carafe |

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to throw away the first infusion?

Awakening is common for Pu-Erh, pressed and roasted teas. When it comes to green and light teas, many people avoid it so as not to lose any of the fine aromas.

### Is Gongfu Cha the same as the Japanese tea ceremony?

No. The Japanese tea ceremony with matcha follows fixed procedures and has its own tradition, see [Preparing matcha](/ratgeber/matcha-zubereiten/). Gongfu Cha is a practical, flexible method.

### Can I use tea bags for Gongfu Cha?

It's possible, but it rarely makes sense. Tea bags usually contain finely broken tea, which releases its substances in the first few seconds. The infusions quickly become bitter and then empty. Whole leaves or pressed tea, which you carefully divide into larger pieces, are suitable for this method.

### How long does tea stay good in gaiwan?

As long as you brew in short intervals, you can enjoy a tea for several hours. However, you should not leave damp leaves that have been left warm for a long time overnight.
