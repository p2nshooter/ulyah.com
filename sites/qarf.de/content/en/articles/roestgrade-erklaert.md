Green coffee is green, smells of hay and peas and, when brewed, tastes more like vegetables than coffee. Only roasting transforms the hard, pale green bean into the fragrant, brown product we know. Well over 800 aroma compounds are created from a few hundred starting materials. The degree of roasting largely determines how the cup tastes in the end. In this guide you will find out what happens during roasting, how light, medium and dark roasts differ and how to find the right roast level for your preparation.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - When roasting, the beans are usually heated to around 200 to 230 °C. They lose water and become larger, lighter and brown.
> - The “First Crack” marks the beginning of a drinkable roast, the “Second Crack” marks the transition to dark roasts.
> - Light roasts emphasize acidity, fruit and origin, dark roasts emphasize bitterness, roasted aromas and body.
> - The caffeine content hardly changes depending on the degree of roasting.
> - There is no uniform scale: “medium” can mean different things between two roasters.

## What happens in the bean when it is roasted

Roasting takes place in several phases. For artisanal drum roasting it typically takes around 10 to 20 minutes, for industrial hot air processes it often only takes a few minutes.

1. **Drying phase:** Green coffee contains around 10 to 12% water. A large part of it evaporates in the first few minutes. The beans turn yellowish and smell like fresh bread or hay.
2. **Browning phase:** From around 150 °C, the Maillard reactions begin, in which sugar and amino acids react with each other. At the same time the sugar begins to caramelize. The bean turns light brown and many of the typical roasted aromas are produced.
3. **First Crack:** At a bean temperature of around 196 to 205 °C, so much pressure builds up from water vapor and carbon dioxide that the bean audibly bursts, similar to popcorn, only quieter. From here the coffee is generally drinkable.
4. **Development phase:** The time after the first crack determines the degree of roasting. The longer the roaster continues to roast the beans, the darker they become and the more acid is broken down.
5. **Second Crack:** A second, finer crackle begins at around 224 to 230°C. The cell structure of the bean continues to break down and oils come to the surface. From here on we speak of dark roasts.

At the end, the coffee is cooled quickly, usually with air, so that the roasting does not continue uncontrollably. When roasted, a bean loses around 12 to 20% of its weight but increases significantly in volume.

### Drum roasting and hot air

Artisan roasters usually work with drum roasters, in which the beans are circulated in a rotating, heated drum. Industrial systems often roast with very hot air in a short time. A long, gentle roast is not a sign of quality in itself, but it gives the roaster more control. Very short roasting times can result in the bean being dark on the outside and not fully roasted on the inside.

## The roasting levels at a glance

There is no binding standard for roast levels. Roasteries use terms such as “cinnamon”, “city”, “full city”, “Viennese” or “Italian” differently. The following classification is a guide:

| degree of roasting | Color of the bean | surface | taste | Typical usage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bright | light brown, cinnamon colored | dry, matt | fruity, floral, pronounced acidity, light body | Hand filter, AeroPress, Cold Brew |
| Means | medium brown, chocolate colored | dry | balanced, sweet, caramel, nutty, moderate acidity | Filter machine, French press, espresso maker |
| Medium dark | dark brown | occasional oily spots | Chocolatey, less acid, more body | Espresso, milk drinks |
| Dark | very dark brown to almost black | oily shiny | Roasted aromas, bitterness, smoky, hardly any acid | Southern Italian espresso, mocha |

Professional roasters often measure the degree of roasting using colorimeters that evaluate the light on ground beans. For you at home, your eyes, nose and tongue are enough.

## Light roasts

Light roasts are finished shortly after the first crack. They retain most of what is in the raw bean: fruit acids, floral notes and the peculiarities of origin, variety and processing. A light Ethiopian coffee can taste of bergamot, jasmine and peach, a Kenyan one of black currant.

At the same time, light roasts are more demanding to prepare. The beans are denser and harder, they dissolve more slowly in the water. That's why they need hot water around 94 to 96 °C and often a slightly finer grind. If they are brewed too cold or too coarsely, they taste sour and unfinished.

## Medium roasts

Medium roasts are the compromise that most people are happiest with. Some of the acidity has been reduced, caramelization and Maillard reaction have developed sweetness and body, but the origin is still recognizable. Typical notes are milk chocolate, hazelnut, caramel and red fruits. Medium roasts work with almost every type of preparation and are forgiving of small mistakes when brewing.

## Dark roasts

Dark roasts are roasted to or above second crack. The roasted aromas now dominate over the origin aromas. The coffee tastes strong, bitter-chocolaty, often smoky, with very little acid and a lot of body. Many people love exactly that, especially in espresso or with milk.

Dark beans are more porous and release their substances easily. So brew them with slightly cooler water, around 88 to 92 °C, and not for too long. The oily surface shows that fats have migrated from the inside to the outside. These oils oxidize more quickly in air, which is why dark roasts age faster and should be used quickly. In fully automatic coffee machines, oily beans can also clog the grinder and the bean feeder.

### Is there such a thing as too dark?

Yes. If roasted for too long, sugar and other substances burn and the coffee tastes ashy and charred. Some very dark supermarket roasts also hide the fact that the green coffee was not particularly good, because roasted aromas cover up defects.

## Roast level and ingredients

**Caffeine** is very heat stable. The degree of roasting only slightly changes the caffeine content per bean. However, because dark beans are lighter and have more volume, a measuring spoon of dark roast contains slightly less coffee and therefore slightly less caffeine than a spoonful of light roast.

**Chlorogenic acids** are strongly broken down during roasting. Dark roasts contain significantly less of it than light roasts. Many people therefore find dark roasts to be easier on their stomachs, although tolerance varies greatly from person to person.

**Acrylamide** is formed during roasting when sugar and the amino acid asparagine react with each other. Contrary to what is often assumed, the content is not highest in very dark roasts: acrylamide forms early in the roast and is partially broken down again over longer roasts.

## Degree of roasting and origin

A good roaster does not choose the degree of roasting according to a pattern, but rather to suit the bean. Dense beans from high altitudes, such as Kenya, Ethiopia or Colombia, have a lot of acidity and complex fruit notes, which a light to medium roast brings out. Beans from lower altitudes, such as many coffees from Brazil, are more likely to have nuts and chocolate and develop in medium to medium-dark roasts. Robusta is usually roasted darker to round out its earthy, tart notes. You can find out more about the growing regions in [coffee growing countries](/ratgeber/kaffee-anbaulaender/).

## Which degree of roasting suits which method

| Method | Recommended roast level | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Hand filter | light to medium | Clarity and acidity come through well |
| Filter machine | medium | balanced, forgives temperature fluctuations |
| French press | medium to medium dark | lots of body, chocolatey notes |
| AeroPress | light to medium | flexible, also for experiments |
| Espresso maker | medium to dark | strong, suits the intensive brewing style |
| Portafilter | medium to dark, for connoisseurs also light | Sweetness and body, rather darker with milk |
| Cold brew | light to medium | little bitterness, natural sweetness |

These are recommendations, not rules. A light espresso can be fantastic, but requires a machine with a stable, high brewing temperature and some experience.

## Filter roasting and espresso roasting

Many roasters offer the same coffee as “filter roasting” and “espresso roasting”. Filter roasting is usually lighter and emphasizes acidity and fruit. The espresso roast is slightly darker and takes longer to develop because espresso acid is highly concentrated and a very light roast in espresso quickly becomes too acidic. You can basically use both roasts for any method, but you have to adjust the grinding level and temperature.

## Find the right roast level for you

1. **Buy two small packs** of the same coffee or origin in light and dark roasts if your roaster offers this.
2. **Brew both the same**, for example in a hand filter with 60 g per liter.
3. **Compare** Acidity, Sweetness, Bitterness and Body. Write down what you like.
4. **Approach it:** If you don't like acidity, choose medium to dark roasts. If you like fruity notes, try light roasts from East Africa.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I know the degree of roasting if it's not on the package?

On the color and surface of the beans. Matt and light brown means light, chocolate brown and dry medium, dark brown and glossy dark. The description also helps: terms like “fruity” or “floral” indicate light roasts, “strong” or “smoky” indicate dark roasts.

### Is a dark coffee stronger?

Dark coffee tastes more intense and bitter, but does not contain more caffeine. “Strength” in the sense of concentration depends on the ratio of coffee to water, not the degree of roasting.

### Why do some beans have an oily shine?

With dark roasts, oils from the interior come to the surface. This can also happen with medium roasts after a few weeks of storage. Oily beans aren't bad, but are a sign of a dark roast or advanced age.

### Can I roast coffee myself?

Yes, with a pan, popcorn machine or small home roaster. It takes some practice and good ventilation. We explain how to do this in the guide [Roast your own coffee](/ratgeber/kaffee-selbst-roesten/).

## Conclusion

The degree of roasting is a question of taste, not quality. Light roasts show the origin, medium ones show the balance, dark bodies and roasted aromas. If you know what you like and adjust the grinding level and water temperature to the roasting process, you will get the best out of every bean.
