You don't need much to make a good cup of tea: water, tea and a container. But if you drink different teas more often, you will quickly notice that the right accessories make a lot of things easier. A sieve in which the leaves have space to unfold, a kettle that switches off to 80 degrees, a small scale for the right amount. At the same time, retailers sell countless tea strainers, tea eggs, pots and gadgets, many of which do more harm than good. This guide shows which accessories are really worth it, what you should pay attention to when it comes to the material and how to care for everything.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - The most important accessory is a **large sieve** or a jug with a large insert so that the leaves have **room to unfold**. Small tea eggs are usually too tight.
> - A **kettle with temperature selection** makes green, white and oolong tea much better.
> - A **fine scale** helps to get the right amount, especially for voluminous teas.
> - **Porcelain and glass** are tasteless and suitable for any tea. **Unglazed clay** absorbs flavors and should only be used for one type of tea.
> - Always **separate the tea completely from the leaf**, otherwise it will sag and become bitter.

## The basic equipment

| Accessories | Why? | Important |
|---|---|---|
| Teapot with large strainer insert | Pour the tea and separate the leaves | Insert reaches almost to the ground |
| Kettle with temperature selection | right temperature for each variety | Levels for 70, 80, 90 and 100 degrees |
| Fine scale | Weigh out the tea | 0.1 gram accuracy |
| Fine sieve | for rooibos and finely chopped teas | Stainless steel, fine mesh |
| Airtight cans | Store tea | lightproof, odorless |
| Tea warmer or warmer | Keep tea warm | only for tea that has already been poured |

With these things you are well equipped for almost any tea. Everything else is pleasure.

## Teapots and materials

### Porcelain

Porcelain is smooth, tasteless and easy to clean. It retains heat well if you preheat it. Porcelain pots are suitable for any tea. Many have a removable strainer insert.

### Glass

In glass jugs you can see the leaves unfold and the infusion change color. This is particularly nice with white tea, flower teas and green tea. Glass is also tasteless, but cools down more quickly. Look for heat-resistant borosilicate glass.

### Cast iron

Japanese cast iron pots, called **Tetsubin**, were originally intended for boiling water. The common cast iron teapots are usually enamelled on the inside and retain heat for a long time. They are heavy and should be dried thoroughly after use. They are suitable for teas that need to stay hot for a long time, but sensitive green teas can get too hot.

### Clay and earthenware

Glazed clay and earthenware behave similarly to porcelain. **Unglazed clay**, such as from Yixing in China or Tokoname in Japan, is porous and absorbs flavors over time. Tea lovers therefore only use such pots for one type of tea. More about this in [Gongfu Cha: Chinese-style tea](/ratgeber/gongfu-cha/).

### Stainless steel

Stainless steel jugs are sturdy and are good for the office or to take with you. Double-walled models keep tea warm for a long time, but be careful: tea that sits in an insulated jug with the leaves will continue to steep and become bitter.

## Strainers, filters and tea infusers

Tea leaves swell significantly when steeped, rolled oolongs expand to many times their volume. There is no space for them in narrow tea eggs and small clip sieves, the water circulates poorly and the tea remains flat.

| Variant | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Large strainer insert in jug or cup | lots of space, easy to take out | must fit the pot |
| Paper filter for hanging | lots of space, no rinsing, good for fine teas | Disposable product |
| Cotton filter | reusable, lots of space | must dry well, takes on flavor |
| Tea infuser, clip strainer | small, cheap | too tight, tea cannot develop |
| Loosely in the jug, then drain through a sieve | perfect development | second pot needed |

An elegant solution is the **two-pot method**: let the tea steep loosely in the first pot and, after the steeping time, pour it completely through a sieve into a second, preheated pot. This means that the tea is separated from the leaf and remains stable in taste, as is also common in the [East Frisian tea culture](/ratgeber/ostfriesische-teekultur/).

## The kettle

A simple kettle is enough for black tea, herbal tea and rooibos, which are infused with boiling water. For green, white and many oolong teas, a device with **temperature selection** is a big improvement. It heats to the selected level and then switches off, some hold the temperature for a while. Without choosing a temperature, it helps to let boiling water cool for a few minutes or add some cold water. [Tea: Brewing Times and Temperatures](/ratgeber/tee-ziehzeiten-und-temperaturen/) explains exactly how this works.

Kettles quickly become calcified with hard water. Lime changes the taste and increases the heating time. Instructions for descaling can be found in [Descaling the coffee machine and kettle] (/ratgeber/entkalken-kaffeemaschine-wasserkocher/).

## Scales and measuring spoons

A “teaspoon of tea” can weigh very different amounts. Fine Assam weighs significantly more per spoon than loose white tea or large oolong leaves. A small precision scale with a resolution of 0.1 grams is therefore a worthwhile, inexpensive purchase. You can use it to repeat recipes and compare teas fairly. If you already have a coffee scale, you can also use it for tea.

## Thermometers and timers

If you don't have a kettle with a temperature selector, you can get by with a simple kitchen thermometer. The most practical are digital penetration thermometers, which display within a few seconds. After a few weeks, you'll know how long it takes your water to cool to 80 degrees after boiling, and you'll need the thermometer less often. A **timer** is at least as important. Whether it's a kitchen timer or a smartphone: those who measure the brewing time instead of estimating it drink bitter tea less often and can make targeted adjustments if an infusion was too strong or too thin.

## Cups and tea bowls

The cup influences how you perceive tea. Thin-walled porcelain sits comfortably on the lips and does not cool the tea too much. A **white inside** shows the color of the brew, which helps when comparing different teas. Smaller cups make sense for fine teas because they are drunk while the tea is warm and aromatic. If you want to seriously compare teas, you can get a **tasting set** like the one used in the tea trade: a small cup with a toothed lid from which the infusion is poured into a bowl. This allows you to try several teas side by side under the same conditions.

## What to look for when buying

You can recognize a good pot by the little things. The spout should come off cleanly without dripping and the lid should not fall out when pouring; Many jugs have a small nose on the lid for this purpose. The handle must fit comfortably in the hand, even when the pot is full, without the fingers touching the hot body. Stainless steel sieve inserts are more durable than those made of plastic. For glass, look for heat-resistant borosilicate glass and for cast iron, look for undamaged enamel. Better one good pot than three mediocre ones.

## On the go and in the office

Even outside of your own kitchen, you don't have to go without good tea. **Tea bottles with a removable strainer** are practical, in which you let the tea steep and then remove the strainer or shield the tea from the leaves using a partition. Without this separation, the tea will continue to steep throughout the day and become bitter. In the office, a large mug with a matching strainer insert and lid is often sufficient. If you don't have a kettle with a temperature selector, pour some cold water into the mug of green tea before adding the boiling water. Pre-portioned teas in small cans or paper bags save you the trouble of weighing. A good insulated bottle with already poured tea is suitable for excursions and hikes.

## Cleaning and care

- **Porcelain, glass, stainless steel:** rinse with hot water after each use. You can remove tar edges with a little baking soda or citric acid and a soft brush.
- **Unglazed clay:** only with hot water, no detergent, let dry open.
- **Cast Iron:** Rinse and dry thoroughly immediately to prevent rust. Do not scrub the enamelled interior.
- **Strainers:** tap out and rinse after use. Fine nets become clogged over time and can be cleaned in a citric acid solution.
- **Thermos flasks and tea bottles:** Clean thoroughly regularly, especially seals in which residue tends to build up.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a separate pot for each type of tea?

Only with unglazed clay. Porcelain and glass are neutral and can be used for all teas if you rinse them well. However, strongly flavored teas such as Earl Gray tend to leave a scent, which is why some people use their own pot for this.

### Are tea bags worse than loose tea?

Tea bags usually contain finely broken tea that steeps quickly and powerfully. They are practical for milk tea and on the go. For fine teas with large leaves, loose tea is usually the better choice. How to store tea can be found in [Store coffee and tea properly](/ratgeber/kaffee-und-tee-lagern/).

### What is a tea cozy and does it harm the tea?

A tea warmer, a fabric hood or a warmer keeps the pot warm. It doesn't do any harm as long as the leaves are no longer in the pot. With leaves, the tea continues to steep under the warmer and becomes bitter.

## Conclusion

A few well-chosen things are enough for good tea: a pot with a large sieve, a kettle with a temperature selector or a thermometer, a precision scale and airtight containers. If you separate the leaf and the infusion in a timely manner and keep your accessories clean, you have already done the most important thing right.
