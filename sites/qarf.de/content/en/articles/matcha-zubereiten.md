Matcha is unlike any other tea. Instead of letting the leaves steep in water and then taking them out again, the whole leaf is broken into a fine powder in water and drunk. The result is bright green, with a velvety foam, a deep, sweet-tart flavor and an intense umami note reminiscent of fresh vegetables and the sea. In Japan, matcha has been at the heart of the tea ceremony for centuries. Today it can also be found in lattes, desserts and pastries. Preparation is easy if you know a few basic rules. This guide shows you how to beat a good matcha and how you can recognize good quality.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Matcha is finely ground, shaded green tea made from **Tencha** leaves. You drink the whole leaf.
> - For a bowl of **Usucha** (thin tea) about **2 grams** of matcha with **60 to 80 milliliters** of water at **70 to 80 degrees**.
> - **Sift** the powder beforehand so that there are no lumps.
> - Beat quickly with the **bamboo whisk** in zigzag or M movements until fine foam is formed.
> - Matcha is delicate: store in an airtight, dark, cool place and use within a few weeks after opening.

## What Matcha is

For matcha, tea plants are **shaded** for a few weeks before harvest. Less light causes the leaves to produce more chlorophyll and more of the amino acid L-theanine. This is where the bright green color and sweet, full-bodied umami flavor come from. After harvesting, the leaves are steamed, dried, stripped of stems and veins and then called **Tencha**. Traditionally, Tencha is slowly ground into a very fine powder in stone mills.

Other Japanese green teas such as Sencha, on the other hand, are rolled and infused. More about the differences can be found in [Green tea: varieties from Japan and China](/ratgeber/gruener-tee-sorten/).

## History and growing areas

Powder tea came to Japan from China. The Zen monk Eisai, who brought tea seeds and the method of brewing powdered tea from China at the end of the 12th century, is considered an important pioneer. In Japan, this developed into a culture of its own over the centuries, which took its form in the 16th century with the tea master Sen no Rikyū, which still has its form today. The best-known growing areas are **Uji** near Kyoto, **Nishio** in Aichi Prefecture and **Yame** on Kyushu; Kagoshima in the south also now supplies a lot of Tencha. In addition, ground green tea is produced in China and other countries. It can be good for the kitchen, but rarely compares to shaded Japanese matcha.

## Recognize qualities

Matcha is available commercially in very different qualities. Terms like “Ceremonial” or “Culinary” are not protected terms, but they provide a rough direction.

| feature | High quality matcha | Plain matcha |
|---|---|---|
| color | bright jade green | olive green, yellowish, brownish |
| Fragrance | fresh, sweet, grassy, seaweed-like | flat, hay-like, dusty |
| Pure taste | sweet, creamy, umami, gentle tartness | bitter, tart, little sweetness |
| texture | very fine like powder | a little rougher |
| Usage | pure as Usucha or Koicha | for lattes, baking, smoothies |

For lattes and pastries, a simpler matcha is sufficient because milk and sugar balance out the tartness. Drinking it straight is worth better quality.

## The equipment

- **Chawan:** a wide tea bowl in which you can brew well. A cereal bowl will also work if necessary.
- **Chasen:** a bamboo broom with lots of fine bristles. It is the most important tool for good foam.
- **Chashaku:** a bamboo spoon. Two heaped spoonfuls equal about 2 grams.
- **Fine sieve:** for sifting the powder.
- Optional: a holder for the broom so that it retains its shape after drying.

## Usucha: thin matcha step by step

1. **Prepare water:** Boil water and let it cool to **70 to 80 degrees**. Water that is too hot makes matcha bitter.
2. **Preheat the bowl and broom:** Pour some hot water into the bowl and dip the broom into it. This makes the bristles soft and flexible. Pour away the water and dry the bowl.
3. **Sifting:** Sieve about 2 grams of matcha into the bowl.
4. **Add water:** Pour in 60 to 80 milliliters of water.
5. **Beating:** Hold the bowl firmly with one hand and the whisk loosely with the other. Strike your wrist quickly back and forth in a zigzag or M motion, not in a circle, and without scraping the floor hard with the bristles. After about 15 to 20 seconds, a fine, light foam will form.
6. **Finishing Touches:** Finally, move the broom slowly along the surface to break up any large bubbles and lift it out in the middle.
7. **Drink immediately:** Matcha settles quickly. Drink it in a few sips.

## Koicha: thick matcha

Koicha is the concentrated form served at the formal tea ceremony. You use about twice as much matcha, around 3.5 to 4 grams, and only 30 to 40 milliliters of water. Instead of whisking, knead the tea in slow, circular motions until it forms a shiny, thick paste without foam. Koicha requires a very high quality, sweet matcha because any bitterness will be amplified.

## The tea ceremony soon

The Japanese tea ceremony, **Chanoyu** or **Sadō**, is much more than a method of preparation. Hosts and guests follow a set routine in which every movement has a meaning. The guiding principles are harmony, respect, purity and silence. A small Japanese sweet is served before the tea, which prepares the bitter taste of the matcha. The guest takes the bowl with both hands, turns it slightly so that he doesn't drink from its best side, and empties it in just a few gulps. You don't have to follow these rules at home, but a little of their peace and quiet is good for every bowl of matcha.

## Matcha latte

1. Strain 2 grams of matcha into a bowl or cup.
2. Add 30 to 50 milliliters of water at around 75 degrees and beat the matcha until there are no lumps.
3. Top up with 150 to 200 milliliters of warm, frothed milk or a plant-based drink. Oat and soy drinks go well.
4. If necessary, sweeten with a little honey, agave syrup or sugar.

For an **Iced Matcha Latte**, beat the matcha with a little water and pour it over a glass with ice and cold milk. How to froth milk is described in [Milk foam and cappuccino](/ratgeber/milchschaum-und-cappuccino/). You can find more cold ideas in [Make your own iced tea](/ratgeber/eistee-selbst-machen/).

## Matcha in the kitchen

Plain matcha works well in baking and desserts. It colors dough, creams and ice cream green and gives a tart, grassy note that goes particularly well with white chocolate, cream, vanilla, sesame and red beans. Always sift the powder together with flour or sugar so that it is evenly distributed. High baking temperatures and long baking times turn the color olive green. Unbaked foods such as panna cotta, tiramisu, pudding or yoghurt are suitable for bright colors. A teaspoon of matcha in a smoothie with banana and spinach adds color and a slight tartness.

## Organic and residues

Because you consume the whole leaf with matcha, all the ingredients in the leaf end up in the bowl, both desired and undesirable. That's why it's worth taking a particularly close look at the origin and quality of Matcha. Reputable traders name the growing region, harvest time and often the producer and have their goods checked for residues. Organic matcha is one way to provide additional protection. ## Typical mistakes

| problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| lump | not sieved | Always pass the powder through a fine sieve |
| very bitter | Water too hot, too much powder, poor quality | 70 to 80 degrees, check the quantity, choose better matcha |
| no foam | Broom too slow or circular, too much water | faster from the wrist, zigzag, reduce water |
| bland, hay-like | old or oxidized matcha | fresh can, store in a cool place |
| Broom breaks | used dry, stored incorrectly | Soak beforehand, dry on holder |

## Bamboo broom care

After each use, rinse the Chasen with warm water only, without detergent. Place it on a holder with the bristles facing down or in an airy place with the bristles facing up to allow it to dry completely. It can get moldy in a closed drawer. Over time, individual bristles break off. Then it's time for a new broom.

## Store matcha correctly

Matcha is one of the most delicate tea products. Because it is finely ground, it has a huge surface area that reacts with oxygen. Light and heat destroy chlorophyll and aroma, and the powder becomes olive green and flat. Store matcha in the original airtight tin or a tight container. Many people store opened matcha in the refrigerator, tightly closed so that it does not absorb any odors. Allow the can to come to room temperature before opening to prevent moisture from forming. After opening, matcha tastes best for about three to six weeks. More in [Store coffee and tea properly](/ratgeber/kaffee-und-tee-lagern/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How much caffeine does matcha have?

Because you drink the whole leaf, you often absorb more caffeine per bowl than you would with a cup of Sencha. The amount depends on the amount of powder. More in [Caffeine in Coffee and Tea](/ratgeber/koffein-in-kaffee-und-tee/).

### Can I make matcha without a bamboo whisk?

Yes, with an electric milk frother or in a well-sealed jar that you shake vigorously. The foam becomes coarser, which is enough for a latte.

### Is matcha healthier than other green tea?

Because you drink the whole leaf, matcha contains more of all the ingredients, including more caffeine. Health claims that matcha is a miracle cure are not proven. Drink it because you like it.

## Conclusion

Good matcha only takes a few steps: sift fresh powder, water at 70 to 80 degrees, quickly hit it with your wrist and drink immediately. With quality appropriate for the purpose, pure for the bowl, easier for latte and kitchen, and cool, airtight storage, each bowl remains bright green and sweet.
