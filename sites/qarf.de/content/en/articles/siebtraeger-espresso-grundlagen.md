A good espresso is a small art. In around 25 to 30 seconds, the machine presses hot water at high pressure through a solid cake of finely ground coffee. The result is a few milliliters, syrupy, with a hazelnut-brown crema, intensely sweet, with a balance of acidity and pleasant bitterness. Precisely because so little liquid carries so much flavor, espresso is hardly forgiving of mistakes. A gram more or less, a click on the mill or a crooked cake changes the cup noticeably. This guide explains the basics that will help you get reliably good results with a portafilter machine at home.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - A tried and tested basic recipe: around **18 grams of coffee** in a double sieve, **36 to 40 grams of espresso** in the cup, **25 to 30 seconds** brewing time.
> - It is controlled primarily via the **grinding degree**: If the espresso runs too fast, grind it finer, if it runs too slowly, grind it coarser.
> - Distribute the coffee grounds evenly and **tamp straight and firmly**.
> - Preheat the machine, portafilter and cup thoroughly.
> - Sour usually means under-extracted, bitter and dry usually means over-extracted.

## What makes an espresso

Espresso is not a type of bean or a roast, but a method of preparation. Three things are characteristic: very finely ground coffee, a water pressure of around nine bar and a short contact time. This dissolves aromatic substances, oils and fine particles in high concentrations. The **Crema** is created because carbon dioxide is dissolved in the coffee under pressure and forms fine bubbles when they emerge, which are stabilized by oils and dissolved substances. A nice crema shows that the coffee is fresh and the extraction is roughly correct, but alone says nothing about the taste.

## The equipment

- **Portafilter machine:** Single circuit, dual circuit or dual boiler. A single-circuit drink is enough to get you started if you mostly drink espresso. Anyone who froths a lot of milk will benefit from a dual circuit or dual boiler.
- **Grinder:** For espresso you need a grinder that is very fine and can be adjusted in small increments. The mill is at least as important to the taste as the machine. More about this in [Coffee grinder: cone or disc grinder](/ratgeber/kaffeemuehle-kegel-oder-scheibenmahlwerk/).
- **Fine scale** with 0.1 gram resolution, ideally with a timer.
- **Tamper** that fits exactly into your sieve.
- Optional: a distribution tool, a knock-off box and a microfiber cloth.

## The brewing recipe: dose, yield, time

Baristas describe an espresso with three values:

| Value | Meaning | Typical starting value |
|---|---|---|
| Dose | Weight of the dry coffee grounds in the sieve | 16 to 18 g in a double sieve |
| yield | Weight of the finished espresso in the cup | about twice the dose |
| reference time | from the start of the pump to the stop | 25 to 30 seconds |

The ratio of dose to yield is called **Brew Ratio**. A ratio of 1 to 2 is a classic espresso. A **Ristretto** is about 1 to 1 to 1 to 1.5 and is denser and sweeter, a **Lungo** is 1 to 3 or more, lighter and often a little more bitter. Weigh espresso by weight instead of by volume, because the crema distorts every milliliter measurement.

## Step by step

1. **Preheat:** Turn the machine on 20 to 30 minutes beforehand with the portafilter in place so that it gets warm too. Place the cup on the cup rest or rinse it with hot water.
2. **Rinsing:** Let water briefly run through the brewing group before using. This removes old coffee and stabilizes the temperature.
3. **Grind and Weigh:** Grind the dose directly into the dry, clean portafilter and check the weight.
4. **Distribute:** Tap the side of the portafilter or use a tool to spread the flour until the surface is even and no lumps or voids remain.
5. **Tamping:** Place the tamper straight on and press firmly until the flour stops compacting. What is important is not a specific force, but that you press evenly, straight and the same way every time.
6. **Clamp and start immediately:** Clamp the portafilter and start the cover without delay so that the flour does not “fry” on the hot group.
7. **Weigh and stop:** Place the cup on the scale and stop at the target yield. Write down the time.
8. **Try and Adjust:** Taste first, then change, and only one thing at a time.

## Adjust the grinding level

The grind size is your most important tool. Record the dose and yield and only change the grind until the time is within the target range:

- **Under 20 seconds:** Grind finer. The espresso is usually light, thin and sour.
- **Over 35 seconds:** Grind coarser. The espresso drips slowly, is dark and often bitter.
- **In the target area:** Now fine-tune to taste.

After every change to the grinder, grind away a small amount because there is still coffee from the old grind in the grinder. More about this in [Setting the grinding level correctly](/ratgeber/mahlgrad-richtig-einstellen/) and why the grinding level and time determine the taste in [Understanding extraction](/ratgeber/extraktion-verstehen/).

## Read the taste

| taste | Probable cause | What you change |
|---|---|---|
| sour, salty, thin, short finish | under-extracted | Grind finer or increase yield slightly |
| bitter, dry, ashy, scratchy | over-extracted | Grind more coarsely or reduce yield slightly |
| sour and bitter at the same time | uneven extraction, channeling | distribute better, tame straight, check sieve |
| flat, without sweetness | old coffee or temperature too low | fresher beans, preheat the machine longer |
| very intense, difficult | Ratio too narrow | Increase yield, direction 1 to 2.5 |

**Channeling** means that the water finds a convenient path through a weak point in the coffee cake and bypasses the rest. You can recognize it by splashes from the sieve, a bright jet early on or small holes in the cake after it has been poured. Causes include unevenly distributed flour, lumps, crooked coffee or a sieve that is too full or too empty.

## Aids for an even cake

Because channeling is the most common cause of unbalanced espresso, there are some simple tools to help with preparation. When **stirring with fine needles**, often referred to as the Weiss distribution technique after its inventor, you move a tool made of thin wires in circles through the flour in the sieve. This breaks up any lumps and distributes the coffee evenly. A **Puck Screen**, a fine metal sieve that you place on the cake after tamping, distributes the water from above more evenly and keeps the shower sieve clean. A **naked portafilter** without a spout shows you from below whether the espresso flows evenly out of the entire sieve or becomes light early in individual areas. None of this is mandatory, but each of these tools makes mistakes visible or prevents them.

## Keep a log

Especially at the beginning, a short protocol helps more than any device. For each bean, write down the dose, yield, time, the mill setting and, in two or three words, the taste. After a few days you will recognize patterns: how much a click on the grinder makes, how the bean changes with age and which ratio you like best. With the next bag of the same type, you'll have achieved your goal in one or two cases.

## Beans for espresso

Espresso concentrates everything, including mistakes. Freshness is crucial: Beans need to rest for about one to two weeks after roasting before they can be used well in espresso, and taste best for a few weeks afterwards. More about this in [Roasting date and freshness](/ratgeber/roestdatum-und-frische/). Espresso roasts or medium roasts with chocolate and nuts are suitable for starters because they are more forgiving than very light roasts. Blends with a little Robusta give more crema and body, see [Arabica and Robusta](/ratgeber/arabica-und-robusta/).

## water and temperature

Espresso consists of over ninety percent water. Water that is too hard will calcify the machine and dampen the flavors; water that is very soft can make the espresso flat and spiky. A water filter or balanced water protects the machine and improves the taste, as described in [Water hardness for coffee and tea](/ratgeber/wasserhaerte-fuer-kaffee-und-tee/). The brewing temperature is usually between around 90 and 96 degrees. Lighter roasts tolerate higher temperatures, darker ones tolerate lower temperatures. If your machine has a temperature control, don't change it until the grind and recipe are correct.

## Care of the machine

- **After each use:** Tap out the portafilter, rinse it briefly, rinse the group, wipe the steam lance immediately after foaming and blow it briefly.
- **Daily:** Wipe shower strainer, empty drip tray.
- **Weekly:** For machines with a three-way valve, backwash with a blind screen and detergent if the manufacturer specifies it. Soak the strainer and portafilter in hot water with cleaning powder.
- **Regularly:** Check the brew group seal and replace the water filter. Descale according to the manufacturer's instructions, see [Descaling coffee machines and kettles] (/ratgeber/entkalken-kaffeemaschine-wasserkocher/).

## Safety when working on the machine

The brewing group, portafilter and steam wand become very hot and hot water can splash when steam is drawn off. Never remove the portafilter during use and wait a moment after use until the pressure has been released. Always place the steam wand in a container or cloth before opening it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Single or double screen?

The double sieve is easier to get started with because it is less sensitive to small differences. A single sieve is more difficult to fill evenly. That's why many people always drink a double espresso or share it.

### Do I need an expensive machine?

For very good espresso, you need a good grinder, fresh beans and clean workmanship. A simple machine with a good grinder often beats an expensive machine with a poor grinder.

### How much caffeine does an espresso have?

An espresso has less caffeine than a large cup of filter coffee because the amount is small, but significantly more per milliliter. More about this in [Caffeine in Coffee and Tea](/ratgeber/koffein-in-kaffee-und-tee/).

## Conclusion

Good espresso comes from repeatability: fresh beans, a fine grinder, measured dosage, evenly distributed and just tamped flour, a well-preheated machine and a clear recipe. If you just adjust the grind and follow the taste, you will soon get espresso that is sweet, balanced and has a long finish.
