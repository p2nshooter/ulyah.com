A good cappuccino depends on the milk foam. Not from the stiff, coarse mountain of foam that stands on the cup like soap foam, but from silky, shiny milk with the finest bubbles that combines with the espresso and tastes sweet, without any sugar. Baristas call them microfoam. The good news: You don't necessarily need an expensive machine for this. With a little practice, you can create fine foam using a steam wand, an electric frother, a stamp jug or even a screw-top jar. This guide explains what happens when foaming, which milk is suitable and how to prepare classic lattes.

> **The most important things in brief**
>
> - Milk froths best **cold** out of the fridge and should end up at around **55 to 65 degrees**. It tastes hotter when cooked and loses its sweetness.
> - Whole milk gives the creamiest foam, low-fat milk produces more but drier foam. When it comes to plant-based drinks, “Barista” versions work best.
> - First draw air briefly on the steam wand, then swirl the milk to break large bubbles into fine ones.
> - After frothing, tap the jug and swirl it until the milk is shiny.
> - Wipe the steam lance immediately and blow it out briefly.

## What happens when foaming

Milk contains proteins, fat, lactose and water. Air is incorporated during foaming. The milk proteins wrap around the air bubbles and stabilize them. The fat makes the foam creamy and full-bodied, but can also make it a little less stable. Heat helps because the proteins develop and milk sugar is perceived as sweeter. But if the milk gets too hot, above around 70 degrees, the proteins change too much, the foam collapses and the milk tastes like boiled milk.

## Which milk froths best?

| milk or drink | Foam behavior | taste |
|---|---|---|
| Whole milk (3.5% fat) | creamy, silky, good for latte art | full, sweet |
| Low-fat milk (1.5% fat) | lots of stable foam, a little drier | lighter |
| Lactose-free milk | usually foams well | a little sweeter because the milk sugar has been broken down |
| Oat drink, barista version | foams well, becomes creamy | mild, grainy |
| Soy drink, barista version | stable, can flocculate with hot, sour coffee | nutty, beany |
| Almond or coconut drink | often foams weaker | own taste |

Barista versions of plant-based drinks usually contain added proteins, fat and acidity regulators that make foaming easier and prevent flocculation in the coffee. With soy drinks, it helps not to froth the milk too hot and to let the coffee cool briefly before mixing the two.

## Foaming at the steam wand

The steam lance of the portafilter machine delivers the finest foam, but requires some practice.

1. **Fill the jug:** Pour cold milk into a cold stainless steel jug, approximately up to the bottom of the spout. The milk needs space to expand.
2. **Blow out the lance:** Open the steam lance briefly above the drip tray to remove condensation.
3. **Dip:** Hold the nozzle just below the surface of the milk, slightly to the side of the center, and open the steam all the way.
4. **Sucking air:** In the first few seconds you will hear a quiet, steady hissing sound as air is drawn in and the milk rises. The more air, the more foam: only very briefly for a flat white, a little longer for a cappuccino.
5. **Create a vortex:** Once there is enough volume, immerse the nozzle a little deeper. The milk should rotate in the jug. This vortex breaks large bubbles into fine ones.
6. **Check the temperature:** Hold your hand on the jug. If it just gets too hot to hold any longer, turn off the steam. With a thermometer, aim for 55 to 65 degrees.
7. **Wipe:** Wipe the lance immediately with a damp cloth and blow it briefly so that no milk dries or is sucked in.
8. **Polishing:** Tap the jug once on the work surface to pop any remaining large bubbles and swirl the milk in a circular motion until it shines like wet paint.

## Jug and tools

A good stainless steel milk jug makes a lot of things easier. For a cappuccino, one with a volume of around 350 milliliters is sufficient, and a jug with around 600 milliliters is suitable for two cups at once. It's important to have a sharp, cleanly shaped snout if you want to try latte art. A clip thermometer helps to get a feel for the temperature at the beginning; After a few weeks you can usually feel it in your hand. Store the jug in the fridge if you want to save time: Cold milk in a cold jug gives you more seconds to froth before the target temperature is reached.

## Foaming without a portafilter

### Electric milk frother

The simplest solution: pour in milk, press the button and you're done. Most devices heat and foam at the same time and have inserts for high or low foam. The foam is often a little firmer than with the steam wand, but very suitable for cappuccinos and lattes.

### French press

Heat the milk in a saucepan or in the microwave to around 60 degrees, pour a maximum of a third of it into the [French Press](/ratgeber/french-press-anleitung/) and pump the stamp up and down quickly ten to twenty times, making small movements on the surface. Then tap and toss. The result is surprisingly fine.

### Hand frother with battery

The small whisk froths warmed milk in a tall mug in about twenty to thirty seconds. Hold it just below the surface until the milk has doubled in volume, then dip it deeper to refine the foam.

### screw-top jar

Pour cold milk into a screw-top jar, no more than halfway, screw the lid on tightly and shake vigorously until the volume has approximately doubled. Then heat briefly in the microwave without a lid so that the foam stabilizes. Be careful, the glass gets hot.

## The milk coffees at a glance

| drink | Construction | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Cappuccino | an espresso, equal amount of foamed milk, a layer of foam, about 150 to 180 ml | balanced, clear coffee taste |
| Flat White | double espresso with a little, very fine foam, around 150 to 180 ml | strong, silky |
| Coffee latte | an espresso with lots of hot milk and a thin layer of foam, 200 to 300 ml | mild, milky |
| Latte macchiato | Glass with hot milk and foam, espresso slowly poured into it, layers | very mild, decorative |
| Cortado | Espresso with about the same amount of warm milk, hardly any foam | small, strong |
| Espresso macchiato | Espresso with a dollop of milk foam | almost pure |

The sizes are guidelines. In Italy, Spain, Australia and Germany the same names are sometimes understood differently.

## Make the perfect cappuccino

1. Prepare an espresso as described in [Portafilter and espresso: the basics](/ratgeber/siebtraeger-espresso-grundlagen/) or use strong coffee from the [Espresso maker](/ratgeber/espressokocher-anleitung/).
2. Froth about 120 milliliters of milk with a medium amount of air.
3. Briefly swirl the espresso and milk so that both flow well.
4. Hold the cup slightly at an angle and first pour the milk from a height into the middle so that it is submerged under the crema.
5. Lower the jug towards the end and let the foam rise to the top. This is where latte art begins: with the right flow and a small movement, a heart or leaf is created.

## Latte Art: the first patterns

Latte art is created from three things: fine, shiny microfoam, a fresh crema and a controlled flow. For starters, the **Heart** is the best pattern. First pour from a height of about four centimeters into the center until the cup is half full. Then lower the jug close to the surface until a white spot appears, let it grow and finally draw a thin stream through the middle. The heart later develops into **Tulip** and **Rosetta**, where you move the jug back and forth to the side.

Water with a drop of detergent, which flows similarly to foamed milk, is of course not suitable for drinking when practicing. This way you can train your hand movement without using liter after liter of milk.

## Hygiene on the lance and frother

Milk residue is an ideal breeding ground for bacteria and forms a hard film. That's why wiping and blowing off the steam wand is part of every foaming process. If milk still gets stuck in the nozzle, the nozzle tip of many machines can be unscrewed and cleaned with a thin needle. There are special milk system cleaners for jugs and frothers; For fully automatic machines, follow the manufacturer's cleaning program, preferably daily.

## Typical mistakes

| problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| large, stiff bubbles | too much air at once, no vortex | Hold the nozzle lower, build up a vortex |
| hardly any foam | Nozzle too deep, milk already warm | nozzle to the surface, start with cold milk |
| Milk screams loudly | Nozzle too deep or steam too weak | Correct position |
| Milk tastes boiled | heated to over 70 degrees | stop earlier, use thermometer |
| Foam separates from the milk | waited too long | Process immediately, toss beforehand |

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I froth milk twice?

Better not. Milk that has already been heated foams poorly and tastes boiled quickly. It's better to use smaller amounts of fresh, cold milk.

### Why is my cappuccino too sweet or too bland?

The sweetness comes mainly from the milk when it is not too hot. The cappuccino often tastes bland if the espresso is too weak or old.

### How do I clean the milk frother?

Immediately after use, rinse with warm water and remove the whisk. Dried milk is much harder to remove and quickly smells sour.

### Can I use UHT milk for foaming?

Yes. Ultra-high temperature milk usually foams well and evenly, but tastes slightly like boiled milk. Fresh whole milk produces a sweeter, rounder cappuccino. It is important for both that the milk is well chilled.

## Conclusion

Silky milk foam is not a question of the expensive machine, but of the technology: cold milk, little air at the beginning, a strong swirl and the end at around 60 degrees. If you practice this a few times, you'll make cappuccinos and flat whites at home that are in no way inferior to a good café.
