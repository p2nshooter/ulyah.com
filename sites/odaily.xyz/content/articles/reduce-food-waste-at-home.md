It is a strange paradox of modern kitchens: we work hard to earn money, spend it on groceries, and then throw a large share of that food away. Wilted herbs, forgotten leftovers, half a bag of spinach, stale bread and overripe bananas end up in the trash every week in many households. In the United States, estimates suggest that around 30 to 40 percent of the food supply is wasted, and households are responsible for a significant portion of that. Wasting food means wasting money, as well as the water, energy and labor used to produce it. The good news is that small changes in how we plan, shop, store and cook can reduce waste dramatically. This guide gives you practical habits you can start today.

## Why food gets wasted at home

Understanding the causes helps you target the solutions. Common reasons for household food waste include:

- **Buying too much**, often because of large packages, sales or shopping without a plan.
- **Poor storage**, which causes food to spoil faster than necessary.
- **Forgetting food** at the back of the fridge or pantry.
- **Cooking too much** and not using leftovers.
- **Confusion about date labels**, leading people to throw away food that is still good.
- **Throwing away edible parts**, like broccoli stems, herb stems and vegetable peels.
- **Changing plans**, such as unexpected dinners out, leaving planned ingredients unused.

## Plan before you shop

Planning is the most effective way to reduce waste.

1. **Check your kitchen first.** Look in the fridge, freezer and pantry before you shop, and plan meals around what needs to be used.
2. **Plan meals for the week.** Decide on four or five dinners, and leave room for leftovers and changes in plans.
3. **Use overlapping ingredients.** Choose recipes that share ingredients, so a bunch of cilantro or a head of cabbage gets used completely.
4. **Make a list and stick to it.** Write down exactly what you need, with quantities.
5. **Be realistic.** If you rarely cook on Fridays, do not buy ingredients for a Friday dinner.

## Shop smart

- **Buy loose produce** when you need only a few items.
- **Think before buying in bulk.** A large bag of potatoes is only a bargain if you use them all.
- **Choose frozen fruits and vegetables** for items you use occasionally; they last for months.
- **Shop more often for delicate items** like berries and salad greens, and stock up on long-lasting staples.
- **Be cautious with special offers** like "buy one, get one free" on perishable foods, unless you have a plan to use or freeze the extra.

## Store food so it lasts

Proper storage can add days or weeks to the life of many foods.

- **Keep the fridge at 40 °F (4 °C) or below** and the freezer at 0 °F (−18 °C).
- **Store produce correctly.** Leafy greens in a container lined with a towel, herbs like a bouquet in water, apples and other ethylene-producing fruits separately from vegetables.
- **Keep bread at room temperature** for a few days, and freeze it for longer storage.
- **Use airtight containers** for leftovers, opened packages and pantry staples.
- **Follow the first in, first out rule**, placing new groceries behind older ones.
- **Create an "eat first" bin** in the fridge for foods that need to be used soon.

## Use the freezer generously

The freezer is one of the best tools against food waste. Freeze foods before they spoil:

- Bread, rolls and tortillas.
- Ripe bananas, for smoothies and banana bread.
- Leftover cooked rice, grains and pasta sauce.
- Meat and fish close to their use-by date.
- Fresh herbs, chopped and frozen in oil or water in ice cube trays.
- Grated cheese and butter.
- Leftover tomato paste, coconut milk and stock in small portions.
- Vegetable scraps for making stock.

Label everything with the contents and date, and keep a list on the freezer door.

## Use every part of the food

Many parts of vegetables and other foods that are often thrown away are edible and delicious.

- **Broccoli and cauliflower stems:** Peel and slice them for stir-fries, soups or slaws.
- **Herb stems:** Parsley and cilantro stems are full of flavor. Chop them into sauces, soups and salsas.
- **Carrot tops:** Blend them into pesto or chimichurri.
- **Vegetable peels and scraps:** Collect onion skins, carrot peels and celery ends in a freezer bag to make stock.
- **Stale bread:** Make breadcrumbs, croutons, bread pudding, French toast or panzanella salad.
- **Citrus peels:** Grate the zest before juicing, and freeze it for baking and cooking.
- **Chicken bones:** Simmer them with vegetables for homemade broth.
- **Overripe fruit:** Use it in smoothies, jam, compote, muffins or baking.
- **Potato skins:** Leave them on for roasting and mashing, or bake them into crispy snacks.

## Cook use-it-up meals

Plan at least one meal a week designed to use up whatever is left in the fridge. Flexible dishes make it easy:

- **Frittatas and omelets** with leftover vegetables, cheese and meat.
- **Fried rice** with leftover rice, vegetables and eggs.
- **Soups** made from vegetables, beans and stock.
- **Stir-fries** with any vegetables and protein.
- **Pasta** with sautéed greens, leftover roasted vegetables or a little sausage.
- **Grain bowls** with leftover grains, vegetables and a sauce.
- **Quesadillas and wraps** with leftover meat, beans and cheese.
- **Smoothies** with soft fruit, wilting spinach and yogurt.

## Cook the right amount

Cooking too much food is a common source of waste. Measure grains and pasta per person, for example about 1/3 to 1/2 cup (60 to 90 g) of uncooked rice or about 3 to 4 ounces (85 to 115 g) of dry pasta for a main course. If you do cook more than you need, plan the leftovers right away: pack them for lunch or freeze them.

## Understand date labels

Many people throw away food simply because it has passed the date on the package. In the United States, most dates indicate quality, not safety:

- **"Best by"** or **"best if used by"** refers to peak quality.
- **"Sell by"** is for store inventory.
- **"Use by"** is the last recommended date for peak quality, and should be taken seriously for highly perishable foods such as fresh meat and fish. It is required on infant formula.

Many foods are still good after their best-by date if stored properly. Use your senses and good judgment, and follow food safety guidelines for perishable foods.

## Preserve the surplus

When you have more produce than you can eat, try simple preservation methods:

- **Quick pickles:** Slice vegetables like cucumbers, onions or carrots and cover them with a mixture of vinegar, water, salt and a little sugar. They keep in the fridge for a couple of weeks.
- **Sauces and jams:** Cook soft tomatoes into a sauce or soft fruit into compote.
- **Drying:** Dry herbs or make oven-dried tomatoes.
- **Freezing:** Blanch and freeze vegetables, freeze fruit on trays.

## Compost what is left

Some waste is unavoidable: eggshells, coffee grounds, banana peels and inedible scraps. Composting turns them into a valuable soil amendment and keeps them out of landfills, where food waste produces methane, a potent greenhouse gas. If you have a garden, a simple compost bin works well. Many cities also offer curbside collection of food scraps or community drop-off points.

## Track your waste

For one or two weeks, note what you throw away and why. You may notice patterns, such as always throwing away salad greens or bread. Once you know your problem foods, you can change how you buy, store or use them. Even small improvements add up to significant savings over a year.

## A four-week starter plan

Changing every habit at once is hard. Trying one new habit each week makes the change stick.

| Week | Focus | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Notice | Keep a simple list of everything you throw away and why. |
| 2 | Plan | Check the fridge before shopping, plan four dinners and shop with a list. |
| 3 | Store | Set up an "eat first" bin, store herbs and greens properly and label leftovers. |
| 4 | Use up | Cook one use-it-up meal, freeze bread and ripe fruit, and start a bag for stock scraps. |

At the end of the month, compare your waste list with the first week. Most households see a clear difference, and the habits by then feel normal rather than like extra work.

## Getting the whole household on board

Food waste goes down fastest when everyone in the home helps. Tell family members about the "eat first" bin and ask them to check it before reaching for something new. Let children choose how to use leftovers, such as their own pizza toppings or a "fridge clean-out" omelet. Serve smaller portions and invite people to take seconds, so less food is left on plates. Agree on simple routines, such as writing new items on the shopping list as soon as something runs out and checking the fridge together before the weekly shop.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much money can I save by reducing food waste?**
It depends on your household, but many families throw away a significant share of the food they buy. Planning, proper storage and using leftovers can save a noticeable amount each month.

**What foods are wasted most often?**
Fresh produce, bread, dairy and leftovers are among the most commonly wasted foods at home.

**Is it safe to eat food after its best-by date?**
Often, yes. Best-by dates refer to quality. Check for signs of spoilage and follow safety guidelines for perishable foods.

**How can I make vegetables last longer?**
Store them correctly, keep ethylene-producing fruits separate, wrap leafy greens in a towel and keep herbs in water like flowers.

**What can I do with vegetable scraps?**
Use them to make stock, add them to soups and sauces, or compost them.

## The bottom line

Reducing food waste at home starts with simple habits. Plan meals and shop with a list, buy only what you need, store food properly and use the freezer generously. Use edible parts like stems and peels, cook use-it-up meals each week and understand what date labels really mean. Preserve surplus produce and compost what is left. These steps will save money, reduce your environmental footprint and help you enjoy more of the food you bring home.
