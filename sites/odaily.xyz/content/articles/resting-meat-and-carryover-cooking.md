You cook a beautiful steak to exactly the right temperature, slice it immediately, and watch a pool of juice spread across the cutting board. Or you take a roast out of the oven at the perfect doneness, only to find it overcooked by the time it reaches the table. Both problems come from the same physics: food continues to cook after it leaves the heat, and its juices need time to settle. Understanding carryover cooking and resting will help you serve juicier meat, cooked exactly the way you like it. This guide explains the science and gives you practical guidelines for steaks, roasts, poultry and fish.

## What is carryover cooking?

When you cook meat, heat moves from the outside toward the center. The outer layers become much hotter than the center. When you take the meat off the heat, this stored heat does not disappear immediately. It keeps flowing inward, raising the temperature of the center even though the meat is no longer on the stove or in the oven. This continued rise in temperature is called carryover cooking.

The amount of carryover depends on several factors:

- **Size and thickness:** Large roasts store much more heat than thin steaks, so their temperature rises more.
- **Cooking temperature:** Meat cooked at high temperatures has a steeper temperature difference between the outside and the center, which causes more carryover.
- **Shape:** Thick, compact cuts retain heat longer than flat, thin ones.
- **Resting environment:** Meat resting in a hot pan or covered tightly continues to cook more than meat resting on a cool board.

## How much does the temperature rise?

These figures are approximate, but they help you plan:

| Food | Typical temperature rise while resting |
| --- | --- |
| Thin steaks, chops and fish fillets | about 2 to 5 °F (1 to 3 °C) |
| Thick steaks and pork chops | about 5 °F (3 °C) |
| Whole chickens | about 5 to 10 °F (3 to 6 °C) |
| Large roasts, such as prime rib, leg of lamb or pork loin | about 5 to 15 °F (3 to 8 °C) |
| Whole turkeys | about 5 to 15 °F (3 to 8 °C) |

Meat roasted at very low temperatures, such as with the reverse-sear method, shows much less carryover, because the temperature inside the meat is more even.

## Plan for carryover: pull early

To end up with meat at your target temperature, remove it from the heat a few degrees before it reaches that temperature. For example, if you want a medium-rare steak at about 130 to 135 °F (54 to 57 °C), take it off the heat at around 125 to 130 °F (52 to 54 °C). For a large roast, pull it even earlier.

Use an instant-read thermometer, inserted into the thickest part of the meat away from bones. Bones conduct heat differently and can give misleading readings.

Keep food safety in mind. The USDA recommends cooking whole cuts of beef, pork, veal and lamb to at least 145 °F (63 °C) with a three-minute rest, ground meats to 160 °F (71 °C) and all poultry to 165 °F (74 °C). Many cooks prefer beef and lamb steaks at lower temperatures for medium-rare; this is a personal choice with some added risk, and people at higher risk of foodborne illness should follow the official guidelines. For poultry and ground meat, make sure the final temperature, after resting, reaches the safe minimum.

## Why resting matters for juiciness

When meat cooks, its muscle fibers contract and squeeze out moisture. Much of this liquid moves toward the center and collects between the fibers. If you cut the meat immediately, this liquid runs out onto the board.

During resting, several things happen:

- **Temperature evens out.** The heat redistributes, so the outer layers cool slightly and the center finishes cooking.
- **Fibers relax.** As the meat cools slightly, the muscle fibers relax a bit and can hold onto more moisture.
- **Juices thicken.** Proteins and gelatin in the juices thicken slightly as they cool, so they stay in the meat rather than running out.

The result is meat that loses less liquid when sliced and tastes juicier.

Some food scientists point out that the amount of juice lost on the board is modest, and that the main factor in juiciness is not overcooking the meat. Still, resting improves the eating experience by evening out the temperature and allowing cleaner slices.

## How long to rest meat

A general rule is to rest meat for about 5 minutes for small cuts and up to 10 percent of the cooking time for larger roasts.

- **Steaks and chops:** 5 to 10 minutes.
- **Chicken breasts and thighs:** 5 minutes.
- **Whole chickens:** 10 to 15 minutes.
- **Pork loin and beef roasts:** 15 to 20 minutes.
- **Whole turkeys:** 20 to 40 minutes, depending on size.
- **Fish fillets:** 2 to 5 minutes, often just the time it takes to plate.

Large roasts and turkeys stay hot for a long time, so resting them for 30 minutes or more is fine and gives you time to make gravy and finish side dishes.

## How to rest meat properly

- **Use a warm plate or board.** Transfer the meat from the hot pan to a cutting board or plate, so it does not continue cooking from the heat of the pan.
- **Tent loosely with foil.** A loose tent of aluminum foil keeps the meat warm without trapping too much steam. Wrapping tightly can make crisp skin or crusts soggy. For crispy-skinned poultry and seared steaks, you can leave them uncovered.
- **Use a rack.** Resting meat on a wire rack prevents the bottom from becoming soggy in its own juices.
- **Save the juices.** Pour any juices that collect on the plate into your sauce or over the sliced meat.

## Slicing for tenderness

After resting, slice meat against the grain, across the direction of the muscle fibers. This shortens the fibers and makes each bite more tender. For steaks like flank, skirt and brisket, slicing against the grain is especially important.

## The reverse sear method

The reverse sear is a technique that reduces carryover and produces evenly cooked meat. Instead of searing first and finishing in the oven, you cook the meat slowly in a low oven, around 225 to 275 °F (105 to 135 °C), until it is a few degrees below your target temperature, then sear it in a very hot pan for a crust. Because the meat is cooked gently, the temperature is even throughout, with little carryover, and resting can be shorter.

## Carryover beyond meat

Carryover cooking affects many other foods too:

- **Eggs:** Scrambled eggs continue to set after leaving the pan. Remove them when they still look slightly wet.
- **Fish and seafood:** Shrimp and fish fillets can overcook quickly. Take them off the heat when just opaque.
- **Vegetables:** Roasted and blanched vegetables continue to soften. Plunge blanched vegetables into ice water to stop cooking.
- **Custards and cheesecakes:** Remove them when the center still jiggles slightly; they set as they cool.
- **Pasta:** Pasta finished in sauce keeps absorbing liquid. Drain it slightly before it is fully al dente.
- **Baked goods:** Cookies and brownies continue to bake on hot pans. Take them out when the centers look slightly underdone.

## Keeping food warm without overcooking

If you need to hold cooked meat before serving, keep it in a warm place without additional heat, such as on a cutting board loosely tented with foil. For longer holding, a low oven set to about 170 to 200 °F (75 to 95 °C) keeps food warm, but it will continue to cook slowly. For safety, hot food should be kept at 140 °F (60 °C) or above if held for longer periods.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does resting meat make it cold?**
Not if you rest it for an appropriate time. Large cuts stay hot for a long time, and the outer layers cool only slightly. Serve on warm plates with hot sauce or gravy.

**Should I cover meat while it rests?**
Loosely tenting with foil keeps it warm. Leave crispy skin and seared crusts uncovered to keep them crisp.

**Why does my roast keep overcooking?**
Carryover cooking raises the temperature after you remove it from the oven. Pull large roasts 5 to 15 °F (3 to 8 °C) before your target temperature.

**Do I need to rest chicken?**
Yes. Resting allows juices to settle and the temperature to even out. Make sure the final temperature reaches 165 °F (74 °C).

**Is it necessary to rest fish?**
Fish fillets need only a short rest, or none at all. Thick pieces of fish benefit from a few minutes.

## The bottom line

Food continues to cook after it leaves the heat, especially large, thick cuts cooked at high temperatures. Plan for carryover by removing meat a few degrees before your target temperature, use an instant-read thermometer and let it rest on a board or rack, loosely tented with foil. Rest small cuts for about 5 minutes and large roasts for 15 to 30 minutes, then slice against the grain. Apply the same thinking to eggs, fish, vegetables and baked goods, and your food will be cooked more precisely, juicier and exactly to your liking.
