Eggs are the ultimate everyday ingredient. They are inexpensive, quick, full of protein and endlessly versatile. They can be breakfast, a fast dinner, the binding in a meatloaf or the base of a cake. Yet many people feel unsure about cooking them. Boiled eggs come out with gray-green rings around the yolk, scrambled eggs turn rubbery, fried eggs stick to the pan and poached eggs dissolve into wispy clouds. Eggs are sensitive to heat, and a minute too long changes everything. This guide gives you clear methods and timings for every classic way to cook eggs, so you can get exactly the result you want.

## A few things to know about eggs

**Freshness matters for some methods.** Very fresh eggs have thick, firm whites that hold together well, which is ideal for poaching and frying. Slightly older eggs, about a week or two old, are easier to peel after boiling, because the air pocket inside grows and the membrane separates more easily from the shell.

**The float test.** To check how old an egg is, place it in a bowl of cold water. A very fresh egg lies flat on the bottom. An older egg tilts or stands on its end. An egg that floats is quite old. Floating does not automatically mean it is spoiled, but crack it into a separate bowl and check the smell before using it.

**Storage depends on where you live.** In the United States, commercial eggs are washed before sale, which removes their natural protective coating, so they must be kept in the refrigerator at 40 °F (4 °C) or below. In many other countries, eggs are not washed and are often sold at room temperature. Follow the practice where you buy them, and keep eggs in their carton in the main part of the fridge rather than in the door, where the temperature changes each time it is opened.

**Room temperature helps.** For boiling and baking, taking eggs out of the fridge 15 to 30 minutes ahead reduces the risk of cracking and makes timings more predictable. If you forget, place them in a bowl of warm tap water for a few minutes.

## Boiled eggs

Most people boil eggs by starting them in cold water, but placing them directly into boiling water gives more consistent timing and often easier peeling.

1. Bring a saucepan of water to a gentle boil. Use enough water to cover the eggs by about an inch (2.5 cm).
2. Lower the eggs gently into the water with a spoon, so they do not crack against the bottom.
3. Reduce the heat so the water bubbles gently, not violently.
4. Cook for the time shown in the table below.
5. Transfer the eggs immediately to a bowl of ice water and leave them for at least five minutes. This stops the cooking and makes peeling easier.

| Result | Cooking time (large eggs) | What you get |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Soft-boiled | 5 to 6 minutes | set white, runny yolk, ideal for dipping toast |
| Jammy | 7 minutes | set white, soft, jam-like yolk, perfect for ramen and salads |
| Medium | 8 to 9 minutes | yolk mostly set with a slightly soft center |
| Hard-boiled | 10 to 12 minutes | fully set yolk, for egg salad and deviled eggs |

The green-gray ring that sometimes appears around a hard-boiled yolk is harmless. It forms when eggs are overcooked or cooled slowly. Following the timings and using an ice bath prevents it.

**Peeling tips:** Gently crack the egg all over by tapping it on the counter, then roll it under your palm. Start peeling at the wider end, where the air pocket is, and peel under running water or in the bowl of water. The water helps lift the membrane away from the white.

**Steaming eggs** is another excellent method. Place eggs in a steamer basket over an inch of boiling water, cover and steam for about the same times as boiling. Many cooks find steamed eggs the easiest of all to peel.

## Scrambled eggs

Scrambled eggs can be made in two styles: soft and creamy with small curds, or fluffy with larger curds. Both start the same way.

1. Crack the eggs into a bowl and add a pinch of salt. Beat them well with a fork or whisk until the yolks and whites are completely combined and no streaks remain.
2. Heat a nonstick pan over medium-low heat and add a small knob of butter.
3. When the butter is melted and foaming gently, pour in the eggs.

**For soft, creamy eggs:** Keep the heat low and stir slowly but constantly with a silicone spatula, scraping the bottom and sides of the pan. Small, soft curds form gradually. Take the pan off the heat while the eggs still look slightly wet and glossy, because they will keep cooking. This takes about three to five minutes.

**For fluffy eggs with larger curds:** Use medium heat. Let the eggs set for a few seconds, then push them gently from the edges toward the center with the spatula, letting the uncooked egg flow into the empty space. Repeat until the eggs are mostly set but still moist, about two minutes.

Add a splash of milk or cream if you like softer eggs, though many cooks prefer just eggs, butter and salt. Fold in cheese, chives or herbs at the very end.

## Fried eggs

A good fried egg has a fully set white without a rubbery, burnt edge, unless you like crispy edges, and a yolk cooked exactly to your liking.

1. Heat a nonstick or well-seasoned pan over medium heat with a little butter or oil.
2. Crack the egg into a small bowl first, which makes it easier to slide into the pan and to remove any shell.
3. Slide the egg into the pan when the fat is hot but not smoking.

Then choose your style:

- **Sunny-side up:** Cook without flipping until the white is set and the yolk is still runny, about three minutes. If the white around the yolk stays translucent, cover the pan with a lid for 30 seconds, or spoon some hot fat over the white.
- **Over easy:** Cook until the white is mostly set, then gently flip the egg and cook for another 15 to 30 seconds. The yolk stays runny.
- **Over medium:** Flip and cook for about one minute for a jammy yolk.
- **Over hard:** Flip, break the yolk if you like, and cook for two to three minutes until the yolk is fully set.

For crispy, lacy edges, use higher heat and a generous amount of oil, and spoon the hot oil over the egg as it cooks.

## Poached eggs

Poached eggs have a reputation for being difficult, but a few tricks make them reliable.

1. **Use fresh eggs.** Their firm whites hold together best.
2. **Strain the egg.** Crack each egg into a fine-mesh sieve set over a bowl and let the thin, watery part of the white drain away for a few seconds. This removes the wispy strands.
3. **Heat the water.** Bring a wide pan of water about 3 inches (8 cm) deep to a gentle simmer, with small bubbles rising but no rolling boil. A splash of vinegar helps the white set faster, though it is optional when eggs are fresh and strained.
4. **Lower the egg in gently.** Tip the egg from a small bowl close to the water's surface.
5. **Cook for three to four minutes** for a set white and runny yolk.
6. **Lift out with a slotted spoon** and drain on a paper towel.

You can poach eggs in advance for brunch. Transfer them into ice water after cooking, keep them in the fridge for up to a day, and reheat them in hot water for about one minute before serving.

## Omelets

A classic omelet is quick and elegant.

1. Beat two or three eggs with a pinch of salt.
2. Heat an 8-inch (20 cm) nonstick pan over medium heat with a little butter.
3. Pour in the eggs. Stir gently with a spatula for a few seconds, then let them settle into an even layer.
4. As the edges set, lift them so the uncooked egg flows underneath.
5. When the top is almost set but still slightly moist, add any fillings, such as cheese, sautéed vegetables, ham or herbs, to one half.
6. Fold the omelet over and slide it onto a plate.

Keep fillings simple and cook them beforehand, because the omelet cooks in only a couple of minutes. Too much filling makes the omelet heavy and hard to fold.

## Baked eggs

Baked eggs are wonderful for serving several people at once.

- **Shirred eggs:** Butter a small ramekin, add a spoonful of cream, crack in one or two eggs, season and bake at 375 °F (190 °C) for 10 to 15 minutes until the whites are set and the yolks are still soft.
- **Shakshuka:** Simmer a spicy tomato and pepper sauce in a skillet, make wells, crack eggs into them, cover and cook gently until the whites are set.
- **Frittata:** Beat eggs with cheese and cooked vegetables, pour into an ovenproof skillet, cook on the stove until the edges set, then finish in the oven at 350 °F (175 °C) until the center is just firm.
- **Muffin-tin eggs:** Beat eggs with vegetables and cheese, pour into greased muffin cups and bake for about 20 minutes. They make a practical breakfast to reheat during the week.

## Egg safety

Raw and undercooked eggs can carry *Salmonella* bacteria. Most healthy adults enjoy soft-boiled or runny eggs without problems, but the risk is higher for young children, pregnant people, older adults and anyone with a weakened immune system. For them, food safety agencies recommend cooking eggs until both the white and the yolk are firm, and cooking egg dishes such as casseroles and quiches to 160 °F (71 °C). Pasteurized eggs, available in many stores, are a safe choice for recipes with raw or lightly cooked eggs.

Other good habits: buy eggs with clean, uncracked shells, keep them refrigerated, wash your hands after handling raw eggs, and refrigerate cooked egg dishes within two hours.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why are my boiled eggs so hard to peel?**
Very fresh eggs are harder to peel. Use eggs that are a week or two old, start them in boiling water or steam them, and cool them in ice water before peeling.

**Should I add milk to scrambled eggs?**
It is optional. A little milk or cream makes the eggs softer, but too much makes them watery. Low heat and constant stirring matter more for creamy results.

**Why do my fried eggs stick?**
The pan may be too cold or not nonstick enough. Use a nonstick or well-seasoned pan, preheat it with a little fat and let the egg set before trying to move it.

**How long do hard-boiled eggs last?**
Hard-boiled eggs keep in the refrigerator for up to one week, ideally in their shells.

**Can I freeze eggs?**
Raw eggs can be frozen out of the shell. Beat them lightly and freeze in portions. Hard-boiled egg whites become rubbery when frozen, so they are better eaten fresh.

## The bottom line

Eggs reward precision and gentle heat. Boil them in already simmering water and cool them in ice water, scramble them slowly in butter, fry them over medium heat until the yolk is just right, and poach fresh, strained eggs in barely simmering water. Omelets need a hot nonstick pan and little filling, and baked eggs feed a crowd easily. Store eggs in the fridge, cook them thoroughly for people at higher risk, and you will have one of the fastest, cheapest and most satisfying meals at your fingertips every day.
