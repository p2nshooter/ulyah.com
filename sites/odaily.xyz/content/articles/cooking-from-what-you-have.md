Most meal planning starts with recipes: you choose what you would like to eat, write a shopping list and buy everything you need. That works, but it can leave the refrigerator full of half-used ingredients and the pantry crowded with things bought for one recipe and never used again. A different approach, sometimes called "pantry-first" or "reverse" meal planning, starts with what you already have. You look at the food in your fridge, freezer and cupboards, plan meals that use it, and shop only for the missing pieces.

Cooking from what you have saves money, reduces food waste and often sparks creativity in the kitchen. This guide explains how to do a quick inventory, prioritize ingredients that need using, build meals around flexible dishes, make sensible substitutions, keep a small set of staples that make everything easier and shop only for the gaps.

## Why start with what you have?

- **Less waste:** ingredients are used before they spoil.
- **Lower grocery bills:** you buy fewer duplicates and fewer impulse items.
- **More creativity:** working with limits often leads to new favorite dishes.
- **Less clutter:** pantry shelves and freezer drawers stay manageable.

It also helps on the nights when you do not feel like cooking, because you already know what you can make.

## Step 1: a quick inventory

Once a week, before you plan meals or go shopping, take ten minutes to check what you have.

### The refrigerator

Look especially for:

- **vegetables and fruit** that are softening or wilting;
- **open packages** of cheese, yogurt, cream or sauces;
- **leftovers** from previous meals;
- **proteins** that need to be cooked soon.

### The freezer

- **forgotten portions** of soups, sauces and cooked grains;
- **bags of frozen vegetables**;
- **meat, fish or tofu**;
- **bread and baked goods**.

### The pantry

- **grains, pasta and rice**;
- **canned beans, tomatoes and fish**;
- **open bags** of nuts, seeds, dried fruit, flour;
- **sauces and condiments**.

Write down what you find, or take photos with your phone. Our guide to [how to stock a pantry](/kitchen/how-to-stock-a-pantry/) explains how to organize shelves so this check becomes quicker every week.

## Step 2: make a "use it first" list

From your inventory, pick out the ingredients that need using in the next few days. Put them at the top of your plan. For example:

- half a bag of spinach;
- two zucchini;
- a container of cooked rice;
- a carton of cream opened yesterday;
- three eggs nearing their date.

Our guide to [food date labels explained](/kitchen/food-date-labels-explained/) helps you understand which dates matter for safety and which are about quality.

## Step 3: choose flexible dishes

Some dishes are naturally good at absorbing whatever you have. These are the backbone of pantry-first cooking.

| Flexible dish | What it can use |
|---|---|
| Frittata or omelette | eggs, vegetables, cheese, cooked potatoes, herbs |
| Fried rice | cooked rice, eggs, vegetables, leftover meat or tofu |
| Soup | vegetables, beans, grains, stock, leftover meat |
| Pasta | almost any vegetable, canned fish, beans, cheese, cream |
| Stir-fry | vegetables, tofu or meat, noodles or rice, sauces |
| Grain bowls | cooked grains, roasted vegetables, eggs, beans, sauces |
| Tacos, wraps and quesadillas | beans, leftover meat, vegetables, cheese |
| Sheet-pan dinners | vegetables, sausages, chicken, chickpeas |
| Curries | vegetables, lentils, chickpeas, coconut milk, tomatoes |
| Savory pies and bakes | vegetables, cooked meat, cheese, pastry or potatoes |

Match your "use it first" list to these dishes. Spinach, zucchini, eggs and cream suggest a frittata; cooked rice and vegetables suggest fried rice. Our guides to [fried rice and leftover grains](/kitchen/fried-rice-and-leftover-grains/), [sheet-pan dinners](/kitchen/sheet-pan-dinners/) and [grain bowls for weeknights](/kitchen/grain-bowls-for-weeknights/) give methods you can adapt.

## Step 4: make sensible substitutions

Recipes are often more flexible than they look. Some reliable swaps:

- **Vegetables:** most vegetables within the same family can replace each other: spinach for kale or chard, zucchini for squash, broccoli for cauliflower.
- **Herbs:** use what you have; parsley is a mild substitute for many fresh herbs, and dried herbs can replace fresh in cooked dishes, using less.
- **Acids:** lemon juice, lime juice and different vinegars can often replace one another.
- **Dairy:** yogurt can stand in for sour cream; milk with a little butter can replace cream in some sauces.
- **Proteins:** chickpeas or white beans for chicken in many stews and curries; canned fish for fresh in pasta.
- **Grains:** rice, quinoa, bulgur, couscous and small pasta are often interchangeable in salads and bowls.

Baking is less forgiving than cooking, because proportions and chemistry matter more. Our guide to [baking soda vs. baking powder](/kitchen/baking-soda-vs-baking-powder/) explains why some swaps do not work.

## Step 5: plan a few meals and leave gaps

Plan three or four dinners around your inventory, leaving one or two nights open for leftovers, a simple meal or something unexpected. A loose plan is easier to follow than a rigid one, and it allows for the evening when someone is late or nobody is hungry.

Our guide to [how to plan a week of meals](/kitchen/how-to-plan-a-week-of-meals/) offers a simple framework you can combine with the pantry-first approach.

## Step 6: shop only for the gaps

Now write a shopping list containing only what you need to complete your planned meals, plus any staples that are running low. Stick to the list as much as possible. Our guide to [smart grocery shopping](/kitchen/smart-grocery-shopping/) has tips for avoiding impulse purchases.

## Staples that make pantry cooking easy

A small, well-chosen set of staples turns odds and ends into meals:

- **olive oil and a neutral cooking oil**;
- **onions and garlic**;
- **canned tomatoes**;
- **canned or dried beans and lentils**;
- **rice, pasta and one or two other grains**;
- **eggs**;
- **a hard cheese**, such as Parmesan or cheddar;
- **stock cubes or concentrate**;
- **soy sauce, mustard, a good vinegar and lemon**;
- **a few spices and dried herbs**; see our guide to [buying, storing and using spices](/kitchen/spices-buying-storing-and-using/);
- **frozen peas, spinach or mixed vegetables**.

With these on hand, almost any leftover vegetable or protein can become dinner.

## A worked example

Imagine opening the refrigerator on a Sunday evening and finding half a head of broccoli, two carrots, a wedge of cheddar, a container of cooked rice from Friday, three eggs, some wilting cilantro and half a can of coconut milk. The pantry holds onions, garlic, canned tomatoes, chickpeas, pasta and a few spices.

A pantry-first plan for the next few days might look like this:

- **Monday:** fried rice with the cooked rice, eggs, broccoli and carrots, finished with cilantro, because cooked rice should be used within a short time.
- **Tuesday:** a quick chickpea curry using onion, garlic, canned tomatoes, the coconut milk and spices, served with fresh rice.
- **Wednesday:** pasta with a simple tomato sauce and grated cheddar, plus any remaining vegetables.
- **Thursday:** a "clear-out" frittata or soup with whatever is left.

The shopping list for the week now contains only what is missing: perhaps fresh herbs, a bag of salad leaves and fruit. Nothing in the refrigerator goes to waste, and every dinner is planned before you set foot in a shop.

## Getting the household involved

Pantry-first cooking works better when everyone knows the plan. A short list on the refrigerator door showing which leftovers need eating first, and which dinners are planned, helps other members of the household reach for the right container rather than opening something new. Children can be given the job of checking the "eat me first" shelf, and older ones can choose and cook one of the week's flexible dishes. Our guide to [feeding a family on a budget](/kitchen/feeding-a-family-on-a-budget/) has more ideas for cooking together.

## Making it a habit

- **Choose a regular day** for your inventory, ideally the day before you shop.
- **Keep an "eat me first" shelf** in the refrigerator for items that need using.
- **Use clear containers** so you can see what you have.
- **Label leftovers** with the date.
- **Cook one "clear-out" meal** each week, such as soup, frittata or fried rice.

Our guide to [reducing food waste at home](/kitchen/reduce-food-waste-at-home/) offers more habits that help.

## Frequently asked questions

### Isn't it easier to just follow recipes?

Recipes are great for learning and for special meals. Pantry-first cooking simply adds a habit of using what you have before buying more, which saves money and reduces waste.

### What if I do not know what to cook with random ingredients?

Start with one of the flexible dishes above. Soups, frittatas, fried rice and pasta can absorb almost anything.

### How do I avoid buying duplicates?

Check the pantry, fridge and freezer before writing your list, and take photos with your phone to check while shopping.

## The bottom line

Cooking from what you have starts with a weekly inventory of the fridge, freezer and pantry, and a "use it first" list of ingredients that need eating soon. Build meals around flexible dishes such as frittatas, fried rice, soups, pasta, stir-fries and grain bowls, make sensible substitutions, plan a few meals with gaps for leftovers, and shop only for what is missing. Keep a small set of versatile staples, and make the inventory a weekly habit. You will waste less, spend less and probably discover a few new favorite meals.
