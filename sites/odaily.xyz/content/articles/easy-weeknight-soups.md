Soup has a reputation for being something that simmers all afternoon on a Sunday. In reality, many of the most comforting soups come together in less than an hour, often in a single pot, using vegetables from the fridge and staples from the pantry. Soup is also one of the most forgiving dishes in the kitchen. It welcomes leftovers, adapts to whatever is in season and often tastes even better the next day. Once you understand the basic structure behind almost every soup, you no longer need a recipe. You can open the fridge, see what is there and turn it into dinner. This guide explains that structure and gives you five weeknight soups to start with.

## The four building blocks of soup

Nearly every soup, from a simple vegetable soup to a spicy lentil stew, follows the same pattern:

1. **Aromatic base:** Vegetables, herbs and spices cooked in fat to build flavor.
2. **Liquid:** Stock, broth, water, tomatoes, coconut milk or milk.
3. **Body:** The main ingredients that give the soup substance, such as vegetables, beans, lentils, grains, pasta or meat.
4. **Finish:** Final touches that add brightness, richness and texture.

If you get each stage right, the soup will taste deep and balanced.

## Step 1: Build the aromatic base

The flavor of a soup begins in the bottom of the pot. Heat a few tablespoons of olive oil or butter over medium heat, add chopped aromatics and cook them slowly until soft and fragrant. This step takes five to ten minutes and is worth every one of them.

Different cuisines use different aromatic combinations:

- **French mirepoix:** onion, carrot and celery.
- **Italian soffritto:** onion, carrot and celery, often with garlic and parsley, cooked in olive oil.
- **Cajun and Creole "holy trinity":** onion, celery and green bell pepper.
- **Asian bases:** garlic, ginger, scallions and sometimes lemongrass or chilies.
- **Indian and Middle Eastern bases:** onion, garlic and ginger with spices such as cumin, coriander and turmeric.

Add salt to the aromatics as they cook. It draws out moisture, helps them soften and seasons the soup from the start. Once the vegetables are soft, add garlic and spices and cook them for another minute until fragrant. A spoonful of tomato paste cooked in at this stage adds depth and color.

## Step 2: Add the liquid

The liquid gives soup its character. Good options include:

- **Homemade or store-bought stock or broth.** Choose low-sodium versions so you can control the salt.
- **Water.** Many excellent soups, especially bean and vegetable soups, use only water. The flavor comes from the aromatics and other ingredients.
- **Canned tomatoes** for rich, tangy soups.
- **Coconut milk** for creamy Southeast Asian and Indian-style soups.
- **Milk or cream** for chowders and creamy soups, usually added near the end.

A useful trick for store-bought broth is to simmer it for ten minutes with a few extra aromatics, such as a parmesan rind, a bay leaf, a few peppercorns or a piece of ginger. It tastes much more homemade.

## Step 3: Add the body

This is where you choose what kind of soup to make. Add ingredients in order of how long they take to cook.

| Ingredient | Approximate simmering time |
| --- | --- |
| Raw potatoes, carrots, parsnips, winter squash in cubes | 15 to 25 minutes |
| Dried red lentils | 15 to 20 minutes |
| Dried brown or green lentils | 25 to 35 minutes |
| Rice | 15 to 20 minutes |
| Small pasta | 6 to 10 minutes |
| Canned beans and chickpeas | 10 minutes to heat and absorb flavor |
| Raw chicken pieces | 15 to 20 minutes, until 165 °F (74 °C) inside |
| Zucchini, green beans, peas | 5 to 8 minutes |
| Spinach, kale, chard | 1 to 5 minutes |

Bring the soup to a boil, then reduce it to a gentle simmer. A hard boil can break up vegetables and make meat tough. Simmer until everything is tender.

**A note on pasta and rice:** These keep absorbing liquid after cooking and can turn mushy in leftovers. If you plan to keep the soup for several days, cook pasta or rice separately and add it to each bowl.

## Step 4: Finish and adjust

The final minutes make the difference between a good soup and a great one. Taste the soup and ask what it needs.

- **Salt:** If the soup tastes flat, add salt a pinch at a time.
- **Acid:** A squeeze of lemon or lime juice, a splash of vinegar or a spoonful of yogurt brightens heavy soups.
- **Richness:** A drizzle of good olive oil, a knob of butter, a spoonful of cream or coconut milk.
- **Heat:** Chili flakes, hot sauce, black pepper or a spoonful of chili oil.
- **Freshness:** Chopped parsley, cilantro, dill, basil or scallions.
- **Texture:** Croutons, toasted seeds, grated cheese, crispy bacon or a dollop of pesto.

## Three styles of soup

### Brothy soups

These are light and clear, with pieces of vegetables, noodles or meat in a flavorful broth. Examples include chicken noodle soup, minestrone and miso soup. The key is a well-seasoned broth.

### Hearty soups and stews

These are thick and chunky, often with beans, lentils, potatoes or grains. Examples include lentil soup, split pea soup and bean chili. Some of the starch breaks down into the liquid, naturally thickening the soup.

### Creamy and pureed soups

These are smooth and velvety, such as tomato soup, butternut squash soup and potato leek soup. Cook the vegetables until very tender, then blend. An immersion blender lets you puree the soup directly in the pot. If you use a countertop blender, let the soup cool slightly, fill the blender no more than halfway and hold the lid with a towel, because hot liquids expand and can push the lid off.

## Making soups creamy without cream

You can make a soup smooth and rich without heavy cream:

- **Blend part of the soup.** Pureeing a third or half of a bean or vegetable soup thickens it while keeping some texture.
- **Add potatoes or white beans.** Their starch creates a creamy texture when blended.
- **Use a little rice or bread.** A slice of bread or a handful of rice simmered and blended into the soup thickens it.
- **Stir in yogurt or coconut milk.** Add yogurt off the heat to prevent curdling.
- **Grate in cheese** such as Parmesan or cheddar just before serving.

## Five weeknight soups to try

### Red lentil soup

Cook onion, carrot and garlic in olive oil. Add cumin, smoked paprika and a spoonful of tomato paste. Add 1 cup (200 g) of rinsed red lentils and about 5 cups (1.2 liters) of stock or water. Simmer for 20 minutes until the lentils collapse. Blend partially, finish with lemon juice and serve with yogurt and bread. Ready in about 35 minutes.

### Tomato and white bean soup

Cook onion and garlic in olive oil, add a can of crushed tomatoes, a can of white beans and stock. Simmer for 15 minutes, blend half the soup and stir it back in. Finish with basil and a drizzle of olive oil. Grilled cheese sandwiches make the perfect side.

### Quick chicken and vegetable soup

Cook onion, carrots and celery in butter, add garlic, thyme and stock. Add bite-sized pieces of raw chicken thigh and simmer for 15 to 20 minutes until cooked through. Add peas, small pasta cooked separately and chopped parsley. Finish with lemon juice.

### Coconut curry noodle soup

Cook garlic, ginger and a couple of spoonfuls of curry paste in oil. Add a can of coconut milk and stock, then shrimp, chicken or tofu and vegetables such as bell peppers and bok choy. Season with fish sauce or soy sauce and lime juice. Pour over cooked rice noodles and top with cilantro.

### Potato and leek soup

Gently cook sliced leeks in butter until very soft, add cubed potatoes and stock and simmer for 20 minutes. Blend until smooth or leave it chunky, stir in a little milk and season well. Top with chives and crispy bacon or croutons.

## Storing and freezing soup

Soup is ideal for cooking once and eating twice. To store it safely:

- Cool soup quickly. Divide large pots into shallow containers and refrigerate within two hours. A big pot of hot soup cools too slowly in the fridge.
- Refrigerated soup keeps for three to four days.
- Most soups freeze well for two to three months. Leave some space in containers because liquid expands as it freezes.
- Soups with cream, potatoes or pasta can change texture after freezing. Freeze the base and add cream or pasta when reheating.
- Reheat soup until it is steaming hot throughout and reaches a boil.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does my soup taste bland?**
It usually needs more salt, acid or a better aromatic base. Cook the vegetables longer at the start, season in layers and finish with lemon juice or vinegar.

**Can I make soup without stock?**
Yes. Many soups taste wonderful made with water, especially when the aromatics are cooked well and the soup is seasoned carefully. A parmesan rind or a spoonful of miso adds savory depth.

**How do I thicken a thin soup?**
Simmer it uncovered to reduce, blend part of it, add mashed beans or potatoes, or stir in a slurry of cornstarch mixed with cold water.

**Should I cover soup while it simmers?**
Partially covering it keeps it from evaporating too quickly while allowing some reduction. Cover it fully if you want to keep all the liquid.

**What can I serve with soup to make a full meal?**
Crusty bread, a grilled cheese or quesadilla, a green salad, rice or a sprinkle of cheese and toasted seeds all turn soup into a satisfying dinner.

## The bottom line

Weeknight soup is simpler than it seems. Build an aromatic base slowly, add a flavorful liquid, choose your body ingredients and add them in order of cooking time, and finish with salt, acid, richness and freshness. Blend for creamy soups, keep pasta separate for leftovers and store soups safely by cooling them quickly. With this method, a pot of comforting, nourishing soup is never more than an hour away.
