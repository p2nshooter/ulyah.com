Few dinners are as reliable as a bowl of pasta. With a box of dried pasta in the cupboard and a few staples like garlic, olive oil, canned tomatoes and cheese, you are never more than twenty minutes away from a satisfying meal. Yet the gap between ordinary and outstanding pasta is surprisingly wide. Overcooked noodles, watery sauce that slides off the pasta, bland flavor or a clump of cheese that refuses to melt: these are common problems with simple solutions. This guide explains how to cook pasta properly, how to bring pasta and sauce together like an Italian cook, and gives you six pantry formulas you can make any night of the week.

## Cooking pasta properly

### Use enough water

Pasta needs room to move as it cooks, otherwise it sticks together. A good guideline is about 4 quarts (4 liters) of water for 1 pound (450 g) of pasta. You can use a little less if you stir often, and some cooks deliberately use less water so it becomes starchier for the sauce. What matters most is that the pasta is fully submerged and has space to move.

### Salt the water well

Pasta absorbs water as it cooks, and this is your only chance to season the pasta itself. Add about 1 to 1½ tablespoons of kosher salt to 4 quarts of water, or roughly 1 tablespoon of table salt. The water should taste pleasantly seasoned, like a mild soup, though not as salty as seawater. Add salt once the water is boiling so it dissolves quickly.

You do not need to add oil to the water. It floats on top and makes the pasta slippery, which stops the sauce from clinging.

### Stir and time it

Add the pasta once the water is at a rolling boil, and stir during the first minute or two, when pasta is most likely to stick. Keep the water boiling.

Package directions give a cooking time, but start tasting a couple of minutes earlier. Perfectly cooked pasta is **al dente**, meaning "to the tooth": tender but with a slight firmness in the center. If you plan to finish the pasta in the sauce, which you should, drain it about one minute before it is fully al dente. It will finish cooking in the pan.

### Save the pasta water

Before draining, scoop out about a cup of the cooking water. This cloudy, starchy water is one of the most important ingredients in Italian cooking. The starch released by the pasta acts as a thickener and emulsifier, helping fat and water combine into a smooth, glossy sauce that clings to every strand.

Do not rinse cooked pasta, except for cold pasta salads. Rinsing washes away the starch that helps the sauce stick.

## Bringing pasta and sauce together

The biggest difference between home pasta and restaurant pasta is what happens in the last two minutes. Instead of pouring sauce over a pile of drained noodles, transfer the slightly undercooked pasta directly into the pan with the sauce. Add a splash of pasta water and toss everything together over medium heat for a minute or two.

During this time, the pasta absorbs flavor from the sauce, the starch thickens it, and the fat and water emulsify into a creamy coating. If the sauce looks dry, add more pasta water. If it looks too loose, keep tossing over the heat. When the sauce coats the pasta and looks glossy rather than watery or oily, it is ready.

Add cheese off the heat or over very low heat, a little at a time, tossing constantly. High heat makes cheese clump and turn stringy.

## Matching pasta shapes to sauces

There are hundreds of pasta shapes, but a simple rule helps: match the shape to the texture of the sauce.

- **Long, thin pasta** such as spaghetti, linguine and angel hair suits smooth, light sauces based on oil, tomato or cream that coat each strand.
- **Wide, flat ribbons** such as fettuccine, tagliatelle and pappardelle hold richer cream and meat sauces.
- **Short tubes** such as penne, rigatoni and ziti catch chunky sauces and work well in baked dishes.
- **Twists and shells** such as fusilli, rotini, orecchiette and conchiglie trap small pieces of vegetables, sausage and pesto in their curves.

These are guidelines, not laws. Use what you have.

## Six pantry pasta formulas

Each of these feeds about four people with 1 pound (450 g) of dried pasta and can be made while the pasta boils.

### 1. Garlic, oil and chili (aglio, olio e peperoncino)

This classic is made almost entirely from pantry staples. While the spaghetti cooks, gently heat about 1/3 cup (80 ml) of olive oil in a large pan with 4 to 6 thinly sliced garlic cloves and a pinch of chili flakes. Cook over low heat until the garlic is pale golden, not brown, which would make it bitter. Add the drained pasta and a generous splash of pasta water, and toss vigorously until the oil and water emulsify into a light sauce. Finish with chopped parsley and, if you like, a squeeze of lemon.

### 2. Quick tomato sauce

Warm olive oil with a couple of sliced garlic cloves, add a 28-ounce (800 g) can of whole or crushed tomatoes and a pinch of salt, and simmer for 15 minutes, crushing the tomatoes with a spoon. A knob of butter or half an onion simmered in the sauce and removed before serving adds sweetness and depth. Toss with pasta and a splash of pasta water, and serve with grated Parmesan and basil.

### 3. Cacio e pepe style

For a simple but rich dish, toast plenty of freshly cracked black pepper in a pan with a little butter or olive oil. Add the pasta and a good splash of pasta water. Off the heat, gradually add finely grated Pecorino Romano or Parmesan, tossing constantly and adding more pasta water as needed until creamy. Finely grated cheese and gentle heat are essential to avoid clumps.

### 4. Lemon, butter and Parmesan

Melt butter in a pan, add the zest of one lemon and a splash of pasta water. Toss in the pasta, then add grated Parmesan and the juice of half a lemon. Toss until glossy, adjust with more pasta water and season with black pepper. Add frozen peas, spinach or leftover roasted vegetables for a fuller meal.

### 5. Tuna, capers and olives

Cook garlic and chili flakes in olive oil, add a can of good-quality tuna in oil with a few spoonfuls of capers and chopped olives. Stir in a spoonful of tomato paste or a few chopped canned tomatoes, then toss with the pasta and pasta water. Finish with parsley and lemon zest. Breadcrumbs toasted in olive oil make a crunchy topping.

### 6. Chickpea and greens

Brown a can of drained chickpeas in olive oil with garlic and a pinch of chili flakes until slightly crisp. Add a large handful of spinach, kale or any greens and cook until wilted. Toss with short pasta, pasta water and plenty of grated cheese. This version is filling, inexpensive and packed with fiber.

## Pantry staples for pasta nights

Keeping a few basics on hand means you can always make dinner:

- Several shapes of dried pasta, long and short.
- Canned tomatoes, tomato paste and passata.
- Good olive oil and a stick of butter in the fridge.
- Garlic, onions and lemons.
- Parmesan or Pecorino in a piece, which keeps for weeks in the refrigerator.
- Chili flakes and black peppercorns.
- Canned tuna, anchovies, capers and olives.
- Canned beans and chickpeas.
- Frozen peas and spinach.
- Dried breadcrumbs for crunchy toppings.

## Making pasta a balanced meal

Pasta does not need to be heavy. To make it a well-rounded dinner, add vegetables directly to the sauce or toss them with the pasta in the last minutes of boiling: broccoli florets, green beans, asparagus and spinach can all cook in the same pot. Add protein with beans, chickpeas, canned fish, eggs, sausage or leftover chicken. Whole-wheat pasta and legume pastas made from chickpeas or lentils offer more fiber, though they cook differently, so watch the time closely.

## Common pasta mistakes

- **Not enough salt in the water:** Pasta tastes flat no matter how good the sauce is.
- **Overcooking:** Pasta continues to cook in the sauce and on the plate. Drain it a minute early.
- **Draining all the water:** Without pasta water, sauces are often too thick or oily.
- **Adding oil to the water:** It prevents the sauce from clinging.
- **Too much sauce:** Italian-style pasta is coated in sauce, not drowning in it.
- **Adding cheese over high heat:** It turns stringy. Add it off the heat with a splash of water.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does my pasta stick together?**
Usually because there was not enough water, it was not stirred at the start, or it sat in the colander too long after draining. Transfer it directly into the sauce.

**How much pasta should I cook per person?**
About 3 to 4 ounces (85 to 115 g) of dried pasta per person for a main course, less if it is a side dish or packed with vegetables.

**Can I cook pasta ahead of time?**
For best results, cook it fresh. If needed, slightly undercook it, toss it with a little oil, cool it quickly and refrigerate. Reheat it in boiling water or directly in the sauce.

**Is fresh pasta better than dried?**
Not better, just different. Fresh egg pasta is tender and suits delicate butter and cream sauces. Dried pasta is firmer and ideal for oil-based, tomato and chunky sauces.

**How do I fix a sauce that is too thick or too oily?**
Add pasta water a splash at a time and toss vigorously over medium heat. The starch helps the fat and water come together into a smooth sauce.

## The bottom line

Great weeknight pasta comes from a few simple habits: plenty of well-salted water, pasta drained just before al dente, a cup of starchy water saved, and a final toss of pasta and sauce together in the pan. With a well-stocked pantry, you can make garlic and chili spaghetti, a quick tomato sauce, a lemony butter pasta or a hearty chickpea and greens dish in the time it takes the water to boil. Master these basics, and pasta night will be fast, flexible and genuinely delicious every time.
