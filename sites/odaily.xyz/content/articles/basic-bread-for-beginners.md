Few things in the kitchen are as satisfying as pulling your own loaf of bread from the oven. The smell fills the house, the crust crackles as it cools, and the first slice with butter tastes better than almost anything you can buy. Many people think homemade bread is difficult or takes all day. In reality, a basic yeast bread needs only four ingredients and about twenty minutes of hands-on work. The rest is waiting while the yeast does its job. This guide takes you through a simple, reliable loaf step by step and explains what happens at each stage, so you understand not only what to do but why.

## The four ingredients

A basic bread dough needs only flour, water, yeast and salt.

- **Flour** provides structure. When flour is mixed with water, two proteins, glutenin and gliadin, form gluten, a stretchy network that traps gas and lets the bread rise. Bread flour has more protein than all-purpose flour, giving a chewier loaf with more volume, but all-purpose flour also makes good bread.
- **Water** hydrates the flour, activates the yeast and allows gluten to form. Lukewarm water, around 90 to 100 °F (32 to 38 °C), helps yeast get started.
- **Yeast** is a living microorganism. It feeds on sugars in the flour and produces carbon dioxide gas and alcohol. The gas makes the dough rise and the fermentation develops flavor.
- **Salt** seasons the bread, strengthens the gluten and slows the yeast slightly, which leads to a more even rise and better flavor.

## Types of yeast

- **Instant yeast**, also called rapid-rise or bread machine yeast, can be mixed directly into the flour.
- **Active dry yeast** has larger granules. Many bakers dissolve it in lukewarm water for a few minutes before adding it to the flour, although modern active dry yeast can often be mixed in directly.
- **Fresh yeast** comes in moist cakes and must be refrigerated. Use about two to three times the weight of dry yeast.

A standard packet of dry yeast in the United States contains 2¼ teaspoons, about 7 grams.

## A simple sandwich or round loaf

This recipe makes one medium loaf.

- 500 g (about 4 cups) bread flour or all-purpose flour
- 350 g (about 1½ cups) lukewarm water
- 7 g (2¼ teaspoons or one packet) instant or active dry yeast
- 10 g (about 1¾ teaspoons) fine salt
- Optional: 1 tablespoon olive oil or softened butter and 1 tablespoon sugar or honey for a softer crust

Bakers often express recipes as percentages of flour weight, called baker's percentages. In this recipe, the water is 70 percent and the salt 2 percent of the flour weight. These ratios make it easy to scale the recipe up or down.

## Step 1: Mix

In a large bowl, combine the flour, yeast and salt. If using active dry yeast and you prefer to activate it, dissolve it in the lukewarm water for five minutes until slightly foamy. Pour the water into the flour mixture, add the oil and sugar if using, and stir with a wooden spoon or your hand until no dry flour remains. The dough will look shaggy and sticky. Cover the bowl and let it rest for 15 to 20 minutes.

This short rest, sometimes called autolyse, gives the flour time to absorb the water. Gluten begins to form on its own, making kneading easier.

## Step 2: Knead

Kneading develops the gluten network that gives bread its structure. Turn the dough onto a lightly floured counter. Push the dough away from you with the heel of your hand, fold it back over itself, turn it a quarter turn and repeat. Continue for about 8 to 10 minutes by hand, or 5 to 7 minutes in a stand mixer with a dough hook on low to medium speed.

At first the dough will be sticky and rough. As you knead, it becomes smoother, more elastic and less sticky. Try not to add too much extra flour, because it makes the bread dry. A bench scraper helps handle sticky dough.

**The windowpane test:** Take a small piece of dough and gently stretch it between your fingers. If it stretches thin enough to let light through without tearing, the gluten is well developed. If it tears easily, knead a few more minutes.

## Step 3: First rise

Shape the dough into a ball and place it in a lightly oiled bowl. Cover with a lid, plastic wrap or a damp towel. Let it rise in a warm place, ideally around 75 to 80 °F (24 to 27 °C), until it has roughly doubled in size. This usually takes 1 to 2 hours, depending on the temperature.

During this time, yeast ferments the dough, producing gas that inflates the gluten network and developing flavor. Cooler temperatures slow the rise but produce more flavor. Many bakers let dough rise slowly in the refrigerator overnight for a deeper taste.

**How to tell if dough has risen enough:** Gently press a floured finger about half an inch (1 cm) into the dough. If the indentation springs back slowly and leaves a small dent, the dough is ready. If it springs back immediately, it needs more time. If it does not spring back at all and the dough sighs and deflates, it has over-risen; shape it and bake it anyway, and next time shorten the rise.

## Step 4: Shape

Gently turn the dough out onto a lightly floured surface and press it to release large gas bubbles.

**For a sandwich loaf:** Press the dough into a rectangle about as wide as your loaf pan. Roll it up tightly from the short end, pinching the seam closed. Place it seam-side down in a greased 9-by-5-inch (23-by-13 cm) loaf pan.

**For a round loaf (boule):** Fold the edges of the dough toward the center, flip it over and use your hands to rotate and tuck it against the counter until it forms a tight ball with a smooth surface. Place it on a sheet of parchment paper.

Shaping creates surface tension, which helps the loaf hold its shape and rise upward in the oven.

## Step 5: Second rise (proof)

Cover the shaped dough loosely and let it rise again until puffy and about 1½ times its original size, usually 45 minutes to 1 hour. For a loaf pan, the dough should rise about an inch (2.5 cm) above the rim. Use the finger test again.

During this time, preheat the oven to 425 °F (220 °C) for a round loaf, or 375 °F (190 °C) for a softer sandwich loaf. If you have a Dutch oven, place it in the oven to preheat for round loaves.

## Step 6: Score and bake

Just before baking, use a very sharp knife or a razor blade to make one or several shallow cuts, about 1/4 to 1/2 inch (5 mm to 1 cm) deep, across the top of the loaf. Scoring controls where the bread expands in the oven and gives it an attractive look.

**For a round loaf in a Dutch oven:** Carefully lift the dough on its parchment paper into the hot pot, cover it and bake for 20 minutes. The lid traps steam, which keeps the crust soft at first so the bread can expand. Remove the lid and bake for another 15 to 25 minutes until deep golden brown.

**For a sandwich loaf:** Bake in the pan for 30 to 40 minutes until golden and hollow-sounding when tapped.

## Step 7: Check doneness

The most reliable test is internal temperature. Insert an instant-read thermometer into the center of the loaf. Lean breads like this are done at about 200 to 210 °F (93 to 99 °C). Enriched breads with milk, butter or eggs are done at about 190 °F (88 °C).

Another test is to tap the bottom of the loaf. A hollow sound suggests it is done.

## Step 8: Cool before cutting

It is tempting to slice the bread immediately, but the inside is still cooking from the remaining heat and moisture is redistributing. Cutting too early makes the crumb gummy. Let the bread cool on a wire rack for at least one hour.

## Troubleshooting

- **Dense loaf:** Not enough kneading, too much flour, under-risen dough or old yeast.
- **Bread did not rise:** Expired yeast, water that was too hot, a cold room or too much salt touching the yeast directly.
- **Flat, spreading round loaf:** Over-proofed dough, weak shaping or too much water.
- **Pale crust:** Oven not hot enough or bread removed too early.
- **Large holes under the crust:** Dough not degassed properly during shaping or proofed too briefly.

## Storing homemade bread

Homemade bread has no preservatives and stales faster than store-bought bread. Store it at room temperature, cut side down on a board or wrapped in a cloth or paper bag, for two to three days. Plastic bags keep it soft but make the crust soggy. Avoid the refrigerator, which makes bread stale faster. For longer storage, slice the bread, wrap it well and freeze it for up to three months. Toast slices directly from frozen.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use all-purpose flour instead of bread flour?**
Yes. The bread will be slightly softer with a little less volume, but still delicious.

**How do I know if my yeast is still good?**
Dissolve a little yeast in lukewarm water with a pinch of sugar. If it foams within 10 minutes, it is active.

**Can I make the dough the day before?**
Yes. After kneading, refrigerate the dough in a covered bowl overnight. The slow rise improves flavor. Let it warm slightly before shaping.

**Why is my bread crust too hard?**
A long bake at high heat produces a thick crust. For a softer crust, bake at a lower temperature, add a little fat and sugar to the dough or brush the warm loaf with melted butter.

**Do I need a stand mixer?**
No. Kneading by hand works perfectly and helps you learn how good dough feels.

## The bottom line

Baking a basic loaf is easier than it looks. Mix flour, water, yeast and salt, knead until smooth and elastic, let the dough rise until doubled, shape it, let it rise again and bake it in a hot oven until it reaches about 200 °F (93 °C) inside. Let it cool before slicing. Once you understand each stage, you can experiment with whole-grain flours, seeds, herbs and longer fermentation, and enjoy fresh homemade bread whenever you like.
