Most recipes are written for four to six people, and most grocery packages are sized for families. If you live alone or with one other person, this creates a familiar set of problems: a head of lettuce that wilts before you finish it, a pot of soup you eat for five days in a row, and the temptation to skip cooking altogether because it seems like too much effort for one plate. Yet cooking for one or two can be wonderfully freeing. You can eat exactly what you like, when you like, and experiment without worrying about anyone else's preferences. With a few smart strategies, you can cook satisfying meals, waste very little and avoid getting tired of leftovers.

## Change how you think about recipes

When cooking for one or two, it helps to stop thinking about single recipes and start thinking about flexible components. Instead of cooking a whole dish that serves six, cook a few building blocks that can be combined in different ways over several days. A pot of rice, a tray of roasted vegetables and a cooked protein can become a grain bowl, a burrito, fried rice and a salad.

At the same time, some recipes are perfectly suited to small portions and quick cooking: omelets, stir-fries, pasta, quesadillas, fish fillets, grain bowls and salads. These form the backbone of a small household's weeknight cooking.

## How to scale recipes down

Many recipes can be halved or quartered easily, but a few things need attention:

- **Use a smaller pan.** If you halve a recipe but use the same large pan, liquids evaporate faster and food can burn. Choose a pan where the food fills the surface without crowding.
- **Watch cooking times.** Smaller amounts often cook faster in the oven and on the stove. Check earlier than the recipe says.
- **Season to taste.** Halving spices and salt exactly is a good start, but always taste and adjust.
- **Handle eggs cleverly.** To halve a recipe that uses one egg, beat the egg and use about half, roughly 2 tablespoons. Save the rest for scrambled eggs.
- **Baking is trickier.** Scaling baking recipes works best with a kitchen scale and the right pan size. Small loaf pans, muffin tins and 6-inch (15 cm) cake pans are useful.

Some dishes, such as soups, stews, sauces and casseroles, are actually easier to make in larger amounts. For these, cook a full batch and freeze portions.

## Shop small and smart

Shopping for one or two requires a different approach than shopping for a family.

- **Buy loose produce.** Pick exactly the number of carrots, apples or potatoes you need instead of buying a large bag.
- **Use the salad bar or bulk bins.** They let you buy small amounts of different vegetables, grains, nuts and spices.
- **Ask at the counter.** Butchers and fish counters will often sell a single chicken breast, a few sausages or one fish fillet.
- **Choose versatile ingredients.** Buy ingredients that can be used in several dishes. A bunch of parsley can go into a salad, a pasta, a sauce and an omelet.
- **Shop more often for fresh food.** A small shopping trip twice a week for fresh produce can reduce waste compared with one big trip.
- **Compare unit prices.** Small packages are often more expensive per unit. Sometimes it is cheaper to buy a larger package and freeze part of it.

## The freezer is your best friend

For small households, the freezer works like a second pantry. It allows you to buy larger, cheaper packages and cook full recipes without waste.

What freezes well for small households:

- **Meat and fish:** Divide packages into single portions, wrap tightly and freeze flat.
- **Bread:** Slice a loaf before freezing and take out one or two slices at a time. Toast directly from frozen.
- **Cooked grains:** Freeze portions of rice, quinoa or farro in flat bags.
- **Soups, stews and sauces:** Freeze in single or double portions.
- **Herbs:** Chop fresh herbs and freeze them in ice cube trays with a little oil or water.
- **Fruit:** Freeze ripe bananas and berries for smoothies and baking.
- **Grated cheese, tomato paste and pesto:** Freeze in small portions or tablespoon-sized dollops.

Label everything with the contents and date, and keep a simple list on the freezer door so you remember what is inside.

## Plan with overlap

A short plan helps avoid waste. Choose meals that share ingredients so that nothing is left over. For example, if you buy a bunch of cilantro, a bag of spinach and a pack of chicken thighs:

- **Monday:** Chicken thighs with rice and sautéed spinach.
- **Tuesday:** Chicken and spinach quesadillas with cilantro salsa.
- **Wednesday:** Fried rice with the remaining rice, spinach and an egg, topped with cilantro.

Each ingredient is used several times in different ways, and the meals still feel varied.

## Cook components, not just meals

A component-based approach is especially useful for small households. Once or twice a week, prepare a few versatile building blocks:

- **A grain:** rice, quinoa, couscous or pasta.
- **A protein:** roasted chicken thighs, hard-boiled eggs, a pot of lentils, baked tofu or a can of beans.
- **Vegetables:** a tray of roasted vegetables and a container of washed greens.
- **A sauce:** vinaigrette, yogurt sauce, peanut sauce or pesto.

With these in the fridge, you can assemble different meals in a few minutes. A grain bowl one day, a wrap the next and a frittata the day after use the same components in completely different ways.

## Quick meals for one or two

These meals are naturally sized for one or two people and take little time:

- **Omelet or frittata** with whatever vegetables and cheese you have.
- **Pasta with garlic, oil and greens**, using a small amount of dried pasta.
- **Stir-fry** with a handful of vegetables and a single portion of protein.
- **Fish fillet** baked or pan-fried with a lemon and herb sauce and a side of vegetables.
- **Quesadilla** with beans, cheese and salsa.
- **Grain bowl** with leftover grains, vegetables, an egg and a sauce.
- **Baked sweet potato** topped with beans, cheese, yogurt and greens.
- **Soup** from the freezer with toast.
- **Shakshuka** made in a small skillet with one or two eggs.

## Making leftovers enjoyable

Leftovers are inevitable, but they do not have to be boring. Transform them instead of simply reheating:

- Turn roast chicken into a salad, soup or tacos.
- Turn leftover vegetables into a frittata or blend them into soup.
- Turn rice into fried rice or rice pudding.
- Turn chili into stuffed peppers or baked potatoes.
- Freeze part of a dish immediately after cooking, so you can enjoy it again in two weeks instead of tomorrow.

## Equipment for small households

A few smaller tools make cooking for one or two easier:

- An 8-inch (20 cm) skillet and a small saucepan.
- A quarter-sheet baking pan, about 9 by 13 inches (23 by 33 cm), for roasting small amounts.
- Small baking dishes or ramekins for individual casseroles and baked eggs.
- A toaster oven or air fryer, which heats up quickly and uses less energy for small amounts.
- Good storage containers in single-portion sizes.

## Making meals feel special

When cooking for yourself, it is easy to eat standing at the counter or in front of a screen. Taking a few minutes to set the table, use a nice plate and sit down can make a simple meal feel more satisfying. Cooking for one is also an opportunity to try new recipes and ingredients without having to please anyone else. For couples, cooking together can be a relaxing way to share the end of the day.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I avoid wasting fresh produce?**
Buy loose produce in the amounts you need, plan meals that share ingredients, store produce properly and freeze anything you cannot use in time.

**Is it cheaper to cook for one than to eat out?**
Yes, especially if you plan, use the freezer and buy versatile ingredients. A home-cooked meal usually costs a fraction of a restaurant meal.

**What should I do with half a can of beans or tomatoes?**
Transfer the leftovers to a covered container in the fridge and use them within three to four days, or freeze them in a small bag.

**How can I enjoy cooking when it is just for me?**
Treat it as time for yourself. Try new recipes, listen to music or a podcast and cook foods you love.

**Which foods are best to freeze in single portions?**
Soups, stews, sauces, cooked grains, meat and fish portions, bread, muffins and burritos all freeze well in individual portions.

## The bottom line

Cooking for one or two is about flexibility. Scale recipes down with smaller pans and careful timing, shop for small amounts of versatile ingredients, and use your freezer to store portions of meat, bread, grains, soups and sauces. Plan meals that share ingredients, cook components that can be mixed in different ways and transform leftovers into new dishes. With these habits, you will waste less, spend less and enjoy delicious, varied meals that suit exactly your taste.
