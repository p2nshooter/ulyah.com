A warming curry is one of the most comforting dinners there is, and it does not have to be a weekend project. With a few pantry staples and a reliable method, you can have a fragrant, saucy curry on the table in under an hour on a weeknight, often in less. Curries are also wonderfully flexible: they welcome whatever vegetables are in the refrigerator, work with chicken, fish, tofu, lentils or chickpeas, and taste even better the next day.

"Curry" is a broad word that covers an enormous variety of dishes from South Asia, Southeast Asia, the Caribbean, East Africa, Japan and beyond, each with its own traditions. This guide does not try to replace those traditions. Instead, it offers a basic method for simple, home-style curries on busy evenings: how to build a flavor base, how to use whole and ground spices or ready-made pastes, how to choose a sauce base, which proteins and vegetables to use, how to balance heat and richness, and what to serve alongside.

## The basic method

Most simple curries follow the same sequence:

1. **Cook the aromatics**: onion, garlic, ginger and sometimes chili.
2. **Bloom the spices** or fry a curry paste in oil.
3. **Add the sauce base**: tomatoes, coconut milk, yogurt, broth or a combination.
4. **Add the main ingredients**: protein and vegetables.
5. **Simmer** until everything is cooked and the flavors have come together.
6. **Finish**: adjust salt, acidity and heat, and add fresh herbs.

Once you know this sequence, you can create many different curries simply by changing the spices, the sauce and the main ingredients.

## Step 1: the aromatic base

- **Onions** give body and sweetness. Cook them slowly in oil until soft and golden; this takes time, but it is where much of the flavor comes from. Our guide to [caramelization](/kitchen/caramelization-explained/) explains what happens as onions brown.
- **Garlic and ginger**, grated or finely chopped, add warmth and fragrance. Add them after the onions are soft, since they burn more quickly.
- **Fresh chilies**, if you like heat.

To save time, you can buy ready-made garlic and ginger paste, or make a batch and freeze it in small portions.

## Step 2: spices or paste

### Using ground and whole spices

Spices release more flavor when they are cooked briefly in oil, a step often called blooming or tempering.

- **Whole spices**, such as cumin seeds, mustard seeds, cardamom pods or cinnamon sticks, can be added to hot oil at the start, until they sizzle and smell fragrant.
- **Ground spices**, such as turmeric, ground coriander, ground cumin, paprika, chili powder or garam masala, are usually added after the onions and cooked for a minute, stirring constantly so they do not burn.

A simple starting blend for a mild, home-style curry might combine ground cumin, ground coriander and turmeric, with chili to taste and a little garam masala added near the end.

### Using curry pastes and powders

Ready-made curry pastes and powders are a perfectly good shortcut on a weeknight.

- **Fry the paste** in a little oil for a minute or two before adding liquid, which deepens its flavor.
- **Taste the paste** first; brands vary widely in heat and saltiness.
- **Add fresh aromatics** such as onion, garlic and ginger to lift the flavor.

Our guide to [buying, storing and using spices](/kitchen/spices-buying-storing-and-using/) explains how to keep spices fresh and flavorful.

## Step 3: the sauce base

| Base | Character | Works well with |
|---|---|---|
| Tomatoes (canned or fresh) | tangy, rich | chicken, chickpeas, lentils, vegetables |
| Coconut milk | creamy, mellow, slightly sweet | fish, shrimp, tofu, sweet potato, squash |
| Yogurt | tangy, creamy | chicken, lamb; add gently to avoid curdling |
| Broth or water | lighter | lentils, vegetables |
| A combination | balanced | most curries |

### Tips for each base

- **Tomatoes:** cook them with the spices until they break down and the oil starts to separate at the edges, which is a sign the base is well cooked.
- **Coconut milk:** shake the can well; simmer gently rather than boiling hard.
- **Yogurt:** stir it in off the heat, a spoonful at a time, or mix it with a little flour or cornstarch first, which helps prevent curdling. Our guide to [why sauces split](/kitchen/why-sauces-split-emulsions/) explains why dairy can separate.

## Step 4: protein and vegetables

### Proteins

- **Chicken thighs**, cut into pieces, stay tender during simmering. Breast meat cooks faster but can dry out.
- **Fish and shrimp** cook very quickly; add them near the end.
- **Tofu**, pressed and cubed, can be pan-fried first for better texture.
- **Chickpeas and other beans**, canned or home-cooked, are quick and filling.
- **Red lentils** cook directly in the sauce in about twenty minutes and thicken it beautifully; see our guide to [cooking dried beans and lentils](/kitchen/how-to-cook-dried-beans-and-lentils/).
- **Eggs**, hard-boiled and halved, make a quick, satisfying curry.

### Vegetables

- **Hardy vegetables**, such as potatoes, sweet potatoes, carrots and squash, need longer cooking; cut them into small pieces and add them early.
- **Medium vegetables**, such as cauliflower, green beans and peppers, go in midway.
- **Tender vegetables and greens**, such as spinach, peas and zucchini, go in near the end.
- **Frozen vegetables** are a great weeknight shortcut.

## Step 5: simmer

Simmer the curry gently, partly covered, until the protein is cooked through and the vegetables are tender. Stir occasionally and add a splash of water if it becomes too thick. Most weeknight curries need between fifteen and thirty minutes of simmering after the main ingredients go in.

If you are using chicken, make sure it is cooked through, following food safety guidance.

## Step 6: finish and balance

Taste the curry before serving and adjust:

- **Salt:** most curries need more than you expect; add gradually.
- **Acidity:** a squeeze of lemon or lime juice brightens the flavor, especially in rich, creamy curries.
- **Sweetness:** a pinch of sugar can balance very tangy tomatoes.
- **Heat:** add chili if it is too mild; if it is too hot, stir in a little yogurt, coconut milk or cream, or serve with plenty of rice and a cooling side.
- **Fresh herbs:** a handful of chopped cilantro or a few basil leaves.
- **A final pinch of garam masala** or a drizzle of spiced oil, in some styles.

Our guide to [how to season food](/kitchen/how-to-season-food/) explains how salt, acid and fat work together.

## What to serve with curry

- **Rice**: plain basmati or jasmine rice is classic; see our guide to [cooking rice perfectly](/kitchen/how-to-cook-rice-perfectly/).
- **Flatbreads**: naan, roti or quick homemade flatbreads; our guide to [flatbreads and pizza dough](/kitchen/flatbreads-and-pizza-dough/) shows how to make them.
- **Cooling sides**: yogurt with cucumber, sliced onions with lemon, a simple salad.
- **Chutneys and pickles**, for sweetness and tang.

## Three weeknight curry ideas

1. **Chickpea and spinach curry:** onion, garlic, ginger, cumin, coriander and turmeric, canned tomatoes, chickpeas and spinach, finished with lemon.
2. **Coconut fish curry:** onion, garlic, ginger, a spoonful of curry paste, coconut milk, chunks of white fish and green beans, finished with lime and cilantro.
3. **Red lentil curry:** onion, garlic, ginger, cumin, turmeric and chili, red lentils, water or broth, finished with a spiced oil and cilantro.

## Building a curry shelf

A few long-lasting ingredients make weeknight curries almost effortless: canned tomatoes, coconut milk, red lentils, canned chickpeas, onions, garlic, ginger, a handful of ground and whole spices, a jar of curry paste and a bag of frozen spinach or peas. With these in the cupboard and freezer, a curry is always within reach, even when the refrigerator looks empty.

## Make ahead and leftovers

Curries are excellent for batch cooking. The flavors deepen overnight, and most freeze well, especially those based on tomatoes, lentils and beans. Cool them quickly, refrigerate or freeze in portions and reheat until steaming hot. Coconut-based and yogurt-based sauces can separate slightly when reheated; stir well and add a splash of water.

Our guides to [cook once, eat twice](/kitchen/cook-once-eat-twice/) and the [freezing food guide](/kitchen/freezing-food-guide/) explain how to make the most of leftovers.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it cheating to use curry paste?

Not at all. Pastes are a convenient way to get complex flavor quickly. Frying them well and adding fresh aromatics makes a big difference.

### How can I make a curry less spicy for children?

Leave out fresh chilies and chili powder, use mild spices and serve chili sauce at the table for adults.

### Why is my curry watery?

It may need more simmering to reduce, or a thickener such as red lentils or a little coconut cream. Our guide to [how starches thicken sauces](/kitchen/how-starches-thicken-sauces/) explains other options.

## The bottom line

A weeknight curry follows a simple sequence: cook onions, garlic and ginger, bloom spices or fry a paste, add a sauce base of tomatoes, coconut milk, yogurt or broth, add protein and vegetables in order of cooking time, simmer until tender and finish with salt, acid, herbs and adjusted heat. Use pastes and frozen vegetables as shortcuts, serve with rice, flatbreads and a cooling side, and make extra, because curries often taste even better the next day.
