Leftovers are one of the best things about home cooking. A big pot of soup can become lunch for days, last night's roast chicken can turn into tacos, and a well-stocked fridge of leftovers can save a hectic weeknight. But leftovers also carry a risk that many people underestimate. Foodborne illness often comes not from cooking itself but from what happens afterward: food left out too long, cooled too slowly, stored too long or reheated inadequately. Fortunately, a few simple habits keep leftovers both safe and delicious. This guide explains how bacteria grow, how to cool and store food properly, how long leftovers last and how to reheat them correctly.

## Why leftovers need care

Bacteria are everywhere, and many are harmless. But some, such as *Salmonella*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Clostridium perfringens*, *Bacillus cereus* and *Listeria monocytogenes*, can cause illness. Cooking kills many bacteria, but not all. Some form heat-resistant spores, and others can contaminate food after cooking from hands, utensils or surfaces.

Bacteria multiply fastest in the **danger zone**, between 40 °F and 140 °F (4 °C and 60 °C). Under ideal conditions, some can double in number in about 20 minutes. Certain bacteria also produce toxins that are not destroyed by reheating. That is why the key to leftover safety is limiting the time food spends in the danger zone.

## The two-hour rule

Refrigerate or freeze perishable food within **two hours** of cooking or taking it out of the fridge. If the temperature is above 90 °F (32 °C), such as at a summer picnic or in a hot car, the limit is **one hour**.

This includes the time food sits on the table during a meal, at a buffet or while you clean up. If you are not sure how long food has been out, it is safest to throw it away.

## Cooling food quickly

Hot food must pass through the danger zone as quickly as possible on its way to the refrigerator. Large amounts of hot food, such as a big pot of chili or a whole roast, cool very slowly, and the center can stay warm for many hours even in the fridge.

To cool food quickly:

- **Divide large amounts** into small, shallow containers, ideally no more than 2 inches (5 cm) deep.
- **Cut large pieces of meat** into smaller portions or slices.
- **Use an ice bath** for soups and stews: place the pot in a sink filled with ice water and stir until the food cools.
- **Leave space** between containers in the fridge, so cold air can circulate.
- **Refrigerate food while it is still warm.** You do not need to wait until it reaches room temperature. Modern refrigerators can handle warm food in small containers.

## Storing leftovers

- Use clean, airtight containers or wrap food tightly.
- Label containers with the contents and the date.
- Store leftovers on upper or middle shelves, above raw meat.
- Keep the refrigerator at 40 °F (4 °C) or below.
- Use clear containers so leftovers stay visible and are not forgotten.

## How long leftovers keep

Most cooked leftovers are safe in the refrigerator for **three to four days**. After that, the risk of foodborne illness increases, even if the food looks and smells fine. Some bacteria, like *Listeria*, can grow slowly even at refrigerator temperatures.

| Leftover | Refrigerator | Freezer for best quality |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cooked meat and poultry | 3 to 4 days | 2 to 6 months |
| Soups and stews | 3 to 4 days | 2 to 3 months |
| Cooked rice and pasta | 3 to 5 days, rice ideally sooner | 1 to 2 months |
| Pizza | 3 to 4 days | 1 to 2 months |
| Casseroles | 3 to 4 days | 2 to 3 months |
| Cooked vegetables | 3 to 4 days | 2 to 3 months |
| Cooked fish | 3 to 4 days | 4 to 6 months |

If you will not eat leftovers within this time, freeze them as soon as possible. Frozen food at 0 °F (−18 °C) stays safe indefinitely, though quality slowly declines.

## Foods that need extra care

Some leftovers deserve particular attention:

- **Cooked rice and pasta:** Rice can contain spores of *Bacillus cereus*, which survive cooking. If cooked rice is left at room temperature, the spores can germinate and produce toxins that reheating does not destroy. Cool rice quickly, refrigerate it within an hour or two and eat it within a few days.
- **Large pots of soups, stews and gravies:** *Clostridium perfringens* thrives in large batches of meat dishes that cool slowly. Divide them into small containers.
- **Seafood:** Eat leftover cooked fish and shellfish promptly.
- **Foods with eggs, mayonnaise or dairy:** Potato salad, egg salad and cream-based dishes should be kept cold and not left out.
- **Takeout and restaurant food:** Count the time from when the food was prepared, including transport. Refrigerate it as soon as you get home.

## Reheating leftovers safely

Reheat leftovers until they reach an internal temperature of **165 °F (74 °C)**, measured with a food thermometer in the thickest part. Sauces, soups and gravies should be brought to a rolling boil.

### In the microwave

Microwaves heat unevenly and can leave cold spots where bacteria survive.

- Cover food with a microwave-safe lid or wrap, leaving a small vent, to trap steam and heat food evenly.
- Spread food in an even layer and arrange thicker pieces toward the outside of the dish.
- Stir or rotate food halfway through heating.
- Let it stand for a minute or two after heating, because heat continues to spread.
- Check the temperature in several places.

### On the stove

Reheat soups, stews and sauces in a saucepan, stirring often, until they boil. Add a splash of water or stock to prevent sticking. Reheat stir-fries and fried rice in a hot skillet until steaming throughout.

### In the oven

Use an oven at 325 °F (160 °C) or higher. Cover food with foil to keep it moist, and uncover it at the end if you want crispness. The oven is good for casseroles, roasted meats and baked dishes, though it is slower.

### Slow cookers are not for reheating

Do not reheat leftovers in a slow cooker. It heats too slowly and can leave food in the danger zone for too long. Reheat food on the stove or in the microwave first, then keep it hot in a slow cooker at 140 °F (60 °C) or above.

## Reheat only once

Reheat only the portion you plan to eat, and avoid reheating the same leftovers repeatedly. Each cycle of cooling and reheating gives bacteria another chance to grow and reduces the quality of the food.

## When to throw food away

Throw leftovers away if:

- They have been in the refrigerator longer than three to four days.
- They were left at room temperature for more than two hours, or more than one hour in hot conditions.
- They smell sour or unusual, look slimy or have mold.
- You are not sure how long they have been stored.

Remember that dangerous bacteria often do not change the look, smell or taste of food. When in doubt, throw it out.

## Leftovers and vulnerable people

Some people are at higher risk of serious foodborne illness: pregnant people, infants and young children, older adults and people with weakened immune systems from illness or medical treatment. For them, it is especially important to follow leftover guidelines carefully, reheat food until steaming hot and avoid foods like cold deli meats and refrigerated smoked fish unless heated.

## Making leftovers enjoyable

Safe leftovers can also be delicious. Transform them into new meals: turn roast chicken into soup or tacos, roasted vegetables into a frittata or blended soup, rice into fried rice, and chili into stuffed potatoes. Adding fresh herbs, a squeeze of lemon or a crunchy topping brings leftovers back to life.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it safe to put hot food directly into the fridge?**
Yes. In fact, it is safer than leaving it out. Divide large amounts into shallow containers so they cool quickly.

**How long can leftovers stay in the fridge?**
Most cooked leftovers are safe for three to four days in a refrigerator at 40 °F (4 °C) or below.

**Why is leftover rice risky?**
Rice can contain bacterial spores that survive cooking and produce toxins if the rice is left warm for too long. Cool it quickly and refrigerate it promptly.

**Can I freeze leftovers after a few days in the fridge?**
Yes, as long as they are still within the three- to four-day window. Freezing earlier gives better quality.

**Does reheating kill all bacteria?**
Reheating to 165 °F (74 °C) kills most bacteria, but not toxins that some bacteria produce. That is why it is important to store food correctly in the first place.

## The bottom line

Safe leftovers depend on limiting the time food spends between 40 °F and 140 °F (4 °C and 60 °C). Refrigerate food within two hours, cool large batches quickly in shallow containers, label and store leftovers properly and eat them within three to four days, or freeze them. Reheat to 165 °F (74 °C) until steaming hot, reheat only once and throw away anything doubtful. With these habits, leftovers remain one of the most convenient and enjoyable parts of home cooking.
