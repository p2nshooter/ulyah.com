A warm biscuit split open with butter melting into its layers, or a tender scone with jam and cream, is one of the simplest pleasures of home baking. Both are quick breads, leavened with baking powder or baking soda rather than yeast, so they go from mixing bowl to table in about thirty minutes. Yet many home bakers end up with biscuits that are flat and dense or scones that are dry and crumbly. The secret to both lies in a few techniques shared with pie crust: keep the fat cold, mix the dough as little as possible and bake in a hot oven. This guide explains how biscuits and scones work and how to make them tall, flaky and tender every time.

## Biscuits and scones: what is the difference?

The words mean different things in different places. In the United States, a **biscuit** is a soft, flaky, savory quick bread often served with breakfast, fried chicken or gravy. In the United Kingdom and much of the Commonwealth, "biscuit" means a cookie, and the closest equivalent to an American biscuit is a **scone**.

The main differences:

- **American biscuits** are usually savory or plain, made with butter or shortening and buttermilk, with little or no sugar and no eggs. They are flaky and tall, with visible layers.
- **British-style scones** often include a little sugar and sometimes egg, and are typically made with milk. They are tender and slightly sweet, often served with clotted cream and jam.
- **American-style scones**, popular in coffee shops, are richer and sweeter, often with cream, eggs and add-ins like fruit or chocolate, and are frequently shaped into wedges.

Despite these differences, the methods are very similar.

## Why cold fat matters

As with pie crust, the flakiness of biscuits and scones comes from pieces of cold fat in the dough. In the hot oven, the fat melts and the water it contains turns into steam, which pushes the dough upward and creates layers. If the fat is too warm, it blends into the flour and the biscuits will be dense and greasy.

To keep the fat cold:

- Cut the butter into small cubes and chill it in the freezer for 15 minutes.
- Or freeze a stick of butter and grate it on the coarse side of a box grater directly into the flour.
- Work quickly and handle the dough lightly with cool hands.
- If the kitchen is warm, chill the shaped biscuits for 15 minutes before baking.

## Why gentle mixing matters

Flour contains proteins that form gluten when mixed with liquid. A little gluten gives biscuits structure, but too much makes them tough and chewy instead of tender. Mix the dough only until it barely comes together. It should look shaggy and a little rough. Small dry patches are fine; they will hydrate as you fold and pat.

## A basic buttermilk biscuit

This recipe makes about 8 to 10 biscuits.

- 2 cups (250 g) all-purpose flour
- 1 tablespoon baking powder
- 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
- 1 teaspoon salt
- 1 teaspoon sugar (optional)
- 1/2 cup (113 g, 1 stick) very cold unsalted butter
- 3/4 to 1 cup (180 to 240 ml) cold buttermilk
- 2 tablespoons melted butter for brushing

### Step 1: Prepare

Preheat the oven to 450 °F (230 °C). Line a baking sheet with parchment paper or use a cast-iron skillet.

### Step 2: Mix the dry ingredients

Whisk the flour, baking powder, baking soda, salt and sugar in a large bowl.

### Step 3: Add the butter

Cut the cold butter into the flour with a pastry cutter, two knives or your fingertips until the pieces are about the size of peas, with some larger pieces. Or grate frozen butter into the flour and toss it with a fork.

### Step 4: Add the buttermilk

Make a well in the center and pour in about 3/4 cup of buttermilk. Stir gently with a fork or spatula until the dough just comes together. If dry flour remains at the bottom, add more buttermilk a tablespoon at a time. The dough should be slightly sticky but hold together.

### Step 5: Fold for layers

Turn the dough onto a lightly floured surface. Pat it into a rectangle about 3/4 inch (2 cm) thick. Fold it in thirds like a letter, turn it 90 degrees and pat it out again. Repeat this folding three or four times. Each fold creates more layers of butter and dough, giving tall, flaky biscuits.

### Step 6: Cut

Pat the dough to a thickness of about 3/4 to 1 inch (2 to 2.5 cm). Use a sharp biscuit cutter or a glass dipped in flour, and **press straight down without twisting**. Twisting seals the edges and prevents the biscuits from rising properly. Alternatively, cut the dough into squares with a sharp knife, which avoids leftover scraps.

Gently press the scraps together once and cut more biscuits. These will be slightly less tender but still good.

### Step 7: Bake

Place the biscuits on the baking sheet. For soft sides, place them so they touch each other; for crisp sides, space them apart. Brush the tops with melted butter or buttermilk. Bake for 12 to 15 minutes until tall and golden brown. Brush with more melted butter after baking.

## Making scones

Scones follow the same method with a few changes. For about 8 scones:

- Use 2 cups (250 g) flour, 1 tablespoon baking powder, 1/2 teaspoon salt and 2 to 4 tablespoons sugar.
- Cut in 6 tablespoons (85 g) cold butter.
- Mix 1/2 cup (120 ml) cold milk or cream with 1 egg and add to the dry ingredients.
- Fold in add-ins such as dried fruit, berries, chocolate chips, citrus zest or grated cheese.
- Pat into a circle about 1 inch (2.5 cm) thick and cut into wedges, or cut rounds.
- Brush with milk or egg wash and sprinkle with coarse sugar.
- Bake at 400 to 425 °F (200 to 220 °C) for 15 to 20 minutes until golden.

Frozen berries should be added while still frozen, to prevent them from bleeding into the dough.

## Making buttermilk substitutes

Buttermilk adds tang, tenderness and acidity that reacts with baking soda. If you do not have any, make a substitute: add 1 tablespoon of lemon juice or white vinegar to a measuring cup, fill it with milk to the 1 cup mark and let it stand for five to ten minutes until slightly thickened. Plain yogurt thinned with a little milk also works.

## Troubleshooting

- **Flat biscuits:** The butter was too warm, the dough was overmixed or rolled too thin, the cutter was twisted, or the baking powder was old.
- **Tough biscuits:** The dough was overmixed or too much flour was added during folding.
- **Dry, crumbly scones:** Too much flour, not enough liquid or overbaking.
- **Biscuits spread sideways:** The dough was too wet or too warm. Chill before baking.
- **Bitter or metallic taste:** Too much baking powder or baking soda. Measure leaveners carefully.
- **Pale tops:** The oven was not hot enough. Brush with butter or egg wash before baking.

## Making biscuits and scones ahead

Unbaked biscuits and scones freeze beautifully. Cut them, place them on a tray and freeze until firm, then store them in a freezer bag for up to three months. Bake them directly from frozen at the same temperature, adding a few minutes to the baking time. This lets you enjoy freshly baked biscuits on a weekday morning with almost no effort.

Baked biscuits and scones are best eaten the day they are made. Leftovers can be wrapped and stored at room temperature for a day or two and refreshed in a warm oven for a few minutes.

## Serving ideas

- **Breakfast biscuits** with butter and honey, or split and filled with eggs and cheese.
- **Biscuits and gravy**, a classic Southern dish with sausage gravy.
- **Strawberry shortcake**, with sweetened biscuits, fresh berries and whipped cream.
- **Scones with clotted cream and jam**, in the British tradition.
- **Savory scones** with cheddar and chives, served alongside soup.

## Frequently asked questions

**What flour is best for biscuits?**
All-purpose flour works well. Many Southern bakers prefer a soft, low-protein flour, which produces especially tender biscuits.

**Can I make biscuits without buttermilk?**
Yes. Use milk with a little lemon juice or vinegar, or thinned yogurt. If you use plain milk, replace the baking soda with extra baking powder.

**Why should I not twist the biscuit cutter?**
Twisting seals the edges, which prevents the layers from separating and rising fully.

**Can I use a food processor?**
Yes, to cut the butter into the flour with short pulses. Mix the liquid in by hand to avoid overmixing.

**How do I keep biscuits soft?**
Bake them close together on the pan so their sides touch, and brush them with melted butter as soon as they come out of the oven.

## The bottom line

Great biscuits and scones depend on cold fat, gentle mixing and a hot oven. Keep the butter cold and in visible pieces, add just enough buttermilk or milk to bring the dough together, fold the dough a few times for layers and cut straight down without twisting. Bake at high heat until tall and golden. Freeze unbaked biscuits for quick breakfasts, and enjoy them warm with butter, jam or gravy.
