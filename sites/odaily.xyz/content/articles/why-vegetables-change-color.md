Drop a handful of green beans into boiling water and, within a minute, they turn a brilliant, almost glowing green. Leave them there too long and they fade to a sad olive gray. Red cabbage can turn blue or green in some cooking water. Purple green beans turn green when cooked. Cauliflower can go slightly yellow, beets stain everything pink, and cut apples and potatoes darken on the board. None of this is random. Each color comes from a particular family of pigments, and each family reacts in its own way to heat, acid, alkaline water and time.

This guide explains the main pigments in vegetables and fruit, why they change during cooking, and practical ways to keep greens bright, reds vivid, whites clean and oranges glowing. It also covers browning, both the delicious kind from roasting and the unwanted kind on cut produce.

## The four main pigment families

| Pigment family | Colors | Found in | Sensitive to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chlorophyll | green | broccoli, spinach, green beans, peas, herbs | heat over time, acid |
| Carotenoids | orange, yellow, some reds | carrots, squash, sweet potatoes, peppers, tomatoes | relatively stable; dissolve in fat |
| Anthocyanins | red, purple, blue | red cabbage, purple carrots and potatoes, berries, red onions | acid and alkali; dissolve in water |
| Anthoxanthins | white, cream, pale yellow | cauliflower, onions, potatoes, white cabbage | alkali, some metals |

Beets and chard stems owe their vivid red and yellow colors to another group, **betalains**, which bleed easily into water and can fade with long cooking.

## Green vegetables: chlorophyll

### Why greens get brighter first

Raw green vegetables contain tiny pockets of air between their cells, which slightly cloud the color. When you drop them into boiling water, that air expands and escapes, and the green suddenly appears brighter and more vivid. This is why briefly blanched vegetables look so fresh.

### Why they turn olive

With longer cooking, the chlorophyll molecule changes. Acids released by the vegetables themselves, along with any acid in the pot, replace part of the chlorophyll molecule, and the green turns olive or grayish. The longer and hotter the cooking, the more this happens.

### How to keep greens bright

- **Cook briefly**, just until tender-crisp.
- **Use plenty of rapidly boiling water**, so the temperature recovers quickly and the vegetables cook fast.
- **Leave the lid off for the first minutes**, a traditional tip intended to let some of the volatile acids escape with the steam.
- **Add acid at the end**, never at the start. A squeeze of lemon on finished broccoli is fine; lemon in the cooking water dulls it.
- **Shock in ice water** after blanching, if you are not serving immediately, to stop the cooking.
- **Steam or stir-fry**, which cook quickly.

Adding baking soda to the cooking water keeps greens very green, but it also makes them mushy quickly and can destroy some vitamins, so most cooks avoid it.

Our guide to [how acids change food](/kitchen/how-acids-change-food/) explains acid's other effects in the kitchen.

### The smell of overcooked greens

Vegetables in the cabbage family, such as broccoli, Brussels sprouts and cabbage, release sulfur compounds when overcooked, producing the strong smell many people dislike. Short cooking keeps both the color and the flavor at their best.

## Orange and yellow vegetables: carotenoids

Carotenoids give carrots, sweet potatoes, pumpkins, squash, yellow and orange peppers, mangoes and tomatoes their color. They are tough pigments:

- **heat does not destroy them easily**, so carrots and squash stay orange even after long cooking;
- **they dissolve in fat, not water**, which is why carrot color seeps into butter or oil rather than the cooking water;
- **cooking with a little fat** can make some carotenoids easier for the body to absorb.

The color of carotenoid-rich vegetables can even seem to deepen during cooking as cells soften. Very long cooking can make it fade a little, but these vegetables are forgiving.

## Red, purple and blue: anthocyanins

Anthocyanins are the natural pH indicators of the kitchen. Their color depends on how acidic or alkaline their surroundings are:

- **in acid**, they are red or pink;
- **near neutral**, they are purple;
- **in alkaline conditions**, they turn blue and then greenish.

### Red cabbage

Red cabbage cooked in plain water, especially hard, alkaline tap water, can turn an unappetizing blue-purple or even greenish. That is why traditional recipes braise it with vinegar, apples or wine, which keep it a vivid red-purple. You can see the effect in your own kitchen: a few drops of vinegar in red cabbage water turn it pink, while a pinch of baking soda turns it blue-green.

### Purple vegetables that turn green

Purple green beans, purple asparagus and some purple-skinned peppers turn green when cooked. Their purple anthocyanins break down or leach out with heat, revealing the green chlorophyll underneath. Roasting or eating them raw keeps more of the purple.

### Leaching

Anthocyanins dissolve in water, so they leach out during boiling, leaving the cooking water purple and the vegetables paler. Steaming, roasting or cooking with less water keeps more color in the food.

### Berries in baking

Blueberries can turn greenish in some baked goods, especially those leavened with a lot of baking soda, which makes the batter alkaline. A little acid in the batter, such as buttermilk or lemon juice, helps keep them blue-purple. Our guide to [muffins and quick breads](/kitchen/muffins-and-quick-breads/) has more on fruit in batters.

## White vegetables: anthoxanthins

The pale pigments in cauliflower, onions, potatoes and white cabbage are usually barely noticeable, but they can turn yellow or cream in alkaline water, and gray or brownish when cooked in some reactive metal pans.

To keep white vegetables white:

- **cook in non-reactive pans**, such as stainless steel or enameled cast iron;
- **add a little acid**, such as lemon juice or a splash of milk, to cauliflower cooking water, a traditional trick;
- **avoid overcooking**, which also brings out strong sulfur smells in cauliflower and cabbage.

## Beets and betalains

Beets, chard stems and some other plants owe their color to betalains. These pigments:

- **bleed easily** into cooking water and onto hands, boards and other ingredients;
- **can fade with long boiling**, especially once beets are peeled and cut.

To keep beets vivid:

- **roast or boil them whole**, with the skin on and the root and some of the stem intact;
- **peel after cooking**, when the skins slip off easily;
- **add them to salads at the last minute**, so they do not stain everything pink;
- **wear gloves** or rub hands with lemon juice to remove stains.

## Browning: the good kind

Not all color changes are losses. When vegetables are roasted, grilled or sautéed at high heat, their natural sugars and proteins brown, producing deep color and rich flavor through caramelization and the Maillard reaction. Our guides to [roasting vegetables](/kitchen/how-to-roast-vegetables/), [caramelization explained](/kitchen/caramelization-explained/) and [the Maillard reaction](/kitchen/maillard-reaction-browning/) explain how to make the most of it.

## Browning: the unwanted kind

Cut apples, pears, bananas, avocados, potatoes and mushrooms darken when exposed to air, because enzymes in the cut cells react with oxygen. This is called enzymatic browning. To slow it:

- **toss cut fruit with lemon or lime juice**;
- **keep cut potatoes submerged in cold water** until cooking;
- **cover cut surfaces** tightly to limit contact with air;
- **cut close to serving time**;
- **cook promptly**, since heat stops the enzymes.

Our guide to [storing fruit, vegetables and herbs](/kitchen/storing-fruit-vegetables-and-herbs/) has more tips for keeping produce fresh.

## Hard water and color

Very hard tap water is often slightly alkaline. It can make green vegetables mushy, turn red cabbage blue and yellow cauliflower. If your vegetables often lose their color, try cooking them in filtered water or adding a small amount of acid where appropriate.

## Quick reference

| Goal | What to do |
|---|---|
| Bright green beans and broccoli | cook briefly in plenty of boiling water; add acid only at the end; shock in ice water |
| Vivid red cabbage | cook with vinegar, apple or wine |
| White cauliflower | non-reactive pan, a little lemon or milk in the water, don't overcook |
| Bright beets | cook whole with skins on; peel after cooking |
| Purple vegetables staying purple | roast or eat raw rather than boil |
| Pale cut fruit | lemon or lime juice; cut just before serving |

## Frequently asked questions

### Is gray, overcooked broccoli less nutritious?

Long cooking reduces some vitamins, especially water-soluble ones, and the flavor suffers. Shorter cooking keeps more color, flavor and nutrients.

### Why did my garlic turn blue-green in a pickle?

Under some conditions, especially in acidic pickles, natural sulfur compounds in garlic react to form blue-green pigments. It looks surprising but is generally considered harmless.

### Can I use baking soda to keep vegetables green?

It works for color but softens vegetables quickly and can destroy some vitamins, so it is usually best avoided.

## The bottom line

Vegetable colors come from four main pigment families, plus the betalains in beets. Chlorophyll brightens when greens are briefly cooked and turns olive with long cooking and acid, so cook greens fast and add acid at the end. Carotenoids in orange vegetables are stable and dissolve in fat. Anthocyanins in red and purple vegetables are red in acid and blue-green in alkali, so cook red cabbage with vinegar or apple. Pale anthoxanthins stay whiter with a little acid and non-reactive pans, and beets keep their color best when cooked whole. Enjoy the browning of roasting, and slow the browning of cut produce with acid, water and quick cooking.
