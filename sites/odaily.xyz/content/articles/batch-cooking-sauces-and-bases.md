Cooking a complete meal every night after work can feel exhausting, while cooking a whole week of meals on Sunday can leave you eating the same dish five times. There is a middle way that many experienced home cooks rely on: batch-cooking the building blocks. A big pot of tomato sauce, a tray of slow-cooked onions, a jar of pesto or a few bags of stock in the freezer can become the start of a dozen different dinners. They take the slow, time-consuming part of cooking out of the weeknight, leaving only the quick, fun part.

This guide explains which sauces and flavor bases are most worth batch-cooking, how to cook, cool, portion and freeze them safely, how long they keep and, most usefully, how to turn each one into several different meals.

## Why batch-cook bases rather than meals?

- **Flexibility:** a base can become pasta one night, soup the next and a stew at the weekend.
- **Less boredom:** you are not eating the same meal over and over.
- **Time savings:** the long, slow parts of cooking, such as softening onions or simmering sauces, are done in advance.
- **Less waste:** you can use up vegetables before they spoil, and portion bases to suit your household.
- **Better flavor:** many bases benefit from long, slow cooking that is hard to manage on a weeknight.

## The best bases to batch-cook

### 1. Tomato sauce

A simple tomato sauce made from canned tomatoes, olive oil, onion and garlic is the most versatile base of all.

- **Basic method:** soften chopped onion in olive oil, add garlic for a minute, then canned tomatoes, salt and a pinch of sugar if needed. Simmer until thick, stirring occasionally. Blend if you like it smooth.
- **Turn it into:** pasta sauce, pizza sauce, shakshuka with eggs, a base for chili or meatballs, tomato soup, baked fish or chicken in tomato sauce, a sauce for beans.

### 2. Soffritto, sofrito and mirepoix

Many cuisines start dishes with a mix of finely chopped vegetables cooked slowly in fat: onion, carrot and celery in Italian soffritto and French mirepoix; onion, garlic, peppers and tomato in many Spanish and Latin American sofritos.

- **Method:** chop the vegetables very finely, or pulse in a food processor, then cook slowly in oil until soft, sweet and lightly golden. This can take thirty to forty-five minutes.
- **Turn it into:** the base of soups, stews, ragù, risotto, braised beans and lentils.

### 3. Caramelized onions

Slowly cooked onions take a long time but add deep, sweet flavor to many dishes.

- **Method:** slice a large quantity of onions, cook them slowly in oil or butter, stirring occasionally, until deep golden and jammy. Our guide to [caramelization explained](/kitchen/caramelization-explained/) explains why patience pays off.
- **Turn it into:** French onion soup, toppings for pizza and burgers, tarts, sandwiches, pan sauces, gravy.

### 4. Curry base

A cooked mixture of onions, garlic, ginger, tomatoes and spices can become many curries.

- **Method:** follow the first steps of our guide to [weeknight curry basics](/kitchen/weeknight-curry-basics/), cooking the aromatics, spices and tomatoes until thick and the oil separates.
- **Turn it into:** chickpea curry, lentil dal, chicken curry, vegetable curry, a sauce for eggs.

### 5. Stock

Homemade stock from vegetable scraps or chicken bones is a classic freezer staple.

- **Method:** simmer bones or vegetable trimmings with water, onion, carrot, celery and herbs for a few hours (vegetable stock needs less), strain and cool quickly.
- **Turn it into:** soups, risotto, braises, pan sauces, cooking grains.

### 6. Pesto and herb sauces

Pesto and other herb sauces, such as chimichurri or salsa verde, freeze well and add instant flavor.

- **Method:** blend herbs, garlic, nuts or seeds, olive oil and cheese for pesto, or herbs, garlic, vinegar and oil for chimichurri. For freezing, some cooks leave out the cheese and stir it in after thawing.
- **Turn it into:** pasta, sandwiches, soups, roasted vegetables, grain bowls, marinades.

### 7. Ragù and meat sauces

A slow-cooked meat sauce is worth making in quantity.

- **Turn it into:** pasta, lasagne, shepherd's pie, stuffed peppers, a filling for baked potatoes.

### 8. Bean and lentil bases

A pot of seasoned beans or lentils can become many dishes. See our guide to [cooking dried beans and lentils](/kitchen/how-to-cook-dried-beans-and-lentils/).

## Cooling, portioning and freezing safely

Food safety is essential with batch cooking, because large quantities of hot food cool slowly.

1. **Cool quickly.** Divide large batches into shallow containers so they cool faster. Do not leave them out at room temperature for longer than your food safety authority recommends.
2. **Refrigerate or freeze** once cool enough.
3. **Portion thoughtfully.** Freeze in amounts you will use for one meal: one or two portions for small households, family-sized portions for larger ones.
4. **Freeze flat.** Fill freezer bags, press out the air and freeze them flat on a tray. They stack neatly and thaw quickly.
5. **Use ice cube trays** for small amounts, such as pesto, garlic and ginger paste or concentrated stock. Once frozen, transfer the cubes to a labeled bag.
6. **Label everything** with the name and date.

Our [freezing food guide](/kitchen/freezing-food-guide/) and our guide to [leftovers safety](/kitchen/leftovers-safety/) explain safe storage and thawing in more detail.

## How long do they keep?

Storage times depend on the ingredients and your freezer. As a general guide, most cooked sauces and bases keep for a few days in the refrigerator and for several months in the freezer while keeping good quality. Follow your national food safety guidance, and use older portions first by placing new ones at the back.

## Thawing and using bases

- **Thaw in the refrigerator overnight** for the best results.
- **Use frozen portions directly** in soups and stews, adding them to the pot and stirring until thawed and hot.
- **Reheat until steaming hot** throughout.
- **Taste and adjust**: frozen sauces sometimes need a little extra salt, acid or fresh herbs.

## Seasoning bases for flexibility

A base that will become several different dishes should be seasoned with restraint. Salt it lightly, use only gentle, versatile herbs such as bay leaves, and leave out strong flavors like chili, smoked paprika or lots of garlic. You can always add those when you turn the base into a particular meal: chili and cumin for a bean stew, basil and Parmesan for pasta, curry spices for a vegetable curry. A heavily seasoned base, on the other hand, locks you into one direction. The same applies to stock: keep it lightly salted so that it can be reduced into a sauce without becoming too salty.

## Making a batch-cooking session efficient

- **Choose two or three bases** per session rather than trying to make everything.
- **Start the longest-cooking base first**, such as stock or caramelized onions.
- **Chop all vegetables at once**, using a food processor if you have one.
- **Use your largest pots** and a second burner.
- **Clean as you go**, so the session does not end with a mountain of washing up.
- **Have containers and labels ready** before you start.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I freeze sauces with cream or cheese?

Dairy-heavy sauces can separate or become grainy after freezing. It is often better to freeze the base without dairy and add cream or cheese when reheating.

### Do frozen herbs and pesto lose their color?

They may darken slightly. Covering pesto with a thin layer of olive oil before freezing helps protect its color.

### How much should I make at once?

Start with an amount your freezer can hold and your household will use within a couple of months. Make more once you know what you use most.

## The bottom line

Batch-cooking sauces and flavor bases gives you the flexibility of fresh cooking with the convenience of ready meals. Tomato sauce, soffritto or sofrito, caramelized onions, curry base, stock, pesto, ragù and seasoned beans are the most useful. Cook them in large batches, cool them quickly in shallow containers, portion and freeze them flat or in ice cube trays, label everything and use the oldest first. With two or three bases in the freezer, a varied week of quick dinners is within easy reach.
