A jar of bright pink pickled onions on the table can transform tacos, salads, grain bowls and sandwiches. Crunchy pickled cucumbers, carrots and radishes add a sharp, refreshing bite to rich dishes and make simple meals feel special. Quick pickles, sometimes called refrigerator pickles, are one of the easiest kitchen projects: no special equipment, no long waiting and no complicated preserving. In ten minutes you can turn a few vegetables into something that lifts meals all week, and use up produce before it spoils.

This guide explains what quick pickles are and how they differ from canned and fermented pickles, gives a basic brine you can adapt, suggests vegetables and flavor combinations, covers jars, storage and how long quick pickles keep, and explains the food safety points that matter.

## What a quick pickle is

A quick pickle is a vegetable or fruit soaked in a seasoned vinegar brine and **stored in the refrigerator**. The vinegar flavors and preserves the vegetables for a limited time, while the cold slows spoilage.

It helps to know the three main types of pickle:

| Type | How it works | Storage |
|---|---|---|
| Quick or refrigerator pickles | vegetables in a vinegar brine | always refrigerated; keep for a limited time |
| Canned or bottled pickles | vinegar brine, sealed jars processed in a boiling water bath using tested recipes | shelf-stable until opened |
| Fermented pickles | vegetables in a salt brine, where friendly bacteria produce acid over days or weeks | usually refrigerated once fermented |

This guide is about quick refrigerator pickles. They are **not** shelf-stable and must stay in the refrigerator. If you want pickles to keep in the cupboard, follow a tested canning recipe from a reputable food safety source exactly.

## The basic brine

A simple starting point many cooks use is:

- **vinegar and water** in roughly equal parts;
- **salt**, about a teaspoon or so per cup of liquid;
- **sugar**, from none to a couple of teaspoons per cup, depending on how sweet you like your pickles.

### Method

1. **Prepare the vegetables**: wash, trim and slice them evenly. Thin slices pickle faster.
2. **Pack them into a clean jar**, along with any spices or herbs.
3. **Heat the vinegar, water, salt and sugar** in a saucepan until the salt and sugar dissolve. You do not need to boil it hard.
4. **Pour the brine over the vegetables**, making sure they are fully covered.
5. **Let it cool**, then close the jar and refrigerate.

Many quick pickles are pleasant to eat within an hour, especially thinly sliced onions, and they improve over a day or two as the flavors develop.

### Choosing vinegar

- **White vinegar** has a clean, sharp taste and does not color the vegetables.
- **Apple cider vinegar** is fruitier and milder.
- **Rice vinegar** is mild and slightly sweet, lovely for Asian-inspired pickles.
- **Wine vinegars** add depth.

Use ordinary commercial vinegar of normal strength, as labeled on the bottle. Very weak, homemade or diluted vinegars are less predictable.

### Hot or cold brine?

- **A hot brine** softens vegetables slightly and helps flavors penetrate quickly; it suits onions, carrots and cauliflower.
- **A cold or cooled brine** keeps delicate vegetables, such as cucumbers and radishes, crunchier.

## What to pickle

Almost any firm vegetable works:

- **red onions**, which turn a brilliant pink;
- **cucumbers**, in spears or slices;
- **carrots**, in sticks or ribbons;
- **radishes**, which turn the brine pink;
- **cauliflower** florets;
- **green beans**, briefly blanched for a more tender bite;
- **beets**, cooked first, then pickled;
- **fresh chilies**, such as jalapeños;
- **cabbage**, shredded;
- **fennel**, thinly sliced;
- **fruit** such as grapes, cherries or firm pears, for something unusual.

Use fresh, firm produce. Pickling does not rescue vegetables that are already soft or spoiling. Our guide to [storing fruit, vegetables and herbs](/kitchen/storing-fruit-vegetables-and-herbs/) helps you keep produce in good condition until you pickle it.

## Flavor combinations

| Pickle | Flavorings |
|---|---|
| Pink pickled onions | white or cider vinegar, a little sugar, black peppercorns, a bay leaf |
| Classic cucumber | white vinegar, dill, garlic, mustard seeds |
| Spicy carrots | cider vinegar, garlic, chili flakes, cumin seeds |
| Asian-style radish or cucumber | rice vinegar, a little sugar, ginger, sesame seeds |
| Curried cauliflower | white vinegar, turmeric, mustard seeds, coriander seeds |
| Sweet-spiced beets | cider vinegar, a little sugar, cinnamon stick, cloves |
| Fennel and orange | white wine vinegar, orange zest, fennel seeds |

Whole spices keep the brine clear and release flavor gradually. Garlic cloves can turn bluish-green in acidic pickles; it looks surprising but is generally considered harmless, as we explain in our guide to [why vegetables change color when cooked](/kitchen/why-vegetables-change-color/).

## Jars and equipment

- **Glass jars** with tight-fitting lids are ideal. Clean them thoroughly with hot, soapy water and rinse well.
- **Non-reactive pans** such as stainless steel or enameled ones for heating the brine, since vinegar can react with aluminum and bare cast iron. Our guide to [how acids change food](/kitchen/how-acids-change-food/) explains why.
- **Lids:** metal lids can corrode with vinegar over time; plastic lids or lids with a protective coating last longer.
- **A clean spoon or fork** for taking pickles out, to avoid introducing bacteria.

## How long do quick pickles keep?

Quick pickles keep for a limited time in the refrigerator, often a few weeks, depending on the vegetable, the brine and how they are handled. Follow your recipe and your national food safety guidance. Some general points:

- **keep them refrigerated at all times**;
- **use a clean utensil** every time;
- **keep the vegetables submerged** in brine;
- **label the jar** with the date;
- **throw them away** if you notice mold, a slimy texture, an unpleasant or off smell, unexpected bubbling or a bulging lid.

Texture changes over time: crisp vegetables gradually soften, and many quick pickles are at their best in the first week or two.

## Food safety points

- **Quick pickles are not canned pickles.** Do not store them at room temperature.
- **Do not reduce the vinegar** far below the proportion in your recipe if you rely on it to preserve the pickles in the refrigerator for longer periods.
- **For shelf-stable pickles**, follow tested canning instructions exactly, including vinegar strength, processing time and jar preparation.
- **Be cautious with low-acid additions**, such as large amounts of oil, which can change how the pickle behaves.
- **Fermented pickles** follow different rules; use a reliable recipe and keep everything clean.

## Pink pickled onions, step by step

Pickled red onions are the most popular quick pickle, and a good one to start with. Slice one red onion into thin rings or half-moons and pack it into a clean jar. Warm equal parts vinegar and water with a pinch of salt and a little sugar until dissolved, add a few peppercorns or a bay leaf if you like, and pour the warm brine over the onions until they are covered. Within half an hour they soften and start turning pink; after a few hours in the refrigerator they become a vivid magenta. They are delicious on tacos, salads, eggs and sandwiches.

## Reducing waste with pickles

Quick pickles are a great way to use vegetables before they spoil: half a cucumber, a few carrots, the end of a cabbage, a handful of radishes. Keep a jar of brine ingredients on hand, and pickling becomes a five-minute habit. Our guide to [reducing food waste at home](/kitchen/reduce-food-waste-at-home/) has more ideas.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I reuse pickle brine?

Brine that has held vegetables becomes diluted and may contain small pieces of food, so many cooks use it once more only for a quick batch to eat within days, or use it in dressings. For best safety and flavor, start a new batch with fresh brine.

### Why are my pickles soft?

They may have been sliced very thin, covered with very hot brine, made from vegetables that were not fresh, or kept for a long time. Use firm produce and cooled brine for crunchier pickles.

### Do I need sugar?

No. Sugar balances the sharpness of vinegar, but you can leave it out or use less.

## The bottom line

Quick pickles are vegetables in a seasoned vinegar brine, stored in the refrigerator. Mix vinegar and water in roughly equal parts with salt and a little sugar, warm to dissolve, pour over prepared vegetables in a clean jar and refrigerate. Flavor them with whole spices, herbs, garlic and citrus zest, and use them on tacos, bowls, sandwiches and rich dishes. Keep them cold, use a clean spoon, keep the vegetables covered, eat them within a few weeks and discard anything that looks or smells wrong. For pickles that keep in the cupboard, follow a tested canning recipe exactly.
