The freezer is one of the best tools for saving time, money and food. It turns a large package of chicken into weeks of dinners, rescues ripe bananas and soft bread before they spoil, and keeps homemade soups and sauces ready for busy evenings. Yet many freezers are full of mysterious, frost-covered packages that no one dares to open. Used well, a freezer preserves food at its best quality and makes everyday cooking much easier. This guide explains how freezing works, what freezes well, how to package food to prevent freezer burn and how to thaw food safely.

## How freezing preserves food

Freezing does not kill most bacteria, but it stops them from growing. At 0 °F (−18 °C), the microorganisms that cause spoilage and foodborne illness become inactive. Freezing also slows down enzymes that cause food to deteriorate. As a result, food kept continuously frozen at 0 °F remains safe indefinitely.

However, quality slowly decreases over time. Ice crystals can damage cell walls, moisture can evaporate from the surface and fats can slowly turn rancid. That is why recommended freezer storage times are about quality, not safety.

## Set the right temperature

Keep your freezer at **0 °F (−18 °C) or below**. Use an appliance thermometer to check, because built-in dials are not always accurate. A freezer that is too warm allows ice crystals to grow larger, damaging food texture, and may allow some bacterial activity.

## The enemy: freezer burn

Freezer burn appears as dry, grayish-brown or white patches on frozen food. It happens when moisture evaporates from the surface of food and turns into ice crystals, often inside the packaging. The affected areas become dry, tough and tasteless. Freezer-burned food is safe to eat, but the damaged parts are unpleasant and are best cut away.

To prevent freezer burn:

- **Remove as much air as possible** from packaging.
- **Use airtight, moisture-proof packaging**, such as freezer bags, rigid freezer containers, heavy-duty foil or vacuum-sealed bags.
- **Wrap food tightly**, pressing the wrap directly against the surface.
- **Cool food before freezing**, to reduce condensation.
- **Keep the freezer temperature stable** and avoid opening the door unnecessarily.

## Packaging tips

- **Freezer bags:** Flexible, space-saving and ideal for soups, sauces, cooked grains, meat portions and vegetables. Press out the air before sealing. Freeze bags flat on a tray; once frozen, they can be stored upright like files.
- **Rigid containers:** Good for liquids and fragile foods. Leave about 1/2 to 1 inch (1 to 2.5 cm) of space at the top, because liquids expand as they freeze.
- **Plastic wrap plus foil or a bag:** For meat, baked goods and casseroles, wrap tightly in plastic wrap first, then in foil or a freezer bag for extra protection.
- **Vacuum sealing:** Removes almost all air and greatly extends quality for meat, fish and vegetables.
- **Ice cube trays:** Freeze small portions of stock, pesto, tomato paste, herbs in oil or baby food, then transfer the cubes to a bag.

**Label everything** with the contents and the date. It is easy to believe you will remember what is in a container, but frozen foods often look alike.

## What freezes well

Many foods freeze beautifully:

- **Raw meat, poultry and fish**, in portions.
- **Ground meat**, flattened in bags for quick thawing.
- **Cooked meats**, such as shredded chicken, pulled pork and meatballs.
- **Soups, stews, chili and curries.**
- **Sauces**, including tomato sauce, meat sauce and pesto.
- **Cooked grains**, such as rice, quinoa and farro.
- **Cooked beans and lentils.**
- **Bread, rolls, tortillas, bagels and pizza dough.**
- **Baked goods**, such as muffins, cookies, cake layers and pie dough.
- **Fruit**, especially berries, bananas, mango and peaches for smoothies and baking.
- **Butter and hard cheese**, grated for cooking.
- **Blanched vegetables**, such as green beans, broccoli and peas.

## What does not freeze well

Some foods change texture after freezing and thawing:

- **Lettuce and raw salad greens**, which become limp and watery.
- **Raw cucumbers, celery and radishes**, which turn soggy.
- **Whole raw eggs in their shells**, which can crack. Eggs can be frozen beaten.
- **Cooked egg whites**, which become rubbery.
- **Mayonnaise and cream-based sauces**, which may separate.
- **Fried foods**, which lose their crispness, though they can be recrisped in the oven.
- **Cooked pasta in light sauces**, which can become mushy.
- **Soft cheeses and yogurt**, which can become grainy, though they are fine in cooked dishes.

## Blanching vegetables

Most vegetables should be blanched before freezing. Blanching means briefly boiling them, then quickly cooling them in ice water. This stops enzymes that would otherwise cause loss of color, flavor and nutrients during freezing.

1. Bring a large pot of water to a boil.
2. Add the vegetables and boil for one to three minutes, depending on the vegetable.
3. Transfer them immediately into a bowl of ice water to stop cooking.
4. Drain and dry them thoroughly.
5. Spread them on a tray to freeze individually, then transfer them to bags.

Freezing items in a single layer on a tray before bagging them, known as open freezing or flash freezing, keeps pieces separate so you can take out exactly the amount you need.

## Suggested storage times for best quality

These are guidelines for quality at 0 °F (−18 °C). Food remains safe beyond these times but may lose flavor and texture.

| Food | Recommended storage time |
| --- | --- |
| Raw steaks, roasts and chops | 4 to 12 months |
| Raw ground meat | 3 to 4 months |
| Raw whole chicken or turkey | about 12 months |
| Raw chicken pieces | about 9 months |
| Lean fish | 6 to 8 months |
| Fatty fish | 2 to 3 months |
| Cooked meat and poultry | 2 to 6 months |
| Soups and stews | 2 to 3 months |
| Bread | about 3 months |
| Fruits and vegetables | 8 to 12 months |

## Safe thawing methods

How you thaw food matters as much as how you freeze it. Never thaw meat, poultry or seafood on the counter at room temperature, because the outside can reach temperatures where bacteria multiply while the inside is still frozen. There are three safe methods.

### In the refrigerator

This is the safest method. Place frozen food on a plate or tray on the bottom shelf, so juices do not drip onto other foods. It takes time: about a day for each 4 to 5 pounds (2 to 2.3 kg) of whole turkey, and about a day for small packages of meat. Food thawed in the fridge can be kept there for a day or two before cooking, and can be refrozen without cooking, though some quality may be lost.

### In cold water

For faster thawing, place food in a leak-proof bag and submerge it in cold tap water. Change the water every 30 minutes so it stays cold. Small packages of meat thaw in about an hour; larger items take several hours. Cook food immediately after thawing with this method.

### In the microwave

Use the defrost setting and cook the food immediately afterward, because some areas may begin to cook during thawing.

## Cooking from frozen

Many foods can be cooked directly from frozen without thawing:

- Frozen vegetables can be added straight to soups, stir-fries and roasting pans.
- Frozen fish fillets and shrimp can be baked, poached or steamed, adding a few minutes to the cooking time.
- Frozen soups and sauces can be reheated gently in a pot.
- Frozen bread can be toasted directly.
- Meat can be cooked from frozen in the oven or on the stove, though it takes about 50 percent longer. Always check the internal temperature with a thermometer.

Avoid cooking frozen meat in a slow cooker, as it may stay in the danger zone too long.

## Keeping the freezer organized

- **Keep a list** on the freezer door of what is inside and when it was frozen.
- **Group similar items** in bins or baskets: meat, vegetables, prepared meals and baked goods.
- **Use the first in, first out principle.**
- **Defrost manual freezers** when ice builds up to about 1/4 inch (6 mm).
- **Keep it reasonably full.** A full freezer holds its temperature better during power outages and uses energy efficiently.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is freezer-burned food safe to eat?**
Yes. It is safe but may be dry and tasteless in the affected areas. Cut those parts away before cooking.

**Can I refreeze thawed food?**
Food thawed in the refrigerator can be refrozen, though quality may suffer. Food thawed in cold water or the microwave should be cooked before refreezing.

**How long can food stay in the freezer?**
Food kept at 0 °F (−18 °C) stays safe indefinitely. For the best quality, follow recommended storage times.

**Do I need to blanch vegetables before freezing?**
For most vegetables, yes. Blanching preserves color, flavor and texture. Some, like onions and peppers, can be frozen raw for cooking.

**Why do my frozen soups crack their containers?**
Liquids expand as they freeze. Leave headspace at the top of containers.

## The bottom line

The freezer keeps food safe indefinitely at 0 °F (−18 °C) and preserves quality when used well. Package food airtight, remove air to prevent freezer burn, label and date everything, and freeze in practical portions. Blanch vegetables, open-freeze items you want to keep separate and follow recommended storage times for best quality. Thaw food safely in the refrigerator, in cold water or in the microwave, and cook many foods straight from frozen. With an organized freezer, you will waste less, save money and always have a meal close at hand.
