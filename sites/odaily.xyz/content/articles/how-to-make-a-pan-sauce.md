You have just seared a chicken breast, a pork chop or a piece of salmon. The meat is resting on a plate, and the skillet is left with a glaze of brown, sticky bits and a little fat. Many cooks put that pan straight into the sink. That is a shame, because those browned bits are packed with flavor, and in about five minutes they can become a glossy, savory sauce that turns a simple weeknight dinner into something that tastes like it came from a good restaurant.

A pan sauce is not a single recipe but a method you can adapt endlessly. This guide explains what makes it work, walks through the five basic steps, suggests flavor combinations for different proteins and vegetables, explains how to fix a sauce that is too thin, too salty or greasy, and covers the equipment that makes the process easier.

## What a pan sauce is

When food browns in a hot pan, proteins and sugars react to create new flavors and a layer of browned residue on the bottom of the pan. Cooks call this residue the **fond**. A pan sauce dissolves the fond in liquid, concentrates it by simmering, and then adjusts the flavor and texture.

The browning itself comes from the Maillard reaction, which we explain in our guide to [browning and the Maillard reaction](/kitchen/maillard-reaction-browning/).

## The equipment

- **A stainless steel or cast-iron skillet** builds the best fond. Nonstick pans are convenient but leave less browning to work with.
- **A flat-edged wooden spoon or spatula** to scrape up the browned bits.
- **A small whisk**, useful for finishing with butter or mustard.
- **Ingredients measured and ready**, because the process moves quickly.

## Step 1: build the fond

Start by cooking your main ingredient well.

- **Pat the meat or fish dry** so it browns rather than steams.
- **Heat the pan before adding fat**, then add a little oil.
- **Do not crowd the pan**, which lowers the temperature and creates steam.
- **Leave the food alone** until it releases easily and has a deep golden crust.

When the food is cooked, move it to a plate and keep it warm. Our guide to [heat control on the stovetop](/kitchen/heat-control-on-the-stovetop/) explains how to get a good sear.

### Watch the fond

The fond should be **brown, not black**. Black, burnt bits taste bitter and will ruin the sauce. If the fond burns, it is better to wipe out the pan and make the sauce with fresh fat.

## Step 2: cook the aromatics

Pour off most of the fat, leaving about a tablespoon. Reduce the heat to medium and add finely chopped aromatics:

- **shallots**, the classic choice for a mild, sweet flavor;
- **garlic**, added after the shallots because it burns more easily;
- **onion, ginger, chili or spices**, depending on the dish.

Stir for a minute or two until they soften and smell fragrant. Do not let them brown too much.

## Step 3: deglaze

This is the moment of magic. Pour in a splash of liquid, about half a cup for two servings, and scrape the bottom of the pan with your spoon. The liquid will bubble and the fond will dissolve into it.

Good deglazing liquids include:

- **wine**, white or red, which adds acidity and depth;
- **broth or stock**, chicken, beef, vegetable or mushroom;
- **cider or apple juice**, lovely with pork;
- **water**, which works perfectly well when you have nothing else;
- **a mix**, such as a little wine followed by broth.

If you use wine or spirits, let them bubble for a minute to cook off the harsh alcohol taste. Take care if pouring alcohol near a gas flame; remove the pan from the heat first.

## Step 4: reduce

Let the liquid simmer until it has reduced by about half or more, and coats the back of a spoon lightly. Reduction concentrates flavor and thickens the sauce. Taste as it reduces: the flavor will become stronger and saltier.

If you want a more substantial sauce, add a second liquid at this stage, such as more broth or a splash of cream, and reduce again.

Any juices that have collected on the plate under the resting meat can go back into the pan now; they are full of flavor. Our guide to [resting meat and carryover cooking](/kitchen/resting-meat-and-carryover-cooking/) explains why meat releases juices as it rests.

## Step 5: finish

The finish gives the sauce its texture and balance.

- **Butter:** take the pan off the heat and whisk in a few small pieces of cold butter. The sauce will turn glossy and slightly thicker. This is the classic restaurant finish.
- **Cream:** stir in a splash and simmer briefly for a richer sauce.
- **Mustard:** a spoonful whisked in adds body and tang.
- **Fresh herbs:** parsley, chives, tarragon, thyme or dill at the end.
- **Acid:** a squeeze of lemon juice or a few drops of vinegar to brighten the flavor.
- **Seasoning:** taste and add salt and pepper only at the end, since reduction concentrates salt.

Pour the sauce over the food and serve immediately.

## A worked example: chicken with lemon and herb sauce

Here is how the method looks in a real weeknight dinner for two.

Pat two chicken breasts dry, season them with salt and pepper, and sear them in a little oil in a stainless steel skillet over medium-high heat until golden on both sides and cooked through. Move them to a warm plate. The pan now holds a light brown fond and some fat.

Pour off all but about a tablespoon of fat, lower the heat to medium and add a finely chopped shallot. Stir for a minute until it softens, then add a sliced garlic clove for thirty seconds. Pour in a splash of dry white wine and scrape vigorously as it bubbles, then add about half a cup of chicken broth. Let it simmer until the liquid has reduced by roughly half and looks slightly syrupy.

Pour in the juices that have collected under the resting chicken. Take the pan off the heat and whisk in two small pieces of cold butter until the sauce turns glossy. Add a squeeze of lemon juice and a small handful of chopped parsley or tarragon, then taste and adjust the salt. Slice the chicken, spoon the sauce over and serve with rice, potatoes or bread to soak up every drop. From start to finish, the sauce takes about five minutes.

## Making it lighter

If you prefer a lighter sauce, you can skip the butter and instead finish with a spoonful of plain yogurt stirred in off the heat, a drizzle of good olive oil, or simply more reduction and fresh herbs. A teaspoon of mustard also adds body without much fat. Low-sodium broth gives you more control over salt, especially when the sauce is reduced a lot.

## Flavor combinations

| Main ingredient | Deglaze with | Finish with |
|---|---|---|
| Chicken | white wine and chicken broth | butter, lemon, tarragon or parsley |
| Pork chops | cider or apple juice | Dijon mustard, a little cream, thyme |
| Steak | red wine and beef broth | butter, black pepper, chives |
| Salmon | white wine or broth | butter, lemon, dill and capers |
| Mushrooms | broth or a little sherry | cream, thyme, black pepper |
| Tofu or vegetables | soy sauce, water and a little rice vinegar | sesame oil, scallions, ginger |

Once you know the method, you can invent your own combinations based on what is in the refrigerator.

## Fixing common problems

- **Too thin:** keep reducing, or whisk in a little more butter off the heat. For a quick fix, stir in a small amount of cornstarch mixed with cold water and simmer briefly. Our guide to [how starches thicken sauces](/kitchen/how-starches-thicken-sauces/) explains the options.
- **Too thick:** add a splash of broth or water.
- **Too salty:** add a little unsalted broth, cream or a squeeze of lemon; next time, use low-sodium broth and season at the end.
- **Greasy or separated:** the butter may have been added over too much heat. Take the pan off the heat, add a spoonful of warm liquid and whisk vigorously. Our guide to [why sauces split](/kitchen/why-sauces-split-emulsions/) explains emulsions.
- **Bitter:** the fond or garlic probably burned. Start again in a clean pan if possible.
- **Flat:** add acid, salt or fresh herbs.

## Pan sauces without meat

You do not need meat to make a pan sauce. Seared mushrooms, roasted vegetables, halloumi or tofu all leave browned bits in the pan. Deglaze with broth, wine or a splash of soy sauce and finish with butter or olive oil, herbs and lemon.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I make a pan sauce in a nonstick pan?

Yes, but you will have less fond and therefore a lighter flavor. Browning aromatics well and using a flavorful broth helps.

### Do I need wine?

No. Broth, cider, juice or even water work. Wine adds acidity and depth, but a squeeze of lemon at the end can provide similar brightness.

### How much sauce does this make?

The method described makes enough for two to four servings. Increase the liquid for more, keeping the same steps.

## The bottom line

A pan sauce turns the browned bits left after searing into a glossy sauce in minutes. Build a good fond by browning food well without burning it, pour off most of the fat, soften shallots or garlic, deglaze with wine, broth, cider or water while scraping the pan, and reduce until concentrated. Finish off the heat with cold butter, cream or mustard, fresh herbs and a little acid, then season at the end. Once you know the steps, you can adapt them to almost any meal.
