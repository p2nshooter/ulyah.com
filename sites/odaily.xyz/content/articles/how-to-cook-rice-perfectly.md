Rice feeds more people around the world than almost any other food, and yet it is one of the dishes home cooks worry about most. It can turn out sticky when you wanted fluffy, crunchy in the middle, scorched at the bottom or soggy and bland. The good news is that perfect rice is not a matter of luck. It comes down to choosing the right method for the type of rice, measuring water carefully, controlling the heat and, most importantly, leaving the lid alone. Once you understand a few simple principles, you can make reliable, delicious rice every time, whether it is a bowl of jasmine rice for a stir-fry, basmati for a curry or brown rice for a grain bowl.

## Know your rice

Different types of rice contain different amounts of starch, and that changes how they cook and what texture they have. Two starches matter most: amylose, which keeps grains separate and fluffy, and amylopectin, which makes rice sticky and creamy.

- **Long-grain white rice:** Grains stay separate and fluffy. Good as a side dish, in pilafs and for fried rice once it has been cooled.
- **Jasmine rice:** A fragrant long-grain rice from Thailand, slightly soft and sticky, wonderful with Southeast Asian dishes.
- **Basmati rice:** A long, slender grain from India and Pakistan with a nutty aroma. The grains become long and separate when cooked properly.
- **Short-grain and medium-grain rice:** Plump grains that cling together, used for sushi, Japanese and Korean dishes, and Mediterranean dishes like paella. Arborio and carnaroli are short-grain varieties used for creamy risotto.
- **Brown rice:** Whole-grain rice with the bran layer still on. It is chewier, nuttier and more nutritious, but needs more water and much longer cooking.
- **Wild rice:** Technically the seed of a water grass, cooked like a grain and often boiled like pasta.

## To rinse or not to rinse?

Rinsing rice removes loose surface starch, which helps grains stay separate and prevents a gluey texture. Most long-grain, jasmine, basmati and sushi rice benefits from rinsing.

Place the rice in a bowl, cover it with cold water, swirl it gently with your hand and pour off the cloudy water. Repeat two or three times until the water is mostly clear. A fine-mesh sieve makes this easy.

There are exceptions. Do not rinse rice for risotto, because its surface starch creates the creamy texture. Enriched rice, common in the United States, has vitamins sprayed on the surface, and rinsing removes some of them. If you rinse enriched rice, you lose some nutrients, but many cooks prefer the texture anyway.

Some rice, especially basmati, also benefits from soaking for 20 to 30 minutes after rinsing. Soaked grains cook more evenly and become longer and more elegant. If you soak rice, reduce the cooking water slightly.

## Water ratios

The amount of water depends on the type of rice, the size of the pot and how tightly the lid fits. The following ratios are good starting points for the absorption method, measured by volume.

| Type of rice | Rice to water | Cooking time (after boiling) | Resting time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Long-grain white | 1 : 1½ to 1 : 1¾ | 15 to 18 minutes | 10 minutes |
| Jasmine | 1 : 1¼ to 1 : 1½ | 12 to 15 minutes | 10 minutes |
| Basmati (soaked) | 1 : 1¼ to 1 : 1½ | 12 to 15 minutes | 10 minutes |
| Short-grain or sushi | 1 : 1¼ | 15 minutes | 10 to 15 minutes |
| Brown long-grain | 1 : 2 to 1 : 2¼ | 40 to 45 minutes | 10 minutes |

Rice package instructions are also useful, because varieties and brands differ. Keep notes about the ratio that works best in your pot, and you will get the same results every time.

## The stovetop absorption method

This is the classic method for fluffy white rice.

1. **Rinse the rice** and drain it well.
2. **Combine rice, water and salt** in a heavy saucepan with a tight-fitting lid. Use about 1/2 teaspoon of salt per cup of rice, or to taste. A little butter or oil is optional.
3. **Bring to a boil** over medium-high heat, uncovered. Stir once to make sure no grains are stuck to the bottom.
4. **Cover and reduce the heat** to the lowest setting as soon as the water boils.
5. **Cook without lifting the lid** for the time in the table. The steam trapped inside is doing the cooking. Every time you lift the lid, steam escapes and the rice cooks unevenly.
6. **Turn off the heat** and let the rice rest, still covered, for about 10 minutes. This allows moisture to spread evenly through the grains.
7. **Fluff with a fork** or rice paddle, gently lifting and separating the grains.

If your stove runs hot even on the lowest setting, place the pot on a smaller burner or use a heat diffuser. A clean kitchen towel placed between the pot and lid during resting absorbs extra moisture and helps produce dry, fluffy grains, but keep the towel well away from the flame.

## The pasta method

The pasta method is very forgiving and works especially well for brown rice, wild rice and basmati.

1. Bring a large pot of salted water to a boil, as you would for pasta.
2. Add the rinsed rice and boil it uncovered, stirring occasionally.
3. Start tasting a few minutes before the expected cooking time. White rice usually needs 10 to 15 minutes, brown rice around 30 minutes.
4. When the grains are tender, drain them in a sieve.
5. Return the rice to the pot, cover it and let it steam off the heat for 10 minutes.

This method makes it almost impossible to burn rice, and the grains turn out separate and light. The downside is that some nutrients are lost in the drained water, and it does not work for sticky rice.

## The oven method

Baked rice is reliable and useful when the stovetop is busy. In a heatproof dish with a lid, combine the rinsed rice with boiling water or stock and salt, using the same ratios as the stovetop method. Cover tightly with a lid or foil and bake at 350 °F (175 °C), about 25 minutes for white rice and about an hour for brown rice. Let it rest for 10 minutes before fluffing.

## Rice cookers

If you eat rice several times a week, a rice cooker is a worthwhile investment. It measures heat automatically, switches to a warming mode when the rice is done and produces consistent results. Follow the cooker's own measuring lines and cup, which may be smaller than a standard measuring cup. Many rice cookers also have settings for brown rice, sushi rice and porridge.

## Adding flavor

Plain rice is wonderful, but small changes add a lot of flavor.

- **Toast the rice:** Cook the drained rice in a little butter or oil for one or two minutes before adding liquid. This is the base of a pilaf and gives a nutty flavor.
- **Use stock:** Replace some or all of the water with chicken or vegetable stock.
- **Add aromatics:** Cook a little chopped onion or garlic before adding the rice, or add a bay leaf, a few cardamom pods, a cinnamon stick or a strip of lemon zest to the water.
- **Use coconut milk:** Replace part of the water with coconut milk for a rich, slightly sweet rice that pairs well with curries.
- **Finish fresh:** Fold in chopped herbs, lime juice, toasted nuts or a pat of butter just before serving.

## Fixing common problems

- **Rice is crunchy:** There was too little water or the heat was too high. Sprinkle a few tablespoons of hot water over the rice, cover and cook on very low heat for another five to ten minutes.
- **Rice is mushy:** There was too much water or the rice was stirred too much. Spread it on a baking sheet to cool and dry, and use it for fried rice or rice pudding.
- **Bottom is burnt:** The heat was too high. Do not scrape the bottom. Spoon out the good rice above. Some cuisines prize a crisp golden layer at the bottom, such as Persian tahdig or Spanish socarrat, but that is done deliberately with fat and careful heat.
- **Rice is sticky when you wanted fluffy:** Rinse the rice more thoroughly next time, reduce the water slightly and avoid stirring while it cooks.

## Storing and reheating rice safely

Cooked rice requires care. Uncooked rice can contain spores of the bacterium *Bacillus cereus*, which survive cooking. If cooked rice sits at room temperature for too long, the spores can grow and produce toxins that cause food poisoning and are not destroyed by reheating.

- Cool leftover rice quickly. Spread it in a shallow container so it cools fast, and refrigerate within an hour or two of cooking.
- Store cooked rice in the fridge at 40 °F (4 °C) or below and eat it within three to four days. Many food safety agencies recommend even shorter times, so the sooner the better.
- Cooked rice freezes very well. Freeze portions flat in bags for up to a few months.
- Reheat rice only once, until it is steaming hot throughout, at 165 °F (74 °C).

## Frequently asked questions

**Why should I not lift the lid while rice is cooking?**
Rice cooks mostly in steam. Lifting the lid lets steam escape, which slows cooking, changes the amount of water and can leave the grains unevenly cooked.

**How much rice should I cook per person?**
About 1/3 to 1/2 cup (60 to 90 g) of uncooked rice per person as a side dish. Rice roughly triples in volume when cooked.

**Is brown rice healthier than white rice?**
Brown rice contains more fiber, vitamins and minerals because it keeps the bran and germ. White rice is still a good part of a balanced diet, especially when served with vegetables and protein.

**Why is my rice still hard even though all the water is gone?**
The heat may have been too high, causing water to evaporate before the rice could absorb it, or the lid may not fit well. Use a heavier pot, a tighter lid and the lowest possible heat.

**Can I cook rice in advance for a party?**
Yes. Cook it, cool it quickly and refrigerate it. Reheat it thoroughly before serving, or keep it hot above 140 °F (60 °C) in a rice cooker or warming dish.

## The bottom line

Perfect rice comes from a few simple habits: choose the right method for the type of rice, rinse when appropriate, measure water carefully, bring it to a boil, then cook on the lowest heat without lifting the lid, and let it rest before fluffing. Adjust the ratios to your pot, add flavor with stock, aromatics or toasting, and treat leftovers with care by cooling them quickly. With a little practice, rice becomes one of the easiest and most satisfying dishes in your kitchen.
