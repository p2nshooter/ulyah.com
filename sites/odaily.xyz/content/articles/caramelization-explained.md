Few transformations in the kitchen are as magical as caramelization. Plain white sugar, heated in a pan, slowly melts, turns pale gold, then amber, then deep mahogany, and fills the kitchen with a rich, buttery, slightly bitter aroma. The same process gives caramelized onions their sweetness, roasted carrots their candy-like edges and crème brûlée its glassy top. Caramelization is simple to describe but full of subtle chemistry, and understanding it helps you make better caramel sauces, sweeter onions and more flavorful roasted vegetables, while avoiding burnt sugar and painful burns.

## What is caramelization?

Caramelization is what happens when sugar is heated to high temperatures. The sugar molecules break apart and recombine into hundreds of new compounds. Some of these compounds are brown, giving caramel its color. Others have complex flavors and aromas: buttery, nutty, fruity, toasty and slightly bitter. The longer and hotter sugar cooks, the darker and more bitter it becomes, and the less sweet it tastes.

Unlike the Maillard reaction, which requires both sugars and amino acids from proteins, caramelization involves only sugar. In many foods, both reactions happen at the same time, which is why browned vegetables, bread crusts and roasted coffee have such complex flavors.

## Temperatures for different sugars

Different sugars begin to caramelize at different temperatures:

| Sugar | Approximate caramelization temperature |
| --- | --- |
| Fructose (fruit sugar) | about 230 °F (110 °C) |
| Glucose | about 320 °F (160 °C) |
| Sucrose (table sugar) | about 320 to 340 °F (160 to 170 °C) |
| Maltose | about 356 °F (180 °C) |

Recent research has shown that sucrose can also begin to break down slowly at lower temperatures when held there for long periods, so caramelization depends on both temperature and time. For everyday cooking, these approximate numbers are useful guides.

## The stages of sugar syrups and caramel

When sugar is dissolved in water and heated, the water evaporates and the temperature of the syrup rises. Confectioners use these stages for candies and desserts:

- **Thread stage, about 223 to 235 °F (106 to 113 °C):** used for syrups.
- **Soft ball, about 235 to 245 °F (113 to 118 °C):** fudge and fondant.
- **Firm ball, about 245 to 250 °F (118 to 121 °C):** caramel candies.
- **Hard ball, about 250 to 265 °F (121 to 129 °C):** nougat and marshmallows.
- **Soft crack, about 270 to 290 °F (132 to 143 °C):** butterscotch and taffy.
- **Hard crack, about 300 to 310 °F (149 to 154 °C):** brittles and lollipops.
- **Caramel, about 320 to 350 °F (160 to 177 °C) and above:** the sugar turns golden to deep amber.

A candy thermometer helps, but color and smell are also good guides for caramel.

## Dry caramel versus wet caramel

There are two main ways to make caramel.

### Dry caramel

Sugar is heated alone in a pan until it melts and browns.

1. Spread an even layer of sugar in a heavy, light-colored saucepan or skillet.
2. Heat it over medium heat. The sugar melts first around the edges.
3. As it melts, gently push the unmelted sugar toward the melted areas with a heatproof spatula, or swirl the pan.
4. Continue until all the sugar is melted and the caramel reaches the color you want.

Dry caramel is fast but requires attention, because it can burn quickly and heats unevenly.

### Wet caramel

Sugar is dissolved in water and cooked until the water evaporates and the sugar caramelizes.

1. Combine sugar with about one-quarter of its weight in water in a heavy saucepan.
2. Stir gently over medium heat until the sugar dissolves completely.
3. Stop stirring once it boils. Swirl the pan gently if needed.
4. Cook until the syrup turns golden to amber.

Wet caramel takes longer but is easier to control, because the water helps distribute heat evenly.

## Preventing crystallization

One common problem with caramel is crystallization: the syrup suddenly becomes cloudy and grainy, turning into a lump of crystals. This happens when sugar molecules regroup into crystals instead of staying dissolved. A single undissolved crystal on the side of the pan or a spoon can trigger a chain reaction.

To prevent crystallization:

- **Dissolve the sugar completely** before the syrup boils.
- **Do not stir** after the syrup boils; swirl the pan instead.
- **Wash down the sides** of the pan with a wet pastry brush to dissolve stray crystals.
- **Cover the pan for a minute** after it comes to a boil, so steam washes down the sides.
- **Add an interfering agent** such as a little corn syrup, honey, lemon juice or cream of tartar. These break sucrose into glucose and fructose or physically interfere with crystal formation.

## Color and flavor

As caramel cooks, its flavor changes dramatically:

- **Pale gold:** mild, sweet, slightly buttery.
- **Medium amber:** rich, balanced sweet and toasty flavor, ideal for most sauces.
- **Deep amber to mahogany:** intense, slightly bitter and complex, great for desserts that need contrast.
- **Black and smoking:** burnt and acrid; start over.

Caramel continues to cook from residual heat after you remove it from the stove. Take it off just before it reaches the color you want, or stop the cooking by adding cream or butter.

## Making a caramel sauce

A simple caramel sauce combines caramelized sugar with cream and butter:

1. Make a dry or wet caramel with 1 cup (200 g) sugar and cook it to medium amber.
2. Remove the pan from the heat and carefully whisk in 6 tablespoons (85 g) butter. The mixture will foam vigorously.
3. Slowly pour in 1/2 cup (120 ml) warm heavy cream while whisking. Warm cream reduces splattering and seizing.
4. Add a pinch of salt or flaky salt for salted caramel.
5. Let it cool slightly. It thickens as it cools.

If the caramel seizes into hard lumps when the cream is added, return it to low heat and stir until the lumps melt again.

## Safety first

Caramel is extremely hot, much hotter than boiling water. At 340 °F (170 °C), molten sugar sticks to skin and causes serious burns. Follow these precautions:

- Use a heavy, deep saucepan to prevent splattering.
- Keep children and pets away from the stove.
- Wear long sleeves and consider oven mitts when adding liquids.
- Add cream or butter slowly and stand back, as the mixture bubbles violently.
- Never taste caramel straight from the pan.
- Keep a bowl of ice water nearby. If caramel touches your skin, immerse the area in cold water immediately.

## Caramelized onions: slow and patient

Recipes often promise caramelized onions in 10 minutes, but true caramelized onions take much longer, usually 40 minutes to an hour. Onions contain natural sugars, as well as water and proteins. As they cook slowly, the water evaporates, and both caramelization and Maillard reactions turn them sweet, soft and deeply brown.

1. Slice onions evenly.
2. Cook them in butter or oil over medium-low heat in a wide pan, with a pinch of salt.
3. Stir occasionally at first, then more often as they brown.
4. If the bottom of the pan starts to darken too much, add a splash of water and scrape up the browned bits. This prevents burning and spreads the flavor.
5. Continue until the onions are jammy and deep golden brown.

A pinch of baking soda speeds up browning by making the onions slightly alkaline, but it also makes them softer and can create a slightly soapy taste if overused.

## Caramelizing vegetables and fruit

Roasting vegetables at high temperatures caramelizes their natural sugars. Carrots, sweet potatoes, parsnips, onions, beets and winter squash become sweet and golden. Fruits caramelize beautifully too: try pears, apples, bananas, peaches or pineapple sautéed in butter and a little sugar, or grilled.

For the best caramelization, use high heat, a single layer and enough space so the food browns rather than steams.

## Crème brûlée and torched sugar

The crisp sugar crust on crème brûlée is made by caramelizing a thin layer of sugar on top of the custard. A kitchen torch heats the sugar quickly so it melts and turns golden without warming the custard underneath. Spread a thin, even layer of granulated sugar, move the torch flame in small circles until the sugar melts and browns, and let it cool for a minute until it hardens into a glassy crust.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why did my caramel turn grainy?**
It crystallized, often because undissolved sugar crystals or stirring triggered crystal formation. Dissolve the sugar fully, avoid stirring after boiling and wash down the sides of the pan.

**Can I make caramel without a thermometer?**
Yes. Watch the color and smell closely. Remove it from the heat when it reaches medium amber and smells nutty and rich.

**How do I clean a pan with hardened caramel?**
Fill the pan with water and bring it to a boil. The caramel will dissolve.

**Why are my caramelized onions bitter?**
They may have cooked too quickly on high heat or burned in spots. Cook them slowly, stir regularly and add a splash of water when they stick.

**Can I use brown sugar for caramel?**
It is possible, but brown sugar contains molasses, which makes it harder to judge the color and easier to burn. White sugar gives more control.

## The bottom line

Caramelization is the browning of sugar under heat, creating color and complex flavors. Different sugars caramelize at different temperatures, and caramel can be made dry or wet. Prevent crystallization by dissolving sugar fully, avoiding stirring and washing down the sides of the pan. Respect the heat of molten sugar, and stop the cooking with butter and cream for a sauce. Apply the same principle slowly to onions and vegetables, and you will unlock deep, sweet, irresistible flavors in your everyday cooking.
