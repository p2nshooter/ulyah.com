Professional cooks often say that a dish which tastes flat, heavy or "missing something" usually needs one of two things: salt or acid. A squeeze of lemon over grilled fish, a splash of vinegar in a pot of lentils, a spoonful of yogurt in a curry or a dash of lime on tacos can make every other flavor sharper and brighter. But acids do much more than season. They change the texture of meat and fish, the color of vegetables, the way beans soften, how milk behaves, how cakes rise and even how long food keeps.

This guide explains what acids are in the kitchen, how they affect flavor, what they do to proteins in marinades and ceviche, how they change vegetables and beans, their role with dairy, eggs and baking, how they help preserve food, and why some pans react badly with acidic ingredients.

## Acids in the kitchen

The acids cooks use every day include:

| Ingredient | Main acid | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|
| Lemons and limes | citric acid | dressings, finishing dishes, marinades, baking |
| Vinegars | acetic acid | dressings, pickles, sauces, marinades |
| Yogurt, buttermilk, sour cream | lactic acid | marinades, baking, sauces |
| Wine | tartaric and other acids | deglazing, braising, sauces |
| Tomatoes | citric and malic acids | sauces, stews |
| Tamarind | tartaric acid | sauces, curries, drinks |
| Cream of tartar | tartaric acid (as a salt) | stabilizing egg whites, baking powder |

Acidity is measured on the pH scale: the lower the pH, the more acidic. Lemon juice and vinegar are strongly acidic; tomatoes and yogurt are mildly acidic.

## Acid and flavor

Acid wakes up the palate. A little acid:

- **balances richness**, cutting through fat in fried food, creamy sauces and fatty meats;
- **balances sweetness**, which is why a little lemon brightens fruit desserts and jam;
- **sharpens other flavors**, making herbs, spices and salt more noticeable;
- **adds freshness** to long-cooked dishes such as stews and soups.

A common technique is to add a **finishing acid** at the end of cooking: a squeeze of citrus or a few drops of vinegar just before serving. Heat dulls the fresh aroma of citrus and some vinegars, so adding them at the end keeps them lively. Our guide to [how to season food](/kitchen/how-to-season-food/) explains how salt, acid, fat and heat work together.

## Acid and proteins: marinades

### What acid does to proteins

Proteins are long chains folded into specific shapes. Acid makes those chains unfold and then tangle together, a process called **denaturation**. Heat does the same thing, which is why acid can make raw fish look "cooked".

### How marinades really work

Marinades usually combine an acid, oil, salt and aromatics. Some points to know:

- **Acid works mainly on the surface.** It penetrates slowly, so it flavors and tenderizes the outer layer rather than the whole piece of meat.
- **Too long in a strong acid** can make the surface mushy, mealy or chalky, especially with fish, seafood and chicken breast.
- **Salt matters as much as acid.** Salt in a marinade seasons the meat and helps it retain moisture, as we explain in our guide to [how salt works and brining](/kitchen/how-salt-works-brining/).
- **Yogurt and buttermilk** are gentle, mildly acidic marinades that tenderize without turning meat mushy, which is why they are used in many traditional chicken dishes.

As a rough guide, delicate fish needs only a short marinating time, chicken pieces a few hours and tougher cuts longer, but always follow your recipe and avoid very long soaks in strong acids. Marinate in the refrigerator, and do not reuse raw marinade as a sauce unless you boil it first.

### Ceviche and food safety

In ceviche, raw fish is "cooked" in citrus juice. The acid changes the texture and appearance of the fish, but **it does not kill harmful bacteria and parasites in the way that proper cooking does**. If you make ceviche at home, use very fresh fish from a trusted source that is suitable for eating raw, keep it cold, and follow your national food safety advice. People who are pregnant, elderly or have weakened immune systems are usually advised to avoid raw fish.

## Acid and vegetables

### Color

Acid affects the natural pigments in vegetables in different ways:

- **Green vegetables** contain chlorophyll, which turns an olive, drab color when cooked with acid. That is why adding lemon juice to green beans or broccoli at the start of cooking dulls their color. Add acid just before serving instead.
- **Red and purple vegetables** such as red cabbage, purple carrots and purple potatoes contain pigments called anthocyanins, which stay bright red or pink in acid and turn bluish or greenish in alkaline conditions. A splash of vinegar keeps braised red cabbage a vivid color. Beets are colored by different pigments that react less to acid but bleed easily into cooking water.
- **White vegetables**, such as cauliflower and onions, can stay whiter with a little acid.

We explore vegetable colors in our guide to [why vegetables change color when cooked](/kitchen/why-vegetables-change-color/).

### Texture

Acid strengthens the pectin that holds plant cell walls together. As a result:

- vegetables cooked with acid **stay firmer**;
- potatoes in a tomato or vinegar-based dish may take longer to soften;
- **beans and lentils** cooked with tomatoes or vinegar from the start can stay hard for a long time. Add acidic ingredients once they are tender, as explained in our guide to [cooking dried beans and lentils](/kitchen/how-to-cook-dried-beans-and-lentils/).

### Browning of cut fruit

Cut apples, pears, bananas and avocados turn brown because of enzymes that react with oxygen. Acid slows these enzymes, which is why a squeeze of lemon keeps cut fruit and guacamole looking fresh for longer.

## Acid and dairy

Acid makes milk proteins clump together. This can be a problem or a technique:

- **A problem:** adding lemon juice or tomatoes to a hot milk or cream sauce can make it curdle. Heat the dairy gently and add acid carefully, or use cream, which is more stable thanks to its higher fat content. Our guide to [why sauces split](/kitchen/why-sauces-split-emulsions/) explains how to rescue a curdled sauce.
- **A technique:** fresh cheeses such as paneer and simple ricotta-style cheeses are made by heating milk and adding an acid, then draining the curds.
- **Buttermilk substitute:** stirring a little lemon juice or vinegar into milk and letting it stand thickens it slightly and adds tang, a common substitute in baking.

## Acid and eggs

- **Whipping egg whites:** a little acid, such as cream of tartar or lemon juice, helps stabilize egg white foam, making meringues and soufflés more stable.
- **Poaching eggs:** some cooks add a splash of vinegar to poaching water, which helps the white set faster; very fresh eggs often hold together well without it. Our guide to [cooking eggs every way](/kitchen/how-to-cook-eggs-every-way/) covers poaching.

## Acid and baking

In baking, acid plays a crucial role in leavening:

- **Baking soda** is alkaline. It needs an acid, such as buttermilk, yogurt, brown sugar, honey, cocoa or lemon juice, to produce carbon dioxide gas that makes batters rise.
- **Baking powder** already contains both an alkali and an acid, so it works with neutral ingredients.

Too much baking soda without enough acid leaves a soapy, metallic taste. Our guide to [baking soda vs. baking powder](/kitchen/baking-soda-vs-baking-powder/) explains the chemistry.

Acid also tenderizes baked goods slightly and affects browning: more acidic batters tend to brown less, and more alkaline ones brown more.

## Acid and starch

Acid can weaken some starch thickeners during long cooking, so a lemon-flavored sauce thickened with cornstarch may thin out if the acid is added early. Adding acidic ingredients towards the end helps. Our guide to [how starches thicken sauces](/kitchen/how-starches-thicken-sauces/) explains why.

## Acid and preservation

Many traditional methods of preserving food rely on acidity, because most harmful bacteria struggle to grow in sufficiently acidic conditions:

- **pickling** in vinegar;
- **fermentation**, in which friendly bacteria produce lactic acid, as in sauerkraut, kimchi and yogurt;
- **jams and preserves**, where fruit acids work alongside sugar.

Safe preservation depends on reaching the right acidity and following tested methods. Our guide to [quick pickles at home](/kitchen/quick-pickles-at-home/) explains refrigerator pickles, which are simple and safe when stored correctly.

## Acid and cookware

Strongly acidic foods, such as tomato sauce, wine-based braises and vinegar reductions, can react with some metals:

- **Aluminum and unlined copper** can react with acids, giving food a metallic taste and discoloring the pan.
- **Bare or poorly seasoned cast iron** can react with long-cooked acidic dishes, affecting the taste and stripping the seasoning.
- **Stainless steel, enameled cast iron, glass and ceramic** are non-reactive and safe for acidic cooking and marinating.

Marinate in glass, ceramic, stainless steel or food-safe plastic containers.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does lemon juice "cook" fish safely?

No. It changes the texture and appearance but does not make raw fish safe in the way heat does. Follow food safety advice and use fish suitable for eating raw.

### Why did my green vegetables turn gray?

They were probably cooked with acid or overcooked. Add lemon or vinegar just before serving to keep them bright.

### Can a marinade be too acidic?

Yes. Strong acids for long periods can make the surface of meat and fish mushy. Balance acid with oil and salt, and marinate for a sensible time.

## The bottom line

Acids brighten flavor, balance richness and sweetness and sharpen other seasonings, especially when added at the end of cooking. They unfold proteins, which is why marinades tenderize the surface of meat and why ceviche looks cooked, though acid does not make raw fish safe. Acid dulls green vegetables but keeps red ones vivid, firms vegetables and slows the softening of beans, curdles milk, stabilizes egg whites, activates baking soda and helps preserve food. Use non-reactive pans for acidic dishes, and keep a lemon or a bottle of good vinegar within reach.
