A homemade nesting box is one of the most beautiful projects for bird lovers. It costs little, is ready in an afternoon and can provide a home for titmice, nuthatches or sparrows for many years. In order for it to be really accepted and for the brood to be safe in it, a few details are important: the right wood, suitable internal dimensions, an entry hole of the right size and a way to clean it.

These instructions show you step by step how to build a classic cavity nesting box that is suitable for titmice and other small cavity nesters. At the end you will find variants for other bird species and the most common mistakes.

## The right material

### Wood

The best choice is **untreated, unplaned solid wood**, around 18 to 20 millimeters thick. The following are well suited:

- **Spruce or pine:** inexpensive, easy to work with, lasts for many years with good protection from rain
- **Larch or Douglas fir:** naturally more weather-resistant
- **Oak:** very durable, but heavy and more difficult to work with

The **rough inside** is important: young birds must be able to cling to it in order to climb up to the entrance hole. If the inside of the wood is planed smooth, roughen it with a rasp or carve horizontal grooves below the entrance hole.

### What you should avoid

- **Particleboard and pressed wood:** swell when exposed to moisture and may contain glue and formaldehyde.
- **Thin plywood panels:** provide poor insulation, the box heats up in the summer and cools down significantly at night.
- **Painted or impregnated wood inside:** Fumes can harm the young birds.
- **Metal and plastic:** become hot or form condensation.

### Protection from the weather

You can treat the box **outside** with an environmentally friendly, water-based wood stain or linseed oil. Inside, the wood remains untreated. A roof overhang and a piece of roofing felt or a narrow sheet of metal at the top also protect against rain.

## Tools and accessories

- Saw (hand saw, jigsaw or circular saw)
- Cordless screwdriver or drill
- Hole saw, Forstner bit or auger bit for the entrance hole
- Wood drill for ventilation and drainage holes, about 6 millimeters
- Folding rule or tape measure, square, pencil
- Rasp or sandpaper
- **Stainless steel screws** or galvanized screws, approximately 3.5 × 35 millimeters
- two small hinges or two nails as a pivot point for the cleaning flap
- a hook, twist lock or wire for closing
- Aluminum nails or a tree-friendly suspension, for example a strip with wire

## The dimensions

A tried and tested tit box has an **inside area of ​​around 12 × 12 centimeters** and a height from the ground to the entrance hole of at least **17 centimeters**. This distance is important so that cats, martens or magpies cannot reach the nest with their paws or beaks.

For 20 millimeter thick wood you need the following parts:

| part | number | Dimensions (width × height) | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Back wall | 1 | 16×35cm | longer than the box, for hanging |
| front wall | 1 | 16×24cm | with entry hole |
| side wall | 2 | 12 × 26 cm at the front, 28 cm at the back | cut diagonally for the roof |
| floor | 1 | 12×12cm | with drainage holes |
| roof | 1 | 20×22cm | with projection to the front |

The dimensions are guidelines. What is important is not the millimeter, but the inner surface of around 12 × 12 centimeters, the depth under the entrance hole and a roof that protrudes to the front.

## The entry hole

The size of the entry hole determines which birds can use the box. Smaller birds prefer small holes because larger competitors and nest robbers stay out.

| diameter | Suitable for |
|---|---|
| 26-28mm | Blue tit, coal tit, marsh tit, crested tit |
| 32mm | Great tit, nuthatch, tree sparrow, pied flycatcher |
| 34-35mm | house sparrow |
| oval hole 30 × 45 mm | Redstart |

The hole is located in the upper third of the front wall, about 4 to 5 centimeters from the roof. You can find out more about box types and hole sizes in the guide [Nest box types and entry hole] (/vogelwissen/nistkastentypen-einflugloch/).

## Step by step

### 1. Cut parts

Mark all the pieces on the board with a pencil and square and saw them. You cut the side walls at an angle so that they are lower at the front than at the back. This means that the roof later has a slight slope towards the front and rain runs off.

### 2. Drill entry hole

Drill the entry hole into the front wall using the hole saw or Forstner bit. Work slowly and place some scrap wood underneath so that the wood on the back doesn't tear out. Sand the edges lightly so the birds don't hurt themselves.

### 3. Roughen the inside

Roughen the inside of the front wall below the hole with the rasp or saw a few shallow grooves. The young birds later climb to the exit.

### 4. Prepare soil

Drill four holes about 6 millimeters in diameter in the ground. Moisture can drain through them. Also cut the corners of the floor slightly to create additional small ventilation gaps.

### 5. Screw the box together

First screw the side walls to the back wall, then the floor between the side walls. The floor should be raised a few millimeters so that rain drips off the walls instead of running into the joints. Pre-drill the screw holes to prevent the wood from cracking.

### 6. Install cleaning flap

So that you can clean the box in the fall, a wall or the roof must be able to be opened. The simplest solution: The **front wall is only attached to the top of the side walls with a nail or screw on the left and right as a pivot point**. Secure it at the bottom with a twist lock or a hook. This way you can swing the front wall outwards. Alternatively, you can attach the roof with hinges.

Important: The closure must be so tight that a marten or raccoon cannot open the flap.

### 7. Install the roof

Screw the roof in place if the front wall is the cleaning hatch, or attach it with hinges if you want the roof to open. The roof should protrude a few centimeters towards the front to protect the entrance hole from rain and robbers. There may be a narrow gap between the roof and side walls to ensure ventilation.

### 8. Protect outside

If necessary, treat the outside of the box with an environmentally friendly glaze or linseed oil. Let it dry and air out well before hanging it up. A piece of roofing felt on the roof extends the lifespan.

### 9. Prepare suspension

The extended back wall offers space for hanging at the top and bottom. Drill holes there for screws or attach a bar to which you can hang the box over a branch using a wire hanger. This way you don't have to drive a nail into the tree. The guide [Hanging the nesting box correctly] (/vogelwissen/nistkasten-richtig-aufhaengen/) explains how and where to hang the box.

## Variants for other birds

### Semi-cave for robins, black redstarts and spotted flycatchers

Instead of a small hole, the front wall only has a **half height**, about 8 to 10 centimeters. The upper part remains open. Half caves are hung lower and well hidden, for example in thick ivy or on a protected house wall. More about robins in the guide [Robins in the Garden](/vogelwissen/rotkehlchen-im-garten/).

### Sparrow colony house

For house sparrows, build a wide box with three or four chambers next to each other, each with its own entry hole of around 32 to 35 millimeters. Sparrows like to breed socially. The guide [House Sparrow: How to Help Sparrows](/vogelwissen/haussperling-spatzen-helfen/) explains why they need help.

### Starling box

Starlings need a larger box with an inner surface of around 15 x 15 centimeters and an entry hole of 45 millimeters. It is hung at least three meters high.

## The most common construction errors

1. **Perch under the entrance hole:** Looks nice, but only helps predators like magpies who cling to it. Tits don't need a perch.
2. **Box too shallow:** If the nest is too close to the entrance hole, a cat can reach into it.
3. **Smooth inner walls:** Young birds cannot come up and fly out.
4. **No drainage:** Water collects on the ground, the nest gets wet, the young freeze.
5. **No cleaning hatch:** A box that cannot be opened will be stuffed with old nests and parasites.
6. **Thin wood or incorrect material:** The box heats up or becomes damp.
7. **Painted on the inside:** Paint and varnish only belong on the outside, if at all.
8. **Holes too large for small birds:** A 32 millimeter hole attracts great tits and sparrows that displace blue tits.

## A project with children

Building nesting boxes is an ideal family project. Children can mark parts, screw in screws and paint the outside of the box with non-toxic paint, preferably in muted natural tones. Adults are responsible for working with the saw and drill. In the spring you will observe together whether and when titmice move in. If you want to take a closer look, you can install a small camera. The guide [Setting up a nesting box camera] (/vogelwissen/nistkastenkamera-einrichten/) describes how this can be done without disruption.

## Care after construction

A nesting box requires little care, but needs to be cleaned once a year: in late autumn, when there are no longer any birds nesting in it. You remove the old nest and check whether everything is still tight and stable. The guide [cleaning the nesting box] (/vogelwissen/nistkasten-reinigen/) describes how to do this.

## Frequently asked questions

**When should the box be ready?** Ideally in autumn or by the end of February at the latest. Boxes hung up in the fall serve as a place to sleep in the winter, and the birds know them when the search for a nesting site begins in the spring.

**How ​​long does a self-made box last?** Made of 20 millimeter thick wood, protected on the outside and covered with roofing felt, often ten years or longer. Boxes made of wood concrete last even longer, but are almost impossible to make yourself.

**Can I put nesting material in it?** No. Tits build their own nests and prefer an empty, clean box. It's better to lay out material nearby in spring.

## Conclusion

A good nesting box is made of untreated wood about 20 millimeters thick, has an inner surface of around 12 × 12 centimeters, at least 17 centimeters depth below the entrance hole, a rough inside, drainage holes, a roof overhang and a cleaning flap. The size of the entry hole determines who moves in: 26 to 28 millimeters for blue tits, 32 millimeters for great tits and nuthatches. Skip the perches and interior paint, and your box will provide a safe home for bird families for years to come.
