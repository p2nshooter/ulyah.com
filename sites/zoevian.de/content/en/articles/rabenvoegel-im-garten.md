Hardly any other family of birds is seen as differently as the corvids. For some, magpies and crows are nest robbers who empty the garden of songbirds. For others, they are among the smartest animals ever, using tools, hiding food and remembering faces for years. Both perspectives have a kernel of truth, but the reality is more complex.

This guide introduces you to the corvids that you see most often in German gardens and cities, explains their behavior and classifies the role they play for other birds.

## Who belongs to the corvids?

The corvids, scientifically known as *Corvidae*, are songbirds, even if their cawing sounds little like singing. The species you will encounter in Germany include:

- **Magpie**
- **Jay**
- **Carrion crow** and in the east the **hooded crow**
- **Rook**
- **jackdaw**
- **Common Raven**
- rarer the **pine jay** in mountain forests

## The magpie

The magpie is distinctive with its black and white plumage and long tail. The wings and tail shimmer **blue, green and purple** in the sunlight. It is about 40 to 50 centimeters long, with the tail making up almost half.

### way of life

Magpies live in pairs in a fixed territory and often stay together for life. Their nest is a conspicuous **twigball with a roof**, high up in trees or large bushes. They often build several nests and only use one of them. Abandoned magpie nests are later used by long-eared owls, kestrels and other birds.

### Intelligence

The magpie was one of the first animals outside of mammals that researchers observed recognizing itself in a mirror. In experiments, magpies tried to remove a colored mark on their own feathers that they could only see in a mirror.

### The myth of the thieving magpie

The reputation that magpies steal shiny objects is largely a myth. In experiments, magpies reacted more cautiously than curiously to shiny objects.

## The jay

The jay is the most colorful corvid: **pink-brown plumage**, a black streak of whiskers, a lightly striped hood and a **bright blue-black banded field** on the wings. The white rump is noticeable in flight.

### The forester among the birds

In autumn, the jay collects acorns, beechnuts and hazelnuts and hides them in the ground, under moss and in cracks in trees. A single bird creates many thousands of hiding places. He finds a surprising number of them, but not all of them. Young oaks grow from the forgotten acorns. The jay contributes significantly to the expansion of oak forests.

### The Guardian of the Forest

Jays warn of dangers, such as foxes, birds of prey or people, with a loud, rough “rattle”**. Other animals react to this. They are also skilled voice imitators: for example, they imitate the call of the common buzzard in a deceptively realistic manner.

### In the garden

At the feeding place, the jay likes to get **whole peanuts, walnuts and sunflower seeds**, which it often carries away in its throat pouch and hides elsewhere.

## Carrion crow, hooded crow and rook

### Carrion crow

The carrion crow is **all black**, including its beak and legs. They usually live in pairs or small family groups and build their nests individually in tall trees. Young, unmated crows form large flocks in winter.

### Hooded crow

The hooded crow lives east of the Elbe. It has **black head, wings and tail but a gray body**. In a narrow strip where the distribution areas meet, there are mixed breeds.

### Rook

The rook is slightly slimmer and has a **longer, more pointed beak**. You can recognize adult birds by the **naked, gray-white skin at the base of the beak**. Rooks breed in **colonies**, often with dozens of nests in a group of trees, even in the middle of cities. It's loud and some residents feel disturbed. However, rooks are protected and their colonies cannot simply be removed.

### Carrion crow or rook?

| feature | Carrion crow | Rook |
|---|---|---|
| beak root | black feathered | naked, gray-white (adult birds) |
| Beak shape | strong, curved | longer, sharper |
| plumage | black with a slight shine | black with a purple sheen, “pants” on the legs |
| brood | Individual nests | colonies |
| Reputation | hard “krah” | deeper, harsher “gah” |

## The jackdaw

The jackdaw is the smallest native corvid, about the size of a pigeon. She is black with a **gray neck** and has striking **light gray, almost white eyes**. Jackdaws live sociably, stay with their partner for life and breed in caves: in old trees, church towers, holes in walls and chimneys. Their call is a bright, happy **“kja”**.

In many regions, jackdaws rely on nesting aids because buildings are being renovated and cave trees are being felled. Special jackdaw boxes that are placed high on church towers or buildings have helped in many places.

## The raven

The common raven is the largest songbird in the world, the size of a common buzzard. It has a **very strong beak**, shaggy throat feathers and a **wedge-shaped tail** in flight. Its call is a deep, sonorous **“krok”**. Ravens were heavily persecuted in Germany for a long time and almost disappeared. Today they have spread again in many regions, but rarely appear in gardens.

## How smart are corvids?

Corvids are among the most intelligent animals. Researchers have observed abilities in them that were long thought only of great apes:

- **Tool Use:** Some species of crows bend wires into hooks to retrieve food from tubes.
- **Planning:** Jays and other jays hide food and relocate when they notice that a member of their species has been watching them.
- **Recognizing faces:** Crows remember people who have done something to them and warn others of their species about them.
- **Cooperation:** Couples and families work together to defend their territory and drive away birds of prey.

## Do corvids eat away the songbirds?

This is the most common question on the topic and it deserves an honest answer.

### Which is true

Magpies, jays and crows raid nests. They eat eggs and nestlings of blackbirds, thrushes and other free-breeding birds. Anyone who observes this in their own garden is understandably affected.

### What the research says

Research in several European countries has shown that corvids are **generally not the cause of large-scale songbird declines**. Where there are lots of magpies, there are often lots of songbirds because both benefit from the same good habitats. The loss of hedges and insects, intensive agriculture, glass surfaces and domestic cats have a much greater impact. Songbirds compensate for nest losses by brooding several times per year. The fact that individual nests are plundered is part of the natural cycle.

### What you can do

Instead of driving away corvids, it makes more sense to offer songbirds **better hiding places**:

- **Dense, thorny shrubs** such as hawthorn, blackthorn, barberry and wild rose, in which nests are well hidden. More about this in the guide [Native shrubs for birds] (/vogelwissen/heimische-straeucher-fuer-voegel/).
- **Nesting boxes with a suitably small entrance hole** that magpies and jays cannot get into. A box should be deep enough to leave about 17 centimeters of space from the hole to the bottom of the nest. Information in the guide [types of nesting boxes and entrance holes] (/vogelwissen/nistkastentypen-einflugloch/).
- **No open food for corvids during the breeding season** in the immediate vicinity of songbird nests.

## Corvids at the feeding place

Corvids are sometimes dominant at the feeding site and crowd out smaller birds. If you mainly want to feed tits and finches, the following will help:

- **Feed silos and dispensers with small openings** where large birds can hardly eat.
- **Protective grid** around feeders with a mesh size of about five centimeters through which small birds can slip, but not large ones.
- **No food on the floor** on a grand scale.

In principle, there is nothing wrong with offering something to corvids, such as whole walnuts or peanuts for the jay. The guide [Feeding birds correctly] (/vogelwissen/voegel-fuettern-grundlagen/) describes how to design a feeding place sensibly.

## Legal protection

All European bird species, including corvids, are protected under European and German nature conservation law. Some species such as carrion crow and magpie may be hunted under certain conditions in certain federal states. For private individuals, however, the following applies: Destroying nests, catching or killing birds is prohibited. Anyone who feels disturbed by a rook colony should contact the local nature conservation authority.

## Watching corvids

Corvids are grateful objects of observation. Pay attention to their social behavior: pairs of jackdaws groom each other's feathers, crows play in the wind, and magpies sometimes hold real gatherings when a member of their species has died. In autumn you can watch jays flying out of the oak forest with their full throat pouches. If you want to go deeper, you can find further tips in the guide [Birdwatching for beginners] (/vogelwissen/vogelbeobachtung-fuer-einsteiger/).

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do crows sometimes attack people?** This almost only happens during the breeding season, when young crows have just left the nest and are still sitting on the ground or on low branches. The parents then defend them with mock attacks. Give the area a small berth; After two to three weeks the nightmare is over.

**Are magpies raiding my nesting box?** A well-built box with a small hole and sufficient depth is hardly accessible to magpies. Open half-caves are more at risk and should therefore be well hidden in dense ivy or a thorny hedge.

**Are corvids becoming more common?** Magpies and crows are more common in cities today than they were a few decades ago because they find food and shelter there. In the open agricultural landscape, however, stocks are stable or declining in many places.

**Can I get rid of corvids from the garden?** Hardly permanently, and that's not necessary. If you don't leave open food on the ground and close garbage cans, you make the garden less attractive for them.

## Conclusion

Magpies, jays, crows and jackdaws are smart, social and adaptable birds that belong in our gardens and cities. They occasionally raid nests, but are generally not to blame for the decline in songbird numbers. If you want to help songbirds, plant dense native bushes, hang safe nesting boxes and design the feeding area appropriately. And if you take a closer look, you will discover behavior in the corvids that never ceases to amaze.
