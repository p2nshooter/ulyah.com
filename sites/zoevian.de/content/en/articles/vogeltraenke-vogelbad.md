There are bird feeders in almost every garden, but a bird bath is much rarer. Water is at least as important for birds as food. Birds drink every day, and they bathe regularly to keep their feathers clean and functional. In hot summers, puddles and ditches dry out, and in winter everything freezes over. This is precisely when a shallow bowl of clean water becomes one of the most valuable places in the garden.

This guide will show you what a good bird bath looks like, where it should be located, how to keep it clean and how to use it in heat and frost.

## Why birds need water

### Drink

Birds constantly lose water through breathing and droppings. Grain eaters such as finches and sparrows, whose food is very dry, need to drink particularly frequently. Insectivores get some of their water needs through food, but still drink regularly.

Most birds drink by dipping their beak into the water, taking a sip, and then lifting their head so the water runs down their throat. Pigeons are an exception: they can suck in the water directly.

### Bathing

Bathing is plumage care for birds. They dip their chest and wings into the water, flap their wings and let the water run between the feathers. They then groom themselves extensively, arrange each feather and distribute fat from the preen gland. Clean, well-organized feathers provide better insulation against cold and wet conditions and are essential for flying.

Blackbirds, starlings, sparrows, tits and robins particularly like to bathe, often several times a day. Sometimes whole groups come one after the other and the water sprays in all directions.

## This is what a good bird bath looks like

### It has to be flat

The most important feature is a **shallow water depth**. Small birds stand in the water when bathing and cannot swim. A bowl that is only one to two centimeters deep at the edge and drops to a maximum of five to eight centimeters towards the middle is ideal. This is how small and larger birds find their appropriate depth.

### Rough instead of smooth

Birds slip easily on glazed or very smooth surfaces. A bowl made of **unglazed clay, natural stone or concrete** provides better support. For smooth bowls, a layer of flat pebbles or a large, flat stone in the middle for birds to land on and drink from helps.

### Big enough

A bowl with a diameter of 30 to 50 centimeters offers space for several birds and is not emptied as quickly by bathing. Smaller bowls also work, but need to be refilled more often.

### Suitable shapes

- **Coasters of large flower pots**, made of clay
- **flat natural stone bowls** or old millstones
- **Bird baths on a base**, about 60 to 100 centimeters high
- **Floor waterers** that stand directly on the ground or a flat stone

Raised birdbaths on a base are safer from cats, ground waterers are often preferred by blackbirds, thrushes and robins because they also drink on the ground in nature.

## The right location

A good place is crucial for whether birds accept the waterer.

1. **Clear view:** When bathing, birds are wet, heavy and unable to fly for a few seconds. You need to see cats and sparrowhawks in time. Therefore, **do not place the waterer directly next to dense bushes** where a robber can sneak up. A distance of two to three meters from the nearest cover is good.
2. **Cover within reach:** At the same time, the birds need a bush or tree nearby where they fly to clean themselves after bathing.
3. **Partial shade:** In full sun, the water warms quickly, algae grows, and it evaporates. A spot in partial shade, such as under a light tree, is ideal.
4. **Distance from the feeding place:** Do not place the waterer directly under the feeding place. Otherwise, feces and leftover food will fall in.
5. **Easily visible:** If you can see the waterer from the window, you have a daily view and can immediately tell when the water is dirty.

## Cleanliness: the most important thing of all

A bird bath is only helpful if the water is clean. Polluted water can harbor pathogens such as trichomonas or salmonella, which make birds sick. More about this in the guide [Keeping the feeding area clean] (/vogelwissen/futterstelle-hygiene-krankheiten/).

This is how you keep the troughs clean:

- **Change water daily.** In summer when there is a lot of activity, try twice a day.
- **Brush the bowl several times a week** with a brush you only use for this purpose and hot water.
- **No additives.** No detergent, no chemicals against algae, no salt. After disinfection, rinse thoroughly in case of illness.
- **For sick birds** temporarily dismantle the waterer and clean it thoroughly.

One advantage of daily water changes: mosquitoes have no chance to develop their larvae in the bowl. This is also important because mosquitoes transmit diseases such as the Usutu virus, which primarily affects blackbirds. More about this in the guide [The Blackbird in the Garden](/vogelwissen/amsel-im-garten/).

## Help with heat

Hot, dry summers are stressful for birds. Natural water sources are drying up and birds have to fly long distances to drink. How to help on hot days:

- **Set up several waterers** in different places so that the birds don't all come together in one place.
- **Fill in fresh water** in the morning and change again in the afternoon if it is very hot.
- Find or create **shade** to keep the water cooler.
- **A small bottom bowl** for hedgehogs who are also thirsty at night is a nice addition.

On hot days, birds can sometimes be seen sitting with their beaks open and their wings spread. This is how they give off heat. This is normal, but shows how much they are suffering from the heat.

## water in winter

In winter, many people forget that birds need water even then. When everything is frozen over, an ice-free waterer for birds can be just as important as food.

### This is how you keep the drinking troughs free of ice

- **Pour lukewarm water several times a day.** No hot water, this can cause the shell to burst and is unpleasant for the birds.
- **Place a ping pong ball** on the water. Slight movement caused by wind delays freezing slightly.
- **Place a dark bowl in the sun** so it warms slightly during the day.
- **Special heated bird baths** are available from specialist retailers. Make sure you have a secure power supply outdoors.
- **Never add antifreeze, salt or glycerin** to water. These substances are toxic or harmful to birds.

### Swimming in frost?

Some birds also bathe in sub-zero temperatures. Experts do not entirely agree on whether this is dangerous in very severe frosts because wet feathers provide poorer insulation. However, healthy birds usually know when to bathe. If you are unsure, in very severe frost you can place a screen or some large stones over the tray so that the birds can drink but not completely submerge.

## Water for insects

Bees, bumblebees, butterflies and other insects also need water, especially on hot days. They can easily drown in a regular birdbath. Therefore, place some flat stones or a piece of wood in the bowl that protrudes above the surface of the water. Or set up a separate, very shallow insect drinker with pebbles or moss. More insects in the garden also mean more food for birds. The guide [Create a bird-friendly garden] (/vogelwissen/vogelfreundlicher-garten-anlegen/) describes how you can promote both.

## Dangers from other water sources

Not every watering hole in the garden is safe for birds:

- **Rain barrels:** Birds and insects fall in and drown because they cannot find purchase on the smooth walls. Cover it or place a wooden exit aid inside.
- **Pools:** Also a trap. A floating exit aid or a board on the edge helps.
- **Steep garden ponds:** A flat bank or shallow water area with stones allows birds to drink and bathe without danger.
- **Watering cans and buckets:** Do not leave standing with water.

## What you can observe at the trough

A bird bath is a wonderful observation spot. You will see how different birds bathe: Blackbirds almost completely submerge themselves and splash extensively, sparrows bathe in groups and bicker as they do so, titmice come briefly and hectically, robins are thorough and careful. In summer, parents bring their young to the watering place. And with a bit of luck, rarer guests such as bullfinches, grosbeaks and gray wagtails also come. The guide [Identifying the most common garden birds](/vogelwissen/haeufigste-gartenvoegel-erkennen/) will help you determine, and the guide [Photographing birds](/vogelwissen/voegel-fotografieren/) explains how you can capture such moments.

## Build a trough yourself

A simple bird bath is quick to make:

1. Take a large, flat clay coaster, about 40 centimeters in diameter.
2. Place a few flat pebbles inside, creating a very flat area on one side.
3. Place the bowl on an upturned clay pot, tree stump, or flat stone, depending on whether you want a ground or elevated waterer.
4. Make sure everything is stable and doesn't tip over when a large bird lands.
5. Fill with fresh water, about two to five centimeters deep.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why aren't birds coming to my waterer?** Often the bowl is too deep, too smooth or is too close to bushes where cats could be lurking. Sometimes birds simply need a few days until they discover a new watering hole.

**Can I use tap water?** Yes. Normal tap water works well. Rainwater from a clean barrel also works.

**Do I need a water feature?** Not necessarily. Quietly splashing or dripping water also attracts birds because they hear the noise. Small solar pumps or a drip container over the bowl are a nice addition.

**How ​​often do I really need to clean?** Change the water daily and brush the bowl several times a week. More often when there is a lot of activity in summer.

## Conclusion

A bird bath is one of the simplest and most effective ways to help birds in the garden. Choose a shallow, rough bowl with a few centimeters of water deep, place it nearby with clear visibility and cover, preferably in partial shade, and change the water every day. In the summer it helps you survive in the heat, and in the winter when kept ice-free it helps you survive. And you get a daily spectacle that no bird feeder can offer.
