Germany is a surprisingly rich country for bird watchers. Between the mud flats of the North Sea and the peaks of the Alps lie moors, river floodplains, large lakes, heaths, old beech forests and cultivated landscapes, each of which has its own unique bird communities. Added to this is the location in the middle of important migration routes: every spring and every autumn, millions of birds migrate across the country, rest on coasts and lakes and create natural spectacles that you won't forget.

This guide will introduce you to some of the most famous and worthwhile places, organize them by habitat and tell you when is the best time to visit.

## First of all: be considerate in protected areas

Most of these places are nature reserves, national parks or bird sanctuaries. Special rules apply there:

- **Stay on the paths**, observe entry bans.
- **Dogs on a leash.**
- **Keep your distance from resting birds.** Every flight costs energy, which migratory birds urgently need.
- **Use observation towers and huts.** They often offer the best views without being distracting.
- **Note the tides** in the Wadden Sea, mudflat hikes only with a guide.

More about considerate observation can be found in the guide [Bird Watching for Beginners] (/vogelwissen/vogelbeobachtung-fuer-einsteiger/).

## On the coast

### The Wadden Sea

The Wadden Sea on the North Sea coast is one of the most important bird areas in the world and is protected as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. In Germany it is part of three national parks in Lower Saxony, Schleswig-Holstein and Hamburg.

- **What you see:** Huge flocks of wading birds such as Dunlins, Common Knots, Bar-tailed Godwits and Oystercatchers, in autumn and spring Brent Geese and Barnacle Geese, as well as Shelducks, Avocets, Terns and Seagulls.
- **Best time:** Spring and especially autumn, when the migratory birds rest. The time around floods is particularly exciting, when the birds move from the flooded mudflats to higher resting places and gather there in large numbers.
- **Tip:** Many national park houses and conservation stations offer guided tours. In autumn, action days around bird migration take place in the region.

### Heligoland

The offshore island of Heligoland is a magnet for migratory birds and bird watchers.

- **What you see:** In spring and autumn, a surprising number of songbirds often rest on the small island, including rare strays from Siberia or North America. Northern gannets, guillemots, razorbills and kittiwakes breed at Lummenfelsen.
- **Best time:** May and June for breeding colonies, September and October for fall migration. In June you can experience the “guillemot jump” when the young guillemots, who are still unable to fly, jump from the rock into the sea.

### Baltic Sea coast and bay

On the Baltic Sea, especially in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, lagoon waters, lagoons and islands offer resting places for geese, swans, ducks and cranes. Sea eagles are comparatively common here.

## Crane resting places

The crane migration is one of the most impressive natural spectacles in Germany. In autumn, tens of thousands of cranes gather at traditional resting places before moving on to the southwest.

- **Rügen-Bock region** in the Western Pomerania Lagoon landscape, one of the largest crane resting places in Central Europe.
- **Linumer Teichland** in Brandenburg, northwest of Berlin.
- **Diepholzer Moornieerung** in Lower Saxony.
- **Other rest areas** in Brandenburg and Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania.

**Best time:** Late September to early November. The most impressive is the evening flight to the roosts, when the cranes fly in in wedge formations and with loud trumpet calls. Many regions offer guided tours and observation platforms so that the sensitive birds are not disturbed.

### Wild geese on the Lower Rhine

A large number of arctic wild geese, especially white-fronted geese, as well as bean geese and other species, overwinter on the Lower Lower Rhine in North Rhine-Westphalia every year. From around November to February they graze in meadows and fields and fly back and forth in large formations between sleeping waters and feeding areas in the morning and evening. Conservation centers in the region offer goose excursions during this time.

## Lakes and wetlands

### Lake Constance

Lake Constance is one of the most important resting places for water birds in Central Europe in winter. Tens of thousands of diving ducks, grebes, coots and seagulls spend the winter here. On the bank there are important protected areas such as the **Wollmatinger Ried** and the **Eriskircher Ried**, where you can observe reed breeders in spring and migratory birds in autumn.

### Federsee

The Federseemoor in Upper Swabia is one of the largest moor areas in southwest Germany. A long **footbridge** leads through reeds and reed meadows to the lake. Marsh harriers, bearded tits, reed warblers and many water birds live here.

### Ismaninger reservoir and Altmühlsee

In Bavaria, the **Ismaninger Speichersee** northeast of Munich and the **Bird Island in the Altmühlsee** are well-known destinations. They provide resting places for ducks, grebes, limousines and seagulls, especially during migration and in late summer when many ducks moult there.

### Dümmer and Steinhuder Meer

The two large shallow lakes in Lower Saxony are surrounded by wet meadows and moors. Geese and ducks rest here, and in the spring black-tailed godwits and lapwings court.

### Rieselfelder Münster

The former sewage fields in the north of Münster are now a protected area with shallow water areas and mud banks. They are particularly known for waders and ducks during migration and are well developed with observation huts.

## floodplains

### Elbe floodplain

The Elbe river landscape, protected as a biosphere reserve among other things, is a paradise for white storks, sea eagles, cranes and meadow nesting birds. The stork village **Rühstädt** in Brandenburg is well known, where many pairs of storks breed on roofs and poles every year.

### Lower Oder Valley

The Lower Oder Valley National Park on the border with Poland is one of the last natural floodplains in Central Europe. In spring, large areas are under water, attracting ducks, geese, swans and wading birds. In summer, species breed here that have become rare elsewhere.

## Forests, heaths and mountains

### Old beech forests

Old beech forests such as those in the **Jasmund**, **Kellerwald-Edersee** or **Hainich** national parks, some of which are UNESCO World Heritage Sites, are habitats for black woodpeckers, middle spotted woodpeckers, stock doves and little flycatchers. The guide [Woodpeckers in Germany](/vogelwissen/spechte-in-deutschland/) explains how you can recognize woodpeckers.

### Heathland landscapes

Heath larks, nightjars, stonechats and tree pipits live in heaths such as the **Lüneburg Heath**. The nightjar can be recognized at night by its purring song.

### The Alps

The **Berchtesgaden National Park** and other Alpine regions are home to birds that are rarely seen elsewhere in Germany: golden eagles, alpine choughs, wallcreepers, alpine dunnocks, snow sparrows and black grouse. The best time for hiking is summer, when the altitudes are free of snow.

### low mountain range

The low mountain ranges are also worth a visit. Mountain forest species such as ring ouzels, pine jays, pygmy owls, rough-legged owls and three-toed woodpeckers live in the high altitudes of the Harz, Black Forest, Bavarian Forest and Ore Mountains, and in some regions also the capercaillie. These species are often secretive and sensitive to disturbance, so it is particularly important to stay on the paths and respect quiet areas.

## Bird watching in the city

You don't have to travel far. Cities are also exciting observation areas:

- **Peregrine Falcons** breed on church towers, power plants and high-rise buildings in many large cities.
- **Goshawks** hunt in city parks, especially in Berlin, Hamburg and Cologne.
- **Nightingales** sing in parks and on railway embankments.
- **Swifts** hunt over the roofs in summer.
- **Waterfowl** winter on city rivers and ponds.

Old cemeteries and parks with large trees are particularly rich in species. You can find tips for identification in the guide [Identifying the most common garden birds] (/vogelwissen/haeufigste-gartenvoegel-erkennen/).

## When and where? An annual calendar

| time | Worthwhile goals |
|---|---|
| January–February | Lake Constance and large lakes for water birds, coasts for geese |
| March–April | Wet meadows with lapwings and black-tailed godwits, river floodplains during floods |
| May–June | Helgoland, moors and reed areas, beech forests, Alps |
| July–August | Moulting places at lakes, Wadden Sea with the first migratory birds |
| September–October | Wadden Sea, Heligoland, crane resting places |
| November–December | Geese on the coasts and Lower Rhine, waterfowl on lakes |

## Plan a birding weekend

If you're visiting a well-known bird area for the first time, you'll get a lot more out of it with a little planning:

1. **Choose season:** Check when the visit is most worthwhile. Crane resting places in October, Heligoland in May or October, Lake Constance in January.
2. **Gather Information:** Many protected areas have visitor centers with maps, viewing tips and up-to-date information. You can also see which species are currently being reported on reporting platforms.
3. **Start early:** The first hours of the day are the most productive almost everywhere. On the coast, the daily schedule also depends on the tides.
4. **Book a tour:** An excursion with local people on the first day will show you the best places and typical species.
5. **Accommodation nearby:** If you stay overnight close to the area, you save travel time and can experience the evening flight of cranes or geese.
6. **Take Notes:** Record what you saw and where. This way you can report your observations later and compare them on your next visit.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a permit to visit protected areas?** Not for the publicly accessible trails. However, some areas are closed all year round or at certain times. Pay attention to the signs on site.

**Are there guided tours?** Yes, many national parks, conservation centers and associations offer guided tours, often free or for a small fee. They are particularly recommended for beginners.

**Can I travel by public transport?** Many destinations such as Heligoland, the Rieselfelder Münster or city parks are easily accessible. For remote rest areas, it's worth taking a look at the region's bus and train connections or taking a bike.

**Where do I see the most species in a day?** On coasts and in large wetlands during migration season. Experienced observers can see well over a hundred species there on a good autumn day.

## Conclusion

Germany offers an enormous variety of bird watching locations from the Wadden Sea and Heligoland to the crane resting places in Brandenburg and Western Pomerania, the large lakes such as Lake Constance and Federsee, the floodplains on the Elbe and Oder to the Alps. The best times are the migration periods in spring and autumn and winter on the waters. Stay on the paths, keep your distance, use towers and huts and join a guided tour. And don't forget: the city park around the corner can also be full of surprises.
