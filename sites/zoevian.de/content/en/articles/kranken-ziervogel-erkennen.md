Birds are masters at hiding illness. In nature they are prey animals, and a bird that shows weakness will quickly become prey or be abandoned by the flock. Budgies, cockatiels and canaries have also maintained this strategy in the apartment. If a pet bird shows clear signs of illness, it is often seriously ill. This makes it all the more important to recognize early and subtle changes and to act quickly.

This guide will help you recognize warning signs, assess emergencies and take the right steps. It does not replace a visit to an avian veterinarian.

## What does a healthy bird look like?

Those who know their birds well notice changes more quickly. A healthy pet bird:

- is **alert and active**, reacts to noises and movements
- has **clear, shiny eyes** with no discharge
- has **clean nostrils** and a clean waxy skin
- wears **smooth, shiny plumage** that fits snugly
- **breathes calmly** and noiselessly, without tail movement
- **eats and drinks** regularly
- releases **formed feces**
- **grooms** regularly
- sleeps at night on **one leg**, with head in back feathers

## Warning signs in behavior

- **Fluffy during the day** and tired even though it's not cold
- **A lot of sleeping** during the day, often on both legs and with his head in his feathers
- **Sitting on the floor** or on the lowest bar
- **Less interest** in partner, toys and surroundings
- **No or little eating**, sitting at the food bowl for a long time without eating
- **Less singing** or calling for no apparent reason such as moulting
- **Sudden aggressiveness** or strong attachment

## Warning signs on the body

### Breathing

- **Tail bobbing**: If the tail goes up and down with every breath, the bird is experiencing respiratory distress.
- **Breathing with an open beak** in peace
- **Breathing noises** such as cracking, whistling or rattling
- **Sneezing** and discharge from the nostrils

### eyes and head

- sticky, swollen or watery eyes
- **Crusts on the beak, wax skin or the edges of the eyes**, often an indication of mites in budgies
- discolored wax skin
- Head tilt or balance problems

### Feces

A bird's droppings consist of three parts: the solid, dark droppings, the white uric acid portion and some clear urine. Changes are important clues:

| Change | Possible clue |
|---|---|
| very watery stool | Diarrhea, kidney problems, stress, lots of fresh food |
| undigested grains in the feces | Indigestion, infection |
| green or yellowish uric acid content | Liver or metabolic disorder |
| Blood in feces | Injury, infection, laying distress, poisoning |
| no poop | Constipation, laying distress, emergency |

Slight fluctuations are normal, for example after eating a lot of cucumbers or when you are excited. If changes persist for longer than a day or if other symptoms appear, you should take action.

### Weight

**Weight loss** is often the first measurable sign of illness, long before the bird becomes abnormal. That's why it makes sense to weigh pet birds regularly, for example once a week using a digital kitchen scale that measures to the nearest gram. A small perch on the scale makes this easier. Write down the values. Significant weight loss within a few days is a reason for a visit to the vet.

The nutritional status can also be assessed by palpation: If the **breastbone** feels pointed and sharp, the bird is emaciated.

### feathers and skin

- **Feather plucking** or bald spots outside of moulting
- **Discolored or deformed feathers**
- **Swelling** of the body, especially in budgies where tumors and fatty growths are common
- **Dirty plumage** on the abdomen

### Legs and feet

- Lameness, swollen joints
- scaly, crusty legs
- Claws that are too long, making it difficult to sit

## Emergencies: Go to the vet immediately

If you see these signs, you should **don't wait** and contact a veterinary practice or emergency veterinary service immediately:

- **Severe shortness of breath**, breathing with an open beak, violent tail wagging
- **Bird sits fluffed up on the ground** and hardly reacts
- **Bleeding**, even from a broken quill, that doesn't stop
- **Suspicion of laying distress:** A female sits fluffed up on the ground, presses, wags her tail, defecates little or no, and her belly is swollen.
- **Suspected poisoning**, for example after nibbling on a poisonous plant or coming into contact with fumes from overheated, non-stick pans
- **Accidents** such as hitting a window, being bitten by another pet, or being trapped
- **Cramps** or balance problems
- **No eating** for more than half a day

## First aid up to a visit to the vet

1. **Quiet:** Place the bird in a quiet, darkened room, away from noise and other animals.
2. **Warmth:** Sick birds cool down quickly. A heat lamp at a distance, a hot water bottle wrapped in a towel under part of the cage floor, or a warm room will help. However, the bird must be able to escape the heat at any time so that it does not overheat.
3. **Easily accessible food and water:** Place bowls on the floor if the bird can no longer get on the perches.
4. **Do not self-treat:** No human medications, no home remedies, and no attempts to remove eggs yourself.
5. **Prepare for transport:** A small transport box with a towel on the floor and a low perch, pre-warmed and covered for the winter.
6. **Collect information:** Note how long the symptoms have been present, what the bird has eaten and how the weight and droppings have changed. A fresh fecal specimen on a clean paper can help with the diagnosis.

## Common diseases at a glance

- **Mites:** In budgies, burrowing mites cause crusty changes on the beak, wax skin and legs. Red mites suck blood at night and hide in crevices during the day.
- **Respiratory diseases:** caused by bacteria, fungi or drafts, often with sneezing, discharge and wheezing.
- **Gastrointestinal diseases:** for example due to yeast or certain fungi such as megabacteriosis, which is known in budgies, which can lead to weight loss, vomiting and undigested grains in the feces.
- **Not laying** and other egg-laying disorders in females.
- **Tumors and fatty tumors**, especially in older and overweight budgies.
- **Feather diseases** such as the “French moult” that occurs in budgies, in which the flight and tail feathers fall out.
- **Parrot disease (psittacosis):** a bacterial infection that can also be transmitted to humans. If suspected, a veterinarian must make the diagnosis. The disease is reportable.

## Vomiting or courtship feeding?

Many male budgies regurgitate food to offer to their partner, a mirror, or their human. This is **courtship feeding** and normal. The bird nods its head rhythmically and appears to be in a good mood. Real **vomiting** looks different: the bird shakes its head, throws food and mucus around itself, the feathers on its head are stuck together, and it appears sick. Then a visit to the vet is necessary.

## The weekly health check

A short, firm check once a week helps to notice changes early. It only takes a few minutes:

1. **Weigh:** Weigh each bird on the kitchen scale and record the weight.
2. **Observing from a distance:** How is the bird sitting? How does he breathe? Is the tail bobbing?
3. **Eyes, nose, beak:** Are the eyes clear, the nostrils clean, the beak smooth and without crusts?
4. **Plumage:** Is it smooth? Are there bald spots, sticky feathers on the abdomen or an unusual number of lost feathers outside of the moult?
5. **Feet and Claws:** Does the bird sit evenly on both feet? Are the claws too long?
6. **Poop:** What does the poop look like on the floor paper? Has anything changed?
7. **Food and water:** Is eating and drinking as usual?

Anyone who checks these points regularly knows their birds well and can recognize deviations before they become an emergency.

## Special risks per type

- **Budgies** are prone to tumors, fatty tumors, thyroid problems and burrowing mites. Obesity is common. Pay attention to swelling on the abdomen and changes to the wax skin.
- **Cockatiels** often injure their wings and quills when panicking at night. Females tend to lay eggs frequently and have difficulty laying eggs. A night light and no nesting box prevent this.
- **Canaries** are susceptible to mites, including air sac mites, which manifest themselves through breathing noises and head shaking. Feather cysts may occur in canaries with heavily ruffled or very dense feathers.

## The right veterinary practice

Not every veterinary practice specializes in birds. Find a practice with experience in avian medicine **early**, before an emergency occurs. Nature conservation and bird associations as well as other bird keepers can provide recommendations. Also clarify which emergency services treat birds in your area.

## Precaution

- **Quarantine** for new birds before joining the others and an initial veterinary examination.
- **Regular weighing** and recording the weight.
- **Balanced diet** with lots of fresh food, see [Feed pet birds properly] (/vogelwissen/ziervoegel-ernaehrung/).
- **Daily free flight** and activities to combat obesity and stress.
- **Hygiene:** Clean bowls daily, change substrate and perching branches regularly.
- **Safe environment:** no poisonous plants, no overheating non-stick pans near the birds, no drafts.
- **Partners of your own kind:** Keeping animals alone is a stress factor that can make you sick. More about this in the guides [Keeping budgies appropriately](/vogelwissen/wellensittiche-artgerecht-halten/), [Keeping cockatiels](/vogelwissen/nymphensittiche-halten/) and [Keeping canaries](/vogelwissen/kanarienvogel-halten/).

## Frequently asked questions

**My bird puffs up in the evening. Is he sick?** Not necessarily. Before sleeping, birds puff up and pull up one leg. A fluffy, tired behavior during the day is worrying.

**If I have mild symptoms, can I just wait and see?** Waiting is risky with birds because their condition can deteriorate very quickly. If you are unsure, call the vet's office and describe the symptoms.

**Can diseases pass from pet birds to humans?** A few, such as parrot disease. Good hygiene, washing hands after cleaning and quarantining new birds reduce the risk. If you have a fever or respiratory problems after contact with sick birds, you should seek medical advice and point out the birds.

## Conclusion

Pet birds hide illnesses for a long time. Therefore, pay attention to subtle changes: fluffing up during the day, sleeping a lot, sitting on the floor, tail bobbing while breathing, changes in stool, weight loss and crusts on the beak or legs. Shortness of breath, bleeding, difficulty laying, suspected poisoning and a bird that hardly reacts are emergencies. Until you visit the vet, rest, warmth and accessible food will help. With an avian veterinary practice, regular weighing, quarantine for new arrivals, good food and species-appropriate husbandry, you can prevent many diseases or treat them early.
