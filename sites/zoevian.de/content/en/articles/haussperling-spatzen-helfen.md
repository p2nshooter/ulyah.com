The sparrow is so commonplace that you hardly notice it anymore. He chits in the hedge in front of the café, bathes in the dust next to the bike rack and grabs crumbs from the beer garden table. At the same time, it is a bird that conservationists have been worried about for years: In many major European cities, such as London and Hamburg, populations have declined sharply since the 1970s. In some neighborhoods where hundreds of sparrows used to live, you can hardly hear any anymore.

This guide introduces you to the house sparrow, explains why it has problems, and shows what you can do in your house and garden to give it more space again.

## Recognize the house sparrow

The house sparrow is around 14 to 16 centimeters long and weighs around 25 to 35 grams. It appears compact, with a strong, cone-shaped granivore beak.

- **Male:** gray upper head, chestnut brown stripe from eye to neck, gray cheeks, black throat flap. In spring the bib is large and jet black, and the beak turns black. In autumn and winter, the bib becomes smaller and more inconspicuous due to the light feather edges, and the beak becomes lighter.
- **Female:** grey-brown, back with dark and light brown stripes, a light stripe above the eyes, no black markings.
- **Juveniles:** similar to the female, often with yellowish beak corners.

It can be confused with the **field sparrow**. In this case, both sexes look the same: chocolate brown parting, white cheeks with a **black spot**, a smaller throat flap. The guide [Confused Pairs in Garden Birds](/vogelwissen/verwechslungspaare-gartenvoegel/) presents other difficult pairs.

## A life in the colony

Sparrows are extremely sociable birds. They breed in loose colonies, forage together, bathe together and sit in dense hedges or bushes during the day where they “chatter” loudly. These meeting points are important for the cohesion of the colony.

### True to location

House sparrows are resident birds. They stay near their breeding grounds all year round and rarely move further than one or two kilometers away. This also means that if a colony disappears, it takes a long time for sparrows to settle again because hardly any birds migrate from other areas.

### Breeding on buildings

The house sparrow is a cultural successor that has lived with humans for thousands of years. It prefers to breed in caves and niches in buildings: under roof tiles, in holes in walls, behind gutters, in roller shutter boxes or in the nests of house martins. The nest is a messy ball of stalks, paper, feathers and wool.

### Lots of broods

A pair of sparrows breeds two to three times, sometimes four times per year. A clutch usually consists of four to six eggs. Breeding takes place for almost two weeks, and after about two more weeks the young leave the nest.

### Dust and water bath

Sparrows groom their feathers by bathing in dry, loose soil. They shake dust into their feathers, which probably helps against feather parasites. Anyone who has a sunny, bare spot in the garden often observes small hollows in which several sparrows “bathe” at the same time.

## What sparrows eat

Adult sparrows feed primarily on seeds and grains, buds and anything left over by humans. But for their young they need **insects** in the first days of life: aphids, small caterpillars, beetles and spiders. Without enough insects, nestlings starve, even if there is an abundance of grain. This is one of the main reasons for the decline.

## Why sparrows are becoming rarer

There is not one reason, but several that work together.

### 1. Missing nesting sites

Modern new buildings have smooth facades without niches. When renovating older houses, roofs are sealed, facades are insulated and holes are sealed. Entire colonies lose their breeding grounds overnight. By the way, a building with sparrow nests cannot simply be closed during the breeding season: the breeding and resting places of wild birds are protected under the Federal Nature Conservation Act, and this must be taken into account when renovating.

### 2. Too few insects

Hardly any insects live in tidy gardens with short lawns, cherry laurel and gravel. The use of insecticides and weed killers also reduces the food supply for the young.

### 3. Lack of coverage

Sparrows need dense hedges and bushes near their breeding grounds to hide from sparrowhawks and cats and to meet as a group. Where hedges are replaced by fences and walls, these retreats are missing.

### 4. Less food in winter

Sparrows used to find lots of grains in chicken yards, horse stables and fields. Today, things are cleaner, stubble fields are plowed up quickly, and wild herbs with seeds have become rare.

### 5. diseases and other factors

Diseases such as bird malaria, air pollutants and noise are also discussed. What role these factors play in detail has not yet been conclusively clarified. What is certain is that sparrows thrive where there are nesting sites, insects and cover.

## Six measures that really help

### 1. Hang up the sparrow colony house

Because sparrows breed in colonies, a **colony house with three or more breeding chambers** is better than a single box. Each chamber has its own entry hole of around 32 to 35 millimeters. Hang it at least three meters high on a house wall, preferably under an eaves, facing east or southeast and protected from the blazing midday sun. To protect yourself from cats and martens, there should be no ledge underneath from which robbers can get to the box. More information in the guide [Hanging the nesting box correctly] (/vogelwissen/nistkasten-richtig-aufhaengen/).

In new buildings and renovations, special **nesting stones** can be installed directly into the facade or insulation. They are hardly noticeable and last for decades. When building projects in the house or in the neighborhood, ask whether such stones are planned. Many cities and nature conservation associations advise on this.

### 2. Plant dense hedges

A free-growing hedge made of native shrubs such as privet, hawthorn, hornbeam, dog rose or cornelian cherry provides cover, a meeting point and, in autumn, berries. It should be close to the breeding site, ideally no more than 20 to 50 meters away. You can find a selection of suitable plants in the guide [Native shrubs for birds] (/vogelwissen/heimische-straeucher-fuer-voegel/).

### 3. Encourage insects

Let a corner of the garden go wild, plant native wildflowers, avoid poison and mow the lawn less often. Even a strip of meadow flowers or a hedge with lots of aphids in spring helps the sparrows to breed. The guide [Creating a bird-friendly garden] (/vogelwissen/vogelfreundlicher-garten-anlegen/) describes what an insect-rich garden looks like.

### 4. Create a sand bath

A sunny spot with fine, dry sand or loose soil, about half a square meter in size, is often used by sparrows for dust bathing. Lay it out so that cats cannot approach without being seen, i.e. with a clear view all around and a bush a few meters away as a escape point.

### 5. A grain offering all year round

Sparrows benefit from a mixed grain diet, such as sunflower seeds, millet, oats and wheat. The guide [All-Season Feeding: Pros and Cons](/vogelwissen/ganzjahresfuetterung-pro-und-contra/) discusses whether and how you should feed in summer. In any case, it is important that the feeding area remains clean, because sparrows come in groups and diseases spread quickly in dirty feeding areas. Information on this can be found in the guide [Keep feeding area clean] (/vogelwissen/futterstelle-hygiene-krankheiten/).

### 6. Leave wild herbs standing

Seeds of dandelion, sow thistle, plantain, chickweed and grasses are natural food for sparrows. If you don't cut everything off in the fall, sparrows and finches will find food until winter.

## What you prefer to avoid

- **Bread and salty leftovers:** They are unhealthy for sparrows and attract rats. More about this in the guide [Mistakes when feeding birds] (/vogelwissen/fehler-beim-vogelfuettern/).
- **Remove nests during breeding season:** This is not only sad, but also not legally allowed.
- **Individual nesting boxes with a hole that is too small:** A 26 millimeter hole is intended for blue tits, a sparrow cannot fit through.

## Watch sparrows

Sparrows are ideal birds for beginner birdwatchers. They are loud, sociable and can be observed up close. Pay attention to the courtship behavior in spring: several males hop around a female with hanging wings and raised tails and chit excitedly. In summer you can see families where the young beg with flapping wings.

Register your sparrows at the large participation counts such as the “Hour of the Garden Birds” in May. This data shows in which regions stocks are stable and where there are problems. The guide [Bird Counts to Participate in] (/vogelwissen/vogelzaehlung-mitmachen/) explains how you can get involved.

## Sparrows and people: a long story

The house sparrow probably allied with the first farmers in the Middle East thousands of years ago and spread with them across Europe and Asia. People later brought it to North and South America, Australia and New Zealand, where it is also common today. Hardly any other bird is so closely tied to human settlements. This also explains why changes to our houses and gardens affect him so strongly.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long does it take to move into a colony house?** If sparrows already live nearby, often only a few weeks. If there is no colony within one or two kilometers, it can take years because sparrows rarely migrate.

**Do I need to clean a colony house?** Yes, once a year in late fall. Sparrows build up a lot of material, and old nests harbor parasites. The guide [cleaning the nest box] (/vogelwissen/nistkasten-reinigen/) explains how to do this.

**Do sparrows make dirt on the facade?** Some droppings collect under the entrance holes. A narrow board about half a meter below the box catches most of it without providing a ladder for cats.

## Conclusion

The house sparrow is a sociable housemate that needs three things above all: niches in buildings for breeding, insects for its young and dense hedges as a meeting place and hiding place. If you hang up a colony house, plant a native hedge, create a sand bath and let a piece of garden grow wild, you have a good chance that the Chilpen will stay or return to your street. And because sparrows remain loyal to their colony, many generations often benefit from your efforts.
