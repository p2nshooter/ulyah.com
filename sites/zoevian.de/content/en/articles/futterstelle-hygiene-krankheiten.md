A feeding station is a meeting place for birds. Dozens of tits, finches and sparrows come by every day, eating from the same place and drinking from the same bowl, leaving behind droppings and saliva. This is exactly what makes feeding stations a place where diseases can spread quickly. The good news: This risk can be greatly reduced with simple, regular hygiene.

In this guide you will find out which diseases occur at feeding places, how to recognize sick birds, what to do in an emergency and how to properly clean the feeder, silo and waterer.

## Why hygiene is so important

In nature, birds usually eat in a scattered manner. At a feeding station, however, many animals come together in a small space, often for weeks. Pathogens that are transmitted via feces, saliva or spoiled food find ideal conditions here. The following are particularly critical:

- **Feeders where the birds sit in the food** and contaminate it with droppings
- **Food that becomes moist** and begins to mold
- **Water bowls** that are rarely changed
- **large amounts of food on the floor** that stays there

## The most important diseases

### Trichomonadosis

Trichomonadosis is caused by a protozoan parasite, *Trichomonas gallinae*. It primarily affects **greenfinches**, but also chaffinches, pigeons and other species. The parasites settle in the throat and esophagus and form plaque there that makes swallowing difficult.

**Signs:**

- Birds sit fluffed up and appear tired
- Remains of food stick to the beak, the feathers around the beak are sticky or wet
- Birds try to eat, but regurgitate the food
- they are easy to approach and hardly escape

The pathogen is transmitted via saliva, for example when sick birds eat food, drop it again or choke into the waterer. Trichomonadosis has contributed to a severe decline in greenfinches in many regions of Europe. According to current knowledge, this pathogen is harmless to humans, dogs and cats.

### Salmonellosis

Salmonella are bacteria that can cause serious illnesses, especially in finches and sparrows, often occurring more frequently in winter. They are transmitted through feces.

**Signs:**

- Birds are apathetic and fluffy
- Diarrhea, dirty feathers on the abdomen
- Birds often die within a few days

Salmonella can also infect **people and pets**. Cats that eat sick birds can become ill. This is why gloves and hand washing are particularly important when cleaning.

### Bird pox

Avian pox is caused by a virus and appears as **wart-like growths** on the head, eyes, beak or feet. In Germany, **great tits** are particularly affected. Some birds recover, but for others the growths prevent them from seeing or eating. The virus is transmitted through direct contact, contaminated seating and stinging insects.

### Pneumonia in blue tits

In some springs, blue tits often become ill with pneumonia, which is caused by the bacterium *Suttonella ornithocola*. The birds appear bloated, breathe heavily and often die within a short time. More about this in the guide about [Great Tit and Blue Tit](/vogelwissen/kohlmeise-und-blaumeise/).

### fungal infections

Moldy food can cause **aspergillosis**, a fungal infection of the respiratory tract. **Aflatoxins**, mold toxins in spoiled peanuts, are also dangerous. Damp, clumpy or musty-smelling food should therefore be thrown in the trash immediately.

### Other diseases

- **Usutu virus:** is transmitted by mosquitoes and mainly affects blackbirds in late summer. More about this in the guide [The Blackbird in the Garden](/vogelwissen/amsel-im-garten/).
- **Avian influenza (bird flu):** mainly affects waterfowl and birds of prey, more rarely songbirds at feeding places. If you find several dead waterfowl or birds of prey, do not touch them and inform the responsible veterinary office.

## How do you recognize sick birds?

Healthy birds are alert, agile and will flee when you approach. Watch out for the following warning signs:

| Signs | Possible reference to |
|---|---|
| fluffed up in mild weather | general illness |
| Remains of food on the beak, choking | Trichomonadosis |
| Diarrhea, dirty abdomen | Salmonellosis |
| Growths on the head or feet | Bird pox |
| heavy, visible breathing | Respiratory infection |
| Bird allows itself to be touched, does not flee | severe weakness |
| several dead birds in a short time | Outbreak of infection |

A single fluffy bird on a frosty morning is usually nothing to worry about, as birds fluff themselves up in the cold to stay warm. However, if a bird appears apathetic even in mild weather or you see several conspicuous birds, you should take action.

## What to do if you see sick birds?

1. **Stop feeding immediately.** Remove all feeding stations and water bowls. This will prevent the birds from continuing to meet in a small space and become infected.
2. **Clean everything thoroughly.** Feeders, silos, holders, waterers and also the floor underneath.
3. **Take a break for at least two to four weeks.** Only when there are no more sick birds to be seen can you slowly start again, ideally with silos instead of open feeders.
4. **Dispose of dead birds safely.** Only handle them with disposable gloves or a plastic bag placed over them. Pack them in a bag and dispose of them in the trash unless an examination is desired.
5. **Report observation.** Conservation groups collect reports of sick and dead birds to detect outbreaks. If bird flu is suspected, such as several dead water birds, the veterinary office is responsible.
6. **You can hand over individual weak birds** to a wild bird station. Call ahead.

## This is how you clean properly

### tool

- Disposable gloves or special cleaning gloves
- a brush used only for feeding areas
- hot water
- If necessary, a mild disinfectant suitable for animal areas
- a bucket not used for food

### Process

1. **Remove leftover food and droppings.** Scrape off any coarse dirt and dispose of old leftover food in the trash, not in the compost.
2. **Scrub with hot water.** In most cases this is enough, especially with regular cleaning.
3. **Disinfect in case of illness.** Use a suitable disinfectant exactly according to the instructions and allow it to take effect for the specified time.
4. **Rinse thoroughly.** Residues of cleaning agents or disinfectants must not remain in the feeder.
5. **Allow to dry completely.** Only then add new food.
6. **Wash your hands.** Even if you wore gloves.

### How often?

| feeding station | Cleaning |
|---|---|
| open feed house | Remove feces and residues daily, thoroughly weekly |
| Feed silo | Thoroughly every one to two weeks, immediately if wet |
| Dumpling and fat block holder | with every refill |
| Feeding table, floor bowl | daily |
| Birdbath | Change the water daily and scrub the bowl several times a week |

## Prevention is better than cleaning

### Feed silos instead of open feed houses

In silos the feed stays dry and is not defecated. They are the best choice from a hygiene perspective. If you like a classic feeder, only put in the amount that will be eaten in one day.

### Several small feeding stations

Distribute the food in several places a few meters apart. This means fewer birds meet at one point and diseases spread more slowly.

### Change place

Occasionally move feeding stations a few feet. This means that less feces and leftover food collect underneath them.

### Good food

Buy high-quality, fresh food and store it in a dry place. Never use damp or musty food. More about this in the guide [Bird food: which type for which bird] (/vogelwissen/vogelfutter-sorten-welcher-vogel/).

### Keep floor clean

Sweep or rake the area under the feeder regularly. Fallen shells and leftover food can become moldy and attract rats.

### Be particularly careful in summer

Heat promotes the growth of bacteria and parasites. Anyone who feeds in summer should observe the hygiene rules even more strictly. The weighing up is described in the guide [All-season feeding: pros and cons] (/vogelwissen/ganzjahresfuetterung-pro-und-contra/).

## A simple weekly routine

If you feed regularly, a fixed routine works best. This means that hygiene does not remain an annoying exception, but rather becomes a habit:

- **Daily:** Change the water in the trough, remove droppings and leftover food from open feeders, and briefly pay attention to any conspicuous birds.
- **Twice a week:** Brush out drinking troughs with hot water, sweep the floor under the feeding areas.
- **Once a week:** Empty the feed silos and holders, clean them with hot water and let them dry.
- **Once a month:** Check food supply, wipe out containers, move feeding places a few meters if necessary.

A small note on the inside of the cupboard where the food is stored reminds you of the dates.

## And your own health?

Some pathogens such as salmonella can also make people sick. You can be on the safe side with simple rules:

- **Gloves** when cleaning and handling dead birds
- **Wash hands** after every contact with feeding places
- **Do not clean feeders in the kitchen**, but outside or in a separate tank
- Accompany **children** when filling and have their hands washed afterwards
- If you have a **weakened immune system** or are pregnant, it is better to leave the cleaning to someone else and seek medical advice if you have any questions

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I treat birds with medication in their feed?** No. Drugs in feed also reach healthy birds and other animals; the dosage cannot be controlled and resistance can arise. Sick individual birds belong in professional hands.

**Are wooden feeders harder to clean?** Yes, wood absorbs moisture and dirt. Plastic, metal or glazed material is easier to keep clean. You should let wooden houses dry particularly thoroughly.

**Do I have to clean in winter when it freezes?** Yes. Many pathogens survive cold well. Then clean in a separate basin on frost-free days or indoors.

**Is a bird with a bald head sick?** Not necessarily. In late summer, some blackbirds and titmice temporarily lose their head feathers during moulting. It grows back. However, if the bird also appears apathetic, there may be an illness behind it.

## Conclusion

Feeding areas are valuable, but only if they are clean. Use food silos, distribute the food in several small areas, remove leftovers daily, clean regularly with hot water and change the water in the trough every day. If you notice sick birds, stop feeding them immediately for a few weeks and clean everything thoroughly. This will make your feeder a place of help for the birds rather than a place where they get sick.
