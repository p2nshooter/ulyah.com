For many people, feeding birds is the first step into bird watching. As soon as there is a suet ball hanging on the tree, the first guests arrive, and suddenly the view from the kitchen window becomes a little nature experience. There are a few basic rules to ensure that feeding really helps the birds and doesn't harm them. The wrong food, dirty feeders or an unfavorable place can cause more harm than good.

This guide explains the basics: when feeding makes sense, which food which birds like, which feeding place is suitable, where it should be and how to keep it clean.

## Why feed at all?

Bird feeding is above all a help for individual birds in difficult times and a wonderful opportunity to experience birds up close. But it is **not a replacement for a good living space**. Most of the bird species that have problems here, such as skylarks, lapwings and partridges, do not come to feeding places at all. The only thing that helps them is protecting their habitats.

Species that are common anyway come to feeders: tits, finches, sparrows, blackbirds, robins, nuthatches and woodpeckers. For them, food can make all the difference in harsh winters, and for us it is a window into their lives. That's a good reason. If you really want to do something for the bird world, combine feeding with a natural garden. The guide [Creating a bird-friendly garden] (/vogelwissen/vogelfreundlicher-garten-anlegen/) describes what this looks like.

## When to feed?

### The classic winter feeding

Most conservation organizations recommend feeding primarily **from November to the end of February**, especially during frost and snow. During this time, insects are rare, the days are short and the nights are long. Small birds then have to absorb a lot of energy to avoid freezing to death.

### Feed all year round?

Whether you should also feed in spring and summer is debated among experts. Some ornithologists believe that year-round feeding makes sense because the natural food supply in many gardens and agricultural landscapes has become scarce. Others warn of risks such as the spread of diseases at feeding stations and that young birds are given the wrong food. We present both positions in detail in the guide [All-season feeding: pros and cons] (/vogelwissen/ganzjahresfuetterung-pro-und-contra/).

### Regularly instead of sporadically

Once you start feeding, stick with it, at least during a period of frost. Birds get used to a reliable food source and plan their day around it. An empty feeding station on an icy morning costs them valuable energy.

## Which food for which bird?

Birds can be roughly divided into two groups:

### grain eater

They have a **strong, cone-shaped beak** and crack seeds and kernels. These include finches such as chaffinches, greenfinches, goldfinches and bullfinches, sparrows and, in winter, titmice.

Suitable are:

- **Sunflower seeds**, peeled or unpeeled. They are the basic food that almost all grain eaters like. Peeled kernels leave less waste.
- **Hemp seeds, millet, oatmeal** and other small seeds, often included in blends.
- **Peanuts**, unsalted and unroasted, chopped if possible or in a close-meshed dispenser so that small birds don't choke on whole nuts.

### Soft food eaters

They have a **thinner, pointed beak** and eat mostly insects in summer. In winter they switch to berries, fruits and soft food. These include robins, blackbirds, thrushes, dunnocks and wrens.

Suitable are:

- **Oat flakes** soaked in vegetable oil or mixed with fat
- **Raisins**, briefly soaked
- **Fruit** such as apples and pears, halved or in pieces
- **Bran** and special **soft food mixtures** from specialist retailers
- **dried insects** or mealworms

### Fatty food

Fatty food is particularly valuable for tits, woodpeckers and nuthatches: a mixture of beef tallow or coconut fat with grains, oat flakes and nuts. How to make it yourself is described in the instructions [Make your own fat balls] (/vogelwissen/fettfutter-meisenknoedel-selber-machen/). The guide [Bird food: which type for which bird] (/vogelwissen/vogelfutter-sorten-welcher-vogel/) provides an overview of all types of food.

### What doesn't belong in the feeder

- **Bread and baked goods:** swell in the stomach, go moldy quickly and have little nutritional value.
- **Salted or Seasoned Leftovers:** Salt is harmful to birds.
- **Bacon, sausage, cheese:** too salty and fatty.
- **Milk:** Birds cannot tolerate lactose.
- **Cheap feed mixes with a lot of wheat and fillers** as well as mixes that may contain seeds of **ragweed**, a plant whose pollen causes severe allergies. When purchasing, look for the words “ambrosia-free” or similar information.

## Which feeding place is the right one?

| feeding station | Advantages | Disadvantages | For whom? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Feed silo** (column with exits) | Food stays dry and clean and birds don't defecate in it | needs to be refilled regularly | Tits, finches, sparrows |
| **Classic feeder** | traditional, beautiful to look at | Lining gets dirty easily and needs to be cleaned daily | many species |
| **Feeding table / shallow bowl** | good for bottom feeders | Hygiene particularly important, danger of cats | Blackbird, robin, dunnock |
| **Dumpling and fat food holder** | simple, little waste | only for fatty food lovers | Tits, woodpeckers, nuthatches |
| **Peanut Dispenser** (wire grid) | Nuts can only be picked out in small pieces | just peanuts | Tits, woodpeckers, nuthatches |

From a hygienic point of view, **feeding silos** are the most recommended because the birds do not sit in the food and do not contaminate it. If you use a classic feeder, only put in as much food daily as the birds eat in a day and remove leftovers and droppings regularly.

## The right place

A good feeding place meets several conditions:

1. **Clear view:** The birds must be able to see approaching cats and birds of prey in good time. Do not place the feeder directly in dense bushes where a cat can lurk.
2. **Cover nearby:** At the same time, there should be a bush or tree about two to three meters away into which the birds can escape in case of danger.
3. **Cat-safe:** Hang feeders at least 1.5 meters high on a post or branch that no cat can jump onto. A cuff on the post keeps climbers away. More about this in the guide [Cats and birds in the garden](/vogelwissen/katzen-und-voegel-im-garten/).
4. **Distance from windows:** Either very close to the window, less than one meter, or more than five meters away. In between, birds gain enough speed when flying up to injure themselves on the window. How to make glass surfaces safer can be found in the guide [Prevent bird strikes on glass panes] (/vogelwissen/vogelschlag-glasscheiben-verhindern/).
5. **Weather protected:** Lining should not get wet. A roof or a silo helps.
6. **Several small spots instead of one big one:** This way the birds spread out, fights become less frequent and diseases spread less.

## Hygiene: the be-all and end-all

Many birds meet in a small space at feeding stations. Pathogens such as salmonella or trichomonas spread easily here, especially through feces and contaminated food. Therefore:

- **Clean droppings and leftover food from feeders every day**, empty and clean feed silos regularly.
- **Hot water** is sufficient in most cases. After cases of illness, a mild disinfectant can be useful, which is then rinsed off thoroughly.
- **Wear gloves** and wash hands thoroughly afterwards.
- **Take Sick Birds Seriously:** If you see fluffy, listless birds, stop feeding them for a few weeks and clean everything thoroughly.

You can find more detailed information in the guide [Keeping the feeding area clean] (/vogelwissen/futterstelle-hygiene-krankheiten/).

## Don't forget water

Birds not only need food, but also water for drinking and bathing, in summer and winter. On frosty days, open water is often harder to find than food. A shallow bowl of clean water, changed daily, is a simple and effective aid. The guide [bird bath and bird bath] (/vogelwissen/vogeltraenke-vogelbad/) describes how to set up a waterer and keep it free of ice.

## How much food?

Only give as much food as the birds will eat in one to two days. If there is still a lot left over the next evening, reduce the amount. Old, damp food spoils and can become moldy. The requirement is higher in frost and snow and significantly lower on mild days.

## What you can observe at the feeding station

The feeding station is a small stage. You will observe how a hierarchy is formed: great tits drive away blue tits, nuthatches drive both away, and the great spotted woodpecker has the right of way anyway. You see greenfinches sitting for hours cracking kernels, while titmice quickly grab a kernel and disappear with it. And you get to know species that you would otherwise never have seen so close. ## Frequently asked questions

**Do birds become addicted through feeding?** No. Research shows that birds only consume a portion of their food at feeders and continue to use natural sources. If a feeding place is no longer available, they look for other places. You shouldn't stop suddenly, especially in severe frost.

**Am I attracting rats?** This can happen if a lot of food falls on the floor and is left lying around. Feed silos, peeled kernels and a small collecting tray under the dispenser help. Do not refeed large quantities in the evening.

**Can I feed on the balcony?** Yes, with a small feeding silo or dumpling holder. Check with the household beforehand and make sure that no food or feces fall onto the balcony below.

**Are suet balls in the net bad?** The nets are a danger because birds can get their feet caught in them. Buy dumplings without a net and hang them in a metal holder.

## Checklist for your feeding station

- Feed silo or feed house with roof
- Space with a clear view and cover at two to three meters
- at least 1.5 meters high, cat-safe
- Distance to windows under one or over five meters
- Sunflower seeds as a basis, plus fatty food and soft food
- no bread, no leftovers, no salt
- daily checks and regular cleaning
- shallow water bowl, fresh daily

## Conclusion

Feeding birds properly is not difficult. Choose high-quality food that suits the birds you want to attract: sunflower seeds and nuts for granivores, oatmeal, fruit and soft food for blackbirds and robins, fatty food for tits and woodpeckers. Place the feeding station safely away from cats and windows, keep it clean and don't forget the water. Then you help the birds through the winter and experience them up close.
