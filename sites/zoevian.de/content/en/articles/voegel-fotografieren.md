A sharp photo of a blue tit with bright plumage and a point of light in its eye, a kingfisher on its perch, a flock of cranes in the evening light: bird photography combines nature observation with craftsmanship and patience. It is demanding because birds are small, fast and shy. At the same time, she has a special responsibility because no photo is worth putting a bird under stress or endangering a brood.

This guide explains what equipment you can start with, which camera settings have proven to work, how to take good pictures and what rules apply to considerate bird photography.

## First the rules

Before it comes to technology, the most important thing: the well-being of the birds always comes before the picture.

1. **No nest photography.** Nests, breeding birds and nestlings will not be photographed if this involves removing branches, uncovering hiding places or disturbing the birds. This can lead to the loss of the brood and is prohibited in protected species.
2. **Keep your distance.** If a bird stops feeding, warns, ducks or takes flight, you are too close. Go back.
3. **No sound dummies**, especially not during breeding season to attract birds.
4. **No drones** near bird colonies, nests and resting flocks.
5. **Observe trails and protected area rules.**
6. **No bait feeding of sensitive species**, such as live mice for owls.
7. **Do not disclose sensitive locations.** Do not share exact locations of owls, breeding birds of prey, or rare species on social media.

Many nature photography associations have incorporated such principles into their code of ethics.

## The equipment

### Smartphone

You can only take bird photos with your smartphone if the birds are very close, for example at the feeding place directly in front of the window. An exciting alternative is **Digiscoping**: You hold the smartphone to the eyepiece of a binocular or spotting scope or attach it with an adapter. This at least creates good receipt photos that help with identification.

### Bridge camera

Bridge cameras have a built-in zoom lens with a very large focal length range. They are comparatively cheap, light and well suited for entry-level use. In weak light they reach their limits because of the small sensor.

### System camera or SLR camera with telephoto lens

For sophisticated bird photography, a camera with interchangeable lenses and a **telephoto lens** is the standard. For small birds you usually need a focal length of **400 to 600 millimeters** (based on 35mm). Modern cameras with fast autofocus and **animal or eye detection** make focusing much easier. Zoom lenses in the range of around 150 to 600 millimeters are a good compromise between price, weight and flexibility.

### Accessories

- **Tripod or monopod** for long waiting times
- **Bean bag** to place the lens on a windowsill, wall or car door
- **camouflage tent or camouflage net**
- **Spare batteries** and memory cards
- **Rain protection** for camera and lens

## Camera settings

### Exposure time

Birds move quickly. In order for the image to be sharp, you need short exposure times:

| situation | Exposure time approximately |
|---|---|
| sitting, calm bird | 1/500 s or shorter |
| active little bird | 1/1000s to 1/2000s |
| Bird in flight | 1/2000 s or shorter |
| deliberate motion blur | 1/30 s to 1/125 s with pull |

A rule of thumb without image stabilization: The exposure time should be at least as short as 1 divided by the focal length, i.e. 1/500 of a second at 500 millimeters.

### Aperture

An **open aperture** like f/5.6 or f/6.3 lets in a lot of light and separates the bird from its surroundings with a blurred background. For multiple birds or if more depth of field is needed, stop down a little, say to f/8.

### ISO

With short exposure times and little light, the ISO sensitivity must increase. Modern cameras still deliver good results even at ISO 1600 to 3200. An **automatic ISO** with a fixed exposure time and aperture is often the most practical solution for birds.

### Autofocus

- **Continuous Autofocus** (AF-C) to allow the camera to follow a moving bird.
- **Small focus field** or **Eye detection** so the camera doesn't focus on branches in the foreground.
- **Burst mode** so you can choose the best one from multiple images.

## Image design

### At eye level

Photos taken at the bird's eye level appear much more intense than shots from above. For waterfowl and ground birds, this often means kneeling or lying down.

### The eye

The eye must be sharp. A small **light reflex in the eye** makes the bird appear alive.

### Light

Soft light in the morning and late afternoon allows colors to shine and avoids harsh shadows. Stand so that the sun is behind you or to the side. **Backlighting** can produce very atmospheric images, for example when the feathers shine from behind, but it is more difficult.

### Background

A calm, even background allows the bird to stand out. The further away the background is, the blurrier it becomes. Watch out for annoying branches, fences or garbage cans.

### Leave space

Leave some space in the image in the direction the bird is facing. A bird looking out of the picture appears constrained.

### show behavior

The most exciting pictures show not just a bird, but a story: a sparrow having a dust bath, a robin with a worm, tits arguing over dumplings, swallows collecting clay.

## Where and how to get good pictures

### At the feeding place

The feeding area in your own garden is an ideal training area. A tip for natural-looking photos: Place a **nice perching branch** in a stand next to the feeding area. The birds often land there first before flying to the feeder, and you get pictures without a feeder in the frame. The guide [Feeding birds correctly] (/vogelwissen/voegel-fuettern-grundlagen/) describes how to design the feeding area safely.

### At the trough

Birds at a watering trough or bathing are wonderful motifs. A shallow bowl with a stone in the middle provides a natural stage. More about this in the guide [bird bath and bird bath](/vogelwissen/vogeltraenke-vogelbad/).

### Out of hiding

- **The Window** is an excellent hiding place. Open it so you don't take photos through the window.
- **The car** appears less threatening to many birds than a human. You can take photos from your car on dirt roads and bodies of water, of course only where parking is permitted.
- **Camouflage tents** are useful if you stay in one place for a long time. Set them up at a distance and give the birds time to get used to it.
- **Observation huts** in protected areas often allow close shots without disturbing.

### On the way

Walk slowly, stop often and do not approach birds directly, but in an arc. If a bird becomes restless, stop or step back.

## Flight shots

Photographing birds in flight is the supreme discipline. A few tips will help:

- **Big, slow birds first:** Seagulls, great blue herons, storks and birds of prey are easier to track than swallows.
- **Use predictable situations:** Seagulls on a harbor pier, cranes roosting at dusk, geese flying from a lake into the fields in the morning.
- **Practice moving along:** Capture the bird early in the viewfinder, move the camera evenly and trigger it.
- **Large focus field:** When shooting outdoors, a larger focus field or automatic field selection can work better than a single point.
- **Correct exposure:** The bird often becomes too dark against a bright sky. An exposure compensation of plus one f-stop helps.

## Seasons for bird photographers

- **Winter:** Feeders and waterfowl, birds in the snow, hoar frost on branches. The light is flat and soft all day long.
- **Spring:** Magnificent plumage, singing males in song stations, courtship displays of the great crested grebes. Pay particular attention to distance when breeding.
- **Summer:** Branches and families, swallows collecting clay, swifts in the evening sky.
- **Autumn:** Migratory birds, cranes and geese in large flocks, thrushes in berry bushes, warm colors in the background.

## A series of exercises to get you started

1. **Week 1:** Photograph tits sitting at the feeding place in front of the window. Goal: a sharp eye at 1/1000th of a second.
2. **Week 2:** Place a natural perch branch next to the feeder and photograph birds on it, with a calm background.
3. **Week 3:** Photograph ducks at eye level at the city pond by lying flat on the ground or kneeling.
4. **Week 4:** Practice seagulls or pigeons in flight and train to follow them.

After each round, look at the pictures on the computer and note what worked and what didn't. This is the fastest way to learn.

## Image editing

Careful editing of brightness, contrast, colors and image detail is part of digital photography. When it comes to nature photos, however, the following applies: the picture should show reality. Anyone adding or removing items should indicate this, especially in competitions.

## Photos for science

Blurry photos can also be valuable, for example to document a rare species, read a ring or document a clinical picture. Many reporting platforms allow you to upload photos of observations. More about this in the guide [Participatory bird counts] (/vogelwissen/vogelzaehlung-mitmachen/).

## Frequently asked questions

**What focal length do I need for garden birds?** For small birds a few meters away, a 400 to 600 millimeter 35mm focal length is ideal. At the feeder in front of the window, sometimes 200 to 300 millimeters is enough.

**Can I film birds in the nesting box?** With a nesting box camera installed before breeding season, yes, without disturbing the birds. The guide [Setting up a nesting box camera] (/vogelwissen/nistkastenkamera-einrichten/) explains how this works.

**Why are my photos blurry?** Common causes include exposure times that are too long, an incorrect focus point, camera shake or heat haze over warm ground. Shorter times, a small field of focus and a stable support help.

## Conclusion

Good bird photos come from patience, consideration and a little technique. With a camera and a telephoto lens between 400 and 600 millimeters, short exposure times, an open aperture, automatic ISO and continuous autofocus, you are well equipped. Shoot at eye level, focus on the eye, soft light and a calm background. Use a window, car or camouflage tent as a hiding place and stick to the rules: no nest photography, no luring with sound dummies, enough distance. Then your pictures will not only be beautiful, but also honest.
