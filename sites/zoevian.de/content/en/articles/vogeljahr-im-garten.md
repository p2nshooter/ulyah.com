A garden is not a fixed place for birds, but a calendar. In January, titmice, sparrows and winter visitors from the north share the feeding area. In April the first song of blackcaps can be heard from the hedge, in May the swifts squawk above the roofs, and in September the swallows gather on the pipes. If you know when something is happening, you will observe more carefully and help the birds exactly when they need it.

This guide will take you month by month through the bird year in an average German garden. The times are guidelines. Depending on the region, altitude and weather, many things are postponed by one to three weeks.

## January: The quiet time with a loud start

January is feeding station month. The days are short, and small birds must eat enough in a few hours to survive a long night of frost. A great tit loses a noticeable amount of its body weight on a cold night.

- **What you see:** Tits, sparrows, blackbirds, robins, nuthatches, great spotted woodpeckers, finches. In some years, winter visitors such as bramblings, siskins and waxwings appear.
- **What you hear:** On mild days the great tit starts with its “zi-zi-bä”. The robin sings its winter song.
- **What you can do:** Keep feeding places clean, offer ice-free water. Counting at the “Hour of the Winter Birds”, which takes place at the beginning of January. How to do this can be found in the guide [Bird Counts to Participate in](/vogelwissen/vogelzaehlung-mitmachen/).

## February: The first returnees

The days are noticeably longer and that triggers spring fever in many birds.

- **What you see:** First starlings return in mild winters. Over the land, the first cranes move northeast, often trumpeting loudly. Skylarks soar above fields singing.
- **What you hear:** Blackbirds and song thrushes sing the first verses, usually at dusk. Great spotted woodpeckers drum on hollow branches.
- **What you can do:** Last good time to hang **new nesting boxes**. Tits are already exploring possible breeding sites. You can find instructions in the guides [Building a nesting box](/vogelwissen/nistkasten-bauen-anleitung/) and [Hanging a nesting box correctly](/vogelwissen/nistkasten-richtig-aufhaengen/). If you need to cut back trees heavily, the end of February is the last opportunity.

## March: Nest building and cutting ban

March is the start of the breeding season.

- **What you see:** Blackbirds carry stalks, sparrows fight over niches. Chiffchaffs and black redstarts are returning from the south, as are the first white storks.
- **What you hear:** The chiffchaff calls its name tirelessly. In the morning the bird concert begins before sunrise.
- **What you can do:** From **1. March**, heavy pruning of hedges, bushes and other trees is prohibited until September 30th according to the Federal Nature Conservation Act. Gentle shaping and care cuts are still permitted, but no nest may be disturbed. Lay out a small box with **nesting material**: animal hair, undyed sheep wool, moss. No synthetic fibers that birds can get tangled in.

## April: The singers are coming

In April, many long-distance migrants return and the hedgerows fill with song.

- **What you see:** Blackcap, Fitis, Barn Swallow, House Martin, Redstart, and from mid-April the cuckoo. Tits lay their eggs.
- **What you hear:** The blackcap with its loud, fluting final part, the cuckoo, in the Fitis forest with its descending tones.
- **What you can do:** Leave nesting boxes alone, even if curiosity is high. A breeding female that is disturbed may abandon the clutch. Keep a special eye on cats. Information about this can be found in the guide [Cats and birds in the garden] (/vogelwissen/katzen-und-voegel-im-garten/).

## May: busy

May is the month with the most bird species and activity.

- **What you see:** At the beginning of May the **Swifts** come back, followed shortly afterwards by the Oriole, Red-backed Shrike and Marsh Warbler. The first branches of blackbirds and titmice are sitting in the bushes.
- **What you hear:** The morning concert is the busiest. If you open your window at five in the morning in May, you can sometimes hear ten or more species at the same time.
- **What you can do:** Take part in the “Hour of the Garden Birds” on the second weekend in May. Leave young birds alone on the ground, see [Young bird found: what to do?](/vogelwissen/jungvogel-gefunden-was-tun/). Leave parts of the lawn unmowed so that insects and food for the young birds grow back.

## June: Second broods and lots of hunger

Many couples have raised their first brood and are starting the second.

- **What you see:** Family groups of tits and sparrows, begging young birds, starlings migrating in flocks across meadows.
- **What you hear:** The singing slowly fades away. The boys' begging calls are particularly loud now.
- **What you can do:** If it is hot, provide **fresh water** and change it daily. How to keep a waterer hygienic can be found in the guide [Birdbath and Birdbath](/vogelwissen/vogeltraenke-vogelbad/).

## July: moult and retreat

In midsummer it becomes noticeably quiet in the garden.

- **What you see:** Many birds molt, which means they change their plumage. They appear disheveled, fly more poorly and retreat into the thicket. Blackbirds with almost bald heads are now not uncommon.
- **What you hear:** Hardly any singing. At the end of July and beginning of August the **Swifts** say goodbye to Africa, often almost unnoticed.
- **What you can do:** Water, water, water. And peace of mind: don't cut hedges too much, even if they're growing lushly.

## August: The train begins

For many birds, their journey south begins in August.

- **What you see:** White storks gather and leave. Cuckoos, orioles and garden warblers quietly disappear. Swallows practice in groups over meadows and ponds.
- **What you hear:** From the end of August, robins start singing again, now the autumn song to define their territory.
- **What you can do:** Leave seed heads of sunflowers, thistles and teasels standing. Goldfinches and greenfinches are happy about it.

## September: Farewell of the swallows

September is the big migration month.

- **What you see:** Barn and House Martins gather on pipes and leave. Chiffchaffs and blackcaps feast on elderberry reserves. Birds of prey fly over the land.
- **What you hear:** At night, especially on clear nights, the calls of migrating birds can be heard.
- **What you can do:** Leave berries hanging on bushes. From **1. In October**, more severe pruning is permitted again. The guide [Native Shrubs for Birds](/vogelwissen/heimische-straeucher-fuer-voegel/) tells you which bushes have a particularly large number of berries.

## October: cranes and winter guests

October is one of the most exciting months for observers.

- **What you see:** Cranes fly in large wedge formations over Germany, geese from Scandinavia and Russia arrive. The first winter guests such as bramblings and redwings appear in the gardens. Jays hide acorns for the winter.
- **What you hear:** The trumpeting calls of cranes high in the sky.
- **What you can do:** **Clean nest boxes** as soon as there are no more brood in them. More about this in the guide [Cleaning the nest box] (/vogelwissen/nistkasten-reinigen/). Leave leaves under bushes and create a pile of brushwood. On the first weekend in October, “EuroBirdwatch”, a joint bird migration observation campaign, takes place in many European countries. The guide [Bird watching in Germany: the best places] (/vogelwissen/vogelbeobachtung-orte-deutschland/) reveals where the migration is particularly good.

## November: The feeding station opens

Many people start winter feeding in November at the latest.

- **What you see:** Tits, finches and sparrows in mixed groups. Robins and blackbirds defend feeding areas.
- **What you hear:** Robins sing, tits grumble, sparrows chatter in the hedges.
- **What you can do:** Set up a feeding station and pay attention to hygiene right from the start. The guides [Feeding birds correctly](/vogelwissen/voegel-fuettern-grundlagen/) and [Mistakes when feeding birds](/vogelwissen/fehler-beim-vogelfuettern/) explain which types of food make sense and which mistakes you should avoid. Autumn is also a good time to plant hedges and shrubs.

## December: surviving the winter

In December it's all about perseverance for the birds.

- **What you see:** Birds puffing themselves up to retain warmth. On cold nights, some titmice and wrens sleep in nest boxes, occasionally even several together.
- **What you hear:** On mild days the robin sings, and from the end of December sometimes the great tit sings.
- **What you can do:** Feed regularly, keep water ice-free, clean feeders. And maybe build a nesting box to hang in February.

## Keep a bird diary

If you want to consciously follow the bird year, it is best to keep a simple diary. For example, write down when you saw the first swift, heard the first blackcap or observed the last swallow. After a few years you will see whether arrival times are shifting. Many observers note that some migratory birds are returning earlier today than they did a few decades ago because springs have become milder. Your notes are not only a nice memory, but also a small piece of nature observation that you can share with researchers via reporting platforms.

## Conclusion

The bird year follows a reliable rhythm: winter feeding and first songs in January, nest building in March, return of the singers in April, peak activity in May, moulting in midsummer, migration in autumn and perseverance in December. Anyone who knows this calendar knows when to hang nesting boxes, when to protect hedges and when to clean the feeding area. This makes the garden a reliable home for birds all year round.
