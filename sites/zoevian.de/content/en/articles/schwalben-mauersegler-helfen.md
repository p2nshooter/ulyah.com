Swallows and swifts are as much a part of summer as long evenings and the buzzing of bees. They hunt insects in rapid flight over roofs, meadows and ponds, and their calls accompany us from May to August. But many people can hardly tell the three most common types apart, and even fewer know that all three require help in many regions. Because they breed on and in our buildings, and that is exactly where their breeding grounds disappear.

This guide will show you how to distinguish between barn swallows, house martins and swifts, why they have a hard time and how you can help them with nesting aids, clay and a little tolerance.

## Three species, two families

Barn Swallow and House Martin are swallows. The swift looks similar to them, but belongs to a completely different family of birds, the swifts. Its closest relatives, as surprising as that sounds, are hummingbirds. The similarity comes from the fact that all three species hunt insects in flight and have developed similar body shapes to do so.

## This is how you distinguish the three

| feature | Barn Swallow | House Martin | Swifts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top | shiny blue-black | blue-black with **bright white rump** | very dark, sooty brown |
| Bottom | creamy white, **red-brown throat and forehead** | pure white | dark, only light throat patch |
| tail | **long tail skewers** | short, slightly forked | short, forked |
| Wings | pointed, rather narrow | shorter, wider | **long, crescent-shaped wings** |
| size | about 17-19 cm with skewers | about 13cm | about 17 cm, wingspan around 40 cm |
| nest | open clay bowl **inside** in stables, barns, garages | closed clay nest **outside** under eaves | **Void spaces** under roofs, in cracks in walls |
| Reputation | chirping, chatting | dry “prrt” | shrill **“srieh”**, often in chorus |
| time with us | around April to September | around April to September | around the beginning of May to the beginning of August |

Notes: **Long spears and red throat** are barn swallows. **White Po** is House Martin. **Very dark and sickle-winged**, with shrill calls in groups, is the common swift.

## The barn swallow

The barn swallow is the classic barn bird. It breeds **inside buildings**: in cowsheds, horse stables, barns, open garages and sheds. Their nest is an open shell of clay lumps and stalks stuck to a beam, lamp or ledge. To do this, she needs **permanently open access**, such as a tilted window, an opening above the door or an open gate.

Because there are fewer and fewer farms with animals and stables are often tightly closed, barn swallows have become rarer in many places. In addition, they find fewer insects in modern, clean stables and there are no open clay areas for building nests.

## The house martin

The house martin builds its nest **outside the house**, directly under eaves, balconies or eaves. It is an almost closed hemisphere made of clay balls with a small entry hole at the top. For a nest, a pair needs several hundred to over a thousand lumps of clay, which they carry in their beaks. House martins like to breed in **colonies**, often with many nests next to each other.

Problems for the house martin are smooth, modern facades to which clay does not adhere well, lack of clay puddles in sealed settlements and, unfortunately, people who remove nests because of the droppings.

## The swift

The swift is a master of the air. It spends almost its entire life flying: it eats, drinks, collects nesting material and even sleeps while flying. Young swifts that leave the nest often do not land for years until they breed themselves. His feet are so short that he **can't take off from the ground**. A swift on the ground therefore always needs help, see [Young bird found: what to do?](/vogelwissen/jungvogel-gefunden-was-tun/).

Swifts breed in **cavities in tall buildings**: under roof tiles, behind gutters and cladding, in holes in the walls of old buildings and church towers. They are loyal to their breeding grounds for many years. When a building is renovated and all the cracks are sealed, an entire colony loses its breeding grounds, often without anyone noticing.

Their evening flying games are typical, in which groups call loudly and fly around the houses at high speed.

## Which is legally valid

All three species are protected, like all European bird species. One rule is particularly important for building breeders: their **reproductive sites** are protected under the Federal Nature Conservation Act. Since swallows and swifts use their nests and breeding grounds year after year, this protection also applies when the birds are in their winter quarters in Africa. Swallow's nests must therefore **not simply be removed**, not even in winter. Renovations that affect breeding sites usually require the involvement of the lower nature conservation authority, and replacement breeding sites are often required.

## This is how you help barn swallows

- **Keep access open:** From April to September, leave a window tilted or an opening of at least about 7 x 7 centimeters so that the swallows can get into the stable, barn or garage.
- **Attach nesting aids:** Ready-made nesting bowls made of wood concrete or small wooden boards under the ceiling, about 10 to 20 centimeters below the roof, provide support for building the nest.
- **Create a clay puddle:** A moist spot with clayey soil nearby helps with nest building.
- **Encourage insects:** Meadows, pastures and a dung heap nearby attract insects.

## How to help house martins

### Artificial nests

**Artificial nests made of wood concrete** are placed under eaves and are often quickly accepted by house martins, especially if there is already a colony breeding nearby. Hang several nests next to each other, because house martins breed sociably. The nests should hang as high as possible, with a clear approach from below.

### poop board

Many people are bothered by the droppings under the nests. The simple solution is a **dropping board**: a narrow board mounted on the wall about 50 to 60 centimeters below the nests. It catches the droppings, keeps the facade clean and must not sit directly under the nest so that cats and magpies do not use it as a springboard.

### Clay puddle

A **clay puddle** nearby is extremely important for house martins, especially in dry springs and in sealed settlements. This is how you create them:

1. Dig a shallow depression of about half a square meter.
2. Fill in loamy soil or clay from the hardware store and mix with a little sand and straw.
3. Moisturize regularly to keep it mushy.
4. Place in an open area with a clear view so that the swallows can see cats in good time when collecting.

Barn swallows also like to use such puddles.

## This is how you help swifts

- Install **Swift boxes** under the edge of the roof, at least five to six meters high, with a clear approach without trees or obstacles in front of them. Several boxes next to each other because swifts breed in colonies.
- **Integrate built-in blocks** directly into the facade or insulation when renovating or new building. They are invisible and last for decades.
- **Preserve existing breeding sites:** If cavities need to be closed when renovating a facade, ask whether swifts breed there. Work should take place outside of the breeding season and a replacement should be created for any breeding site lost.
- **Locking calls:** New boxes will be accepted more quickly if you play swift calls over a small loudspeaker nearby during the settlement period. This method is used by experts in settlement projects.

## When breeding sites are lost during renovation

Many colonies disappear because no one thinks about the birds during renovations. If you are planning or notice a renovation in your house or on your street:

1. **Check early** whether swallows or swifts are breeding on the building. In the summer evenings, look at where birds fly in and out.
2. **Inform the lower nature conservation authority** or ask a nature conservation association for advice.
3. **Plan to work outside of the breeding season**, i.e. from around mid-August to March, depending on the species.
4. **Plan for replacement breeding places**, ideally in the form of built-in stones or boxes on the same facade.

## The trip to Africa

All three species spend the winter south of the Sahara. Barn and house martins migrate in large flocks in September and October, often after days of gathering on power lines. Swifts leave us at the end of July or beginning of August, often almost unnoticed, and overwinter in tropical and southern Africa. They cover many thousands of kilometers along the way and rely on a good supply of insects along the route. Droughts in the wintering areas, the loss of resting places and fewer insects in Europe are taking a toll on populations. It is all the more important that they find safe breeding grounds when they return in spring. You can find out more about the phenomenon of bird migration in the guide [Migratory birds and winter guests] (/vogelwissen/zugvoegel-und-wintergaeste/).

## Watch swallows and swifts

On warm summer evenings it's worth just looking up. Swifts hunt at high altitudes when the weather is good and come lower when there is a storm. House martins often fly in groups over roofs and gardens, while barn swallows tend to fly more closely over meadows and water areas. If you have binoculars at hand, you can see the differences in wing shape and color while flying. The guide [Binoculars for bird watching] (/vogelwissen/fernglas-vogelbeobachtung-kaufen/) gives tips on choosing binoculars.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do swallows return to the same nest every year?** Often yes. Many pairs return to their breeding site from the previous year and repair the old nest.

**Why do swallows fly low in bad weather?** In damp, cool weather, insects fly lower and the swallows follow them. So the old farmer's rule has a kernel of truth.

**How ​​do I recognize a young swift on the ground?** It has dark plumage with light feather edges, long wings and can hardly move on the ground. He always needs help from an expert station.

**Can I remove house martin nests if they are disturbing?** No. The nests are protected all year round. A poop board solves the problem with dirt.

## Conclusion

Barn Swallows, House Martins and Swifts are summer visitors that depend on our buildings. The barn swallow breeds inside in stables and can be recognized by its tail spikes and red throat, the house martin outside under eaves with a white rump, the swift in cavities in high buildings with sickle-shaped wings and shrill calls. Open stable windows, artificial nests, swift boxes, clay puddles and droppings boards help them. And those who think about birds when renovating often save entire colonies.
