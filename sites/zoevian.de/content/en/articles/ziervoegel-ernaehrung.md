Many pet birds get the same thing throughout their lives: a bowl of grains, sometimes accompanied by millet. But grains alone won't make a bird healthy. In nature, budgies, cockatiels and canaries eat half-ripe seeds, fresh grasses, herbs, leaves, buds and occasionally insects. A one-sided diet with dry grains often leads to obesity, vitamin deficiencies, liver damage and plumage problems in pet birds.

This guide explains what a balanced diet looks like for the most common pet birds, how you grow sprouted food yourself, which foods are poisonous and how you get birds used to new food.

## The building blocks of a healthy diet

A good diet for grain-eating pet birds consists of several building blocks:

1. **Grain mixture** as a basis
2. **Fresh food** made from vegetables, herbs and wild plants
3. **Sprout feed** and semi-ripe seeds
4. **Fruit** in small quantities
5. **Minerals** such as lime and grit
6. **Fresh water**

## The right mix of grains

Each type of bird needs its own mix:

| bird species | Main ingredients | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **budgie** | different types of millet, shiny seeds, some oats | low-fat seeds |
| **Cockatiel** | Millet, shiny seeds, oats, buckwheat, a few sunflower seeds | Be aware of your tendency to be overweight |
| **Canary** | Turnips, gloss seeds, hemp, linseed, niger seeds, oat kernels | Use your own canary mix |

### Recognize quality

- **Fresh and Clean:** Grains should not be dusty, clumpy or musty.
- **Germinability:** A simple test shows quality: If you soak and germinate a handful of grains, most should germinate after a day or two.
- **No additives:** Colorful grains, honey, sugar or baked goods in the mixture are unnecessary.

### How many grains?

Many birds get too much grain. A rough guide for budgies is around **one to two teaspoons per bird per day**, supplemented with plenty of fresh food. A little more for cockatiels. If you are unsure, let an avian veterinary practice help you find the right amount for your birds, for example by weighing them regularly.

### pellets

Some veterinarians recommend replacing some of the grains with **pellets**, a pressed complete food with balanced nutrients. If you want to introduce pellets, do so slowly and seek advice.

## Fresh food: on the menu every day

Fresh food provides vitamins, minerals, fiber and water. It should be offered **daily**, preferably in the morning when the birds are hungry.

### vegetables

For example, the following are well suited:

- Cucumber, zucchini, pepper, carrot, fennel
- Broccoli, cauliflower, kohlrabi including leaves
- Lamb's lettuce, chicory, endive, romaine lettuce in moderation
- Corn on the cob, fresh and unseasoned
- Pumpkin, beetroot in small quantities

Vegetables should be **well washed**, preferably organic and not seasoned or cooked. Be careful of possible high nitrate content in salads grown in greenhouses in winter.

### Herbs and wild plants

Wild plants are particularly valuable because they come closest to natural food:

- **chickweed**, a favorite of many birds
- **Dandelion**, leaves and flowers
- **Daisies**, shepherd's purse, broadleaf and ribwort plantain
- **Semi-ripe grasses** with seed heads, such as bluegrass or millet
- **Culinary herbs** such as dill, basil, cress, lemon balm

Only collect wild plants in places that have not been fertilized, sprayed or soiled by dogs, i.e. not on the side of the road or in dog parks.

### fruit

Fruit contains a lot of sugar and should therefore only be offered **in small quantities**, for example once or twice a week:

- Apple and pear without core
- Berries such as blueberries, raspberries, strawberries
- Melon, kiwi, grapes in small pieces

The **seeds of apples, pears and stone fruits** contain substances that can produce hydrogen cyanide and should be removed.

## Grow germinated food yourself

Germinated food is particularly rich in vitamins and protein and is similar to the half-ripe seeds that birds eat in nature. It is particularly valuable during the **moult**, in **young birds** and in the breeding season.

### That's how it works

1. **Soaking:** Soak a small amount of grain mixture or special sprouting seeds in cold water for about 12 to 24 hours.
2. **Rinse:** Rinse thoroughly in a fine sieve under running water.
3. **Germinate:** Let stand in a sieve or germination container at room temperature for 24 to 48 hours, rinsing several times a day.
4. **Feeding:** As soon as small seedlings are visible, rinse again, drain and feed.
5. **Remove leftovers:** Germinated food spoils quickly. Anything that has not been eaten after a few hours must be removed.

### Hygiene is crucial

Germinated food can quickly become moldy or colonized by bacteria. If it smells sour or musty, is slimy, or has a coating on it, throw it away. Clean germination containers thoroughly after each pass.

## Minerals and grit

- **Lime:** A **sepia shell** or **mineral stone** provides calcium, especially important for females that lay eggs.
- **Grit:** Fine stomach grit helps digest grains. It should be offered in a separate bowl, not in bulk.
- **Vitamin and mineral supplements** only after consultation with a veterinary practice. An overdose of certain vitamins can also be harmful.

## water

Fresh, clean water is essential. It should be changed **daily**. Drinking and bathing water must be offered separately. If you give vitamins or medications in the water, do so only as directed by a veterinarian and change the water more often.

## What pet birds are not allowed to eat

| Food | Why dangerous? |
|---|---|
| **Avocado** | contains a substance that is toxic to birds; even small amounts can be fatal |
| **Chocolate and cocoa** | Theobromine is toxic to birds |
| **Coffee, black tea, cola** | Caffeine damages the heart and circulation |
| **Alcohol** | even small amounts are dangerous |
| **Onions, Garlic, Leeks** | can damage red blood cells |
| **Raw legumes** such as beans | contain toxic proteins |
| **Raw or green potatoes** | contain solanine |
| **Rhubarb** | Oxalic acid |
| **Seeds of pome fruit and stone fruit** | can form hydrogen cyanide |
| **Salted, spiced, sugared** | puts strain on the kidneys and metabolism |
| **Dairy products** | Birds cannot tolerate lactose |
| **Bread and baked goods** | hardly any nutritional value, they go moldy quickly |
| **Mouldy food** | Mushroom toxins can cause serious illnesses |

**Houseplants** can also be poisonous, such as dieffenbachia, philodendron, ivy, poinsettia and oleander. Make sure that birds cannot nibble on it when flying freely.

## Get birds used to new food

Birds are often **very suspicious** when it comes to food. What they don't know, they don't touch at first. With a little patience, the change can still be made:

- **Always offer:** Some birds take weeks before trying a new vegetable.
- **Various shapes:** Vegetables in pieces, grated, stuck on a skewer or clamped between the bars.
- **Have a demonstration:** When one bird eats, others often try. Sometimes it also helps if you visibly eat a piece of cucumber yourself.
- **Offer in the morning** when the birds are hungry, before the grain bowl is filled.
- **Don't starve:** Never eliminate grains completely to force a change. This is dangerous.

## Hygiene at the feeding place

Good food is of little use if bowls and feeding places are dirty. A few basic rules:

- **Clean bowls daily**, not just refill them. There is often spoiled feed or droppings hidden under the grain husks.
- **Blow away husks**: Many birds leave empty husks in their bowls. A full bowl can therefore be deceptive. Blow away the husks every day and check whether there are still enough grains left.
- **Remove fresh food after a few hours**, especially in summer, to prevent it from wilting or fermenting.
- **Store food supplies dry and cool**, in sealed containers, and watch out for food moths.
- **Several bowls** so that lower-ranking birds don't miss out.

## Deal with food

In nature, birds spend a large part of the day looking for food. The bowl in the cage can be reached in just a few minutes. If you offer food in a more interesting way, you will keep your birds busy:

- **Place vegetables on skewers** or clamp them between the bars.
- **Wrap the grains in paper** or hide them in cardboard rolls.
- **Feeding boards with holes** in which grains are hidden.
- **Fresh branches with buds** and half-ripe grasses for plucking.
- **A shallow bowl of grass sod** for birds to forage for seeds.

This means that eating becomes a task that challenges the body and mind, and there is less chance of boredom.

## Avoid being overweight

Obesity is one of the most common problems among pet birds. It is caused by too many high-fat seeds such as sunflower seeds and hemp, too many treats such as millet, snack sticks with honey and too little exercise. The consequences are fatty liver disease, joint problems and a shorter life expectancy. Regular **weighing** with a kitchen scale, an adjusted amount of grains, plenty of fresh food and daily free flight help.

## Special phases of life

- **Moulting:** increased need for protein and vitamins. Germinated food, including some egg food for canaries, is helpful.
- **Breeding and egg laying:** additional calcium requirements for females. Prepare sepia bowl.
- **Age:** Older birds move less and often need less energy-rich food.
- **Illness:** Always coordinate diet with your veterinary practice.

The guide [Identify a sick pet bird] (/vogelwissen/kranken-ziervogel-erkennen/) describes how you can recognize signs of illness early.

## An example of a daily schedule

For two budgies a day might look like this:

- **Morning:** a plate with cucumber slices, pepper strips and a bunch of chickweed, plus fresh water.
- **Morning:** the measured amount of grains.
- **Afternoon:** a small portion of sprouted food or half-ripe grass during free flight.
- **Two to three times a week:** a small piece of apple.
- **Occasionally:** a short piece of foxtail millet as a reward during training.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I feed foxtail millet every day?** Better not. Millet is high in energy and makes you fat quickly. A short piece once or twice a week or as a reward during training is enough.

**Do pet birds need insects?** Grain-eating pet birds generally do not need insects. When moulting or rearing, additional protein can be useful, for example through sprouted food or egg food for canaries.

**How ​​do I know if my bird is well fed?** A healthy bird has smooth, shiny feathers, is active, and maintains a stable weight. Regular weighing shows changes early.

## Conclusion

A healthy diet for pet birds consists of a species-appropriate mixture of grains in moderate quantities, daily fresh food made from vegetables, herbs and wild plants, regular sprouted food, little fruit, a source of lime and grit and fresh water. Avocado, chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, onions, raw beans and leftovers are taboo. With patience, even skeptical birds can get used to new food. This means budgies, cockatiels and canaries stay slim, fit and have shiny feathers.
