Bird watching is one of the most accessible hobbies out there. You don't need a club, expensive equipment or a long journey. There are birds everywhere: in the garden, in the city park, in the cemetery, at the quarry lake and on the railway embankment. Nevertheless, many beginners feel insecure. Where do I start? What do I have to buy? How do I get close enough without disturbing the birds?

This guide answers these questions and gives you everything you need for the first few months.

## The basic equipment

### Binoculars

The most important tool is a good pair of binoculars. For most beginners, **8×42 binoculars** are ideal: They have a large field of view, a calm image and enough light intensity for twilight. Make sure you have a close focusing distance of no more than two to three meters and a waterproof housing. The guide [binoculars for bird watching] (/vogelwissen/fernglas-vogelbeobachtung-kaufen/) explains everything else about the selection.

### Determination book

An identification book with painted panels of all the clothes, distribution maps and information about habitat and voice is indispensable for the beginning. A handy format is good for when you're on the go, but it can be more detailed for when you're at home.

### Notebook

A small notebook with a pencil that can write even when it's wet belongs in every jacket pocket. More on this below.

### Smartphone

The smartphone has now become a useful companion: for identification apps, listening to bird calls, taking photos through binoculars and reporting observations. But it neither replaces your own observation nor the book.

### Clothing

- **Muted Colors:** Green, brown, gray and beige are less noticeable than bright red or white.
- **Quiet fabrics:** Rustling jackets give you away.
- **Weatherproof:** Birds are out and about even in drizzle, and they are often particularly active after a shower.
- **Sturdy shoes:** for damp meadows and field paths.

## Where you start

### Your own garden or balcony

The best place to start is your own garden or balcony, especially with a winter feeder. Here the birds come close and you can compare features in peace. The guide [Recognizing the most common garden birds] (/vogelwissen/haeufigste-gartenvoegel-erkennen/) shows you which species you see there most often.

### Parks and cemeteries

Old parks and cemeteries with large trees, hedges and quiet corners are little bird paradises in the middle of the city. Here you can find woodpeckers, nuthatches, treecreepers, warblers, thrushes and, in winter, sometimes long-eared owls sleeping in conifers.

### waters

Ponds, lakes, rivers and gravel pits are particularly exciting in winter when many ducks, grebes and geese come from the north. Waterfowl are usually large and easy to see. The guide [Identifying waterbirds] (/vogelwissen/wasservoegel-erkennen/) provides an overview.

### Forest edges and field hedges

Transitions between forest and open land are particularly rich in species. Here forest birds meet birds of the open landscape. In spring, yellowhammers, whitethroats and red-backed shrikes sing here.

### Protected areas

Later, it's worth taking a trip to nature reserves, the Wadden Sea, large lakes or crane resting places. Many have observation towers and huts. You can find a selection in the guide [Bird watching in Germany: the best places] (/vogelwissen/vogelbeobachtung-orte-deutschland/).

## When you see the most

- **Early in the morning:** The first few hours after sunrise are the most active time. Birds sing and look for food.
- **Late afternoon:** A second, smaller period of activity before dusk.
- **Spring:** March to June is the time of singing, courtship and breeding. The birds are conspicuous and wear their magnificent plumage.
- **Autumn:** September and October are the time of migration. Some days thousands of birds fly overhead.
- **Winter:** The trees are bare, the birds are easier to see, and many species congregate at feeders and waterways.
- **Midsummer:** rather quiet because many birds are moulting and hiding.

## This is how you move

### Slow and calm

Move slowly, stop often and speak quietly. Many birds do not flee from people per se, but from sudden movements.

### Standing still is worth it

Find a good spot, perhaps at the edge of a forest or on the bank, and stand or sit still for ten to fifteen minutes. The birds get used to you and often come closer.

### Sun on your back

Stand so that the sun is behind you. Then the colors shine and you won't be blinded. In the backlight, every bird appears black.

### Pay attention to noises

Follow chants and calls. Pay particular attention to warning calls: When blackbirds complain and tits scold, there may be an owl, sparrowhawk or cat sitting nearby.

### Use paths

Stay on trails, especially in protected areas. Paths are predictable for birds, and you can often see more than just across the terrain from them.

## Rules for considerate observation

The welfare of the birds always comes first. These principles have become accepted by bird watchers worldwide:

1. **Keep your distance:** If a bird becomes agitated, warns or flies away, you are too close.
2. **Leave nests alone:** Never visit nests, do not bend branches to the side, and do not photograph nesting birds if this will disturb them.
3. **No sound dummies during breeding season:** Playing songs to attract birds stresses them out and can deter them from breeding.
4. **Observe protected area rules:** There are good reasons for entry bans, path restrictions and dog leash requirements.
5. **Don't scare resting birds:** Migratory birds and winter visitors need their energy. Every escape costs reserves.
6. **Responsibly report rare species:** For very rare or disturbance-sensitive species such as breeding owls or birds of prey, do not publicly disclose the exact location.
7. **Respect private property:** Ask before entering fields or gardens.

## The first hundred species: an orientation

A hundred bird species sounds like a lot, but it's easily accessible in a year if you visit different habitats. A rough guide to where you can find which species:

- **Garden and settlement, about 25 to 30 species:** House and tree sparrow, great tit, blue tit, coal tit and long-tailed tit, blackbird, song thrush, robin, dunnock, wren, chaffinch, greenfinch, goldfinch, bullfinch, starling, wood pigeon, turkish dove, magpie, carrion crow, jay, great spotted woodpecker, nuthatch, Black redstart, blackcap, chiffchaff, swift, house martin.
- **Parks, cemeteries and forest, another 15 to 20 species:** Treecreeper, Green Woodpecker, Black Woodpecker, Mistle Thrush, Garden Warbler, Fitis, Spotted Flycatcher, Grosbeak, Tawny Owl, Sparrowhawk, Goshawk, Goldcrest, Oriole, Pied Flycatcher.
- **Fields and hedges, another 10 to 15 species:** Skylark, yellowhammer, linnet, whitethroat, red-backed shrike, kestrel, common buzzard, red kite, pheasant, lapwing, barn swallow, tree sparrow, fieldfare.
- **Waters, another 20 to 30 species:** Mallard, tufted duck, pochard, golden-eye duck, common teal, mute swan, greylag goose, Canada goose, Egyptian goose, coot, moorhen, great crested grebe, little grebe, gray heron, great egret, cormorant, kingfisher, black-headed gull, gray wagtail, wagtail, reed warbler, Reed bunting.
- **Winter and migratory season, another 10 to 15 species:** Brambling, redwing, siskin, waxwing in some years, crane, bean and white-fronted goose, whooper swan.

Just a garden, a park, a dirt road and a lake nearby are enough to break the hundred. ## Keep notes

Notes are the birder's memory. For each observation, write down:

- **Date, time and location**
- **Weather**
- **Type and number**
- **Behaviour:** singing, carrying food, with young, pulling
- **Characteristics** If you are not sure about the identification, it is best to do a small sketch

Many bird watchers keep **lists**: a garden list of all the species they have ever seen in the garden, an annual list, or a life list. That's a nice incentive, but not a must.

## Share observations

Your observations are also valuable for science. You can share them via online reporting platforms and during large participation campaigns such as the “Hour of the Garden Birds” or the “Hour of the Winter Birds”. This data helps professionals identify inventory changes. The guide [participating bird counts] (/vogelwissen/vogelzaehlung-mitmachen/) explains how this works.

## Observe with others

Nature conservation associations and ornithological clubs regularly offer **guided tours and excursions**. Observing together is not only more fun, you also learn faster. Experienced observers will show you what is important and know the good places in the region. Many groups are particularly happy about beginners.

## Observe with children

Children are often enthusiastic bird watchers. A few tips:

- **Short trips** of one hour instead of long hikes
- **Your own, robust binoculars** in the right size
- **Easy tasks:** Find five birds that are red or discover the loudest singer
- **Feeding place at the nursery window**
- **Build a nesting box** and watch together to see who moves in, see [Build your own nesting box](/vogelwissen/nistkasten-bauen-anleitung/)

## Security

- **Ticks:** Check meadows and the edge of the forest when you return.
- **Weather:** On coasts and wetlands, pay attention to weather and tides.
- **Sun protection:** Never look at the sun with binoculars.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many species can I see in the first year as a beginner?** If you travel regularly and visit different habitats, you can easily see 80 to 120 species in the first year.

**Do I have to get up early?** It's worth it, but it's not a must. A walk in the afternoon or a look at the feeding area during breakfast also counts.

**What do I do if I can't identify a bird?** Write down the characteristics, take a photo if possible and ask later in a group of observers. “Indefinite” is completely fine and also happens to professionals.

**Is bird watching expensive?** No. Apart from binoculars and a book, there are hardly any costs. Many tours run by nature conservation organizations are free.

## Conclusion

To get started with bird watching, you need good binoculars, an identification book, a notebook and, above all, curiosity. Start in your own garden, visit parks, waterways and forest edges, get out early in the morning and move slowly. Keep your distance, do not disturb nests and follow the rules in protected areas. Write down your observations, share them in participatory activities and join a group. With every walk you'll discover more, and soon you'll wonder how you could have missed all those birds before.
