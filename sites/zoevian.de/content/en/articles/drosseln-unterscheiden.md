Thrushes are one of the most familiar birds in our gardens and forests, and yet they are constantly confused. A spotted thrush on the lawn could be a song thrush, a mistle thrush or a young blackbird. In winter, fieldfares and redwings appear from the north, often in large groups. However, the species can be easily distinguished with a few clear characteristics.

This guide introduces you to the six types of thrushes that occur regularly in Germany and shows you how you can recognize them.

## What thrushes have in common

Thrushes are medium-sized songbirds with strong legs, an upright posture and a medium-length, straight bill. They often look for their food on the ground, hop over lawns and meadows, stop, tilt their heads and then peck. In summer they eat mainly earthworms, insects and snails, and in autumn and winter they eat many berries and fruits. Several species are among the best singers in Europe.

## The blackbird

The blackbird is the most common and well-known thrush.

- **Male:** all black, with **orange-yellow beak** and yellow eye ring.
- **Female:** dark brown, breast slightly spotted or washed out, beak brownish.
- **Juveniles:** reddish brown with light spots, especially on the chest. They are often mistaken for song thrushes.
- **Voice:** fluting, calm singing, excited “tix-tix-tix” as a warning call, persistent “tschink” in the evening.

Female blackbirds and young birds also appear darker overall than spotted thrushes and do not have a light belly with sharply defined spots. More about the blackbird in the guide [The Blackbird in the Garden](/vogelwissen/amsel-im-garten/).

## The song thrush

The song thrush is slightly smaller than the blackbird and is one of the most common thrushes in gardens with trees, parks and forests.

- **Top:** plain **warm brown**.
- **Underside:** creamy white to yellowish with **many small, teardrop- or arrow-shaped dark spots**.
- **Underwing:** in flight **yellowish-orange**.
- **Singing:** very loud and varied, with short motifs that she repeats **two to three times**, such as “didi-didi-didi, tjuwi-tjuwi”. She often sings from high tree tops, especially at dusk.
- **Call:** a short, sharp “zip”, often heard during night migration.
- **Special feature:** The song thrush likes to eat snails and crack them on a stone, the “thrush forge”. Anyone who finds a stone in the garden with lots of snail shell shards has found them.

The song thrush is a short-distance migrant. Most leave Germany in the fall for southwestern Europe and return in March.

## The mistle thrush

The mistle thrush is the largest native thrush, larger than the blackbird.

- **Top:** **grey-brown**, colder than the song thrush.
- **Underside:** whitish with **large, round, dark spots** that almost merge on the chest.
- **Underwing:** in flight **knows** what distinguishes it from the song thrush.
- **Tail:** with light outer edges.
- **Flight:** undulating, with wings folded between wing beats.
- **Posture:** very upright, appears strong and “proud”.
- **Singing:** Blackbird-like, but shorter, less varied, more melancholic verses. She sings in late winter, often even in bad weather.
- **Call:** a rasping, dry “torrr” that is reminiscent of winding up a wooden rattle.

She gets her name from her love of mistletoe berries. By excreting the sticky seeds, it ensures that the mistletoe spreads through the treetops.

## The Fieldfare

The fieldfare is about the same size as the blackbird and is strikingly colorful for a thrush.

- **Head and rump:** **gray**.
- **Back:** **maroon**.
- **Chest:** orange-brown with dark spots, belly white.
- **Tail:** black.
- **Call:** a loud, rough **“smack-smack-smack”**, especially in flight and when excited.
- **Behavior:** very sociable, often breeds in loose colonies and migrates in large flocks over fields and orchards in winter.

In Germany, the fieldfare breeds mainly in the middle and east; in winter, many birds from Scandinavia and Eastern Europe come. When it snows, they often invade gardens with fallen fruit. Breeding fieldfares defend their colonies together and splash droppings on crows and birds of prey as they swoop down.

## The redwing

The redwing is the smallest of our thrushes, slightly smaller than the song thrush.

- **Head:** conspicuous **white streak above the eyes** and light whisker streak.
- **Flanks and underwings:** **rust red**, hence the name.
- **Chest:** dashed instead of spotted.
- **Call:** a thin, drawn-out **“pull”** that is often heard on clear nights in October when large groups are moving over Germany.

The redwing breeds in Scandinavia, Finland and Russia and is primarily a migrant and winter visitor from October to March. It likes to mingle with fieldfares and eats hawthorn, rowan and ivy berries as well as fallen fruit.

## The ring ouzel

The ring ouzel is a thrush of the mountains.

- **Male:** black to dark brown with a broad **white crescent on the chest**.
- **Female:** browner, with a weaker, often brownish washed-out breast band.
- **Wings:** conspicuously brightly lined.
- **Habitat:** Mountain forests at the tree line in the Alps and some low mountain ranges.

In the lowlands, ring ouzels can be seen migrating mainly in April and October, sometimes in meadows or fields between other thrushes.

## The most common mix-ups

### Young blackbird or song thrush?

Young blackbirds have **red-brown spots all over their body**, including on their backs, appear dark and disheveled overall and often have a short tail. Song thrushes have a **clean, light belly** with dark drops and a **solid brown back**.

### Song Thrush or Mistle Thrush?

The mistle thrush is **larger, grayer** and has **larger, rounder spots**. In flight it shows **white underwings**, the song thrush yellowish-orange. The mistle thrush often stands very upright on open lawns, while the song thrush tends to stay near bushes.

### Fieldfare or mistle thrush in winter flock?

Note the fieldfare's **gray head and rump** and **brown back** and its loud "schack" call. Mistle thrushes are a solid gray-brown color and have a buzzing sound.

## Encourage thrushes in the garden

Thrushes need three things:

1. **Open lawns and meadows** with loose soil where they can look for worms, ideally not constantly mowed short.
2. **Dense shrubs and trees** for breeding.
3. **Berries and fallen fruit** for autumn and winter: rowan, hawthorn, elderberry, ivy, mistletoe, apples.

Leaving apples lying around in the fall or putting halved apples on the lawn on frosty days often attracts fieldfares and redwings. The guide [Native Shrubs for Birds] (/vogelwissen/heimische-straeucher-fuer-voegel/) provides a list of suitable shrubs. ## Observe thrushes throughout the year

Anyone who follows thrushes throughout the year will experience an exciting change:

- **January and February:** Blackbirds and mistle thrushes occupy territories, the mistle thrush sings from tree tops even on mild days. Fieldfares and redwings migrate in flocks over orchards and fields.
- **March:** The song thrushes return and their loud song with repeated motifs fills the evenings. The last redwings are heading north.
- **April to June:** Peak breeding season. Blackbirds are often already breeding their second brood, and spotted young birds sit on the lawns. Ring ouzels migrate in April and can sometimes be seen in the lowlands.
- **July and August:** It gets quiet. Many thrushes molt and stay hidden. Elderberry and mountain ash bear the first ripe fruits.
- **September and October:** Song thrushes leave, redwings and fieldfares arrive from the north. On clear nights you can hear the train calls.
- **November and December:** Flocks of thrushes eat hawthorn, viburnum and ivy berries, and when it snows they come into the gardens to eat fallen fruit.

A notebook with the first and last observation data of each species shows how the comings and goings shift after a few years.

## Hear thrushes

Thrushes are excellent singers. A nice introduction to learning bird songs is to listen to blackbirds and song thrushes next to each other in the spring: the blackbird sings calmly and with a flute, without repetitions, the song thrush sings loudly and with repeated motifs. In autumn it is worth listening on clear nights: the thin “pull” of the redwings and the “zip” of the song thrushes reveal the nocturnal migration. More about this in the guide [Learn bird sounds] (/vogelwissen/vogelstimmen-lernen/).

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do I see so many thrushes in winter?** The local birds include winter visitors from Northern and Eastern Europe, especially fieldfares and redwings, but also blackbirds. More about this in the guide [Migratory birds and winter guests] (/vogelwissen/zugvoegel-und-wintergaeste/).

**Do thrushes eat snails?** Yes, the song thrush in particular likes to eat shell snails, blackbirds also eat slugs. A garden without slug pellets is a win for them.

**Are thrushes related to robins?** Yes, both are broadly considered thrush-like birds. Today, robins and nightingales are mostly counted as flycatchers.

**Where do mistle thrushes breed?** High in trees, often in the forks of deciduous or coniferous trees, usually early in the year, as early as March.

## Conclusion

The six native thrushes can be easily distinguished if you pay attention to a few characteristics: the blackbird is black or dark brown, the song thrush is warm brown with small drops and repeats its motifs in song, the mistle thrush is larger, grayer, has large spots and has white underwings, the fieldfare has a gray head and a brown back and calls “schack”, the redwing has a white stripe over the eyes and red flanks, and the ring ouzel bears a white breast moon. You can bring many of them into your garden with berries, fallen fruit and open lawns.
