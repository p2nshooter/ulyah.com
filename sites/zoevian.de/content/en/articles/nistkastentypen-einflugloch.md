Not all nesting boxes are the same. A blue tit needs something different than a black redstart, and a swift won't be able to handle a tit box. If you want to help specific birds, you should know what types of boxes there are, which hole sizes and internal dimensions which species prefer and where the boxes should hang.

This guide gives you an overview of the most important types of nesting boxes for the garden, house and surrounding area, with a large overview table and information on materials and hanging.

## Cave breeders and semi-cave breeders

Before we talk about the individual boxes, it's worth taking a look at how birds breed in nature. A rough distinction is made between:

- **Cave nesters:** They breed in closed caves with small entrances, for example in old woodpecker holes or rotten knotholes. These include tits, nuthatches, tree sparrows, starlings, pied flycatchers and redstarts.
- **Semi-cave nesters:** They prefer niches that are half-open at the front, such as holes in walls, crevices in rocks or niches under roof beams. These include black redstart, spotted flycatcher, wagtail and robin.
- **Free-breeders:** They build their nest freely in trees and bushes, like blackbirds, chaffinches or greenfinches. Nesting boxes are not suitable for them. You can help them with dense hedges and bushes, see [Native shrubs for birds](/vogelwissen/heimische-straeucher-fuer-voegel/).
- **Building breeders:** Species such as swifts, house martins, barn swallows, house sparrows and jackdaws breed on or in buildings and need special nesting aids.

The information is a guideline. Manufacturers and nature conservation associations sometimes specify slightly different dimensions. The **hole size** is particularly important because it determines who fits in it.

## Cave boxes for tits and co.

The classic nesting box is a closed wooden box with a round entrance hole. It is the most frequently used nesting aid of all.

### Why hole size is so important

Small birds prefer small holes. A **26 to 28 millimeter** hole allows blue tits, coal tits, and marsh tits in, but keeps great tits, sparrows, and larger competitors out. A hole of **32 millimeters** is suitable for great tits, nuthatches and tree sparrows, but blue tits can also use it if they prevail against the competition.

If you want to help both species of tits, hang up two boxes with different hole sizes. You can find out more about the two species in the guide [Great Tit and Blue Tit](/vogelwissen/kohlmeise-und-blaumeise/).

### Important details

- **Depth:** There should be at least **17 centimeters** from the bottom of the hole to the ground so that cats, martens and magpies do not reach the nest.
- **No perch:** It only helps predators.
- **Rough inner wall** under the hole for young birds to climb out.
- **Cleaning flap** for annual cleaning.

You can find detailed construction instructions in the guide [build your own nesting box] (/vogelwissen/nistkasten-bauen-anleitung/).

## Semi-caves

Half caves have a front wall that only reaches about half the height. The upper part is open. They come in many variations, some with two smaller openings or a wide slit.

### For whom?

- **Black Redstart:** likes to breed on buildings, under eaves, on carports or in barns.
- **Spotted Flycatcher:** likes semi-caves on house walls with ivy or wild vine.
- **Pair Wagtail:** breeds in niches on buildings, often near water.
- **Robins and Wrens:** prefer semi-caves very deep and well hidden, for example in thick ivy or in a hedge. More about this in the guide [Robins in the Garden](/vogelwissen/rotkehlchen-im-garten/).

### What to pay attention to?

Half caves are more easily accessible to magpies, jays and cats. They should therefore be **hidden**, for example in dense vegetation or under an eaves, and not where a cat can jump from below.

## Colony house for sparrows

House sparrows breed socially. A **colony house** with three or more chambers next to each other, each with its own entrance hole, is ideal for them. It is hung on buildings, at least three meters high and, if possible, under an eaves. You can read why sparrows need help and what else they need in the guide [House Sparrow: How to Help Sparrows](/vogelwissen/haussperling-spatzen-helfen/).

## Starling box

Starlings are larger than titmice and need a larger box with an entry hole of around **45 millimeters**. Starling boxes hang from trees or buildings at least three meters high. Starlings are not always welcome in fruit-growing areas because they eat cherries in summer and grapes in autumn. However, they are valuable for nature conservation because their populations have declined in many regions.

## Nesting aids on buildings

### Swifts

Swifts breed in cracks and cavities under gutters, behind cladding and in holes in the walls of high buildings. Because they often lose their breeding grounds after renovations, special **Swift boxes** are important. They are flat and wide, have an oval entrance hole, which is usually on the underside or on the side, and are installed directly under the edge of the roof at a height of at least five to six meters, with a free approach. Swifts breed in colonies, so several boxes next to each other are good.

### Swallows

- **House Martins** build mud nests on the outside of buildings under eaves. Artificial nests made of wood concrete help where there is no clay or the facade is too smooth. A dropping board about 50 centimeters below the nests protects the facade.
- **Barn Swallows** breed indoors in stables, barns and garages. They need open access, such as a tilted window or an opening in the door, and happily accept nesting bowls on beams.

The guide [Swallows and swifts at the house] (/vogelwissen/schwalben-mauersegler-helfen/) describes this in detail.

### nesting stones

In new buildings and renovations, **nesting stones** can be installed directly into masonry or insulation. They are hardly noticeable, last for decades and require hardly any care. They are available for sparrows, swifts, tits and bats.

## Large boxes for owls and birds of prey

There are large special boxes for kestrels, barn owls, tawny owls and jackdaws. They are usually attached to barns, church towers, tall buildings or old trees and require experience in planning and installation. Many nature conservation groups look after such boxes on a voluntary basis and are happy to receive support. If you have a suitable location, such as a barn with an open gable, you can contact a regional nature conservation association.

## Material: wood or wood concrete?

| material | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Wood | cheap, easy to build yourself, good insulation | Depending on the type of wood and protection, it lasts from several to many years |
| Wood concrete (mixture of wood fibers and concrete) | very durable, breathable, protects well against woodpeckers and martens | more expensive, difficult, difficult to make yourself |
| plastic, metal | – | unsuitable: overheating, condensation |

Wooden boxes are a good choice for the garden. In places where woodpeckers or martens cause problems, wood concrete is worthwhile.

## How many boxes for which garden?

- **Small terraced house garden:** a 26 or 32 millimeter tit box and a semi-cave on the house wall.
- **Middle garden:** two tit boxes with different holes at least ten meters apart, a half-cave and a sparrow colony house on the house.
- **Large garden with trees:** additionally a starling box, a box with an oval hole for the redstart and nesting aids on the house for swifts or swallows.

The guide [Hanging the nesting box correctly] (/vogelwissen/nistkasten-richtig-aufhaengen/) explains more about where and how the boxes should be hung.

## Don’t forget nesting aids for non-birds

If you are already hanging up nesting boxes, you can also think about other garden inhabitants who are closely linked to birds. **Bat boxes** look similar to bird boxes, but have a narrow entry slot at the bottom and a rough inner wall to which the animals cling. They are hung high on house walls or trees, as sunny as possible. Bats hunt the same insects at night that birds live on during the day and are part of a living garden. **Insect hotels** made of wooden blocks with drilled holes, reed stalks and clay offer nesting places for wild bees. They are not bird food, so they should not be hung directly next to feeding places, but they do promote insect diversity in the garden and thus also provide food for young birds. The guide [Creating a bird-friendly garden] (/vogelwissen/vogelfreundlicher-garten-anlegen/) shows how the two fit together.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I offer one box for multiple species?** A box with a 32 millimeter hole is used by multiple species, such as great tits, nuthatches or tree sparrows. If you want to promote small tits specifically, you need a smaller hole.

**Why is my entrance hole suddenly smaller?** Then there was probably a nuthatch there. He covers holes that are too big with clay until they fit him.

**Why is the hole suddenly bigger?** Woodpeckers or squirrels have hacked or gnawed it open to get eggs or young. A metal protective ring around the hole prevents this.

**Are colorful decorative nesting boxes suitable?** Many decorative houses are too small, have incorrect hole sizes, perches and no cleaning hatch. When purchasing, pay attention to the dimensions in this guide.

## Conclusion

Which nesting box is the right one depends on the type of bird. Closed boxes with a hole size of 26 to 32 millimeters are suitable for titmice and other small cavity nesters, semi-caves for black redstarts and robins, colony houses for sparrows and special nesting aids on buildings for swifts and swallows. Pay attention to the appropriate hole size, sufficient depth, rough interior walls and a cleaning hatch, and choose the location carefully. This way you can specifically help the birds that live in your area.
