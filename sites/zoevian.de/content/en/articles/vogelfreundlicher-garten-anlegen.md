Bird feeders and nesting boxes are a good start. But the biggest difference for birds is the garden itself. A garden in which native bushes grow, insects buzz, leaves remain and water is available, offers birds everything they need to live: food all year round, hiding places, nesting places and material for building nests. And the nice thing is: a garden like this often takes less work than a proper lawn with a cherry laurel hedge.

This guide will show you how to make your garden bird-friendly step by step, regardless of whether you have a large garden, a terraced property or just a balcony.

## What birds need in the garden

Birds have four basic needs:

1. **Food:** Insects and spiders, especially for raising young, as well as seeds, berries and fruits.
2. **Cover:** dense bushes, hedges and trees to protect against cats, birds of prey and weather.
3. **Nesting sites:** Caves, niches, dense bushes or nesting boxes.
4. **Water:** for drinking and bathing.

A bird-friendly garden meets all four needs in the most natural way possible.

## Step 1: Plant native trees

Native shrubs and trees are the basis. They provide food for insects, and birds find food for their young on these insects. However, many exotic ornamental trees such as cherry laurel, thuja or forsythia are hardly usable for our insects.

**Particularly valuable are:**

- **Hawthorn:** thorny, dense, flowers for insects, red fruits for birds, ideal for nests
- **Black Elderberry:** Berries in late summer, loved by blackcaps, blackbirds and starlings
- **Rowberry (rowanberry):** bright red berries in autumn
- **Blackthorn:** thorny, early flowering, berries in winter
- **Dog rose and other wild roses:** Rose hips until winter
- **Dogwood, privet, Pfaffenhütchen, cornelian cherry, serviceberry**

The guide [Native Shrubs for Birds] (/vogelwissen/heimische-straeucher-fuer-voegel/) offers a detailed list of plants with flowering and fruiting times.

### Hedge instead of fence

A **free-growing hedge** made up of various native shrubs is one of the most valuable elements in a bird garden. It provides nesting sites, cover, insects and berries in a small space. Instead of a precisely trimmed thuja hedge, it is worth having a mixed hedge that is only cut back in sections every few years. Important: According to the Federal Nature Conservation Act, severe pruning is only permitted from October 1st to the end of February.

### A tree if possible

If you have space, plant a native deciduous tree such as field maple, hornbeam, bird cherry or a fruit tree. Trees provide singing stations, nesting places and a huge number of insects. An old oak tree is home to hundreds of species of insects.

## Step 2: Wildflowers instead of lawn desert

Close-cropped lawns are largely useless to birds, apart from blackbirds who search for worms on them. A **wildflower meadow** or just a strip of native flowering plants attracts insects and provides seeds in late summer.

- **Meadow flowers** such as daisies, meadow sage, knapweed, wild carrots and scabious attract insects.
- **Seed heads** of sunflower, thistle, teasel, chicory and evening primrose are food for goldfinches, greenfinches and sparrows in autumn.
- **Mow less often:** Even a piece of lawn that is only mowed two to three times a year develops into a thriving habitat.

Leave the seed heads in the fall and cut them back in the spring. Wild bees and other insects also overwinter in the hollow stems.

## Step 3: Allow for clutter

For birds, a “tidy” garden is often an empty garden. A few wild corners make a big difference.

### Leave leaves lying around

Foliage under bushes is a habitat for woodlice, beetles, worms and larvae. Blackbirds, robins and thrushes rummage through it for food. Rake leaves from the lawn onto beds and under hedges instead of throwing them away.

### Heaps of brushwood and dead wood

A pile of branches and twigs is a hiding place for wrens, robins and dunnocks, a wintering place for hedgehogs and a habitat for insects. Dead wood, such as an old tree stump or a lying trunk, harbors beetle larvae that woodpeckers and nuthatches like to eat.

### Nettles

A corner with nettles is a nursery for butterfly caterpillars such as peacock butterflies and little tortoiseshells and is therefore a food source for tits.

## Step 4: Offer water

Water is just as important to birds as food. A simple shallow bowl is enough, a natural garden pond with a flat bank is even better.

- **Bird bath:** flat, with a rough surface, fresh water daily. Explained in detail in the guide [bird bath and bird bath] (/vogelwissen/vogeltraenke-vogelbad/).
- **Garden pond:** with a shallow water zone, stones and bankside plants. It attracts insects and serves as a bath and watering place for birds.
- **Cover rain barrels** to prevent birds and hedgehogs from falling in.

## Step 5: Create nesting areas

In addition to dense hedges for outdoor breeders, cave and niche breeders need suitable places.

- **Nesting boxes** for tits, nuthatches and sparrows. The guide [Build your own nesting box](/vogelwissen/nistkasten-bauen-anleitung/) provides instructions and explains the right place [Hang the nesting box correctly](/vogelwissen/nistkasten-richtig-aufhaengen/).
- **Half caves** for robins, black redstarts and spotted flycatchers.
- **Ivy and Virginia creeper** on walls and facades provide nesting places and cover in winter. Ivy blooms late in the year and bears berries in the winter.
- **Old trees with caves** should be preserved if possible, as long as they are secure.
- **Nesting material:** In spring you can offer animal hair, undyed sheep's wool, moss and short natural fibers in a basket. And a small, moist puddle of clay helps swallows build their nests.

## Step 6: Avoid poison

Insecticides, slug pellets and weed killers destroy the birds' food supply and can directly poison them. A bird-friendly garden does not require poison.

- **Aphids** are decimated by tits, sparrows, ladybirds and lacewings if you give them time.
- The song thrush eats **snails**, as well as hedgehogs, toads and ground beetles.
- **Caterpillars** on fruit trees are the main food for tit young. A pair of tits with a nest in the garden is the best biological pest control.

## Step 7: Defuse hazards

A bird-friendly garden is also a safe garden.

- **Glass panes:** Large windows, winter gardens and glass balcony railings are deadly traps. The guide [Prevent bird strikes on glass panes] (/vogelwissen/vogelschlag-glasscheiben-verhindern/) explains how you can make them visible.
- **Cats:** Thorny bushes around nesting areas, cat repellent belts on trees and sensibly placed feeding places help. More in the guide [Cats and birds in the garden] (/vogelwissen/katzen-und-voegel-im-garten/).
- **Nets:** Fruit and berry nets can catch birds. If you use them, keep them tight and check them daily.
- Do not leave the **robot lawn mower** running at night because hedgehogs and other animals can be injured.

## A care calendar for the bird garden

| period | What to do |
|---|---|
| January–February | Maintain feeding area and water, hang new nesting boxes, last heavy pruning by the end of February |
| March–April | Offer nesting material, clay puddles for swallows, only trim hedges carefully, sow wildflowers |
| May–June | Allow lawns to partially grow, leave breeding areas alone, water in hot weather |
| July–August | Change the water daily and leave the seeds standing |
| September | Leave the berries hanging, make planting plans for fall |
| October–November | Clean nesting boxes, plant hedges and bushes, rake leaves under bushes, create piles of brushwood |
| December | Winter feeding, keep water ice-free, leave seed heads for food |

## Small areas and balcony

Even if you only have a small garden or balcony, you can do a lot:

- **Balcony boxes with native flowering plants** such as bluebells, thyme, lavender as bee pasture or nasturtiums attract insects.
- **A small shrub in a container**, such as a serviceberry or a wild rose, provides berries.
- **A shallow water bowl** on the balcony is often used.
- **A small feed silo** in winter.
- **ivy or wild vine** on the facade if the landlord agrees.

## How quickly does this have an impact?

Some changes appear immediately. A bird bath is often visited after just a few days. A new hedge, on the other hand, needs a few years until it is dense enough to breed. Wildflower meadows develop over two to three years. Patience is worth it: the garden becomes more lively with each passing year. Many gardeners report that after a few years species appear that they have never seen before, such as goldfinches on the teasels, redstarts in the box or green woodpeckers on the unmown lawn.

## Example: a terraced house garden in three years

A typical terraced house garden with around 150 square meters of lawn and a thuja hedge on the fence shows what the redesign can look like in practice.

- **In the first autumn**, part of the thuja hedge is replaced by a mixed hedge of hawthorn, privet, dog rose and cornelian cherry and two nesting boxes are hung. The leaves remain under the new bushes and a pile of brushwood forms in one corner.
- **In the first spring**, a strip about one meter wide along the hedge is no longer mowed and is sown with a native wildflower mixture. A shallow trough is added.
- **In the second year** the wildflowers bloom, the hedge slowly closes, and a pair of blue tits nest in one of the boxes. Instead of cherry laurel, a rock pear now grows at the seat.
- **In the third year** the hedge is dense enough for blackbirds to breed in it. In autumn, goldfinches get seeds from the teasels, and fieldfares eat the rose hips.

The effort is manageable and the maintenance effort is even reduced because less mowing and cutting has to be done.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I have to redesign my entire garden?** No. Even a native hedge, a wildflower corner, a pile of brushwood and a watering trough make a big difference.

**Does a natural garden look messy?** It can be very beautiful. A good solution is to combine well-maintained areas such as paths and seating areas with wild corners.

**Does a garden close to nature attract rats and mosquitoes?** Rats mainly come where there is leftover food or a lot of food on the ground. Mosquitoes tend to be fewer in a garden with birds, bats, dragonflies and amphibians as long as there are no open water containers in which they can breed undisturbed.

## Conclusion

A bird-friendly garden primarily needs native plants, a little clutter, water, nesting places and no poison. Plant a mixed hedge of hawthorn, elderberry, wild roses and other native shrubs, let a piece of meadow bloom, rake leaves under the bushes, create a pile of brushwood and set up a watering trough. Defuse dangers from glass and cats and hang up suitable nesting boxes. This way you create a habitat where birds can find food, shelter and a home all year round.
