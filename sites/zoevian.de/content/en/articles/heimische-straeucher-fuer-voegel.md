If you want to help birds permanently, plant bushes. A single hawthorn provides flowers for insects in spring, nesting sites in its thorny branches in summer, and red fruit for blackbirds, thrushes, and waxwings in fall. Native shrubs are a source of food, nursery and shelter at the same time. Many exotic ornamental trees such as cherry laurel or thuja always look green, but are largely useless for our birds and insects.

This guide introduces you to 16 native shrubs that are particularly valuable for birds and shows you how to plant a bird hedge from them.

## Why native?

Native plants have evolved together with our insects over a very long time. Many caterpillars, beetles and aphids are specialized on certain native trees. These insects are the most important food for young birds, because almost all songbirds feed their nestlings insects, even if the parents eat grains themselves.

You will therefore find much more life on a native hedge than on a hedge made from exotic trees. There are also berries and fruits that ripen exactly when migratory birds need energy for the journey or when resident birds need to get through the winter.

### Local trees

Even better than “native” is **local**: plants grown from seeds from your own region. They are adapted to the climate and soil and adapt to local insects. Many tree nurseries and nature conservation associations offer such trees.

## The stars in portrait

### Hawthorn: the all-rounder

Hardly any shrub is as valuable as the hawthorn. Its flowers in May attract numerous insects, many species of caterpillars live on its leaves, the thorny branches offer safe nesting places, and the red fruits often hang until late in winter. It is suitable for free-growing hedges as well as for cut hedges.

### Elderberry: concentrated feed for migratory birds

In late summer, when warblers, robins and starlings build up reserves for migration, elderberries are one of the most important food sources. One abundant elder bush can attract dozens of birds on some days. Elderberry grows quickly and tolerates almost any soil.

### Rowan: the rowan tree

The name itself gives it away: the rowan berries are eaten by many species of birds. It is a small tree that can also be found in gardens and offers a colorful picture in autumn. The berries are not recommended raw for humans, but are a feast for birds.

### Blackthorn: thorns as protection

The blackthorn is one of the first bushes to bloom in the year, before the leaves emerge, making it an early pasture for insects. Their dense, thorny branches are ideal nesting places for red-backed shrikes, yellowhammers and warblers. It forms runners and is therefore more suitable for larger gardens or field hedges.

### Ivy: winter food and hiding place

Ivy blooms late in the year and is one of the last sources of nectar for insects. Its berries only ripen in late winter, when other fruits have long since been eaten. Densely overgrown walls and trunks provide nesting places for blackbirds, robins and wrens and places to sleep in winter. Ivy generally does not harm healthy trees.

## The year in the bird hedge

A well-mixed hedge offers something in every season:

- **Late winter:** Cornelian cherry, hazel and willow are the first to bloom and feed early insects. Ivy berries ripen and provide food for blackbirds and wood pigeons when there is little else left.
- **Spring:** Blackthorn and hawthorn bloom white, aphids and caterpillars appear in large numbers, just when titmice and sparrows are feeding their young.
- **Summer:** Serviceberry and blackberry bear fruit, warblers and blackbirds breed their second brood in the dense branches.
- **Autumn:** Elderberry, mountain ash, dogwood and pine tree provide energy for migrating birds.
- **Winter:** Rose hips, sloes, privet and viburnum berries hang for a long time and help thrushes, bullfinches and waxwings through the cold season.

## Plant a bird hedge

### Planning

- **Mixture:** Plant at least five to seven different species. This means that the food supply is distributed throughout the year.
- **Plan for thorny species:** Hawthorn, blackthorn, wild roses and barberry protect nests from cats and magpies.
- **Stagger heights:** Tall shrubs such as elderberry and mountain ash in the middle or to the back, low ones such as serviceberry and wild roses to the front.
- **Width:** A free-growing hedge needs two to three meters wide. If you have less space, choose species that are easy to cut, such as privet, hornbeam and hawthorn.

### Planting time

The best time to plant is **autumn, from October to November**, when the plants have shed their leaves. Planting can also be done in early spring. **Bare-rooted shrubs** are cheaper than container-grown plants and grow well when planted during the dormant season.

### Planting distance

- in a row about 1 to 1.5 meters between plants
- When planted in two rows, the rows are staggered about one meter apart
- Observe the legal boundary distances of the federal state to neighboring properties

### Planting step by step

1. Dig a planting hole about twice the size of the root ball.
2. Slightly shorten the roots of bare-root plants and remove damaged roots.
3. Insert the plant, fill in the soil, press down lightly.
4. Pour in vigorously.
5. Mulch with leaves or grass clippings to retain moisture and suppress competition.
6. Water in dry conditions during the first two summers.

## Care

- **Cut little:** A free-growing hedge is only thinned out in sections every few years, never all at once. This means that parts are always left as a nesting place and source of food.
- **Note cutting times:** According to the Federal Nature Conservation Act, heavy cuts are only permitted from **1. October until February 28th or 29th** allowed. During the breeding season, only gentle shaping and care cuts are permitted, and even then no nest may be damaged.
- **Leave fruit hanging:** Don't harvest all the berries. What remains on the bush is winter food.
- **Leave leaves under the hedge:** It nourishes the soil and provides a habitat for insects.

## Exotics that you can do without

| woody plant | problem | Home alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Cherry laurel | little use for insects, spreads in forests | Privet, yew, holly |
| Thuja (tree of life) | hardly usable for insects and birds | Hornbeam, field maple, yew |
| Forsythia | Flowers without significant nectar | Cornelian cherry, willow |
| Bamboo | invasive, with no benefit to local animals | Hazelnut, dogwood |

If you already have exotic plants in your garden, you don't have to remove them immediately. Gradually supplement them with native shrubs and replace them when renewal is due anyway.

## Small gardens and balconies

Something is possible even in a small area:

- **Rock pear, wild roses or cornelian cherry** in a large bucket
- **Ivy or wild vine** on a wall
- **currants and gooseberries** are fruiting bushes that birds also appreciate
- a **hawthorn hedge** as a narrow boundary

## And if there is room for a tree?

Trees ideally complement a bird hedge. They serve as singing stations, provide caves for woodpeckers and tits and are home to an enormous number of insects. The following are particularly suitable for gardens:

- **Field maple:** remains relatively small, tolerates pruning and is an important insect tree.
- **Hornbeam:** as a tree or dense hedge that keeps the leaves for a long time and therefore provides cover even in winter.
- **Bird cherry:** Blossoms in April, cherries in summer, which blackbirds, starlings and orioles love.
- **Wild apple and wild pear:** Fallen fruit in autumn and winter.
- **Sal willow:** one of the earliest bee pastures of the year, where many insects buzz and tits hunt in spring.
- **Pedunculate Oak:** for very large gardens, it hosts more species of insects than almost any other native tree.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are poisonous shrubs dangerous to birds?** No. Plants such as Pfaffenhütchen, privet or ivy are poisonous to humans, but their fruits are easily eaten by birds. Anyone who has small children plants them where there is no play and explains that berries in the garden are not eaten.

**How ​​long does it take for a hedge to bear fruit?** Many shrubs bear their first fruit two to three years after planting. A hedge is usually dense enough for breeding after three to five years.

**Can I harvest the berries myself?** Elderberry, blackthorn, rose hip and cornelian cherry can be processed. But only harvest a part and leave the rest hanging for the birds.

## Conclusion

Native shrubs are the most effective long-term help for birds in the garden. Hawthorn, elderberry, rowan, blackthorn, wild rose, dogwood, privet, viburnum and ivy provide insects, nesting sites, cover and berries throughout the year. Plant a mixed hedge with thorny species, preferably in autumn and from local sources, cut it rarely and in sections and let the fruit hang. After a few years you will see how much life such a hedge brings to the garden.
