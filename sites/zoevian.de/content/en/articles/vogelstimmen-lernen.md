Anyone who opens the window at five in the morning in May will hear a concert of many voices. For most people, it's a beautiful but inscrutable mess. However, if you know a few voices, you will suddenly hear individual singers: the whistling blackbird on the roof ridge, the blaring song of the chaffinch, the tireless "chiff-chap" from the maple tree, the loud trill of the tiny wren. Knowing bird calls is like learning a new language. And like any language, it's best learned step by step.

This guide will show you how to learn bird songs, which species to start with, which mnemonics help and how to use apps sensibly.

## Why bird sounds are so important

Many birds are heard before you see them. In leafy forests, in dense hedges or at dusk, hearing is often the observer's most important sense organ. Some species can even be reliably distinguished by their voice alone, such as chiffchaffs and fitis, marsh and willow tits or garden and forest treecreepers. If you know the voices, you will also discover many more birds and know where to look.

## Singing and shouting

Birds have two types of vocalizations:

- **Song:** a longer, often complex sequence of sounds that males in particular use to mark their territory and attract females. You can hear singing mainly in spring and early summer.
- **Calls:** short sounds that occur all year round, for example contact calls in a group, warning calls in case of danger, begging calls from young or migration calls at night.

At the beginning it is worth learning the **songs** of the most common species. Later the calls come along, which are often even more helpful because you hear them all year round.

## The ten most important voices to start with

| Art | How it sounds | Mnemonic bridge | When to hear |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Great Tit** | bright, two-syllable, repeated motif | “zi-zi-ba, zi-zi-ba” | from January |
| **blackbird** | calm, flute, melodic, without repetitions | “the flute on the roof” | February to July, especially at dusk |
| **Chaffinch** | blaring verse, gets faster, ends with a flourish | “Am, am, am I not a handsome groom?” | March to July |
| **Robin** | sparkling, sloping, like a small waterfall | “Beads Rolling Down a Staircase” | almost all year round, even in winter |
| **Chiffchaff** | two tones alternating, monotonous | “zilp-zalp-zilp-zalp” | March to September |
| **Wren** | surprisingly loud, blaring, with trills | “the smallest bird with the loudest voice” | almost all year round |
| **House Sparrow** | simple chitting | “tchilp, tchilp” | all year round |
| **Blackcap** | quiet chatter, then a loud whistle at the end | “the song with the rollover” | April to July |
| **black redstart** | scratchy, pressed, like crumpled paper | “crunching paper in the morning” | March to October, often before dusk |
| **wood pigeon** | dull, five-syllable cooing | “Gru-guuh-gu, gru-gu” | February to September |

### A few more classics

- **Goldammer:** “How, how, how I loved you” with a drawn out final note, especially in an open landscape with hedges.
- **Singing Thrush:** loud, changing motifs, each repeated two to three times.
- **Nuthatch:** loud, whistling rows like “tüi-tüi-tüi”.
- **Greenfinch:** drawled, nasal “dschuuuii”.
- **Great Spotted Woodpecker:** short, hard “kix” and rapid drumming in spring.
- **Cuckoo:** of course “ku-kuck”, from mid-April.

## This is how you do it

### 1. Start small

Don't learn twenty voices at once. Start with **three to five species** that are common in your garden or on your commute. When you recognize these with certainty, add the next three.

### 2. Combine seeing and hearing

The best way to learn a voice is to see the singer doing it. Follow the song until you spot the bird and watch it sing. This connection between image and sound is particularly memorable.

### 3. Listen actively

Describe to yourself what you hear: Is it high or low? Melodic or scratchy? Does the bird repeat motifs? Is the verse going faster or slower? How long is it? Such descriptions help with remembering and comparing with recordings.

### 4. Compare recordings

Listen to the songs of the species you want to learn at home, for example with a bird song CD, a book with sound samples, or an app. Hear similar species right behind each other, such as Blackbirds and Song Thrushes or Chiffchaffs and Fitis.

### 5. Repeat

Do the same round regularly, ideally several times a week. So you hear the same birds over and over again and after a while you recognize them without thinking.

### 6. Learn with others

Many nature conservation groups offer **bird song hikes** in the spring, often early in the morning. With an experienced companion, you will learn more in two hours than in weeks alone.

## Understanding the morning concert

In the spring, birds begin singing in a typical order depending on how sensitive they are to light. Roughly:

1. **Black redstart** and **robins** often begin in deep twilight, long before sunrise.
2. **Blackbird** and **Singing Thrush** follow shortly afterwards.
3. **wren**, **great tit** and **wood pigeon** join in.
4. **Chaffinch**, **Chiffchaff** and **Blackcap** will be added a little later.
5. **House Sparrow** and **Starling** only become really active when it is light.

The exact order varies with weather, season and location, but anyone who gets up early for a week will recognize the pattern. The morning concert is busiest in **April and May**.

## When is the best time?

- **Season:** March to June is peak singing season. In winter only a few species sing, especially robins and wrens, and towards the end of winter great tits, blackbirds and mistle thrushes join in.
- **Time of day:** The first hour before and after sunrise is the most productive. Dusk is also beautiful, especially for blackbirds, song thrushes and robins.
- **Weather:** Calm winds, dry mornings are ideal. In strong winds and rain, birds sing less and are harder to hear.

## Use apps correctly

Bird song recognition apps that analyze a recording on your smartphone are now surprisingly good. Well-known examples are Merlin Bird ID and BirdNET. They can help you classify unknown voices. A few notes:

- **Take this as a suggestion:** Apps make mistakes, especially with background noise, multiple birds singing at the same time, impersonators like the starling, or rare species.
- **Check:** Compare the suggestion to a reference shot and, if possible, to the bird you see.
- **Don't let it be replaced:** If you determine every voice immediately via the app, you won't learn it yourself. Try to guess yourself first and use the app to check.
- **Be Considerate:** Do not play bird songs loudly outdoors to attract birds, especially during breeding season. This disturbs the animals and can prevent them from feeding their young.

## A four-week plan for spring

- **Week 1 (early March):** Great tit, blackbird and robin. These three sing early in the year and can be heard almost everywhere. Go into the garden or balcony for ten minutes every morning.
- **Week 2:** Add wren, house sparrow and wood pigeon. Try to spot each singer as they sing.
- **Week 3 (end of March):** The first migratory birds arrive: Chiffchaff and Black Redstart. Plus the chaffinch, which is now really getting going.
- **Week 4 (April):** Blackcap and Song Thrush. Listen consciously to the blackbird and song thrush next to each other.

After four weeks you will know twelve of the most common voices in gardens and parks and recognize a large part of the morning concert. In May you can continue with Fitis, Garden Warbler, Girlitz and Greenfinch.

## Bird sounds and health

Many people find listening to birdsong relaxing. Studies suggest that environmental bird sounds may be associated with better well-being. Whether that's true or not, ten minutes in the morning with a cup of tea by the open window and trying to name every singer is a nice way to start the day.

## Typical stumbling blocks

### copycat

Some birds imitate others. The **star** incorporates calls from buzzards, orioles, lapwings and even cell phone ringtones into his song. The **Jay** imitates the common buzzard. The **Swamp Warbler** copies dozens of species. If you hear a “buzzard” in the middle of the city, it might be a starling.

### Regional dialects

Like humans, birds also have dialects. Chaffinches in different regions sing slightly different final flourishes, yellowhammers vary their song. This makes learning exciting, but also a little more difficult.

### Similar sounding species

- **Blackbird and song thrush:** The song thrush repeats its motifs, the blackbird does not.
- **Chiffchaff and Fitis:** two alternating tones for the Chiffchaff, a softly descending verse for the Fitis.
- **Great Tit and Nuthatch:** Both have clear, repeated sounds. The great tit alternates between two tones, the nuthatch usually repeats the same whistle in quick succession.

## Exercise ideas

- **Voice Diary:** Write down what types you hear every morning. After a month you will see how the concert grows in the spring.
- **Five Minute Count:** Stand in one place for five minutes and count all the species you hear. This is also the basis of many scientific bird counts, see [Participating bird counts](/vogelwissen/vogelzaehlung-mitmachen/).
- **Close your eyes:** Sit in the garden, close your eyes and try to determine the direction and style of each singer.
- **Calls in Fall:** Watch for migratory calls at night in October, such as the thin “pull” of the redwing. More about this in the guide [Migratory birds and winter guests] (/vogelwissen/zugvoegel-und-wintergaeste/).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do females also sing?** In many species, it is primarily the males who sing. But there are exceptions, such as the robin, where females also sing in autumn and winter.

## Conclusion

The best way to learn bird songs is step by step: start with a few common species, see and hear singers, actively describe them, compare recordings and regularly walk the same circuit. Mnemonics like “zi-zi-bä” or “How, how, how I love you” help with memorization, apps are useful control aids, but do not replace your own learning. Anyone who starts in March will hear a concert in May in which every singer has a name.
