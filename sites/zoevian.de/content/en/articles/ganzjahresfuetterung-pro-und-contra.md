Hardly any question is discussed as passionately among bird lovers as this one: Should you only feed birds in winter or all year round? For a long time, the rule in Germany was that the feeding place was closed in spring so that the birds could raise their young on natural food. Some well-known ornithologists and several bird protection organizations abroad now expressly recommend feeding in summer too. Others remain skeptical.

In this guide, we present both sides fairly, explain what we know about the benefits and risks, and show what summer feeding should look like if you decide to do so.

## Why the question even comes up

In the past, birds in villages and in the countryside found plenty of food all year round: wild herbs with seeds on roadsides and field edges, insects over flowering meadows, spilled grain on farms. Today many landscapes have been cleared out. Hedges are missing, meadows are mowed early and often, pesticides reduce the number of insects, and gardens are often landscaped with lawn, cherry laurel and gravel. This has led many observers to wonder whether birds need help outside of winter, too.

## The arguments for year-round feeding

### Lack of food is not just a winter problem

Proponents point out that the time of greatest need for food is not winter, but the **breeding season**. A pair of tits with ten nestlings has to bring in thousands of caterpillars in three weeks. If the parents quickly find energy-rich food themselves, they have more time and energy to collect insects for the young.

### The attitude of well-known experts

One of the best-known supporters in Germany is the ornithologist Peter Berthold, who headed the Radolfzell bird observatory for a long time. He has been promoting year-round feeding for years to compensate for the loss of natural food sources. In the UK, major bird conservation organizations have long recommended feeding birds all year round, and summer feeding is widespread there.

### Evidence from research

Studies, especially from Great Britain, suggest that fed birds sometimes breed earlier, are in better condition and in some cases raise more young. However, the results are not uniform and depend on species, region and feed.

### Education and nature experience

Those who feed all year round experience more: families with begging young birds, the moulting, the coming and going of migratory birds. This strengthens the connection to nature, especially for children.

## The arguments against year-round feeding

### Diseases at feeding places

Where many birds meet in a small space, pathogens spread more easily. In summer, when it is warm and humid, bacteria and parasites multiply more quickly in food and water. A well-known example is **trichomonasis**, a parasitic disease that primarily affects greenfinches and is transmitted via contaminated food and water. **Salmonella** can also occur frequently in unclean feeding places. The guide [Keeping the feeding area clean] (/vogelwissen/futterstelle-hygiene-krankheiten/) explains how to prevent this.

### Wrong food for nestlings

Young songbirds need soft, protein-rich food, especially insects, in the first days of life. If parents feed hard kernels or **whole peanuts** to their young, they can choke or have digestive problems. Therefore, whole nuts and large pieces of food are strongly discouraged during the breeding season.

### Only a few species benefit

Common and adaptable species are the most common at feeding sites. Endangered birds of the open landscape such as skylarks, partridges and lapwings do not benefit. Critics also worry that feeding could shift competition between species, for example in favor of great tits over smaller or shyer species.

### Robbers are attracted

A busy feeder attracts not only songbirds, but also sparrowhawks, cats, magpies and rats. During the breeding season, this can endanger nearby nests.

### The danger of overlooking the real problem

A fundamental objection is that feeding stations soothe the conscience, but do not change the loss of habitats. More important than sunflower seeds in July are local hedges, flowering meadows and gardens without poison.

## What does science say at the end of the day?

The honest answer is: **According to current knowledge, responsible year-round feeding does not harm the common garden birds and can help them in food-poor areas.** The decisive factor is how they are fed. However, poorly done feeding with dirty feeding places and unsuitable food can be harmful. And no amount of feeding replaces good habitat. Many nature conservation associations in Germany therefore take a middle line: If you want to feed all year round, you can do so, but you should adhere to strict hygiene rules and at the same time design the garden in a natural way.

## Pros and cons at a glance

| For year-round feeding | Against year-round feeding |
|---|---|
| natural food is scarce in many places | increased risk of illness, especially in warm weather |
| Parents save energy for hunting insects | Unsuitable food can harm nestlings |
| Studies show some improved condition and breeding success | Common species in particular benefit |
| intensive nature experience all year round | attracts robbers and rats |
| internationally widespread practice | distracts from protecting habitats |

## This is how to ensure safe summer feeding

If you decide to feed all year round, follow these rules:

### 1. Hygiene comes first

- Use **feed silos** in which the feed stays dry and does not go to waste.
- In the summer, clean feeding areas thoroughly with hot water **at least once a week**, more often if there are many visitors.
- Only give as much food as will be eaten in one day. Remove wet or clumped food immediately.
- Change the water in troughs daily.

### 2. The right food

- **Peeled sunflower seeds** and high-quality, ambrosia-free grain mixtures.
- **Peanuts only chopped** or in a narrow-meshed dispenser from which whole nuts cannot be removed.
- **Soft food** and dried insects, preferably soaked.
- **No fatty food in heat:** It becomes soft, can become rancid and sticks feathers. In summer it is better to avoid it or use special heat-resistant products and only offer small quantities.
- **No leftovers, no bread.**

### 3. Small amounts, multiple locations

Instead of one large feeding place, it is better to have several small ones at some distance. This way the birds spread out and disease has less of a chance.

### 4. If you are sick, take a break immediately

If you see fluffy, apathetic birds that are unlikely to escape, or greenfinches with sticky beaks and food residue on their faces, stop feeding for at least two to four weeks and clean everything thoroughly. If there are frequent cases, inform a wild bird station or a nature conservation association.

### 5. Do not place food directly next to nests

During the breeding season, keep feeding stations a few meters away from nest boxes and hedges with nests. This way you avoid territorial fights and don't attract nest robbers directly to the nests.

## The best year-round feeding: a natural garden

No matter what you decide: the most valuable food for birds grows in the garden itself.

- **Native shrubs** such as hawthorn, elderberry, blackthorn and dogwood provide insects in spring and berries in autumn. You can find a selection in the guide [Native shrubs for birds] (/vogelwissen/heimische-straeucher-fuer-voegel/).
- **Wildflowers and herbs** attract insects and provide seeds in late summer.
- **Piles of leaves and dead wood** are home to beetles, woodlice and larvae.
- **No poison:** Any aphid that is not treated with spray is potential tit food.

The guide [Creating a bird-friendly garden] (/vogelwissen/vogelfreundlicher-garten-anlegen/) describes how you can design a bird-friendly garden step by step.

## What about winter?

The well-known rules apply to winter feeding: best from November to February, reliably and hygienically, with energy-rich food such as sunflower seeds and fatty food. The guide [Feeding birds correctly] (/vogelwissen/voegel-fuettern-grundlagen/) provides a complete overview.

## A decision-making aid for your garden

Not every garden is the same. These questions will help you make a decision that suits your situation:

1. **What does the environment look like?** If you live on the edge of a cleared agricultural landscape or in a new development area with little greenery, the natural food supply is often scarce. Moderate summer feeding can then make sense. In an old residential area with large trees, hedges and meadows it is less necessary.
2. **Do you have time for hygiene?** Summer feeding means weekly cleaning, more frequently in mid-summer. If this is not possible in everyday life, pure winter feeding is the better choice.
3. **Are there cats in the neighborhood?** Many cats and a busy feeding area during the breeding season are an unfavorable combination because nearby branches are particularly at risk.
4. **What can you change about the garden itself?** If you can only invest part of your energy, a native hedge or a wildflower corner will bring more in the long term than a full feed silo in July.

### A possible annual plan

- **November to February:** full winter feeding with sunflower seeds, fatty food, soft food and water.
- **March to June:** Small amounts of peeled kernels and chopped nuts in the silo, no fatty food when it is warm, weekly cleaning, fresh water every day.
- **July to October:** Reduce feeding to a minimum or pause, leave seeds and berries in the garden, continue to operate the watering hole.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is summer feeding prohibited?** No. There is no general ban on feeding garden birds in Germany. In some cities, however, feeding pigeons or waterfowl is prohibited.

**Do birds get lazy if I feed in summer?** No. Birds use feeders as a supplement. Insects remain essential to raising young, and parents continue to seek them out.

**Which birds come to the food in summer?** Especially great and blue tits, sparrows, greenfinches, chaffinches and starlings, often with their young. Many soft feeders such as robins find enough insects in summer and come less frequently.

**How ​​do I know that there is too much?** If food is left lying around, moldy or rats appear, the amount is too large. Then reduce and clean thoroughly.

## Conclusion

Whether all year round or just in winter: both ways are acceptable if you take them responsibly. Year-round feeding can help common garden birds, but carries risks from disease and unsuitable food, especially in summer. These risks can be greatly reduced with clean silos, peeled kernels, chopped nuts, small quantities and fresh water. However, the most lasting help remains a garden in which birds find their own food.
