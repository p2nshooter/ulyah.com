How are the sparrows doing in Germany? Are blackbirds becoming fewer? Are the swifts coming back sooner? Such questions can only be answered if many people observe and count over many years. Experts alone cannot do this. That's why bird lovers all over Germany are invited to take part, from counting once in their own garden to regularly recording fixed routes.

This guide introduces the most popular participation activities, explains how to count correctly, and shows what happens to your data.

## Why counting is important

Birds are good indicators of the state of our environment. When insects disappear, hedges are missing or diseases appear, you often notice it first in the birds. But in order to recognize changes, you need data: many observations, in many places, over many years.

This type of research in which citizens collect data is called **citizen science** or **citizen science**. It has a long tradition in ornithology. Many findings, such as the decline in farmland birds, the spread of the Usutu virus in blackbirds or the consequences of trichomoniasis in greenfinches, would hardly have been possible without the help of volunteers.

## Hour of winter birds

The **Hour of the Winter Birds** is a large participatory event by nature conservation associations that takes place every year on a weekend at the beginning of January.

- **What:** Count all the birds you see in your yard, balcony, window, or park for an hour.
- **Who:** Anyone who would like to take part, without prior knowledge. Also children and school classes.
- **Aim:** An overview of which birds live in gardens and settlements in winter and how this changes over the years.

Typical results show, for example, how much winter guests such as bramblings and waxwings vary from year to year and how mild winters affect the number of overwintering species. The guide [Migratory Birds and Winter Visitors](/vogelwissen/zugvoegel-und-wintergaeste/) describes which species you can expect in winter.

## Garden bird hour

The **Hour of the Garden Birds** takes place every year in **May**, usually on the second weekend in May.

- **What:** Count for one hour which birds live in gardens, parks and settlements during the breeding season.
- **Special feature:** In May the summer birds such as swifts, swallows and warblers are back. The results therefore show a different picture than in winter.
- **Goal:** Identify long-term trends in garden birds.

For many years in a row, house sparrow, blackbird, great tit, starling and blue tit were among the most commonly reported species. The guide [Recognizing the most common garden birds] (/vogelwissen/haeufigste-gartenvoegel-erkennen/) shows you how to recognize them safely.

## This is how you count correctly

There are simple rules to ensure that the data is comparable. The organizers publish the exact participation instructions every year, but the basic rule is:

1. **One hour, one place:** Find an observation spot and stay there for the entire hour.
2. **Record maximum number:** For each species, record the **highest number of birds you saw at the same time**. If you first see three great tits, then two, and then four, write down **four**, not nine. This way you avoid counting the same birds multiple times.
3. **Only certain determinations:** If you don't know for sure, leave it out or write it down as "undetermined" if the form says so.
4. **Even zero counts:** If you hardly see any birds for an hour, that is also important information. Please report anyway.
5. **Report:** You enter the results online, via the app or in other ways offered by the organizers, with the location and date.

## Preparing for count day

- **Set up feeders and waterers** well in advance in winter so that the birds know the place.
- **Have an identification guide ready:** Organizers often offer counting cards with pictures of the most common species.
- **Keep binoculars, pen and list** handy.
- **Choose a time:** There are usually more birds active in the morning than in the afternoon.
- **Create peace:** Keep pets indoors, don't run around in the garden all the time.

## Reporting platforms for the whole year

If you don't just want to count once a year, you can enter observations at any time on **online reporting platforms**. The portal **ornitho.de**, which is operated by specialist ornithological associations, is widespread in Germany. There you can report observations with the exact location, number and behavior. International platforms are also used.

Advantages:

- Your observations are incorporated into distribution maps, atlases and scientific evaluations.
- You can manage your own observations for years.
- You'll see what others are observing around you and learn where rare species can be seen.

For sensitive species such as breeding owls, birds of prey or rarities, the exact locations are often automatically hidden to avoid disturbance. Please also be careful not to share such information publicly on social media.

## Scientific monitoring programs

For advanced users, there are long-term recording programs that work according to fixed methods:

- **Monitoring of common breeding birds:** Volunteers walk a fixed route several times each spring and record all breeding birds. The official inventory trends for Germany are calculated from this data.
- **Waterbird counting:** In winter, waterbirds on lakes, rivers and coasts are counted on fixed dates.
- **Special recordings:** for example for white storks, cranes, owls or building nesters.

These programs require a good knowledge of the species, especially the voices, and the willingness to stick with them for years. Anyone who is interested can contact the ornithological associations in their state. Many offer introductions.

## EuroBirdwatch and other actions

On the **first weekend in October** the **EuroBirdwatch** takes place in many European countries. Nature conservation associations invite you to observe bird migration, often with spotting scopes and experts on site. There are also regional campaigns, such as counting swifts, swallow nests or cranes. You can find information about this from the nature conservation associations in your region.

## Report ring finds

If you find a **ringed bird**, dead or alive, or can read a ring with binoculars, report the number to the bird observatory listed on the ring. Any feedback helps to better understand migration routes, lifespan and settlement behavior. You will usually receive feedback on where and when the bird was ringed.

## What happens to your data

The results of the participation activities are evaluated by the organizers and usually published a few weeks later. For example, they show:

- **Trends:** Which species are increasing and which are decreasing?
- **Regional differences:** Where are sparrows common and where rare?
- **Anomalies:** Where did diseases appear, where were certain species suddenly missing?

Data from simple garden counts are not as statistically accurate as those from structured monitoring programs because participants and locations change from year to year. Due to the large number of reports, they still provide valuable information and draw attention to changes that can then be specifically investigated.

## Counting with children and school classes

Bird counts are ideal for families, daycare centers and schools. There are often separate materials, counting cards and activities for children. Children learn about species in a playful way and experience that their observations count for science. The guide [Bird watching for beginners] (/vogelwissen/vogelbeobachtung-fuer-einsteiger/) also gives tips for getting started with children.

## Typical errors when counting

Even experienced participants sometimes make the same mistakes. These tips will make your data even more reliable:

- **Totaling instead of maximum number:** If you add up all the sightings, you count the same birds multiple times. Especially for tits and sparrows, which constantly come and go, the maximum number is the right method.
- **Confusions:** Female chaffinches are often reported as sparrows, young blackbirds as thrushes, and dunnocks as sparrows. If in doubt, leave it out. The guide [Mixed-up pairs in garden birds] (/vogelwissen/verwechslungspaare-gartenvoegel/) offers help.
- **Place and time not noted:** Without precise information, reports cannot be meaningfully evaluated.
- **Set up a fresh feeding place shortly beforehand:** If you set up a feeding place just for the counting day, you will change the result. It is better to have a feeding station that has existed for a few weeks, or none at all.
- **Only report special days:** Anyone who only reports when there were many or rare birds distorts the picture. Even a quiet day is valuable information.

## From counting to protecting

Many people who have taken part in a bird count stick with it. Some join a local group, look after nesting boxes, map swifts or look after an orchard. An hour at the window can turn into a lively commitment. The guide [Create a bird-friendly garden] (/vogelwissen/vogelfreundlicher-garten-anlegen/) provides ideas for your own garden.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I have to know all species to participate?** No. Only report what you know for sure. Even counting the ten most common species is a valuable help.

**What if there are hardly any birds on my counting day?** This is also important information. Please report your result anyway.

**Can I also count in the park?** Yes, many activities allow this. Choose a fixed spot and write it down.

**Can I count in multiple locations?** Yes, but each count is reported separately, with its own location and time. This way the data remains comparable.

**Do I need an account?** Usually not for the big participation campaigns. For reporting platforms that store your observations for years, you usually create a free user account.

**Do birds that only fly over count?** This is regulated by the participation instructions for the respective campaign. If in doubt, only count birds that are in your observation area.

## Conclusion

Bird counts are a simple way to turn your enjoyment of birds into a contribution to nature conservation. When the winter birds are in January and the garden birds are in May, an hour at the window is enough. It is important to note only the highest number seen at the same time for each species and to only report reliable determinations. If you want more, use reporting platforms or take part in monitoring programs. Every message helps to understand how our birds are doing.
