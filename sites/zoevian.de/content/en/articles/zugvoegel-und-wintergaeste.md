Every autumn, billions of birds leave Europe for the south, and every spring they return. The swifts above our roofs spend the winter in southern Africa, the cuckoo flies as far as the Congo, and some of the robins in our garden come from Sweden in the winter. Bird migration is one of nature's great wonders, and it happens right above our heads.

This guide explains which birds migrate and which stay, who comes to us in winter, how birds find their way and when you can best watch the migration.

## Why do birds migrate?

The most important reason is **food**. Many birds feed on insects that are rarely found in the Central European winter. Swifts and swallows, which only eat flying insects, would starve here. In the south they find enough food even in winter. It's worth returning in spring because the long days and the abundance of insects in the north offer ideal conditions for raising young.

The pull is innate. Young birds that have never migrated before know when to leave and in which direction. In many species the young even fly alone, without their parents.

## Train types: who goes, who stays?

### Long-distance migrants

They spend the winter south of the Sahara, in tropical or southern parts of Africa. These include:

- **Swift**, Barn Martin and House Martin
- **cuckoo**, oriole, nightingale, red-backed shrike
- **Redstart**, Pied Flycatcher, Fitis
- **White Stork** (some of the storks now overwinter in Spain)

You can find out more about swallows and swifts in the guide [Swallows and swifts at the house](/vogelwissen/schwalben-mauersegler-helfen/).

### Short-distance migrants

They only migrate to the Mediterranean, southwestern Europe or North Africa. These include:

- **Song Thrush**, Chiffchaff, Blackcap (partly)
- **black redstart**, wagtail, skylark
- **lapwing**, crane, lots of geese

### Partial puller

In these species, only some birds migrate, others stay. Females and young birds often migrate while males stay in the territory. Examples:

- **Blackbird**, **Robin**, **Chaffinch**, **Starling**, **Wood Pigeon**

More about this in the guides [The Blackbird in the Garden](/vogelwissen/amsel-im-garten/) and [Robin in the Garden](/vogelwissen/rotkehlchen-im-garten/).

### resident birds

They stay near their breeding site all year round:

- **House Sparrow**, Field Sparrow
- **Great tit**, blue tit (predominantly), nuthatch
- **Magpie**, carrion crow, jay (partly migrations)
- **Great Spotted Woodpecker**, Green Woodpecker

### Invasive birds

Some species do not come every year, but rather irregularly in large numbers when food becomes scarce in their homeland. People talk about **invasions**. The best-known invasive bird is the **waxwing**: In some winters, groups of these conspicuous, berry-hungry birds suddenly sit in mountain ash and mistletoe in the middle of inner cities, while in other years you don't see a single one. **Brazhins**, **Crossbills** and **Jays** also make invasive appearances in some years.

## How do birds find their way?

Birds use several orientation systems that complement each other:

- **Sun compass:** Diurnal migrants orientate themselves on the position of the sun and thereby compare the time of day with their internal clock.
- **Star compass:** As young birds, night migrants learn the pivot point of the starry sky around the Polar Star and use it as a guide.
- **Magnetic Sense:** Many birds can sense the Earth's magnetic field. Experiments with robins and other songbirds have shown that they adapt to this.
- **Landmarks:** Coastlines, rivers, mountains and places familiar to experienced birds serve as guideposts.
- **Smells:** In some species, such as seabirds, the sense of smell also seems to play a role.

Many small songbirds migrate **at night** when it is cooler, the air is quieter and birds of prey are not hunting. During the day they rest and eat. Larger birds such as storks and birds of prey migrate **during the day** and use thermals, rising warm air, to save energy.

## The big trips

- **The common swift** flies thousands of kilometers every year to southern Africa and spends almost all of its time in the air.
- **The cuckoo** migrates alone at night, including young birds that have never met their parents because they were raised by host birds.
- **The white stork** chooses two routes depending on its origin: western migrants fly via Gibraltar to West Africa, eastern migrants via the Bosporus and the Middle East to East and South Africa. They avoid long stretches over the open sea because there are no thermals there.
- **The crane** moves over Germany in large, loudly trumpeting wedge formations. In the fall, tens of thousands rest at large gathering points before moving on to France and Spain.

## How researchers examine the train

Much of what we know about bird migration comes from **ringing**. Birds get a light, numbered metal ring on their leg. If a ringed bird is later caught, observed or found dead in another location, its path can be traced. In Germany, three bird observatories coordinate ringing. Anyone who finds a dead ringed bird should report the ring number, location and date to the bird observatory indicated on the ring.

Today, modern methods are being added: tiny **gelocators** that record the length of the day and use this to calculate where you are, **GPS transmitters** for larger birds such as storks, cranes and cuckoos, some of whose journeys can be followed live on the Internet, and **radar devices** that make entire waves of migration across Europe visible at night. They show that on some autumn nights millions of birds migrate over Germany at the same time.

## Preparing for the trip

Before migrating, many birds eat up their reserves. Small songbirds like garden warblers can significantly increase their body weight in just a few weeks and create fat deposits that they burn during flight. Elderberry and blackberry berries are particularly important. At the same time, many species moult their plumage before migration so that they can leave with fresh feathers.

## What climate change is changing

Observers and researchers have been noticing changes for decades:

- **Earlier return:** Many short-distance migrants now return earlier in the year than they did a few decades ago.
- **Shorter migration routes:** Some species increasingly overwinter closer to their breeding grounds. A well-known example is the blackcap: some of the Central European birds no longer migrate to the southwest, but to the northwest to the British Isles, where the winters are mild and many people feed. This change was researched at the Radolfzell Bird Observatory, among others.
- **More winterers:** White storks, black redstarts, chiffchaffs and starlings stay more often in Germany even in winter.
- **Time mismatch:** Long-distance migrants who base their departure time in Africa on the length of the day sometimes arrive too late when the caterpillar time in our country has moved forward.

## Dangers on the train

The train is dangerous. Many birds don't survive it. In addition to natural dangers such as storms, cold snaps and crossing the Sahara and the Mediterranean, there are man-made ones:

- **Loss of habitat** at rest areas, for example due to the draining of wetlands
- **Illegal hunting and bird trapping**, especially in the Mediterranean
- **Collisions** with lighted buildings, glass, power lines and wind turbines
- **Droughts** and changes in African wintering areas

## Watch the bird migration

### When?

| time | What happens |
|---|---|
| February–March | first returnees: cranes, starlings, skylarks, lapwings |
| April | Return of many songbirds, swallows, cuckoos |
| Beginning of May | Swifts, orioles, red-backed shrikes |
| End of July–August | Swifts and storks leave |
| September | Swallows, warblers, birds of prey migrate |
| October | Highlight of the autumn migration: cranes, thrushes, finches, geese |
| November | Winter guests have arrived |

### Where?

- On **coasts**, peninsulas and islands where migrating birds gather
- At **mountain passes** and mountain ridges that birds of prey and storks use
- On large **lakes and wetlands** with resting areas for waterfowl and cranes
- In your **own garden**, especially on clear October nights: If you stand quietly outside, you can hear the calls of migrating thrushes and robins

The guide [Bird watching in Germany: the best places] (/vogelwissen/vogelbeobachtung-orte-deutschland/) presents specific observation locations. On the first weekend in October, EuroBirdwatch takes place in many European countries, where nature conservation associations offer migratory bird watching for everyone in many places. The guide [Participatory bird counts] (/vogelwissen/vogelzaehlung-mitmachen/) describes how you can make your own observations available to research.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do birds know when to fly?** The length of the day is the main signal. An internal annual clock ensures that migratory restlessness also occurs in birds that live in captivity without natural light.

**How ​​high do migratory birds fly?** Most songbirds migrate at a height of a few hundred to two or three thousand meters. Some species cross mountains at even higher altitudes.

**Do birds fly the same path every year?** Many species use similar routes and even the same resting places. Experienced birds know their paths, young birds follow their innate direction and learn.

**Can I help migratory birds in the garden?** Yes. Berry bushes such as elderberry and mountain ash provide energy for the migration, a water trough helps with rest, and bird-proof windows protect against collisions.

## Conclusion

Birds migrate because food becomes scarce in winter. Long-distance migrants such as swifts and cuckoos fly to tropical Africa, short-distance migrants such as the song thrush only fly to the Mediterranean, and partial migrants such as blackbirds and robins sometimes stay here. In winter, guests come from the north, from bramblings to waxwings. Birds find their way using the sun, stars, magnetic field and landmarks, and climate change is changing their times and routes. If you look and listen carefully in spring and autumn, you will experience this natural wonder right on your doorstep.
