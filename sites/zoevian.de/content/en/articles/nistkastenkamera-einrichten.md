Watch live how a blue tit builds its nest out of moss, how ten tiny eggs lie in the nest, how naked chicks beg with open beaks and how they jump out into the open one after the other three weeks later: a nesting box camera makes this possible without you ever having to open the box. It is a highlight of spring for many families and a wonderful way to introduce children to nature.

To ensure that the camera doesn't harm the birds, it's all about the right technology, the right time and a few clear rules. This guide explains everything step by step.

## The most important rule: remain trouble-free

The camera must not disturb the birds with light, heat, noise or by handling the box. This means:

- **Installation only outside of the breeding season**, preferably in autumn or winter.
- **No visible light** in the box. Infrared lighting is not or barely visible to most birds.
- **No work on the box** once birds have moved in. If the camera fails during breeding, it stays off.
- **No intervention**, even if something sad happens in the nest.

## Which camera is suitable?

| Type | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Wi-Fi camera** | Easy setup, picture on your smartphone | needs power supply and good WiFi on the box |
| **Network camera with PoE** (power via network cable) | stable image, power and data via one cable | Cable must be laid |
| **Camera with cable and monitor** | independent of the Internet, robust | Cable, own screen required |
| **Battery or solar camera** | no cable | limited running time, solar module needs sun |

**PoE network cameras** are particularly reliable for a fixed location near the house. If you don't want to lay a cable, use a **Wi-Fi camera** with a power supply if a socket is accessible.

### Important properties

- **Close-up:** The camera must be able to focus at around 10 to 20 centimeters. Many normal surveillance cameras are set for longer distances. Cameras that are specifically offered for nesting boxes have suitable lenses.
- **Wide angle:** so that the entire nest is in the picture.
- **Infrared night vision:** Infrared LEDs with a wavelength of **940 nanometers** are also invisible to humans and are considered particularly inconspicuous. LEDs with 850 nanometers glow faintly reddish and are also barely noticeable to most birds.
- **Microphone:** With sound you can hear the begging of the chicks and the contact calls of the parents.
- **Weatherproof:** Cable entry and housing should be protected against moisture.

## The right box

A camera needs space. Many manufacturers offer **camera nesting boxes** that have a separate compartment at the top for the camera. Those who build themselves plan:

- **Internal dimensions** as with the normal tit box, approximately 12 × 12 centimeters base area.
- **Additional height** of a few centimeters in the roof for the camera so that the distance to the nest is sufficient.
- **An opening** in the intermediate base for the lens, possibly with a clear acrylic pane for protection.
- **A cable gland** that is sealed against rain.
- **Ventilation** to prevent heat from building up.

The entry hole depends on the species you want to observe: 26 to 28 millimeters for blue tits, 32 millimeters for great tits and nuthatches. More about this in the guides [Build your own nesting box](/vogelwissen/nistkasten-bauen-anleitung/) and [Types of nesting boxes and entry holes](/vogelwissen/nistkastentypen-einflugloch/).

## Installation step by step

1. **Choose time:** between October and February when there is no brood in the box.
2. **Test the camera in advance:** Set it up in the house or garage, check the image, sharpness and night vision.
3. **Mount the camera:** Attach it in the camera room, align the lens in the middle of the nesting room.
4. **Lay cable:** UV-resistant, well attached, without forming a climbing aid for cats. The cable should not run freely over a branch that a cat is using.
5. **Hang the box:** like a normal nesting box, at a height of two to three meters, facing east or southeast, see [Hanging the nesting box correctly](/vogelwissen/nistkasten-richtig-aufhaengen/).
6. **Check image:** from the outside without opening the box again.
7. **Done:** From now on the box will be left alone.

## Power and network

- **PoE:** A network cable supplies the camera with power and data. A PoE injector or a PoE-capable router in the house supplies the power. This is the most stable solution.
- **Wi-Fi with power supply:** The camera needs a socket within reach, ideally a protected outdoor socket. The WiFi connection on the box should be strong enough, otherwise the picture will stop.
- **Solar and battery:** For cameras with a solar module, the module must hang in the sun, but the box itself must hang in partial shade. The battery can become empty at night and in continuous rain.

For all solutions, pay attention to **safe outdoor electrical systems**, i.e. devices and plug connections for outdoor use and a residual current circuit breaker. If you are unsure, ask a qualified electrician.

## Privacy and security

- The camera should only look into the inside of the box, not onto neighboring properties or public paths.
- Change the camera's **default passwords** and keep the software up to date to prevent strangers from gaining access.
- When sharing a **livestream** publicly, do not reveal the exact location.

## What you will see

A typical tit year in the camera:

| period | What happens |
|---|---|
| Winter | Tits sometimes spend the night in the box |
| March–April | Exploration, first pieces of moss, nest building |
| April | Egg laying, usually one egg per day, covered with nesting material |
| April–May | Breeding, about two weeks, the female sits almost continuously |
| May | Hatch, the chicks are naked and blind |
| May–June | Feeding, countless approaches every day, the chicks grow rapidly, quills appear |
| after 18-21 days | Flying out, often within a few hours |

The guidebook [Great Tit and Blue Tit] (/vogelwissen/kohlmeise-und-blaumeise/) also describes how breeding occurs.

### If something goes wrong

Not every brood succeeds. In cold, wet springs, parents find too few caterpillars and chicks die. Sometimes one parent disappears and the partner is unable to raise the child alone. This is hard to watch, especially for children. Don't intervene anyway. Additional feeding in the box, opening or removing chicks usually does more harm than good and is not permitted for wild birds. Explain to children that such losses are part of nature and that is why titmice lay so many eggs.

## After breeding

When all the young have fledged, you can clean the box as usual in the fall. Be careful not to damage the camera or its cover. More about this in the guide [Cleaning the nest box] (/vogelwissen/nistkasten-reinigen/). A good time to check cables and brackets.

## Use recordings

Many cameras can record videos or store images. This way you can capture special moments, such as hatching or fledging. If you have long recordings, you can turn them into short films using editing programs. And if you observe abnormalities, for example sick adult birds, such recordings can help experts.

## Observe with children

A nesting box camera is a great family project. So that children get as much of it as possible:

- **Keep a breeding diary:** Write down what happens every day: the first piece of moss, the first egg, the number of eggs, the first bird hatched, the fledging. A calendar on the refrigerator door is great for this.
- **Practice counting:** How many times do the parents come to feed in ten minutes? In the morning, at midday, in the evening? Children are often amazed at how much work the parent birds do.
- **Painting and crafting:** Children can capture the development of the chicks in pictures.
- **Prepare for losses:** Explain before brooding that not all chicks survive and that this is normal in nature. This way it hits children less hard when it happens.
- **Experience flying out:** The day of flying out is the highlight. When the chicks start crowding around the entrance hole and looking out for a long time, it's usually only a matter of hours.

## Costs and effort

A simple nesting box camera with WiFi is available for a reasonable amount, but complete sets with a box, camera and cable cost a little more. There may also be a cable, an external socket or a PoE injector. Installation takes an afternoon, and after that the camera hardly needs any care. If you use a camera for several years, you should check cables, seals and brackets every autumn.

## Observe other species with a camera

It's not just tits that can be observed this way. Cameras are also available for **half-caves** where robins or black redstarts breed, for **sparrow colony houses** where you can follow several families at the same time, and for **swift boxes** under the roof. Nature conservation associations and bird observatories also operate public webcams on stork nests, peregrine falcon nests on church towers or barn owl boxes, which can be followed live on the Internet. If you don't have a suitable place yourself, you'll find a good replacement there.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the camera bother the birds?** As far as we know, a properly installed camera without visible light is not noticed by the birds or is quickly ignored. It is crucial that the installation takes place before the breeding season and that no one handles the box during breeding.

**Can I install a camera in an inhabited box?** No. Wait until fall.

**Why do I only see a gray image at night?** In infrared light, the camera shows black and white images. That's normal.

**How ​​much power does a nesting box camera use?** Usually only a few watts, similar to a small LED lamp. This is only a small cost over the course of a breeding season.

**Can I equip several boxes with cameras?** Yes, as long as you hang them with enough distance. Pay attention to the bandwidth of your network.

## Conclusion

A nesting box camera gives you a unique insight into the birds' family life. Choose a camera with close-up, wide angle and invisible infrared light, install it in a suitable box with a separate camera compartment and mount everything in autumn or winter. Ensure a secure power and network connection, protect your neighbors' privacy and leave the box alone while breeding. Then in spring you can experience live how moss and feathers become a nest and how tiny eggs become fledged tits.
