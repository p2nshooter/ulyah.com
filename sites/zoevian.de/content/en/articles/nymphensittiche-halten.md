With their pop-up feather hoods, orange cheek patches and friendly nature, cockatiels have many fans. They are slightly larger and calmer than budgies, like to learn to whistle melodies and often develop a close bond with their people. However, if you want to keep cockatiels, you should know that they need a lot of space, company and time and can live for twenty years.

This guide explains what cockatiels need for a species-appropriate life and what special features you should know.

## Small cockatoos from Australia

Cockatiels, like budgies, come from the interior of Australia. Despite their name, they are not true parakeets, but rather the **smallest representatives of the cockatoos**. You can tell by the **feather hood** that they put up or put on depending on their mood.

In nature, they live in pairs and flocks, move around in search of water and grass seeds, and like to perch on dead branches, where their gray plumage camouflages them well.

## Appearance and genders

Cockatiels are about **30 to 33 centimeters** long, almost half of which is the long tail. The wild form is **grey** with white wing areas and orange-red cheek spots.

- **Male:** Face and hood strong **yellow**, cheek patch bright orange, underside of tail solid dark.
- **Female:** Face gray with only a little yellow, cheek patch paler, **underside of tail banded yellow-gray**, underside of wings often spotted.

In young birds, both sexes look like females, the males only change color after the first moult. In many breeding forms such as Lutino, white-headed or piebald, the gender is more difficult to identify. A vet can diagnose it for sure.

## Never alone

Like all parrot birds, cockatiels are **social animals** that need a partner of their own kind. A person cannot replace this, even if a cockatiel kept alone bonds closely to him. Keeping animals alone often leads to behavioral problems such as constant screaming, feather plucking or excessive fixation.

Harmonize well:

- **Pairs** of males and females
- **Two males** who often get along well
- **Small groups** with enough space

**Society with budgies** is possible if there is enough space and each species has at least one partner of its own. Cockatiels are usually the quieter ones, and you have to make sure that the more lively budgies don't harass them. More about budgies in the guide [Keeping budgies appropriate for their species] (/vogelwissen/wellensittiche-artgerecht-halten/).

## Place: Aviary instead of cage

Cockatiels are good flyers and need a lot of space. Because of their long tail and size, regular cages are almost always too small. Recommended are:

- an **indoor aviary** that is significantly larger than a budgie cage and in which the birds can flap their wings several times,
- or a **bird room** where they move freely.

There is also **daily, several-hour free flight** in a bird-safe room. The guide [planning an aviary and bird room] (/vogelwissen/voliere-und-vogelzimmer-planen/) describes how to design an aviary and bird room and make the room safe.

### Furnishings

- **Natural branches** in different thicknesses that you can gnaw on
- **Climbing and seating options** at different heights
- **Multiple food and water bowls**
- **Bathing opportunity**: Many cockatiels like to be gently “sprayed” with lukewarm water using a spray bottle
- **Nibbleable material** such as twigs, cork and undyed paper

## Night panic: a typical problem

Cockatiels are prone to what is known as **night panic**. All it takes is a noise, a light from the street or a vibration, and the birds flutter wildly through the cage or aviary in complete darkness. They can seriously injure themselves on bars and branches, especially on the wings.

This is how you prevent:

- **A small night light** in the room so that the birds can orientate themselves at night
- **A quiet place to sleep**, not against an outside wall with cars driving by
- **Enough distance** between seating branches and trellis
- **Don't cover completely**, a cloth over the cage can worsen the panic if the birds can't see anything

If it does happen: turn on the lights, talk to the birds calmly and give them time to calm down. Then look for injuries, especially bleeding quills.

## Powder dunes and dust

Like all cockatoos, cockatiels have **powder down**: special feathers that constantly crumble into a fine, white powder. This powder cares for the feathers, but also spreads throughout the room. This can be stressful for people with allergies or respiratory problems. Regular ventilation, vacuuming and an air purifier help. If you are prone to allergies, you should test whether you can tolerate the dust before purchasing.

## feed

Cockatiels need a **varied diet**:

- **Grain mixture for cockatiels** with millet, glossy seeds, oats and low-fat seeds such as sunflower seeds
- **Fresh food** daily: Vegetables such as peppers, carrots, cucumbers, zucchini, corn, broccoli, lettuce without nitrate contamination, plus herbs
- **Fruit** in moderation
- **germinated feed** and semi-ripe grasses, especially during moulting
- **lime and grit**, about a sepia bowl
- **Fresh water**

Cockatiels tend to become overweight if they get too many fatty seeds and treats and don't fly enough. Millet is a treat, not a staple food. Which foods are poisonous can be found in the guide [Feed pet birds properly] (/vogelwissen/ziervoegel-ernaehrung/).

## Whistle and Talk

Many male cockatiels learn to whistle melodies, and some also learn individual words. Females whistle less often. Loud **contact calls** are also typical, especially when a partner or person leaves the room. These calls are part of normal behavior. If you have very noise-sensitive neighbors, you should keep this in mind.

## Understand body language

Cockatiels show their mood very clearly, especially with their hoods:

- **Hood loosely positioned, slightly slanted:** relaxed, curious, attentive.
- **Hood raised steeply:** excited, surprised or alarmed, for example at an unknown noise.
- **Hood laid flat, body crouched:** anxious or defensive. Sometimes there is a hissing sound. Then the bird needs distance.
- **Feathers fluffed up, eyes half closed in the evening:** tired and ready to sleep. On the other hand, persistent fluffiness during the day can be a sign of illness.
- **Beak grinding before falling asleep:** a sign of contentment.
- **Wings slightly spread apart, heart shape made of wings in the male:** Courtship behavior.

If you know these signals, you can respond better to your birds and avoid conflicts.

## Employment and training

Cockatiels are curious and capable of learning. Boredom quickly leads to problems such as plucking or constant calling. Good employment ideas are:

- **Foraging:** Wrap grains in paper, put them in cardboard rolls or hide them in a bowl with scraps of paper.
- **Fresh twigs** with buds and bark for gnawing, for example from willow, hazelnut or unsprayed fruit trees.
- **Climbing landscapes** with ropes, ladders and swings outside the aviary.
- **Clicker training** with small rewards: Many cockatiels quickly learn to climb onto your hand on command, touch a target with their beak or fly back to the aviary. This also makes visits to the vet easier.

It's important to change toys regularly to keep them interesting and to keep training sessions short, just a few minutes a day.

## Typical posture errors

- **Lonely kept**, often with the argument that the bird would otherwise not become tame
- **Cage too small** in which the long tail constantly hits the grid
- **No night light**, increasing the risk of night panic
- **Too many sunflower seeds** and treats
- **Not enough free flights**
- **Nest box in a cage**, although it is not intended to be bred

## A day in the life of a cockatiel

What a species-appropriate daily routine can look like:

- **Morning:** Lights on, fresh water and fresh food. The birds preen themselves, call and eat extensively. Many people like to take a short spray shower with lukewarm water.
- **Morning:** several hours of free flight in a bird-safe room, with outdoor seating, a climbing tree and feeding toys.
- **Lunch:** Rest period. Cockatiels like to doze for one to two hours on an elevated branch.
- **Afternoon:** activity again, a short workout, fresh twigs to gnaw on.
- **Evening:** Return to the aviary, final feeding, then rest in a slightly lit room with a night light.

A fixed rhythm gives the birds security and helps avoid night panic and stress.

## Health

- **Look for an avian veterinary practice** early on.
- **Check your weight** regularly with a kitchen scale, as weight loss is often the first sign of illness.
- **Typical problems** include obesity, difficulty laying in females, respiratory diseases and injuries caused by night panic.
- **New birds** should always be quarantined and examined first.

The guide [Identifying a sick pet bird] (/vogelwissen/kranken-ziervogel-erkennen/) describes how you can recognize a sick bird.

## Avoid breeding

Cockatiels breed easily in captivity if they can find a nesting box or dark cave. If you don't want to breed, don't offer a nesting box, avoid dark corners and boxes in which the birds could breed, and make sure you get enough rest at night. Frequent egg laying puts a lot of strain on the female and can lead to laying distress, an emergency.

## Life expectancy and responsibility

Well-kept cockatiels live **15 to 20 years**, some older. Anyone who chooses them assumes responsibility for a long period of life. Also plan for vacations, moves and possible life changes. Many cockatiels are waiting for a new home in animal shelters and bird agencies.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do cockatiels become tame?** With patience, many become very trusting, especially if they were introduced to people early on. Coercion and holding on destroy trust.

**Are cockatiels loud?** They are quieter than many larger parrots, but can be loud with their contact calls.

**Can I keep cockatiels with a dog or cat?** Only with great caution. A predator in the same room means stress for the birds, and a single bite or scratch can be fatal. Free flight only without other animals in the room.

**How ​​do I know that my cockatiels are comfortable?** Happy birds groom themselves extensively, eat well, whistle and call, scratch each other and grind their beaks quietly in the evening.

**Can I keep cockatiels outside?** This is possible in a well-protected outdoor aviary with a frost-free shelter. They need protection from drafts, moisture, cold and predators.

## Conclusion

Cockatiels are gentle, sociable little cockatoos that need a partner of their own kind, plenty of space in the aviary or bird room, and daily free flight. A night light protects against night panic, regular ventilation helps against powder dust, and a varied diet with few high-fat seeds keeps them healthy. Anyone who offers them this will gain loyal companions for many years, who will bring joy every day with their hood and their melodies.
