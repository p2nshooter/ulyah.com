Some bird species are so similar that even experienced observers have to look twice. For some couples a single feature helps, for others only the voice. Anyone who knows these classic pairs of mix-ups will be safer and experience less frustration when observing them.

This guide introduces you to ten pairs and groups that are particularly often confused in the garden and near settlements, each with the most important distinguishing features.

## Why mix-ups are so common

Many closely related bird species have only diverged relatively recently. They share similar habitats, have similar food and therefore look similar. Differences that are difficult for us to recognize are often perceived by the birds themselves through their voices or through color signals in the ultraviolet range. In addition, we usually only see birds for a few seconds, often against the light or half hidden in the foliage. Under these conditions, subtle differences in cap or leg color are not sufficient. That's why it's worth knowing one or two reliable key characteristics for each couple and paying particular attention to them.

## 1. Female house sparrow, dunnock and female chaffinch

Three small brown birds that often peck together on the ground under the feeder.

| feature | House sparrow ♀ | Dunnock | Chaffinch ♀ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beak | thick, conical | **thin, pointed** | conical, slightly slimmer |
| head | grey-brown with a light stripe above the eyes | **slate gray** on head and chest | olive gray |
| Wings | striped, indistinct band | brown striped | **two striking white wing bands** |
| behavior | sociable, loud | solitary, inconspicuous, mouse-like | often in small groups |

**The Key:** Pay attention to the beak first. Thin and pointed means dunnock. Then onto the wings: white bands mean chaffinch.

## 2. House sparrow and tree sparrow

| feature | House sparrow ♂ | Field sparrow |
|---|---|---|
| Vertex | grey | **chocolate brown** |
| cheek | grey | **white with black spot** |
| Throat bib | big, black | small, black |
| Genders | different | **same color** |
| Habitat | Town center, houses | more like outskirts, allotments, field edges |

**The key:** the black cheek patch. More about sparrows in the guide [House sparrow: how to help sparrows] (/vogelwissen/haussperling-spatzen-helfen/).

## 3. Marsh Tit and Willow Tit

One of the most difficult couples ever. Both are grey-brown with a black cap and small black chin patch.

| feature | Marsh Tit | Willow Tit |
|---|---|---|
| cap | **shiny** black, small | **matt** black, extends further into the neck |
| Chin stain | small, sharply defined | slightly larger, blurry |
| Wings | monochrome | often **bright wing field** |
| shape | petite | looks “bull-necked”, head bigger |
| Reputation | **“pitschü”**, spicy | **drawn, nasal “duh-dah-dah”** |
| Habitat | Deciduous forest, parks, gardens | moist forests, softwood, moors |

**The key:** the voice. Visual features are helpful, but not always clear, even for experts. More about tits in the guide [Great Tit and Blue Tit] (/vogelwissen/kohlmeise-und-blaumeise/).

## 4. Black redstart and common redstart

Both of them constantly tremble with their rusty red tails.

| feature | Black redstart | Redstart |
|---|---|---|
| male | **soot black to dark gray**, white wing area | **orange-red breast, black throat, white forehead**, gray back |
| Females | **grey-brown**, dark overall | warmer brown, **light belly** with a touch of orange |
| Habitat | Buildings, roofs, quarries, cities | Gardens with old trees, orchards, sparse forests |
| brood | Half cave, niches on buildings | Tree hollows, nesting boxes with oval holes |
| time with us | March to October, some overwinter | April to September |

**The Key:** Males are easy to distinguish. For females, the overall impression helps: Black redstart females appear dark gray, while redstart females appear lighter and warmer. The guide [nest box types and entry hole] (/vogelwissen/nistkastentypen-einflugloch/) presents box types for both.

## 5. Chiffchaff and Fitis

Two small, olive-green warblers that hardly differ visually, but sing completely differently.

| feature | Chiffchaff | Fitis |
|---|---|---|
| singing | **“zilp-zalp-zilp-zalp”**, monotonous | **softly descending, melancholic verse** |
| legs | mostly **dark** | mostly **light**, flesh-colored |
| Wings | shorter | Hand wings longer, wings appear more pointed |
| Overeye streak | rather unclear | somewhat clearer, more yellowish |
| behavior | frequently wags its tail | rocks less often |
| Return | already in March | more likely in April |

**The Key:** Without singing it is difficult to differentiate. Leg color is a clue, but not certain proof. The guide [Learn Bird Sounds](/vogelwissen/vogelstimmen-lernen/) explains how to learn songs.

## 6. Blackcap and Garden Warbler

| feature | Blackcap | Garden warbler |
|---|---|---|
| cap | **Male black, female reddish brown** | no cap |
| plumage | grey | **plain grey-brown**, without any noticeable features |
| singing | chattering, ends with a loud, **whistling “rollover”** | long, evenly rippling verses |

**The Key:** The blackcap's cap is distinctive. The garden warbler can be recognized precisely because it has no features.

## 10. Young blackbird, starling in winter plumage and song thrush

Three spotted birds on the lawn, causing confusion, especially in summer and autumn.

- **Young Blackbird:** overall reddish brown, spotted, long tail, hops. She often looks a bit disheveled.
- **Star in winter plumage:** dark with many **light spots**, short tail, pointed beak, runs purposefully.
- **Song Thrush:** **light belly with dark drops**, warm brown upperside, hops.

**The key:** Bright spots on a dark background mean starling, dark spots on a light background mean song thrush, a reddish-brown overall picture with spots mean young blackbirds. More about the blackbird in the guide [The Blackbird in the Garden](/vogelwissen/amsel-im-garten/).

## Two bonus pairs for advanced users

### Garden treecreeper and forest treecreeper

Both are small, brown-and-white-flecked birds with a thin, curved beak that climb tree trunks in a spiral pattern. Visually, they are almost indistinguishable: the treecreeper is a little more pure white underneath and often has a clearer stripe above the eyes, the treecreeper appears browner on the flanks and has a slightly longer beak. Certainly the identification is almost only possible through song: the treecreeper sings short, rhythmic verses that are reminiscent of “ti-ti-ti-tiroit”, the forest treecreeper sings a fine, descending verse with a trill at the end. The treecreeper is more common in gardens and parks, while the treecreeper is more common in coniferous and mixed forests.

### Sparrowhawk and hawk

Both are birds of prey with short, round wings and long tails that rush through gardens at lightning speed. The sparrowhawk is significantly smaller, about the size of a pigeon in the female and even smaller in the male. The hawk is as big as a buzzard, appears bulkier and has a stronger chest. A bird of prey that hits a tit at the feeder is usually a sparrowhawk. More about this in the guide [Identifying birds of prey](/vogelwissen/greifvoegel-erkennen/).

## Exercise ideas

- **Specifically search for pairs:** Choose a single pair for a walk, such as Chiffchaff and Fitis in April, and pay attention only to them.
- **Compare photos:** Place photos of both species next to each other and mark the differences with arrows.
- **Play voices:** Listen to the calls of both species back-to-back until you can tell the difference without thinking.
- **Note observations:** Write down which characteristics you used to make a determination. This way you can see which features are most reliable for you.

## General tips for difficult couples

1. **Use voice:** For many couples, voice is the most secure feature.
2. **Consider habitat:** Where the species reside is often a strong clue.
3. **Check season:** Some species are only with us at certain times.
4. **Combine several features:** A single feature can be deceptive, three matching features are already very reliable.
5. **When in doubt, leave it blank:** Write down “marsh or willow tit” if you are unsure. This is more honest than a false determination and is expressly permitted when counting birds.

## When mix-ups are particularly common

In **late summer** the gardens are full of young birds in their first, often inconspicuous coats, and at the same time many adult birds are moulting. This is the most difficult time for regulations. In **winter**, winter guests from the north join in, such as bramblings mingling with chaffinches, or redwings among blackbirds. In **spring**, however, most males wear their most conspicuous dress and sing, which makes identification much easier. If you're just starting out, it's best to start in March or April.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why do females often look so inconspicuous?** In many species, females breed alone and have to be well camouflaged on the nest. The striking coloring of the males, on the other hand, is used to choose a partner and to defend their territory.

**Are there hybrids?** Yes, hybrids occur in some closely related species, for example carrion and hooded crow or house and Italian sparrows. But they are rarely a problem in the garden.

**How ​​do I deal with young birds?** Memorize the juvenile plumage of common species, especially blackbirds, robins, starlings and great tits. Good identification books show them next to the adult birds.

## Conclusion

Mixed-up pairs are part of every bird watcher's everyday life. For many, a single feature helps: the thin beak of the dunnock, the black cheek patch of the tree sparrow, the cap of the blackcap, the bright spots of the starling. For others, such as marsh and willow tits or chiffchaffs and fitis, the vote decides. Combine several characteristics, consider the habitat and season and, if in doubt, don't be afraid to only note the genus.
