Anyone who consciously looks into their own garden for the first time is often surprised at how many different birds are there. Tits and sparrows jostle at the feeder, a blackbird looks for worms on the lawn, a robin sings in the hedge, and swifts circle high above the roof in summer. The good news: Most of the birds you see in the garden belong to a manageable group of around twenty species. Anyone who recognizes these with certainty has already understood a large part of the bird world on their doorstep.

This guide introduces you to the most common garden birds in Germany, sorted by group. For each species you will find the characteristics that are really important, a note about the voice and the typical confusion.

## This is how you go about determining

Before we talk about the individual types, a short guide. Experienced bird watchers don't look at color first, but at four things:

1. **Size and shape:** Is the bird the size of a sparrow, blackbird or pigeon? Is he plump or slim, does he have a long or short tail?
2. **Beak:** A short, strong conical beak belongs to a granivore such as a finch or sparrow. A thin, pointed beak suggests an insectivore such as a robin or wren.
3. **Behavior:** Is the bird hopping or running? Does he climb branches upside down? Does he wag his tail?
4. **Color and drawing:** Only now are the head pattern, wing bands and chest color added.

This order helps because colors can change dramatically depending on the light, season and age of the bird, but the shape does not. Our guide [Learn to identify birds] (/vogelwissen/voegel-bestimmen-lernen/) explains how you can systematically train your eyes.

## The sparrows: house sparrow and tree sparrow

### house sparrow

The house sparrow is regularly the most frequently reported garden bird in the large bird censuses carried out by nature conservation associations. He lives sociably in small groups, chirps loudly from hedges and gutters and likes to take a dust bath in the dry ground.

- **Male:** gray crown, chestnut brown neck, black throat flap that is particularly large and dark in spring, gray cheeks.
- **Female:** plain grey-brown, with a light stripe behind the eye and striped back.
- **Voice:** the well-known “Tschilp”, often in chorus.

### Field sparrow

The tree sparrow is somewhat smaller and more delicate. Males and females look the same. The safest feature is the **black cheek patch on white cheek**, plus a **chocolate brown parting**. He tends to live on the outskirts of town, in allotments and fields. You can read why sparrows have become rarer in many cities and how you can help them in the guide about the [House Sparrow](/vogelwissen/haussperling-spatzen-helfen/).

## The tits

### Great tit

The great tit is the largest and most common of our tits. It has a **black head with white cheeks**, a **yellow underside** and a **black longitudinal stripe** across its chest and belly. In males this stripe is wide and reaches between the legs; in females it is narrower. Their song is a bright, two-syllable “zi-zi-bä” that can be heard from January onwards.

### Blue tit

Smaller, more colorful and more agile: the blue tit has a **sky blue crown**, a white cheek with a black eye stripe, blue wings and a yellow underside without a wide central stripe. She likes to do gymnastics upside down on thin twigs and suet balls. Our guide about [Great Tit and Blue Tit](/vogelwissen/kohlmeise-und-blaumeise/) compares both species in detail.

### More tits

The **coal tit** (small, black head with white neck patch, no yellow breast), the **marsh tit** (grey-brown with a small black cap) and the **long-tailed tit** also appear at the feeder. The latter is a tiny, rounded bird with a very long tail that travels in groups through the trees and makes delicate “Sri-sri” calls.

## Thrushes: Blackbird and Song Thrush

### Blackbird

Almost everyone knows the blackbird. The **male is jet black with a bright orange-yellow beak** and yellow eye ring. The **female is dark brown**, often with a slightly spotted chest area, and has a brownish beak. Young blackbirds are reddish brown and spotted and are therefore often mistaken for thrushes. The song of the blackbird is a melodic, fluting song that is performed in spring at dusk by roof ridges and antennae. More about her life can be found in the guide [The Blackbird in the Garden](/vogelwissen/amsel-im-garten/).

### Song Thrush

The song thrush is slightly smaller than the blackbird, warm brown above and **light with many small, dark drop spots** underneath. It repeats individual motifs of its song two or three times in a row, which distinguishes it well from the blackbird. Her “thrush forge” is also typical: a stone on which she makes snail shells.

## Robin, wren and dunnock

### Robin

Small, plump, with **orange-red chest and forehead** framed by a gray border. The robin is surprisingly trusting and follows people when they are gardening because digging reveals insects and worms. It even sings in winter because both sexes then defend their own territory. More about this in the guide about the [Robin](/vogelwissen/rotkehlchen-im-garten/).

### wren

One of the smallest birds in Europe: brown, round like a ball, with a **steep tail**. He scurries like a mouse through piles of brushwood and thick bushes. His singing is surprisingly loud, blaring and contains rapid trills.

### Dunnock

An inconspicuous bird that is often mistaken for a sparrow. Note the **slate gray head and chest** and **thin, pointed beak**. Dunnocks usually peck inconspicuously on the ground under hedges and bushes.

## Finches

### Chaffinch

The chaffinch is one of the most common breeding birds in Germany. The **male** has a **blue-gray crown, a reddish-brown breast** and two striking **white wing bars**. The female is olive brown, but has the same white wing bars. His singing is a blaring verse that ends with a typical flourish.

### Greenfinch

Strongly built, with a thick, light beak, **olive green plumage** and **bright yellow markings** on the edge of the wings and tail. In spring you can hear his drawled, nasal “dschuuii”. Greenfinches have become rarer in many places in recent years due to a parasitic disease, trichomoniasis. Clean feeding places help, see [Keeping feeding places clean](/vogelwissen/futterstelle-hygiene-krankheiten/).

### goldfinch

The goldfinch or goldfinch is distinctive: **red face, black and white head, golden yellow wing bar**. He loves thistle, teasel and sunflower seeds. If you leave a few seed heads in the fall, you have a good chance of being visited.

### Bullfinch

The bullfinch or bullfinch is a stocky finch with a **black cap** and a short, black beak. The male has a **bright pink breast**, the female has a grey-brown breast. Bullfinches are rather quiet and give themselves away with a quiet, soft “djü”.

## Starlings, pigeons and corvids

### Star

From a distance the starling appears black, up close its plumage shimmers **green and purple**, with many bright spots in autumn and winter. It has a pointed beak, which is yellow during breeding season, and a short tail. On the ground he runs purposefully instead of hopping. Starlings are gifted imitators and incorporate cell phone ringtones or alarm systems into their songs.

### Wood pigeon

You can recognize our largest pigeon by its **white neck patch** and **white wing stripes** in flight. Their call is a muffled, five-syllable “gru-guuh-gu, gru-gu.”

### Magpie, carrion crow and jay

The **Magpie** is black and white with a long, shimmering green tail. The **Carrion Crow** is completely black with a strong beak. The **Jay** is pink-brown with bright **blue-black banded wing feathers** and a white rump that stands out in flight. Our guide to [Corvids in the Garden](/vogelwissen/rabenvoegel-im-garten/) explains what the bad reputation of these birds is all about.

## Other frequent guests

### Black redstart

A bird of roofs and walls. The **male is sooty black** with white wing patches, the female is grey-brown. Both have a **rust-red tail** that they constantly shake. Its scratchy song often begins before dawn.

### Nuthatch

The nuthatch is the only native bird that can climb **upside down down the tree trunk**. It has a **blue-gray upper surface, a black eye stripe** and an orange-beige underside. It gets its name from “gluing”: it uses clay to reduce the size of breeding cavities that are too large.

### Great spotted woodpecker

Black and white patterned with **red undertail coverts**. The male also has a red spot on his neck. Great spotted woodpeckers like to visit suet balls and peanut dispensers. How to distinguish it from its relatives can be found in the guide about [woodpeckers in Germany](/vogelwissen/spechte-in-deutschland/).

### Swifts

Swifts are almost only seen in the air: **crescent-shaped, long wings**, dark plumage, shrill “srieh” calls. They are only with us from the beginning of May to the beginning of August and breed in crevices in buildings. Many people confuse them with swallows; What the difference is and how to help both can be found in the guide about [swallows and swifts](/vogelwissen/schwalben-mauersegler-helfen/).

## The most common mix-ups at a glance

| You see... | It could be... | Pay attention to... |
|---|---|---|
| small brown bird on the ground | House sparrow, dunnock, female chaffinch | Beak shape: thick in sparrows and finches, thin in brownnocks; white wing bars on the chaffinch |
| spotted bird on the grass | young blackbird, song thrush, starling in winter plumage | Spot pattern on light breast (thrush), dots on dark background (starling), reddish-brown overall impression (young blackbird) |
| black bird with yellow beak | Male blackbird, starling during breeding season | long tail and hopping gait (blackbird), short tail and running gait (starling) |
| Black-capped titmouse | Marsh Tit, Coal Tit, Great Tit | white neck spot (coal tit), yellow breast with stripes (great tit), small shiny cap without neck spot (marsh tit) |
| Bird with reddish breast | Robin, bullfinch, chaffinch | orange-red breast with gray edge (robin), black cap (bullfinch), white wing bars (chaffinch) |

## When you see which birds

Not all species are in the garden all year round. Most of the titmice, sparrows, blackbirds, robins, nuthatches, great spotted woodpeckers, magpies and wood pigeons are here all year round, although some species are joined by birds from Northern and Eastern Europe in winter. **Summer guests** such as swifts, house and barn swallows, redstarts and blackcaps spend the winter in southern Europe or Africa. **Winter guests** such as bramblings, redwings and waxwings only appear in the cold season, in large numbers in some years and not at all in others. Our [Bird Year in the Garden](/vogelwissen/vogeljahr-im-garten/) gives you an overview of the year.

## Tips for getting started

- **First observe just one feeding area.** This is where the birds come close and you can compare features at your leisure.
- **Learn the common ones thoroughly.** Anyone who knows house sparrows, great tits, blackbirds and chaffinches will recognize deviations immediately.
- **Note what you see.** Date, time, type and number. After a year you will have a little diary of your garden.
- **Listen.** Many birds are heard before you see them. With the first five to ten voices, the garden suddenly comes to life. Our guide [Learn bird sounds](/vogelwissen/vogelstimmen-lernen/) shows how to do this.
- **Take a pair of binoculars.** Even a simple pair of 8×42 binoculars will reveal details hidden from the naked eye. Which model is suitable can be found in the guide [binoculars for bird watching] (/vogelwissen/fernglas-vogelbeobachtung-kaufen/).

## Why it's worth knowing your garden birds

Anyone who recognizes birds sees their garden with different eyes. You will notice which plants are particularly popular, when the first young birds will fly out and whether the new nesting box has proven itself. Many garden bird species have lost populations in recent decades because of a lack of insects, nesting places and natural corners. Any garden with native shrubs, some wild growth and a clean water source helps. And anyone who reports their observations in one of the large participatory censuses helps experts understand how our birds are doing. The guide on [participating bird counts](/vogelwissen/vogelzaehlung-mitmachen/) explains how this works.

## Conclusion

Twenty species are enough to classify the vast majority of encounters in the garden. First look at size, shape and beak, then at behavior and finally at the colors. Start with the most common species, learn their voices, and record your observations. After just a few weeks you will notice that “some little brown bird” has become a house sparrow, a dunnock or a female chaffinch.
