The robin is one of the most popular birds in Germany. It looks friendly with its large dark eyes and bright orange chest, often comes within one meter of people when gardening and even sings on gray winter days. However, behind the gentle appearance there is an amazingly combative character: robins defend their territory with great determination, even against their own reflection.

In this guide you will learn how robins live, why they sing in winter, where they breed and how you can support them in your garden.

## Recognize the robin

The robin is about 14 centimeters long and weighs only 15 to 20 grams. It appears rounded, especially when it fluffs up in the cold.

- **Face and chest:** orange-red, from forehead to chest. This bib is bordered on the sides **by a bluish gray hem**.
- **Top:** olive brown.
- **Belly:** whitish.
- **Legs:** long and thin, good for hopping on the ground.
- **Beak:** thin and pointed, typical of an insectivore.

Males and females look the same. **Young birds** do not yet have an orange breast. They are brown and have light and dark spots all over, which camouflages them well in the bushes. Only after the first moult in late summer do they get the typical chest plumage.

### Risk of confusion

Sometimes robins are confused with bullfinches. However, the bullfinch has a black cap, a short, thick beak and the male has a rather pinkish-red breast. The male chaffinch also has a reddish breast, but wears conspicuous white wing bands. More about this in the guide [Identifying finches](/vogelwissen/finken-erkennen/).

## A bird with a territory

Robins are extremely solitary creatures. Outside the breeding season, every bird, whether male or female, defends its own territory against members of its species. If a strange robin enters, it is first warned with song. If that doesn't help, what follows is an air of showmanship with a bristling chest and, ultimately, sometimes a violent fight.

The orange chest is the signal. Researchers have observed decades ago that robins will attack even a stuffed robin or just a tuft of orange feathers. This is why a robin may repeatedly fly into a window or car mirror in which it sees its reflection. If this happens to you, it helps to temporarily cover the outside of the window or add patterns to it. You can find information about glass surfaces in the guide [Prevent bird strikes on glass panes] (/vogelwissen/vogelschlag-glasscheiben-verhindern/).

## Why robins sing in winter

In most bird species, only the males sing and only during breeding season. Things are different with robins. Because in autumn and winter each animal has its own territory, both males and females sing. The winter song sounds thinner and somewhat more wistful than the spring song, but is unmistakable: a sparkling, descending series of high tones that looks like a small waterfall.

Pairs form from late winter onwards. The female gives up her territory and moves to the male. Then it is mainly the male who sings again, now stronger and more varied.

Robins also like to sing at dusk and in artificial light. If you hear birds singing under a street lamp at night, you are often hearing a robin and not, as many people assume, a nightingale.

In addition to singing, the robin has a typical call: a hard, clicking **“tick”** that is repeated quickly when excited, like the ticking of an old clock. How you can reliably recognize such calls can be found in the guide [Learn bird sounds] (/vogelwissen/vogelstimmen-lernen/).

## The companion when digging

When you're digging, weeding, or raking leaves in the garden, it often doesn't take long for a robin to appear. It sits on the spade, on a fence post or on a nearby branch and waits. As soon as a worm or larva becomes visible, it shoots down.

This behavior has a natural origin: in the forest, robins follow wild boars and other large animals that break up the ground while digging. The gardener takes on this role in the garden. This closeness is not a sign of tameness, but of cleverness.

## What robins eat

Robins feed primarily on **insects, spiders, worms and small snails** that they find on the ground. In autumn and winter **berries and soft fruits** are added, such as elderberry, ivy, Pfaffenhütchen, hawthorn and rowan.

At the feeding place they like to get:

- **Soft food** from specialist retailers or oat flakes mixed with vegetable oil or fat
- **dried insects** or mealworms, preferably soaked
- **Raisins** and chopped fruit
- finely chopped, unsalted peanuts

On the other hand, robins hardly crack hard grains such as sunflower seeds. Because they forage on the ground, they prefer to take food from a **flat feeding table or directly from the ground** near bushes. Make sure that cats cannot approach there unseen. You can read more about types of food in the guide [Bird food: which type for which bird] (/vogelwissen/vogelfutter-sorten-welcher-vogel/).

## brood and nest

### Where robins breed

Robins build their nest **close to the ground or directly on the ground**, well hidden. Typical places are:

- Niches between tree roots or in embankments
- dense ivy on walls and tree trunks
- Voids in wood piles, compost piles or dry stone walls
- old watering cans, flower pots or buckets lying in a hedge
- **Half-cave nesting boxes** with a large, open front

### The clutch

The nest is a soft shell made of moss, leaves and stalks, lined with hair and fine roots. The female usually lays **five to six eggs**, cream-colored with reddish spots. It breeds for about 13 to 14 days while the male provides it with food. The young stay in the nest for almost two weeks and are then fed by their parents for another three weeks. A second brood follows in many years.

### Protect the nesting site

Because robins nest so deeply, their nests are at risk from cats, hedgehogs, martens and mice. A dense, thorny bush or a piece of wild ivy offers better protection than an open bed edge. Do not disturb nesting robins, even if the nest is in an unusual location such as the garden shed. Wild birds and their nests are protected by law.

## A semi-cave for the robin

If you want to offer robins a nesting place, a **half cave** is suitable: a wooden box whose front is only half closed. This means the bird can fly in and out and still have protection from above and from the sides.

This is how you attach them:

1. **Low but protected:** at about one to two meters high, well hidden in thick ivy, Virginia creeper or a hedge.
2. **Orientation:** to the east or southeast, not in the blazing sun and not on the weather side.
3. **Cat Safe:** not directly over a fence, ledge or area from which a cat can jump.

You can find construction instructions in the guide [Building a nest box](/vogelwissen/nistkasten-bauen-anleitung/), and the guide [Nest box types and entry hole](/vogelwissen/nistkastentypen-einflugloch/) shows which box types are available.

## Do robins move away in winter?

The robin is a **partial migratory**. Some of the German robins stay here in winter, while others move to southwestern Europe and the Mediterranean. Females migrate more often than males, who prefer to stay in their territory. At the same time, robins come to us from Scandinavia and Eastern Europe. The robin that sits at your feeder in January could also come from Sweden. You can read more about the phenomenon in the guide [Migratory birds and winter guests] (/vogelwissen/zugvoegel-und-wintergaeste/).

The train mainly takes place at night. Robins orientate themselves, among other things, on the earth's magnetic field. The ability to sense the magnetic field has been particularly thoroughly researched in experiments with robins.

## How to make your garden robin-friendly

### Leaves and clutter

Robins love gardens with a bit of clutter. In autumn, leave leaves lying under bushes, pile brushwood in a pile and leave a corner with nettles and wild herbs. Insects, spiders and worms live in such corners and there are places to hide.

### Dense, native shrubs

Hedgerows of hawthorn, hazel, privet, dogwood and wild roses provide food, cover and nesting sites. Ivy on walls or trees is a particularly valuable habitat because it provides cover in winter and berries late in the year. You can find a list of plants in the guide [Native Shrubs for Birds](/vogelwissen/heimische-straeucher-fuer-voegel/).

### No poisons

Robins eat almost exclusively animal food as long as it exists. Slug pellets, insecticides and weed killers destroy their food source and can harm the birds themselves.

### water

Robins like to use a shallow trough with clean water for drinking and bathing, especially in midsummer and when there is frost. You can read how to use a waterer hygienically in the guide [bird bath and bird bath] (/vogelwissen/vogeltraenke-vogelbad/).

### Keep an eye on cats

Because robins forage on the ground and nest deeply, they are particularly at risk. Feeders should provide a clear view, and thorny bushes around nesting areas keep cats away. More about this in the guide [Cats and birds in the garden](/vogelwissen/katzen-und-voegel-im-garten/).

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I tame a robin?** Robins often become very trusting at the feeder, some even take mealworms from the palm of their hand. That's nice, but it's still a wild bird. Only feed species-appropriate food and always give him the choice to fly away.

**Why do I hardly see robins in the summer?** During the moult in midsummer, robins retreat into the thick bushes and hardly sing. They are then quiet and inconspicuous, but usually still there.

**How ​​old do robins live?** Most only one to two years because many young birds do not survive their first winter. Individual birds reach a significantly older age.

## Conclusion

The robin is a small bird with a big personality: it sings in winter, defends its territory vigorously and follows the gardener when digging. You can recognize it by its orange-red breast with a gray border, the young by their spotted brown plumage. With a hidden semi-cave, thick hedges, leaves left lying around, soft food on the ground and a garden without poison, you offer it everything it needs to live.
