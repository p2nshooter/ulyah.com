Every spring, countless people call wild bird sanctuaries and veterinarians because they have found a young bird. He sits on the grass, on the sidewalk or in the bushes, can't fly properly and beeps. The first impulse is almost always to take him and save him. In most cases, this is exactly what is wrong, because the bird is neither abandoned nor in distress. He is in a completely normal phase of his development and his parents are nearby.

This guide will help you to correctly assess the situation. You will learn the difference between a nestling and a branchling, find out when a bird really needs help, and what you can do step by step.

## The most important question: nestling or branchling?

Young songbirds go through two phases. How you treat a bird you find depends on what stage it is in.

### The nestling

A nestling belongs in the nest. He's not ready to leave it yet.

- It is **naked or only covered with fluff**, sometimes you can see spiny quills.
- The **eyes are often still closed**.
- He **can't hop, can't reach and can't sit**, but lies on his stomach.
- He seems helpless and cools down quickly.

A nestling outside the nest is **in distress**. He fell out of the nest, was kidnapped by a predator or the nest was destroyed.

### The branchling

A branchlet intentionally left the nest. Many songbirds such as blackbirds, thrushes, robins, titmice and sparrows leave the nest before they can fly properly.

- He is **fully feathered**, although the feathers still look a bit ruffled.
- The **tail is often still short** and the wings are not yet fully grown.
- He can **hop, flutter, hold on** and sits upright.
- He beeps loudly to show his parents where he is.

A branchlet is **not abandoned**. His parents continue to feed him, often for several weeks. They only come by briefly every few minutes to quarter of an hour and in between they look for food. If you just look briefly, you might not see them at all.

## Decision support

| What you see | What it means | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| feathered bird, hops, sits upright, no visible damage | Branchling, normal | Leave it alone, observe from a distance |
| feathered bird on the street, bike path or near a cat | Branch in danger | place in the nearest bush a few meters away |
| naked or downy bird on ground, nest visible and reachable | Nestling, fallen out of the nest | carefully put back in the nest |
| Nestling, nest not accessible or destroyed | Nestling in distress | Spare nest nearby, observe, call station if necessary |
| Bird is bleeding, has a hanging wing, has been grabbed by cat | injured | Wild bird station or veterinarian immediately |
| Bird appears apathetic, fluffy, eyes half closed | sick or weakened | Wild bird station or veterinarian |

## What you do with a scion

In the vast majority of cases, the best help is: **do nothing and keep your distance.**

1. **Go away.** Move far enough away that the parents dare to feed, at least 20 to 30 meters, preferably out of sight.
2. **Observe from a distance.** With binoculars or from the window, you can usually see an adult bird coming and feeding in an hour or two.
3. **Keep dangers away.** Bring dogs and cats into the house as much as possible and discourage children from chasing or petting the bird.
4. **Only take him somewhere else if he is in immediate danger**, such as in the middle of a street. Then place him in the nearest thick bush, no more than a few meters away, so that the parents can find him again via his calls.

### Is it true that parents reject young birds if a human touches them?

No. This is a common myth. Most songbirds have a weak sense of smell and rely primarily on sight and voice. They continue to feed their young even if a human has touched them. Nevertheless, the following applies: Only touch young birds if it is really necessary, because every touch means stress.

## What you do with a nestling

A nestling needs help quickly because it cools down and cannot eat itself.

### Step 1: Find the nest

Look around. The nest is often located directly above it in a bush, a hedge or on the wall of the house. If it is intact and accessible, **carefully** put the nestling back. Warm it briefly in your hands beforehand if it feels cold.

### Step 2: Build a replacement nest

If the nest is destroyed or cannot be reached, you can build a replacement nest: a small basket, a plastic bowl with holes in the bottom, or a flower pot padded with kitchen paper or dry grass. Attach it as close as possible to the original nesting site, protected from sun, rain and cats. Put the nestling in and move away.

### Step 3: Observe

Observe from a safe distance whether the parents accept the replacement nest. This may take a while. If after about one or two hours no adult bird has come or the nestling is weaker, you need professional help.

### Step 4: Call professionals

Call a **wild bird station, wildlife rescue center or veterinary practice** near you. You can find addresses through the nature conservation associations, animal protection associations or the lower nature conservation authority in your city or district. Until handover:

- **Keep warm:** Place the bird in a small cardboard box lined with paper towels and place on a hot water bottle wrapped in a towel. The heat should be comfortable, not hot.
- **Keep quiet and dark:** Lid with air holes on it, place in a quiet place, don't constantly look into it.
- **Do not feed without consultation:** The wrong food can be fatal. Bread, milk, minced meat and earthworms are unsuitable for many young birds.
- **Do not drip water into the beak:** The bird can inhale the water and suffocate.

## Special cases

### Swifts on the ground

Swifts are an exception. They have very short legs and long wings and **cannot take off from the ground**. A swift on the ground, whether young or adult, always needs help. Put it in a box and call a station that knows about swifts. Don't throw it in the air to make it fly. Young swifts rely on special insect food that you cannot provide at home. You can find out more about these special birds in the guide about [Swallows and Swifts](/vogelwissen/schwalben-mauersegler-helfen/).

### Young owls

Young long-eared owls and tawny owls leave the nesting hole long before they can fly and sit as fluffy “branches” in trees, sometimes on the ground. They climb back up with their beaks and claws. Leave her alone. Only if a young owl is obviously injured or sitting on a road should you carefully place it on a nearby branch, preferably with gloves.

### Ducklings and goslings

Ducklings without a mother, wandering alone, have lost their family. They usually cannot be reunited if the mother is not in sight. In this case, call a station.

### Brought by the cat

A bird caught in a cat's mouth is **always an emergency**, even if it looks unharmed. Cat saliva contains bacteria that cause life-threatening infections through even tiny wounds. Take him to the vet or the station quickly. The guide [Cats and Birds in the Garden](/vogelwissen/katzen-und-voegel-im-garten/) describes how you can avoid such accidents.

## Which is legally valid

Wild birds are protected. In principle, it is forbidden to catch them or take them from the wild. However, the Federal Nature Conservation Act expressly allows injured, helpless or sick animals to be temporarily taken in in order to nurse them back to health. They must then be released immediately as soon as they can sustain themselves. In practice this means: hand the bird over to a specialist station. Raising young birds yourself is difficult, and birds that are imprinted on humans often have poor chances in the wild.

## Prevention: How to protect young birds in the garden

- **Leave cats indoors in the morning during the breeding season:** There are a lot of branches out and about, especially in the early hours of the morning. More about this in the guide [Cats and birds in the garden](/vogelwissen/katzen-und-voegel-im-garten/).
- **Do not cut hedges during the breeding season.** Severe pruning is prohibited between March and September anyway.
- **Plant dense shrubs:** They provide hiding places for branches. You can find a selection in the guide [Native shrubs for birds] (/vogelwissen/heimische-straeucher-fuer-voegel/).
- **Mowing the lawn with a view:** Branchlings like to sit in the tall grass. Walk the area first.
- **Keep water sources shallow:** Young birds drown in deep rain barrels and pools every year. An exit aid or a lid prevents this. The guide [bird bath and bird bath] (/vogelwissen/vogeltraenke-vogelbad/) describes a safe waterer.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long are branches fed?** Depending on the species, about two to four weeks. During this time they learn to fly and look for food themselves.

**Can I put food out for a branch?** This is not necessary. The parents look after it, and a food bowl is more likely to attract cats and crows.

**What do I do at night?** A sapling usually spends the night alone in the bushes. That's no reason to bring him into the house.

**What if it rains?** Branches are feathered and can tolerate rain. They seek shelter under leaves. Naked nestlings, on the other hand, need the nest or the warmth of their parents.

## Conclusion

Most young birds people find are offspring that are healthy and cared for by their parents. For them, distance is the best help. A naked or downy nestling, on the other hand, belongs back in the nest or in a replacement nest, and if the parents don't come, in a wild bird station. Injured birds, birds from cat's mouths and swifts on the ground always need immediate professional help. Knowing these differences saves bird lives, sometimes by doing nothing.
